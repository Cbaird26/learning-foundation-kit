@article
### Hypothyroidism

In iodine-sufficient countries, **Hashimoto thyroiditis** is the usual cause: autoimmune destruction with lymphocytic infiltrate and germinal centers, **anti-TPO** and anti-thyroglobulin antibodies, Hürthle cells and a firm goiter. Other causes: thyroidectomy, radioiodine, neck radiation, drugs (lithium, amiodarone) and, worldwide, iodine deficiency. Features: fatigue, cold intolerance, weight gain, constipation, dry skin, hair loss, bradycardia, delayed relaxation of deep tendon reflexes, myxedema, carpal tunnel syndrome, hyperlipidemia, raised creatine kinase, hyponatremia, and in pregnancy risk to fetal neurodevelopment. Congenital hypothyroidism (screened at birth) must be treated promptly to prevent intellectual disability.

Diagnosis is **high TSH with low free T4**. Treat with **levothyroxine** on an empty stomach, separated by about 4 hours from calcium, iron and antacids, titrated to TSH. **Subclinical hypothyroidism** (high TSH, normal free T4) is treated if TSH is persistently above about 10 mIU/L, or if the patient is pregnant or attempting pregnancy, has positive anti-TPO with symptoms, or other indications; guidelines differ on lower TSH elevations, especially in older adults.

**Myxedema coma** is decompensated severe hypothyroidism with hypothermia, bradycardia, hypoventilation, hyponatremia and altered mentation, often precipitated by infection, cold or sedatives. Give IV levothyroxine **plus IV hydrocortisone** (to cover coexisting adrenal insufficiency), passive rewarming, ventilatory support and treatment of the trigger.

### Hyperthyroidism (thyrotoxicosis)

**Thyrotoxicosis** is the clinical state of thyroid hormone excess; **hyperthyroidism** is overproduction by the gland. Symptoms: weight loss despite appetite, heat intolerance, palpitations (atrial fibrillation), tremor, anxiety, diarrhea, lid lag, proximal weakness, oligomenorrhea. Labs: **low TSH with high free T4 and/or T3**. Next, determine whether the gland is overactive using **radioactive iodine uptake (RAIU)**:

- **High, diffuse uptake: Graves disease.** Thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulins activate the TSH receptor. Specific features: **ophthalmopathy** (proptosis, from retro-orbital inflammation), **pretibial myxedema**, diffuse goiter, bruit. Orbitopathy can worsen after radioiodine in smokers.
- **High, patchy or focal uptake**: toxic multinodular goiter or a toxic adenoma (focal "hot" nodule with suppression of the rest of the gland).
- **Low uptake**: **thyroiditis** (stored hormone leaks out), exogenous thyroid hormone, or iodine load. **Subacute granulomatous (de Quervain) thyroiditis** follows a viral illness and gives a painful tender gland with high ESR and a thyrotoxic phase followed by hypothyroid phase and then recovery; treat pain with NSAIDs or steroids and use a β-blocker for symptoms (antithyroid drugs do not work). Painless (silent) and postpartum thyroiditis are autoimmune.

**RAIU percentage** $= \dfrac{\text{neck counts} - \text{background}}{\text{standard counts} - \text{background}} \times 100$. A normal 24-hour uptake is roughly 10 to 30%.

Treatment of Graves disease: a **β-blocker** (propranolol) for symptoms, then one of three definitive options: **thionamide** (methimazole, preferred; **propylthiouracil in the first trimester** and in thyroid storm) for 12 to 18 months; **radioactive iodine**, which causes hypothyroidism (contraindicated in pregnancy and breastfeeding); or **thyroidectomy**. Adverse effects: agranulocytosis (fever and sore throat mean stop drug and check a count), PTU hepatotoxicity, and methimazole embryopathy in early pregnancy.

**Thyroid storm** is life-threatening thyrotoxicosis with fever, tachycardia, agitation or delirium, GI symptoms and heart failure, precipitated by infection, surgery or iodine. Treat with β-blocker (propranolol), a thionamide (PTU) to block new synthesis, **iodine given at least an hour after the thionamide** to block release, glucocorticoids (which also reduce T4 to T3 conversion), cooling and supportive care.

### Thyroid nodules and cancer

A nodule starts with **TSH**. If TSH is low, perform a radioiodine scan (a hot nodule is rarely malignant). If normal or high, perform **ultrasound** and **fine-needle aspiration** based on size and features. **Papillary carcinoma** (most common; "Orphan Annie" nuclei, psammoma bodies, BRAF or RET/PTC; lymphatic spread, excellent prognosis). **Follicular carcinoma** (capsular or vascular invasion, hematogenous spread; cannot be diagnosed on FNA). **Medullary carcinoma** (C cells, calcitonin, amyloid; MEN 2). **Anaplastic carcinoma** (older adults, aggressive).

@example
title: Thyrotoxicosis with a low uptake
problem: A 38-year-old woman has 3 weeks of palpitations, tremor and a tender neck after a viral upper respiratory infection. TSH is below 0.01 mIU/L, free T4 is 3.1 ng/dL, ESR is 78 mm/h. Neck counts 800, background 400, standard 20400. What is the diagnosis and treatment?
step: TSH is suppressed and free T4 high: thyrotoxicosis. Now decide whether the gland is overproducing.
step: RAIU $= (800 - 400)/(20400 - 400) \times 100 = 2\%$, which is low.
step: Low uptake with a tender gland, high ESR and recent viral illness indicates subacute granulomatous (de Quervain) thyroiditis. Stored hormone is leaking from damaged follicles.
step: Treat symptoms with a β-blocker and pain with NSAIDs (steroids if severe). Thionamides are ineffective because the gland is not overproducing. Warn that a hypothyroid phase may follow.

@mistakes
- Using antithyroid drugs for thyroiditis, where hormone synthesis is not increased.
- Giving iodine before a thionamide in storm, which can supply more substrate for hormone synthesis.
- Giving levothyroxine without hydrocortisone in myxedema coma.
- Ordering a radioactive iodine uptake in pregnancy or breastfeeding.

@practice self
prompt: Explain how you would choose between methimazole, radioactive iodine and thyroidectomy for a 30-year-old woman with newly diagnosed Graves disease who hopes to become pregnant within a year. Name at least one risk of each option.
hint: Consider pregnancy planning, teratogenicity, permanence and the need for lifelong replacement.
solution: Methimazole is an effective first choice and can be continued for 12 to 18 months with remission in some patients; risks are agranulocytosis, hepatotoxicity and, in early pregnancy, embryopathy (so propylthiouracil is used in the first trimester, or a plan is made to switch). Radioactive iodine is definitive but causes hypothyroidism, requires avoiding pregnancy for several months afterward and can worsen orbitopathy. Thyroidectomy is definitive and rapid, with risks of hypoparathyroidism, recurrent laryngeal nerve injury and lifelong levothyroxine. With a pregnancy planned, a reasonable plan is to treat with an antithyroid drug, adjust the agent around conception, or consider definitive therapy and replacement before pregnancy, with shared decision making.
