@article
### Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)

Severe insulin deficiency, with excess glucagon, catecholamines and cortisol, causes unrestrained lipolysis and hepatic **ketogenesis** (acetoacetate and β-hydroxybutyrate), an **anion-gap metabolic acidosis**, hyperglycemia with osmotic diuresis and profound volume depletion. It is classic in type 1 diabetes. Triggers: infection, missed insulin, new-onset diabetes, myocardial infarction, pancreatitis, steroids and **SGLT2 inhibitors** (which can cause **euglycemic DKA**). Features: polyuria, polydipsia, nausea, abdominal pain, **Kussmaul respirations**, fruity breath, dehydration, altered sensorium.

**Diagnosis**: glucose of about 200 mg/dL or higher (or known diabetes), serum ketones (β-hydroxybutyrate about 3 mmol/L or higher, or urine ketones 2+ or more), arterial or venous **pH below 7.30** and/or **bicarbonate below 18 mEq/L** (the 2024 international consensus uses "and/or"), with a raised anion gap:

$$\text{AG} = \text{Na}^+ - (\text{Cl}^- + \text{HCO}_3^-).$$

Total body potassium is depleted even when the serum level is normal or high, because insulin deficiency and acidosis shift potassium out of cells; correcting acidosis and giving insulin will lower serum potassium. Sodium is diluted by hyperglycemia; **corrected Na** $= \text{Na} + 1.6 \times (\text{glucose} - 100)/100$.

**Management (typical adult approach, check current protocols).**
1. **Isotonic saline first**: about 1 to 1.5 L in the first hour, then adjust to hydration and sodium.
2. **Check potassium before insulin.** If K⁺ is below 3.3 mEq/L (3.5 in the 2024 consensus), hold insulin and replace potassium first; if 3.3 to 5.2 (potassium replacement starts below 5.0 in the 2024 consensus), add about 20 to 30 mEq of potassium per liter of fluid, aiming for a serum level of 4 to 5; if above 5.2 (5.0 in 2024), do not give potassium and recheck every 2 hours.
3. **Regular insulin IV infusion**, typically **0.1 units/kg/hour** (a 0.1 units/kg bolus is optional), continued until the ketoacidosis resolves (anion gap closed, or about 12 or lower, with bicarbonate of at least 15 to 18 mEq/L and pH above 7.3).
4. **Add dextrose** (for example 5% dextrose in hypotonic saline) when glucose falls to about 200 to 250 mg/dL, so that insulin can continue to clear ketones without hypoglycemia. Falling glucose does not mean the acidosis is resolved.
5. **Bicarbonate only if pH is below 6.9**. Replace phosphate if severe (below about 1.0 mg/dL) or with cardiac or respiratory compromise.
6. Look for and treat the **precipitant**. Monitor glucose hourly and electrolytes every 2 to 4 hours.
7. **Transition**: give subcutaneous basal insulin with a rapid-acting dose and **overlap by 1 to 2 hours before stopping the infusion**, otherwise rebound ketoacidosis follows because intravenous insulin has a half-life of minutes.

Complications: hypokalemia, hypoglycemia, and **cerebral edema** (mostly children, with rapid osmolality shifts). **Mucormycosis** can complicate DKA, with black nasal eschar.

### Hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state (HHS)

Typically type 2 diabetes in older adults with an acute illness. Enough insulin prevents ketogenesis, so hyperglycemia is extreme (above about 600 mg/dL) with **effective osmolality** above about 320 mOsm/kg (the 2024 consensus uses effective osmolality above 300 or total osmolality above 320), severe dehydration, altered mentation and little or no ketosis (pH above 7.30, bicarbonate above 18 mEq/L, or 15 or more in the 2024 consensus). Effective osmolality $= 2\text{Na}^+ + \text{glucose}/18$ (urea is not included because it crosses membranes freely). Treat with large volumes of isotonic saline first, potassium repletion, and insulin at a lower rate once potassium is at least 3.3 mEq/L (3.5 in the 2024 consensus) and fluids are running; lower sodium and osmolality gradually.

### Hypoglycemia

**Whipple triad**: symptoms of hypoglycemia, a documented low glucose, and relief when the glucose is raised. ADA levels: level 1 is below 70 mg/dL; level 2 is below 54 mg/dL; level 3 is any severe event with altered mental or physical state needing help. Symptoms: adrenergic (tremor, sweating, palpitations, anxiety) and neuroglycopenic (confusion, seizure, coma). **Hypoglycemia unawareness** develops after recurrent episodes.

Treat a conscious patient with **15 to 20 g of fast-acting carbohydrate** (glucose tablets, juice) and recheck in 15 minutes; if unable to take oral intake, use IV dextrose (for example, 25 g) or **intramuscular, subcutaneous or nasal glucagon**.

In patients without diabetes, causes include drugs (insulin, sulfonylureas, alcohol, quinolones), critical illness, liver failure, adrenal insufficiency, and tumors. A supervised **fast** (up to 72 hours) with simultaneous glucose, insulin, C-peptide, proinsulin, β-hydroxybutyrate and a sulfonylurea screen identifies the cause:

| Cause | Insulin | C-peptide | Proinsulin | β-hydroxybutyrate | Sulfonylurea screen |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Insulinoma | high | high | high | low | negative |
| Exogenous insulin | high | **low** | low | low | negative |
| Sulfonylurea | high | high | high | low | **positive** |
| Non-islet tumor (IGF-2) | low | low | low | low | negative |

@example
title: Managing DKA step by step
problem: A 22-year-old woman, 60 kg, has type 1 diabetes and presents with vomiting and deep breathing. Glucose 540 mg/dL, Na 130, K 4.0, Cl 90, HCO₃ 6 mEq/L, venous pH 7.05, β-hydroxybutyrate 6.5 mmol/L. What are the first steps and what insulin rate is used?
step: Diagnosis: the anion gap is $130 - (90 + 6) = 34$, with pH 7.05 and bicarbonate 6: severe DKA.
step: Start isotonic saline immediately, about 1 to 1.5 L over the first hour.
step: Check potassium before insulin: K⁺ is 4.0 (between 3.3 and 5.2; the 2024 consensus limits of 3.5 and 5.0 give the same answer), so insulin may start and about 20 to 30 mEq of potassium per liter of fluid is added.
step: Start IV regular insulin at $0.1 \times 60 = 6$ units/hour.
step: Add dextrose when glucose is about 200 to 250 mg/dL, continue insulin until the gap closes (about 12 or less) with bicarbonate at least 15 to 18, and overlap subcutaneous basal insulin 1 to 2 hours before stopping the drip. Bicarbonate is not needed, since pH is above 6.9.

@mistakes
- Starting insulin before checking potassium; insulin lowers potassium and can cause dangerous arrhythmia.
- Stopping the insulin infusion when glucose falls instead of when the anion gap closes.
- Stopping the IV insulin without an overlap with subcutaneous insulin.
- Missing euglycemic DKA in a patient taking an SGLT2 inhibitor.
