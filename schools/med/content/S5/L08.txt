@article
The **islets of Langerhans** contain **β cells** (insulin, C-peptide and amylin), **α cells** (glucagon), **δ cells** (somatostatin, which inhibits both) and PP cells. They make up 1 to 2% of the pancreas by mass.

### Making insulin

Preproinsulin is cleaved to **proinsulin**, which is cleaved in secretory granules to **insulin plus C-peptide**, released in equimolar amounts. Insulin has a half-life of about 5 minutes, but C-peptide lasts longer (about 30 minutes) and is a useful marker of endogenous insulin secretion because **commercial insulin contains no C-peptide**.

### Glucose-stimulated secretion

1. Glucose enters the β cell through **GLUT2**.
2. **Glucokinase** (the glucose sensor) phosphorylates it, and metabolism raises the ATP:ADP ratio.
3. ATP **closes the K-ATP channel** (Kir6.2 and SUR1 subunits), depolarizing the cell.
4. Voltage-gated calcium channels open, calcium enters, and insulin granules undergo exocytosis.

**Sulfonylureas** (glipizide, glyburide) bind SUR1, close the channel and force insulin release even when glucose is low, hence the risk of hypoglycemia. **Incretins** (**GLP-1** from L cells and **GIP** from K cells) amplify insulin secretion, only when glucose is high, and GLP-1 suppresses glucagon, slows gastric emptying and promotes satiety; the enzyme **DPP-4** degrades them. Oral glucose provokes more insulin than IV glucose (the incretin effect). Amino acids, especially arginine and leucine, and parasympathetic input also stimulate insulin; α₂-adrenergic input inhibits it.

### Insulin action

Insulin binds a **receptor tyrosine kinase**, which activates IRS proteins and the **PI3K–Akt pathway**, causing **GLUT4** translocation to the membrane in skeletal muscle and adipose tissue (not the liver, brain or red cells, which use other transporters). Effects:

- **Liver**: increases glycogen synthesis and lipogenesis, decreases gluconeogenesis, glycogenolysis and ketogenesis.
- **Muscle**: increases glucose uptake, glycogen and protein synthesis.
- **Adipose**: increases glucose uptake and triglyceride synthesis (lipoprotein lipase), inhibits hormone-sensitive lipase.
- **Potassium**: stimulates Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, driving potassium into cells, which is why insulin is used for hyperkalemia and why it can cause hypokalemia.

### Glucagon and counterregulation

**Glucagon** acts on hepatocytes through Gs and cAMP to stimulate glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis and ketogenesis. In the **fed state**, insulin dominates (anabolism). During **fasting**, insulin falls and glucagon rises: glycogen is used first (hours), gluconeogenesis follows, and after several days of fasting the liver produces **ketone bodies** as an alternate fuel for the brain. In hypoglycemia, the body responds in order of glucagon, epinephrine, then cortisol and growth hormone.

### Measuring glycemia

**HbA1c** reflects glycation of hemoglobin over the red cell lifespan, weighted toward the last 2 to 3 months. It is falsely low when red cell turnover is high (hemolysis, recent bleeding, iron or B12 treatment response) and may be unreliable in hemoglobin variants, CKD and pregnancy. Estimated average glucose:

$$\text{eAG (mg/dL)} = 28.7 \times \text{HbA1c} - 46.7.$$

**HOMA-IR** estimates insulin resistance: fasting glucose (mg/dL) × fasting insulin (µU/mL) / 405.

### Connecting to hypercalcemia

PTH-dependent and PTH-independent hypercalcemia continue to appear in cumulative exams. Review: measure calcium (corrected), then PTH; with a high PTH, check urine calcium to separate hyperparathyroidism from FHH; with a low PTH, look for PTHrP, calcitriol and malignancy.

@example
title: Estimating average glucose from HbA1c
problem: A patient's HbA1c is 8.0%. Estimate the average glucose and say how this compares with 6.5%.
step: Use the standard formula: eAG $= 28.7 \times 8.0 - 46.7$.
step: $28.7 \times 8 = 229.6$, and $229.6 - 46.7 = 182.9$ mg/dL.
step: For 6.5%: $28.7 \times 6.5 - 46.7 = 139.85$ mg/dL.
step: An HbA1c of 8.0% corresponds to an average glucose about 43 mg/dL higher than 6.5%, so each 1 percentage point equals about 28.7 mg/dL. Treatment targets are individualized; caution is needed if red cell turnover is abnormal.

@mistakes
- Saying that insulin needs GLUT4 for glucose entry into all tissues; the liver, brain and red cells do not depend on it.
- Interpreting a low HbA1c in a patient with hemolysis as good control.
- Using insulin level instead of C-peptide to detect exogenous insulin; assays vary and commercial analogs may not be detected.
- Forgetting that sulfonylureas cause hypoglycemia because they bypass the glucose sensor.
