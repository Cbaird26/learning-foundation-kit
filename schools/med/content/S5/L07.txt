@article
Calcium is needed for muscle contraction, neuronal excitability, clotting and signalling, so serum levels are tightly held. About half of serum calcium is **ionized (free, active)**, about 40% is bound to albumin, and the rest is complexed with anions. Low albumin lowers the total calcium but not the ionized level. A common correction is

$$\text{corrected Ca} = \text{measured Ca} + 0.8\,(4.0 - \text{albumin in g/dL}),$$

and an ionized calcium measurement is better when albumin is abnormal or the situation is unclear. Acidemia increases the ionized fraction; alkalosis (hyperventilation) decreases it and can cause tetany.

### The regulators

- **PTH** (chief cells; secretion is suppressed by calcium acting on the **calcium-sensing receptor**, CaSR). PTH **raises calcium and lowers phosphate**: it stimulates osteoblasts to express RANKL and so increases osteoclast bone resorption; increases calcium reabsorption in the distal tubule; inhibits phosphate reabsorption in the proximal tubule; and increases renal 1α-hydroxylase to make calcitriol. Continuous excess resorbs bone; intermittent dosing (teriparatide) is anabolic.
- **Vitamin D**: skin (UVB) or diet gives cholecalciferol; the liver makes 25-hydroxyvitamin D (the storage form, measured clinically); the kidney 1α-hydroxylase makes **1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (calcitriol)**, the active form, which increases intestinal absorption of calcium and phosphate and supports bone mineralization.
- **FGF23** (from osteocytes) increases phosphate excretion and suppresses calcitriol; levels are very high in CKD.
- **Calcitonin** (C cells) lowers calcium modestly; it has little role in normal physiology.

### Hypercalcemia

Symptoms: "stones (nephrolithiasis), bones (pain, fractures), groans (constipation, nausea, pancreatitis), psychiatric overtones (depression, confusion)", plus polyuria from nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, shortened QT, and dehydration. First measure PTH:

- **PTH high or inappropriately normal** (PTH-dependent): **primary hyperparathyroidism** (solitary adenoma about 80 to 85%, multiglandular hyperplasia, rarely carcinoma) with high calcium, **low phosphate** and high urinary calcium. **Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia (FHH)**, from an inactivating CaSR mutation, mimics it but has low urinary calcium: a calcium-to-creatinine clearance ratio $= \dfrac{U_{Ca} \times S_{Cr}}{S_{Ca} \times U_{Cr}}$ below about 0.01 suggests FHH. **Lithium** and thiazides also raise calcium by PTH-related mechanisms.
- **PTH suppressed** (PTH-independent): **malignancy** (PTHrP in squamous cell carcinoma and others; osteolytic metastases or myeloma; lymphoma making calcitriol), granulomatous disease (sarcoidosis: macrophage 1α-hydroxylase), vitamin D intoxication, milk-alkali syndrome, hyperthyroidism, immobilization. Malignancy is the commonest cause in hospitalized patients.

Treatment of significant hypercalcemia: **isotonic saline** to restore volume, then a **bisphosphonate** (zoledronic acid) or denosumab for malignancy (calcitonin gives a fast but short effect), glucocorticoids for calcitriol-mediated causes (sarcoidosis, lymphoma), treatment of the underlying disease, and dialysis in severe cases. **Parathyroidectomy** is indicated in primary hyperparathyroidism for age under 50, calcium more than 1 mg/dL above the upper limit, osteoporosis or fragility fracture, kidney stones or eGFR below 60 mL/min, or symptoms. **Cinacalcet** (CaSR agonist) is an option for those unable to have surgery.

### Hypocalcemia

Causes: **hypoparathyroidism** (after thyroid or neck surgery, autoimmune, DiGeorge syndrome), vitamin D deficiency, CKD (low calcitriol and high phosphate cause secondary hyperparathyroidism), hypomagnesemia (impairs PTH release and action), pancreatitis, **pseudohypoparathyroidism** (target resistance, high PTH; Albright hereditary osteodystrophy with short fourth metacarpals). Signs: perioral and fingertip paresthesias, **Chvostek sign** (facial twitch on tapping the facial nerve), **Trousseau sign** (carpal spasm with a blood pressure cuff inflated above systolic pressure), tetany, seizures, laryngospasm and a **prolonged QT**. Symptomatic or severe hypocalcemia needs **IV calcium gluconate**; chronic hypoparathyroidism needs oral calcium and calcitriol or active vitamin D, with magnesium repletion.

### Bone disease

- **Rickets** (children) and **osteomalacia** (adults): defective mineralization from vitamin D deficiency or phosphate loss; low or low-normal calcium, low phosphate, **high PTH and high alkaline phosphatase**; bone pain and bowing. Treat with vitamin D.
- **Osteoporosis**: low bone mass with fragility fractures; diagnosed by DXA **T-score of −2.5 or lower**, or a hip or vertebral fragility fracture. $T = (\text{BMD} - \text{young-adult mean})/\text{SD}$. Screen women from age 65. Treatment: calcium, vitamin D, weight-bearing exercise, a **bisphosphonate** (alendronate; esophagitis, atypical femur fracture, osteonecrosis of the jaw), denosumab (RANKL antibody), or an anabolic agent (teriparatide) in severe disease.
- **Paget disease of bone**: focal disordered remodeling with **very high alkaline phosphatase** and normal calcium and phosphate; bone pain, skull enlargement, hearing loss, increased risk of osteosarcoma; treat with a bisphosphonate.

@example
title: Working up hypercalcemia
problem: A 56-year-old woman has constipation, polyuria and kidney stones. Serum calcium is 11.4 mg/dL, albumin 4.0 g/dL, phosphate 2.2 mg/dL (low), creatinine 0.9 mg/dL and PTH 92 pg/mL (reference 15 to 65). Urine calcium is 30 mg/dL and urine creatinine is 100 mg/dL. What is the diagnosis?
step: Albumin is normal, so no correction is needed: calcium is truly high.
step: PTH is high with hypercalcemia, so the hypercalcemia is PTH-dependent (a normal parathyroid would be suppressed).
step: Calcium–creatinine clearance ratio $= (30 \times 0.9)/(11.4 \times 100) \approx 0.024$. A ratio above about 0.02 argues against FHH, which gives a ratio below 0.01.
step: High calcium, low phosphate, high PTH, stones and a normal ratio indicate primary hyperparathyroidism, most often from a single adenoma. She has stones, so she meets criteria for parathyroidectomy; localize the adenoma (sestamibi scan or ultrasound) before surgery.

@mistakes
- Treating a low total calcium in hypoalbuminemia without correcting for albumin or measuring ionized calcium.
- Operating on FHH, which is benign and unresponsive to parathyroidectomy.
- Giving a loop diuretic before volume repletion in hypercalcemia.
- Forgetting magnesium repletion in refractory hypocalcemia.

@quiz mc
prompt: A 70-year-old woman with limited sun exposure has diffuse bone pain and proximal weakness. Calcium is 8.1 mg/dL, phosphate 2.0 mg/dL, PTH 190 pg/mL and alkaline phosphatase elevated. What is the most likely diagnosis?
choice: Primary hyperparathyroidism.
choice: Paget disease of bone.

solution: Vitamin D deficiency lowers calcium absorption, causing secondary hyperparathyroidism that lowers phosphate. Defective mineralization gives bone pain, weakness and high alkaline phosphatase. In osteoporosis, calcium, phosphate and alkaline phosphatase are normal.
