@article
Pituitary disease arises from mass effect, hormone excess or hormone deficiency. A **microadenoma** is under 10 mm; a **macroadenoma** is 10 mm or larger. A macroadenoma growing upward compresses the optic chiasm and causes **bitemporal hemianopia** (loss of the temporal visual fields), and can cause headache.

### Prolactinoma

The most common functioning pituitary adenoma. Women present with galactorrhea, oligo- or amenorrhea and infertility (high prolactin suppresses pulsatile GnRH); men often present late with a macroadenoma, low libido, erectile dysfunction and headache or visual loss. Exclude other causes first: **pregnancy**, primary hypothyroidism (raised TRH stimulates prolactin), D2-blocking drugs (antipsychotics, metoclopramide), renal failure, and the **stalk effect** (any mass compressing the stalk removes dopamine, usually giving prolactin below about 100 ng/mL). A prolactin above about 200 ng/mL strongly suggests a prolactinoma. A very large tumor can give a falsely low assay result (the **hook effect**); dilute the sample if a large adenoma has a surprisingly low prolactin. **First-line treatment is a dopamine agonist**, usually cabergoline (bromocriptine is an alternative and is preferred in pregnancy attempts). Surgery is for drug failure or intolerance.

### Acromegaly

Growth hormone excess, almost always from a somatotroph adenoma. In adults it causes coarse features, enlarging hands and feet, prognathism, macroglossia, carpal tunnel syndrome, sweating, hypertension, diabetes and cardiomyopathy; in children before epiphyseal closure it causes gigantism. **Screen with IGF-1**. **Confirm with an oral glucose tolerance test**: after 75 g of glucose, GH normally falls below about 1 ng/mL (a lower cutoff is used with newer assays); in acromegaly it does not suppress (it may paradoxically rise). Then MRI. Treatment is **transsphenoidal surgery**; for residual disease use somatostatin analogs (octreotide, lanreotide), the GH-receptor antagonist pegvisomant (which raises measured GH, so follow IGF-1), or cabergoline.

### Hypopituitarism

Causes: pituitary adenoma or its surgery or radiation, **Sheehan syndrome** (ischemic necrosis after postpartum hemorrhage; first symptom is often failure to lactate), **pituitary apoplexy** (hemorrhage into an adenoma: sudden severe headache, visual loss, ophthalmoplegia, hypotension), traumatic brain injury, infiltrative disease and empty sella. Gonadotropins and GH are usually lost earliest; TSH and ACTH later. Check paired levels: low target hormone with low or inappropriately normal pituitary hormone (low free T4 with low or normal TSH, low testosterone with low LH, low 8 AM cortisol with low ACTH). **Replace glucocorticoid before thyroid hormone**, because thyroxine accelerates cortisol clearance and can precipitate adrenal crisis. In central hypothyroidism titrate to free T4, not TSH. Pituitary apoplexy with hemodynamic instability needs urgent IV hydrocortisone and neurosurgical review.

### Water balance: ADH

**Central diabetes insipidus (DI)**: too little ADH (head trauma, pituitary surgery, tumor, idiopathic). **Nephrogenic DI**: kidneys do not respond (lithium, hypercalcemia, hypokalemia, V2 receptor mutation). Both cause polyuria (over 3 L/day) with dilute urine and, if thirst is impaired or access to water is limited, hypernatremia. In the **water deprivation test**, urine osmolality stays low (under about 300 mOsm/kg) despite dehydration. Then give desmopressin: urine osmolality **rises by more than 50%** in central DI and rises little in nephrogenic DI. **Primary polydipsia** concentrates urine after deprivation and the plasma sodium is low-normal. Treat central DI with desmopressin; treat nephrogenic DI by removing the cause, a low-sodium diet and a thiazide.

**SIADH** is excess ADH (small cell lung cancer, CNS disease, pulmonary disease, drugs such as SSRIs and carbamazepine). It gives **euvolemic hyponatremia** with low plasma osmolality, inappropriately concentrated urine (over 100 mOsm/kg) and urine sodium above 40 mEq/L, once thyroid, adrenal and renal causes are excluded. Treat the cause and restrict fluid; tolvaptan (V2 antagonist) or salt tablets and urea are alternatives. Correct sodium by no more than about 8 mEq/L in 24 hours in chronic hyponatremia to avoid **osmotic demyelination**.

Free water deficit in hypernatremia: $\text{TBW} \times (\text{Na}/140 - 1)$, where TBW is about 0.6 times body weight in kilograms for an adult man.

@example
title: Water deprivation test in a patient with polyuria
problem: A 45-year-old man, 70 kg, passes 6 L of urine a day after pituitary surgery. Serum sodium is 156 mEq/L. After water deprivation his urine osmolality is 220 mOsm/kg; after desmopressin it is 600 mOsm/kg. Interpret the result and estimate his free water deficit (TBW 0.6 times weight).
step: Urine osmolality did not rise with dehydration (220 mOsm/kg), so ADH action is absent. This is DI (central or nephrogenic).
step: After desmopressin the increase is $(600 - 220)/220 \approx 173\%$, well above 50%. The kidney responds to exogenous ADH, so the problem is lack of ADH: central DI, consistent with the history of surgery.
step: TBW $= 0.6 \times 70 = 42$ L. Deficit $= 42 \times (156/140 - 1) \approx 4.8$ L.
step: Treat with desmopressin and replace the deficit slowly (enterally or with hypotonic fluids), lowering sodium by no more than about 10 mEq/L per day to avoid cerebral edema.

@mistakes
- Diagnosing a prolactinoma from a modestly elevated prolactin without excluding pregnancy, hypothyroidism, drugs and the stalk effect.
- Using random GH to screen for acromegaly instead of IGF-1 with a glucose suppression test.
- Starting levothyroxine before glucocorticoid replacement in a patient with possible adrenal insufficiency.
- Calling hyponatremia SIADH without confirming euvolemia and excluding hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency.
