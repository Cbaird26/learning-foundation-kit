@article
The thyroid is made of **follicles**: spheres of follicular cells around a lumen filled with **colloid**, the storage form of hormone, which is rich in thyroglobulin. Between follicles lie parafollicular **C cells** that make calcitonin. Thyroid hormone sets metabolic rate and is needed for brain development in the fetus and infant.

### Synthesis, step by step

1. **Iodide uptake.** The basolateral **sodium–iodide symporter (NIS)** concentrates iodide in the cell by secondary active transport. TSH increases NIS expression. Perchlorate and thiocyanate compete for NIS.
2. **Oxidation and organification.** At the apical membrane, **thyroid peroxidase (TPO)** oxidizes iodide (using hydrogen peroxide) and attaches iodine to tyrosine residues on **thyroglobulin**, forming monoiodotyrosine (MIT) and diiodotyrosine (DIT).
3. **Coupling.** TPO also couples iodotyrosines: DIT + DIT gives **T4**; MIT + DIT gives **T3**.
4. **Storage.** Iodinated thyroglobulin is stored in colloid; the gland holds weeks of hormone.
5. **Release.** TSH triggers endocytosis of colloid, lysosomal proteolysis frees T4 and T3 into blood.

**Thionamides** (methimazole, propylthiouracil) inhibit TPO, blocking organification and coupling. **Excess iodide** transiently inhibits organification (the **Wolff–Chaikoff effect**); a normal gland escapes. A gland with autonomous nodules given an iodine load may become hyperthyroid (**Jod-Basedow phenomenon**). The thyroid releases about 90% T4 and 10% T3; most circulating T3 (about 80%) is made from T4 outside the thyroid.

### Transport and activation

About 99.97% of T4 and 99.7% of T3 is bound, mainly to **thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG)**, plus transthyretin and albumin. Only free hormone acts. **T4** is a prohormone with a half-life of about 7 days; **T3** is the active form (half-life about 1 day), mostly made outside the thyroid. **Type 1 and type 2 deiodinases** convert T4 to T3; **type 3** converts T4 to inactive **reverse T3 (rT3)**. Selenium is needed for deiodinases.

TBG excess (pregnancy, oral estrogen, hepatitis) raises total T4 and T3 but leaves free hormone and TSH normal. TBG deficiency (androgens, glucocorticoids excess, nephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis) lowers totals. This is why guidelines use TSH and free T4, not total T4.

### Actions

Thyroid hormone binds a nuclear receptor and increases transcription. It raises basal metabolic rate and oxygen consumption (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase), increases heat production, upregulates **β₁-adrenergic receptors** (tachycardia, wide pulse pressure, tremor), increases cardiac output, gluconeogenesis and lipolysis, raises LDL receptor expression (so hypothyroidism raises LDL), speeds gut motility and bone turnover, and drives growth and neuronal maturation.

### Control and test interpretation

TRH stimulates TSH, which stimulates growth, iodine uptake and hormone release; T3 (made in the pituitary from T4) and T4 inhibit TRH and TSH. The TSH–free T4 relationship is **inverse and log-linear**: a small change in free T4 produces a large opposite change in TSH, so **TSH is the most sensitive screening test** when the pituitary is healthy.

| Pattern | TSH | Free T4 | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary hypothyroidism | high | low | thyroid failure |
| Subclinical hypothyroidism | high | normal | early failure |
| Primary hyperthyroidism | low | high | thyroid excess |
| Subclinical hyperthyroidism | low | normal (T3 normal) | early excess |
| Central hypothyroidism | low or normal | low | pituitary or hypothalamus |
| Euthyroid sick syndrome | normal or low | normal or low | low T3, high rT3 in acute illness |

In **non-thyroidal illness** (sepsis, fasting, major surgery), cytokines and glucocorticoids inhibit type 1 deiodinase and raise type 3, so T3 falls and rT3 rises. Do not treat with thyroid hormone; retest after recovery. In the first trimester, **hCG** (a glycoprotein sharing the alpha subunit with TSH) weakly stimulates the TSH receptor, so TSH is lower than usual; pregnancy-specific reference ranges are used. Biotin supplements can interfere with some immunoassays and produce falsely low TSH and falsely high free T4.

**Levothyroxine** replacement is typically about 1.6 µg/kg/day in a young adult (a typical dose for study; older patients and those with heart disease start lower). Because T4 has a half-life of about 7 days, a new dose reaches steady state in about 5 to 6 weeks, so recheck TSH after about 6 weeks.

@example
title: Same total T4, different diagnoses
problem: A 26-year-old woman starts an oral estrogen contraceptive. Three months later, labs show total T4 13 µg/dL (reference about 5 to 12), TSH 1.8 mIU/L and free T4 1.2 ng/dL (normal). She has no symptoms. Does she have hyperthyroidism?
step: Total T4 is mildly high. Total T4 includes bound hormone, so first ask whether binding protein changed.
step: Oral estrogen increases hepatic synthesis of TBG, which raises the bound pool and total T4.
step: Free T4 is normal and TSH is normal, so the axis senses normal active hormone. A hyperthyroid patient would have a suppressed TSH.
step: Conclusion: no thyroid disease; the abnormality reflects TBG excess. No treatment is needed.

@mistakes
- Ordering or interpreting total T4 alone, especially in pregnancy, estrogen use or liver and kidney disease.
- Treating low T3 during acute illness as hypothyroidism.
- Confusing the Wolff–Chaikoff effect (iodine excess inhibits hormone synthesis) with the Jod-Basedow phenomenon (iodine load causes hyperthyroidism in autonomous tissue).
- Rechecking TSH 2 weeks after a levothyroxine dose change, before steady state.
