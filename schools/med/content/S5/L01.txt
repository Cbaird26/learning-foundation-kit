@article
An **endocrine** hormone is made in one tissue, travels in blood, and acts on a distant target cell that carries the right receptor. Almost every endocrine disease reduces to one of three errors: too much hormone, too little hormone, or a target that does not respond. Learning the axes lets you predict which lab values move together.

### Three chemical classes

- **Peptide and protein hormones** (insulin, GH, ACTH, PTH, TSH) are made as precursors, stored in vesicles and released by exocytosis. They are water soluble, circulate mostly free, have short half-lives (minutes) and bind **cell-surface receptors**. Many use G proteins: Gs raises cAMP (ACTH, TSH, FSH, LH, glucagon, PTH, CRH, GHRH, ADH at V2), Gq raises IP3/DAG and calcium (GnRH, TRH, oxytocin, ADH at V1), and Gi lowers cAMP (dopamine at D2 receptors). Insulin and IGF-1 use receptor tyrosine kinases; GH and prolactin use JAK2-associated cytokine receptors.
- **Steroid hormones** (cortisol, aldosterone, sex steroids, vitamin D) derive from cholesterol, are lipid soluble, diffuse out of the cell as they are made rather than being stored, travel bound to carrier proteins and act on **intracellular (nuclear) receptors** to change gene transcription. Effects are slower and last longer. Half-lives are minutes to hours for the free hormone, but the bound pool buffers them.
- **Amine hormones** are of two kinds. Catecholamines (epinephrine, norepinephrine) behave like peptides: short-lived, surface receptors. Thyroid hormones (T3, T4) are iodinated tyrosines that behave like steroids: carrier-bound, nuclear receptors, long half-life (T4 about 7 days).

### Binding proteins and the free hormone

Only **free** hormone enters cells and feeds back on the axis. Total hormone assays include the bound fraction, so a change in binding protein changes the total without changing the biologically active free level. Oral estrogen raises thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG) and corticosteroid-binding globulin (CBG), raising total T4 and total cortisol while free levels are normal. The fraction free is simply $1 - \text{fraction bound}$.

### The hypothalamic–pituitary axes

The hypothalamus releases hormones into the **hypophyseal portal** vessels, which carry them to the **anterior pituitary** (adenohypophysis). The **posterior pituitary** (neurohypophysis) is different: ADH (vasopressin) and oxytocin are made in the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei and travel down axons to be released from the posterior pituitary.

| Hypothalamic signal | Pituitary hormone | Target and feedback |
|---|---|---|
| CRH | ACTH | Adrenal cortisol inhibits CRH and ACTH |
| TRH | TSH (and prolactin) | Thyroid T3/T4 inhibit TRH and TSH |
| GnRH (pulsatile) | LH, FSH | Gonadal sex steroids and inhibin |
| GHRH (somatostatin inhibits) | GH | IGF-1 from liver; IGF-1 inhibits GH, stimulates somatostatin |
| Dopamine (inhibits) | Prolactin | Prolactin stimulates dopamine release |

TSH, FSH, LH and hCG share an identical **alpha subunit**; the beta subunit gives specificity. Prolactin is the only anterior pituitary hormone under predominantly **inhibitory** control, so cutting the pituitary stalk or giving a D2 blocker **raises** prolactin while lowering the others.

### Reading paired levels

Feedback means a healthy axis keeps the pituitary hormone and the target hormone moving in **opposite** directions. The pair tells you the level of the lesion:

- **Primary** (target gland): target hormone low, pituitary hormone high (low cortisol, high ACTH).
- **Secondary** (pituitary): target hormone low and pituitary hormone low or inappropriately normal (low free T4 with low or normal TSH).
- **Tertiary** (hypothalamus): same pattern as secondary; releasing hormone testing is not routine, so secondary and tertiary are usually grouped as **central**.
- Excess follows the same logic: high target hormone with suppressed pituitary hormone points to autonomous gland production; high target hormone with high or normal pituitary hormone points to autonomous pituitary (or ectopic) secretion.

Hormones released in pulses or with a daily rhythm need timed samples. ACTH and cortisol peak near 8 AM; GH is pulsatile, so a random GH is unreliable and **IGF-1** is used as an integrated marker. Excess is confirmed with a **suppression test** (give a feedback signal and see if secretion fails to fall); deficiency is confirmed with a **stimulation test** (give a secretagogue and see if secretion fails to rise).

@example
title: Placing a lesion from a pair of values
problem: A 34-year-old woman has fatigue, cold intolerance and constipation. Free T4 is 0.4 ng/dL (reference about 0.8 to 1.8) and TSH is 0.6 mIU/L (reference about 0.4 to 4.0). Where is the lesion?
step: Free T4 is low, so the thyroid hormone axis is underactive.
step: With a failing thyroid gland (primary), low T4 would remove negative feedback and TSH would be well above normal.
step: Here TSH is normal, which is inappropriately low for such a low T4. The pituitary is not responding to the missing feedback.
step: This is a central (secondary or tertiary) pattern. Next steps: check other pituitary axes (8 AM cortisol, prolactin, LH/FSH with sex steroid, IGF-1) and obtain pituitary MRI. Start glucocorticoid replacement before thyroid hormone if adrenal insufficiency is possible.

@mistakes
- Reading a low T4 with a "normal" TSH as normal; the TSH must be judged against the T4, and a normal TSH is inappropriate when T4 is low.
- Treating total hormone as free hormone: pregnancy, oral estrogen and liver disease change binding proteins and the total level.
- Forgetting that prolactin rises when the hypothalamic connection is lost, because its control is inhibitory (dopamine).
- Using a random GH level to diagnose acromegaly instead of IGF-1 and a glucose suppression test.

prompt: A peptide hormone has a plasma half-life of 30 minutes and a starting concentration of 160 pg/mL, with no further secretion. What is the concentration after 2 hours?
answer: 10
check: 160 * 0.5 ** (120 / 30)
tol: 0.01
unit: pg/mL
hint: Count how many half-lives fit into 2 hours.
hint: 120 minutes is 4 half-lives, so multiply by $(1/2)^4$.
solution: Four half-lives: $160 \times (1/2)^4 = 10$ pg/mL.

@quiz num
prompt: A hormone is infused at a constant rate and clears with first-order kinetics. Using the approximation that steady state is reached after 5 half-lives, what percentage of the final steady-state concentration is reached after exactly 5 half-lives?
answer: 96.875
check: (1 - 0.5 ** 5) * 100
tol: 0.1
unit: %
hint: After each half-life, half of the remaining gap to steady state closes.
hint: The fraction still missing after 5 half-lives is $(1/2)^5$.
solution: Fraction reached $= 1 - (1/2)^5 = 0.96875$, which is 96.875%.

@quiz mc
prompt: A 41-year-old woman has an 8 AM cortisol of 3 µg/dL and an ACTH of 8 pg/mL (reference about 10 to 60). Which interpretation is best?
choice: Primary adrenal insufficiency, because cortisol is low.
choice*: Central (secondary or tertiary) adrenal insufficiency, because ACTH is not elevated in response to low cortisol.
choice: Cushing syndrome, because ACTH is low.
choice: Normal axis; the values reflect diurnal variation.
hint: What should ACTH do when cortisol is low and the pituitary is healthy?
solution: In primary adrenal failure, low cortisol removes feedback and ACTH is high. Low cortisol with low or inappropriately normal ACTH indicates a central cause. A morning value of 3 µg/dL is not explained by diurnal rhythm.
