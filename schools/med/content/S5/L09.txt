@article
**Diabetes mellitus** is chronic hyperglycemia from insufficient insulin action.

### Types

- **Type 1**: autoimmune destruction of β cells (islet autoantibodies: GAD65, IA-2, insulin, ZnT8; HLA-DR3 and DR4), absolute insulin deficiency, onset often in childhood or young adulthood, prone to ketoacidosis, **low C-peptide**.
- **Type 2**: insulin resistance (obesity, inactivity, genetics) with progressive relative β-cell failure, slow onset, usually older and overweight; ketoacidosis is less common. C-peptide is normal or high early. Islet amyloid (amylin) deposits are typical.
- **Gestational diabetes**: glucose intolerance first recognized in pregnancy (screen at 24 to 28 weeks); risk for macrosomia, neonatal hypoglycemia and later type 2 diabetes.
- **Other**: monogenic diabetes (MODY, autosomal dominant, young onset, no obesity, often glucokinase or HNF1A mutations), pancreatic disease (pancreatitis, cystic fibrosis, hemochromatosis, pancreatic cancer), glucocorticoids and other drugs.

### Diagnosis (American Diabetes Association criteria)

| Test | Prediabetes | Diabetes |
|---|---|---|
| Fasting plasma glucose (8 h fast) | 100 to 125 mg/dL | 126 mg/dL or higher |
| 2-hour glucose after 75 g OGTT | 140 to 199 mg/dL | 200 mg/dL or higher |
| HbA1c | 5.7 to 6.4% | 6.5% or higher |
| Random glucose with classic symptoms | not applicable | 200 mg/dL or higher |

In the absence of unequivocal hyperglycemia, abnormal results are confirmed by repeating the test (or two different tests from the same sample). Screen adults beginning at age 35 and earlier if overweight with risk factors.

### Complications

The mechanisms include polyol pathway flux (aldose reductase converts glucose to **sorbitol**), advanced glycation end-products and oxidative stress. Microvascular complications are the hallmark and are reduced by tight glycemic control.

- **Retinopathy**: nonproliferative (microaneurysms, hemorrhages, hard exudates, macular edema) then **proliferative** (neovascularization, vitreous hemorrhage, tractional detachment). Annual dilated eye exam; laser, anti-VEGF injections.
- **Nephropathy**: hyperfiltration, then albuminuria and **Kimmelstiel–Wilson nodular glomerulosclerosis**. Screen yearly with urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio and eGFR; albuminuria is 30 to 300 mg/g (moderate) and above 300 mg/g (severe). Treat with an ACE inhibitor or ARB, an SGLT2 inhibitor, and BP control; finerenone is an option in selected patients.
- **Neuropathy**: distal symmetric "stocking-glove" sensory loss, autonomic (gastroparesis, orthostasis, erectile dysfunction), mononeuropathy. Annual foot exam with a 10 g monofilament.
- **Macrovascular**: accelerated atherosclerosis (coronary disease, stroke, peripheral artery disease) is the leading cause of death. Treat BP (goal below about 130/80), use a statin from age 40 to 75 (the 2026 ACC/AHA guideline starts therapy at 40 or older), and consider aspirin only for selected secondary prevention.

### Treatment

**Type 1**: lifelong insulin, usually **basal–bolus** (long-acting basal insulin plus rapid-acting insulin with meals) or a pump. Typical total daily dose is about 0.5 units/kg/day, half as basal and half as mealtime (typical figures for study; adjust to the patient). Mealtime dose uses an **insulin-to-carbohydrate ratio (ICR)** and a **correction factor (CF)** (mg/dL drop per unit):

$$\text{dose} = \frac{\text{carbohydrate (g)}}{\text{ICR}} + \frac{\text{current glucose} - \text{target}}{\text{CF}}.$$

Teaching rules estimate ICR $\approx 500/\text{TDD}$ and CF $\approx 1800/\text{TDD}$ for rapid-acting insulin, where TDD is total daily dose.

**Type 2**: lifestyle change and **metformin** first (reduces hepatic gluconeogenesis, activates AMPK, causes no hypoglycemia, weight neutral; adverse effects are GI upset, vitamin B12 deficiency and rarely lactic acidosis; avoid at eGFR below 30). Choose additional drugs by comorbidity: for **established atherosclerotic disease, heart failure or CKD**, use an **SGLT2 inhibitor** and/or a **GLP-1 receptor agonist** with proven benefit, independent of the HbA1c. Insulin is used when HbA1c is very high, there are catabolic symptoms, or other drugs are inadequate. The usual target for many adults is HbA1c below 7%, individualized (looser for frail patients, tighter if safely achievable).

@example
title: Calculating a mealtime insulin dose
problem: A man with type 1 diabetes has a glucose of 250 mg/dL before a meal containing 60 g of carbohydrate. His ICR is 1 unit per 15 g, his correction factor is 1 unit per 50 mg/dL and his target is 100 mg/dL. What rapid-acting dose does he take?
step: Carbohydrate coverage: $60/15 = 4$ units.
step: Correction: $(250 - 100)/50 = 3$ units.
step: Add the two components: $4 + 3 = 7$ units of rapid-acting insulin before the meal.
step: He should review the dose against recent hypoglycemia, activity and any insulin still active from a prior dose ("insulin on board").

@mistakes
- Diagnosing diabetes on a single mildly abnormal value without symptoms and without confirmation.
- Forgetting the A1c limitations in anemia, hemolysis and hemoglobinopathies.
- Delaying SGLT2 inhibitor or GLP-1 agonist therapy in heart failure, CKD or atherosclerotic disease because the HbA1c is near goal.
- Using metformin without checking eGFR, or omitting yearly screening for albuminuria, retinopathy and foot disease.
