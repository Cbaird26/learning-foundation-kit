@article
The adrenal gland has a **cortex** and a **medulla** with different origins. The cortex (mesoderm) makes steroid hormones in three layers, from outside in, remembered as **GFR**: the zona **g**lomerulosa (aldosterone), the zona **f**asciculata (cortisol) and the zona **r**eticularis (adrenal androgens, such as DHEA and androstenedione). The **medulla** (neural crest) is made of chromaffin cells that secrete catecholamines: mostly epinephrine, plus norepinephrine.

### Steroidogenesis

All cortical steroids start from cholesterol. The rate-limiting step is delivery of cholesterol into mitochondria by **StAR** protein and its conversion to **pregnenolone** by cholesterol side-chain cleavage enzyme (P450scc, desmolase). ACTH acts through Gs and cAMP to increase StAR and the enzymes. Key enzymes:

- **17α-hydroxylase**: needed for cortisol and sex steroids (not for aldosterone). It is absent in the glomerulosa.
- **21-hydroxylase**: needed for both cortisol and aldosterone.
- **11β-hydroxylase**: final step to cortisol (and to corticosterone).
- **Aldosterone synthase**: found only in the glomerulosa.

Remember that the 17-, 21- and 11-hydroxylation steps are in the order 17, 21, 11 on the route to cortisol; blocks at each step produce a different pattern of excess precursors and hormones (week 6).

### Regulation

**Cortisol**: hypothalamic CRH stimulates pituitary corticotrophs to cleave **POMC** into ACTH (and β-endorphin and melanocyte-stimulating hormones). ACTH stimulates the fasciculata and reticularis. Cortisol inhibits both CRH and ACTH (negative feedback). Secretion is **pulsatile and circadian**: peak at about 8 AM, nadir near midnight; stress, fever, hypoglycemia and surgery raise it. ACTH also trophically maintains the zona fasciculata, so exogenous glucocorticoids cause atrophy and later adrenal insufficiency.

**Aldosterone** is controlled mostly by the **renin–angiotensin system** and by **plasma potassium**; ACTH has a minor, short-term effect. Angiotensin II and hyperkalemia stimulate aldosterone. This explains why secondary adrenal insufficiency (lack of ACTH) does not cause hyperkalemia, while primary adrenal failure does.

### Cortisol: transport and actions

About 90% of circulating cortisol is bound to **corticosteroid-binding globulin (CBG)** and albumin; free cortisol is active. Oral estrogen and pregnancy raise CBG and total cortisol. Cortisol actions are metabolic, cardiovascular and immune:

- raises glucose (gluconeogenesis, insulin resistance), promotes proteolysis in muscle and lipolysis with central fat redistribution
- **permissive** for catecholamine effects on vessels (maintains blood pressure and responses to epinephrine)
- anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive: inhibits phospholipase A2 expression, cytokines and lymphocyte function; causes **neutrophilia** with lymphopenia and eosinopenia
- decreases bone formation, thins skin, and in the fetus stimulates lung surfactant production

### Protecting the mineralocorticoid receptor

Cortisol binds the mineralocorticoid receptor as avidly as aldosterone and circulates at much higher concentrations. In the kidney, **11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 (11β-HSD2)** converts cortisol to inactive cortisone. If this enzyme is inhibited (glycyrrhetinic acid in real black licorice) or deficient, cortisol overstimulates the receptor, causing hypertension, hypokalemia and metabolic alkalosis with **low renin and low aldosterone** (**apparent mineralocorticoid excess**).

### Catecholamine synthesis

Tyrosine → DOPA (**tyrosine hydroxylase**, rate-limiting) → dopamine → norepinephrine (dopamine β-hydroxylase) → epinephrine (**PNMT**, induced by cortisol, which is why epinephrine is made mostly in the medulla). Breakdown by COMT and MAO gives **metanephrines** and vanillylmandelic acid (VMA).

### Measuring adrenal function

Serum 8 AM cortisol, late-night salivary cortisol (free cortisol, reflecting loss of the nadir), 24-hour urine free cortisol, dexamethasone suppression and the cosyntropin (ACTH) stimulation test are covered in the next lesson. Plasma aldosterone with plasma renin activity (PRA) gives the **aldosterone-to-renin ratio (ARR)**, the screening test for primary aldosteronism; a ratio above about 20 to 30 (ng/dL per ng/mL/h) with a raised aldosterone is a positive screen. Drugs such as β-blockers and spironolactone alter results.

### Glucocorticoid equivalence

Approximate anti-inflammatory equivalents: hydrocortisone 20 mg = prednisone 5 mg = methylprednisolone 4 mg = dexamethasone 0.75 mg. Hydrocortisone has the most mineralocorticoid activity, and dexamethasone has essentially none and a long half-life. Fludrocortisone is a potent mineralocorticoid.

@example
title: Converting glucocorticoid doses
problem: A patient has taken prednisone 10 mg daily for 6 months and now needs an equivalent dose of hydrocortisone while hospitalized. Using prednisone 5 mg = hydrocortisone 20 mg, how much hydrocortisone is equivalent, and what is the main risk if prednisone is stopped abruptly?
step: Use the equivalence ratio: hydrocortisone to prednisone is $20 : 5$, or 4 mg of hydrocortisone per 1 mg of prednisone.
step: Hydrocortisone equivalent $= 10 \times 4 = 40$ mg per day (usually divided).
step: After months of exogenous glucocorticoid, ACTH is suppressed and the fasciculata atrophies.
step: Abrupt cessation causes adrenal insufficiency; taper the dose, and consider stress dosing during illness or surgery.

@mistakes
- Assuming adrenal insufficiency from low ACTH causes hyperkalemia; aldosterone depends on angiotensin II and potassium, not ACTH.
- Interpreting a total cortisol without considering CBG changes (oral estrogen, pregnancy).
- Forgetting that dexamethasone has no mineralocorticoid effect and is not measured by most cortisol assays.
- Mixing up the zones: the outer layer makes aldosterone, the middle layer makes cortisol.
