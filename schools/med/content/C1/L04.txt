@article
**Metabolism** is the sum of a cell's chemical reactions. **Catabolic** pathways break molecules down and release energy; **anabolic** pathways build molecules and consume it. The currency is **free energy**, the energy available to do work.

### Free energy
$$\Delta G = \Delta H - T\,\Delta S,$$
where $\Delta H$ is the change in enthalpy, $T$ is the absolute temperature in kelvin and $\Delta S$ is the change in entropy. If $\Delta G < 0$ the reaction is **exergonic** and spontaneous; if $\Delta G > 0$ it is **endergonic**. At equilibrium $\Delta G = 0$. The sign of $\Delta G$ says whether a reaction can go, not how fast: speed is set by the activation energy.

### ATP coupling
ATP hydrolysis to ADP and P$_i$ has $\Delta G^{\circ\prime} \approx -30.5$ kJ/mol. Cells couple it to endergonic reactions, often by phosphorylating a substrate, so the **net** $\Delta G$ of the coupled process is negative. Free energies of coupled reactions add.

### Enzymes
**Enzymes** (mostly proteins) lower the **activation energy** by stabilizing the transition state. They do not change $\Delta G$ or the equilibrium position. The substrate binds the **active site**, which reshapes slightly (induced fit). Many enzymes need **cofactors** (metal ions) or **coenzymes** (NAD$^+$, FAD, many derived from vitamins). Rate depends on temperature and pH: it rises with temperature until the protein denatures, and each enzyme has an optimum pH (pepsin about 2, most cellular enzymes near 7.4).

### Michaelis-Menten kinetics
For many enzymes the initial velocity is
$$v = \frac{V_{\max}[S]}{K_m + [S]}.$$
$V_{\max}$ is the rate when all enzyme is saturated. $K_m$ is the substrate concentration at which $v = V_{\max}/2$; a low $K_m$ means high apparent affinity. On a **Lineweaver-Burk** plot, $1/v$ against $1/[S]$ is a straight line with y-intercept $1/V_{\max}$ and x-intercept $-1/K_m$.

### Inhibitors
- **Competitive:** binds the active site and competes with substrate. $V_{\max}$ is unchanged; apparent $K_m$ rises to $K_m(1 + [I]/K_i)$. High substrate can overcome it. Lines on a Lineweaver-Burk plot meet at the y-axis. Example: methotrexate and dihydrofolate reductase.
- **Noncompetitive:** binds a site other than the active site on enzyme or enzyme-substrate complex. $K_m$ is unchanged; apparent $V_{\max}$ falls to $V_{\max}/(1 + [I]/K_i)$. More substrate cannot overcome it. Lines meet at the x-axis.
- **Irreversible:** forms a covalent bond (aspirin acetylates cyclooxygenase).
- **Allosteric regulation:** a regulator binds a separate site and shifts the enzyme between active and inactive forms, often giving a sigmoidal curve. **Feedback inhibition** has the end product of a pathway inhibit an early enzyme, preventing waste.

To **predict the effect of an inhibitor on rate**, first decide which parameter it changes ($K_m$ or $V_{\max}$), compute the apparent value, then substitute into the Michaelis-Menten equation at the given $[S]$.

@example
title: Predicting the rate with a competitive inhibitor
problem: An enzyme has $V_{\max} = 80\ \mu\text{mol/min}$ and $K_m = 4$ mM. At $[S] = 4$ mM, find the rate without inhibitor and with a competitive inhibitor present at a concentration equal to its $K_i$.
step: Without inhibitor, $[S] = K_m$, so $v = V_{\max}/2 = 40\ \mu\text{mol/min}$.
step: A competitive inhibitor at $[I] = K_i$ multiplies $K_m$ by $1 + 1 = 2$, so apparent $K_m = 8$ mM. $V_{\max}$ is unchanged.
step: With inhibitor, $v = 80 \times 4/(8 + 4) \approx 26.7\ \mu\text{mol/min}$.
step: The rate fell by about one third. At very high $[S]$ it would return toward $80\ \mu\text{mol/min}$, which is the signature of competitive inhibition.
@example
title: Coupling ATP hydrolysis to an unfavorable reaction
problem: Glucose + P$_i$ to glucose-6-phosphate has $\Delta G^{\circ\prime} = +13.8$ kJ/mol. ATP hydrolysis has $\Delta G^{\circ\prime} = -30.5$ kJ/mol. What is the net $\Delta G^{\circ\prime}$ if the two are coupled, as in hexokinase?
step: Add the free energies of the two reactions: $+13.8 + (-30.5)$.
step: The net is $-16.7$ kJ/mol, which is negative, so the coupled reaction proceeds.
@mistakes
- Saying an enzyme changes $\Delta G$ or shifts the equilibrium; it only speeds approach to equilibrium.
- Mixing up the two inhibitor types: competitive changes apparent $K_m$ only, noncompetitive lowers $V_{\max}$ only.
- Using Celsius instead of kelvin in $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$, or forgetting that $\Delta S$ is often given in J rather than kJ.
- Assuming a negative $\Delta G$ means a fast reaction.
@practice num
prompt: A reaction has $\Delta H = +20$ kJ/mol and $\Delta S = +100$ J/(mol K). What is $\Delta G$ in kJ/mol at 298 K?
answer: -9.8
check: 20 - 298 * 100 / 1000
tol: 0.05
unit: kJ/mol
hint: Convert $\Delta S$ to kJ/(mol K) first.
hint: $T\Delta S = 298 \times 0.100 = 29.8$ kJ/mol.
solution: $\Delta G = 20 - 29.8 = -9.8$ kJ/mol, so the reaction is spontaneous at $298$ K because the entropy term outweighs the enthalpy.
@practice num
prompt: An enzyme follows Michaelis-Menten kinetics with $V_{\max} = 100\ \mu\text{mol/min}$ and $K_m = 2$ mM. What is the rate at $[S] = 6$ mM, in $\mu$mol/min?
answer: 75
check: 100 * 6 / (2 + 6)
tol: 1%
unit: µmol/min
hint: Substitute into $v = V_{\max}[S]/(K_m + [S])$.
hint: $100 \times 6 / 8$.
solution: $v = 100(6)/(2 + 6) = 75\ \mu\text{mol/min}$.
@practice mc
prompt: A drug is a competitive inhibitor of an enzyme. Compared with no drug, which change is expected?
choice: $V_{\max}$ decreases and $K_m$ is unchanged.
choice*: The apparent $K_m$ increases and $V_{\max}$ is unchanged.
choice: Both $K_m$ and $V_{\max}$ decrease.
choice: The apparent $K_m$ decreases and $V_{\max}$ increases.
hint: Competitive inhibition can be overcome by saturating substrate.
solution: Because very high substrate outcompetes the inhibitor, $V_{\max}$ is reached eventually. More substrate is needed to reach half-maximal rate, so the apparent $K_m$ increases.
@practice num
prompt: An enzyme has $K_m = 2$ mM. A competitive inhibitor is present at $[I] = 4$ mM with $K_i = 2$ mM. What is the apparent $K_m$ in mM?
answer: 6
check: 2 * (1 + 4 / 2)
unit: mM
hint: Apparent $K_m = K_m(1 + [I]/K_i)$.
hint: $[I]/K_i = 2$.
solution: Apparent $K_m = 2(1 + 4/2) = 2 \times 3 = 6$ mM.

@quiz num

@quiz mc
