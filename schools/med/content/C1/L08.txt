@article
The **cell cycle** is the ordered series of events by which a cell duplicates its contents and divides. **Interphase** has three parts: **G$_1$** (growth, normal function; the cell decides whether to commit), **S** (DNA synthesis) and **G$_2$** (preparation for mitosis). **M phase** includes mitosis and cytokinesis. Cells that stop dividing enter **G$_0$**.

### Mitosis
- **Prophase:** chromosomes condense; each has two **sister chromatids** joined at the centromere; the spindle forms.
- **Prometaphase:** the nuclear envelope breaks down; microtubules attach to kinetochores.
- **Metaphase:** chromosomes align at the metaphase plate.
- **Anaphase:** cohesin is cleaved and sister chromatids separate to opposite poles.
- **Telophase and cytokinesis:** nuclei re-form and the cytoplasm divides (a contractile ring of actin in animals).

A human cell has 46 chromosomes in G$_1$. After S phase it still has 46 chromosomes but 92 chromatids; DNA content doubles from $2C$ to $4C$ and returns to $2C$ in each daughter cell.

### Control by cyclins and CDKs
**Cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs)** are always present but active only when bound to a **cyclin**, whose levels rise and fall. G$_1$/S cyclin-CDK complexes drive entry to S phase; mitotic cyclin-CDK (**MPF**) drives entry to mitosis. **Checkpoints** stop the cycle if something is wrong:
- **G$_1$/S** (restriction point): is the cell large enough, with favorable signals and undamaged DNA?
- **G$_2$/M:** is DNA fully and accurately replicated?
- **Spindle assembly checkpoint:** are all chromosomes attached before anaphase?

The **Rb** protein binds the transcription factor **E2F** and keeps it inactive. Cyclin D-CDK4/6 phosphorylates Rb, releasing E2F to activate S-phase genes. **p53** is activated by DNA damage; it stops the cycle by inducing the CDK inhibitor **p21** and triggers repair or **apoptosis**.

### From growth factor to cell cycle: the RTK-Ras-MAPK pathway
1. A growth factor (for example EGF) binds its **receptor tyrosine kinase**; receptors dimerize and phosphorylate each other on tyrosines.
2. Adaptor **Grb2** binds the phosphotyrosines and recruits **SOS**, a nucleotide exchange factor.
3. SOS makes **Ras** exchange GDP for GTP (**Ras-GTP**, active).
4. Ras-GTP activates **Raf**, which phosphorylates **MEK**, which phosphorylates **ERK**.
5. ERK enters the nucleus and activates transcription factors; **cyclin D** is made; Rb is phosphorylated; the cell passes the G$_1$/S checkpoint.

### Cancer
Cancer results from mutations in genes that drive proliferation or restrain it.
- **Proto-oncogenes** become **oncogenes** by gain of function: a Ras mutation such as G12V abolishes GTP hydrolysis so Ras is stuck on; EGFR mutations or HER2 amplification make receptors over-active; BRAF V600E activates the kinase constitutively; BCR-ABL is an always-on tyrosine kinase in chronic myeloid leukemia. These are targets for tyrosine kinase inhibitors (erlotinib, imatinib) and BRAF inhibitors.
- **Tumor suppressors** act by loss of function and generally require both alleles to be lost: **p53**, **Rb**, and NF1 (a Ras-GAP that speeds Ras inactivation).

@example
title: Tracing a RTK mutation to uncontrolled division
problem: A tumor cell has a Ras G12V mutation. Explain why it divides without growth factor.
step: Normally Ras is active only when SOS loads GTP after an RTK signal, and Ras's own GTPase activity switches it off.
step: G12V abolishes GTP hydrolysis, so Ras stays GTP-bound whether or not growth factor binds.
step: Active Ras keeps Raf-MEK-ERK on, so cyclin D keeps being made and Rb is phosphorylated.
step: E2F is released, the G$_1$/S checkpoint is passed, and the cell divides without an external signal. This is a gain-of-function oncogene.
@example
title: Duration of mitosis from a cell count
problem: In an asynchronous culture with a 20-hour cycle, 25 of 500 cells are in mitosis. How long does mitosis last?
step: The fraction of cells in a phase equals the fraction of the cycle that phase occupies: $25/500 = 0.05$.
step: Duration $= 0.05 \times 20\ \text{h} = 1$ hour.
@mistakes
- Saying chromosome number doubles in S phase; it is the chromatid number and DNA content that double.
- Thinking CDKs change concentration; it is the cyclins that rise and fall.
- Calling Ras a tumor suppressor; Ras mutations are gain of function, so Ras is an oncogene.
- Forgetting that tumor suppressors typically need both alleles inactivated, whereas an oncogene needs only one hit.

@practice self
prompt: Trace an RTK pathway from growth factor binding to progression through the G$_1$/S checkpoint, and explain two different ways a mutation in this pathway can contribute to cancer.
hint: Name each component in order, starting with receptor dimerization and ending with Rb phosphorylation.
solution: Growth factor binding dimerizes the receptor and causes autophosphorylation on tyrosines. Grb2 binds the phosphotyrosines and recruits SOS, which loads GTP onto Ras. Ras-GTP activates Raf, then MEK, then ERK. ERK moves to the nucleus and activates transcription of genes including cyclin D. Cyclin D-CDK4/6 phosphorylates Rb, releasing E2F, and the cell enters S phase. Mutations contribute to cancer, for example: an activating EGFR or BRAF mutation, or a GTPase-dead Ras, keeps the pathway on without a signal (oncogene, gain of function); loss of Rb or p53, or of NF1 which inactivates Ras, removes brakes on the pathway (tumor suppressor, loss of function).
