@article
The plasma membrane is a **phospholipid bilayer**: hydrophilic heads face the water on both sides and hydrophobic tails form the interior. In the **fluid mosaic model**, proteins float in the lipid sheet and lipids move laterally. Cholesterol buffers fluidity: it restrains movement at high temperature and prevents tight packing at low temperature. Unsaturated tails and shorter tails increase fluidity. Membrane proteins are **integral** (spanning the bilayer) or **peripheral**, and carbohydrates on the outer face (glycoproteins and glycolipids) mediate recognition.

### Selective permeability
Small nonpolar molecules (O$_2$, CO$_2$, steroid hormones) and small uncharged polar molecules (water, slowly) cross freely. Ions and large polar molecules (glucose) cannot cross without a protein.

### Passive transport
Movement down a gradient, with no energy input:
- **Simple diffusion** through the lipid.
- **Facilitated diffusion** through channels (ion channels, aquaporins) or carriers (GLUT1). It is saturable and specific, but still runs only downhill.
- **Osmosis** is the diffusion of water across a selectively permeable membrane toward the side with higher solute concentration.

### Tonicity and osmotic pressure
**Tonicity** compares solute concentration, counting only solutes that cannot cross the membrane. In a **hypotonic** solution, water enters and an animal cell swells and may lyse; in a **hypertonic** solution, water leaves and the cell shrinks (crenation); in an **isotonic** solution there is no net movement. Plant cells have walls, so they become turgid in hypotonic fluid. The van 't Hoff relation gives the osmotic pressure
$$\Pi = iMRT,$$
where $i$ is the number of particles per formula unit (about 2 for NaCl), $M$ is molarity, $R = 0.08206\ \text{L atm mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$ and $T$ is in kelvin. Normal body-fluid osmolarity is about 300 mOsm/L.

### Active transport
Moving a solute **against** its gradient needs energy.
- **Primary active transport** uses ATP directly. The **Na$^+$/K$^+$ ATPase** pumps 3 Na$^+$ out and 2 K$^+$ in per ATP hydrolyzed, so the cell interior ends up negative, K$^+$-rich and Na$^+$-poor. Cardiac glycosides such as digoxin inhibit it.
- **Secondary active transport** couples uphill movement of one solute to downhill movement of another. SGLT in the intestine and kidney brings glucose in with Na$^+$, using the gradient made by the Na$^+$/K$^+$ ATPase. Symporters move both in the same direction and antiporters in opposite directions.
- **Vesicular transport:** endocytosis (phagocytosis, receptor-mediated uptake of LDL via clathrin-coated pits) and exocytosis. In cystic fibrosis a defective CFTR chloride channel disturbs ion and water movement across epithelia.

### Equilibrium potentials
An ion with a concentration gradient and a permeable channel reaches electrochemical equilibrium when the electrical force balances the chemical one. At $37^\circ$C, with $z$ the charge on the ion and concentrations outside and inside,
$$E_{\text{ion}} \approx \frac{61.5\ \text{mV}}{z}\,\log_{10}\frac{[\text{ion}]_{\text{out}}}{[\text{ion}]_{\text{in}}}.$$
This is the **Nernst equation**. A resting neuron is permeable mainly to K$^+$, so its potential is near $E_K$ (about $-90$ mV).

@example
title: Predicting what a saline solution does to a cell
problem: A red blood cell with a total osmolarity of 300 mOsm/L is placed in 0.2 M NaCl. Treat NaCl as fully dissociated and impermeant. Will the cell swell or shrink?
step: Compute the osmolarity of the solution: $0.2\ \text{M} \times 2$ particles per NaCl $= 0.4$ Osm/L $= 400$ mOsm/L.
step: The solution has more impermeant solute than the cell ($400 > 300$), so it is hypertonic to the cell.
step: Water leaves the cell by osmosis toward the higher solute concentration, so the cell shrinks (crenation).
@example
title: Equilibrium potential of a divalent ion
problem: Calcium is 2 mM outside a cell and 100 nM in the cytosol. What is $E_{\text{Ca}}$ at $37^\circ$C? Use $z = +2$.
step: Put both concentrations in the same unit: $2\ \text{mM} = 2\times10^{-3}$ M and $100\ \text{nM} = 1\times10^{-7}$ M, so the ratio out/in is $2\times10^{4}$.
step: Take the base-10 log: $\log_{10}(2\times 10^{4}) \approx 4.30$.
step: Multiply by $61.5/2 = 30.75$ mV: $E_{\text{Ca}} \approx +132$ mV. The positive sign means inward Ca$^{2+}$ movement is strongly favored.
@mistakes
- Treating facilitated diffusion as active transport; it needs a protein but no energy and runs downhill only.
- Counting the water movement direction backwards: water moves toward the side with more impermeant solute.
- Forgetting the factor $i$ for dissociating solutes such as NaCl or CaCl$_2$ when computing osmolarity.
- Using the same concentration units inconsistently, or leaving out the charge $z$, in the Nernst equation.
