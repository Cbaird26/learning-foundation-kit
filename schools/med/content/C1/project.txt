@project
title: Enzyme kinetics from supplied data
overview: You will analyze supplied measurements of an enzyme reaction: compute reaction rates from a time course, plot how temperature and pH change the rate, and explain the shapes of the curves in terms of protein structure and activation energy. All data below are invented for this exercise.
goal: Compute initial reaction rates from product-versus-time data and from single-time measurements.
goal: Plot rate against temperature and against pH and identify the optimum and the limits of each curve.
goal: Explain each curve using activation energy, collision rate, protein denaturation and ionization of amino acid side chains.
goal: Predict how a competitive and a noncompetitive inhibitor would change the curve of rate against substrate concentration.
step: Read the supplied time course. At 25 C and pH 7 the product formed (micromoles, umol) at 0, 2, 4, 6 and 8 minutes was 0, 10, 20, 30 and 40. Plot product against time, confirm that it is linear, and compute the rate as the slope in umol/min.
step: Read the temperature data. At pH 7, the product formed in a 5 minute assay was 8, 16, 30, 52, 30 and 4 umol at 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 and 60 C. Convert each to a rate in umol/min by dividing by 5.
step: Plot rate against temperature on graph paper or in a spreadsheet. Mark the optimum temperature, and compute the ratio of rates between 20 C and 30 C (the Q10 for that interval).
step: Read the pH data. At 37 C, the product formed in a 5 minute assay was 6, 20, 40, 50, 34 and 12 umol at pH 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9. Convert to rates and plot rate against pH. Mark the optimum pH.
step: Write an explanation of the temperature curve: why the rate rises up to the optimum (activation energy, more molecules with enough energy, more frequent collisions) and why it falls sharply above it (denaturation changes the folding of the protein and the shape of the active site, which is irreversible).
step: Write an explanation of the pH curve: why the rate peaks near pH 7 and falls on both sides (changes in the ionization of active-site side chains and in the ionic and hydrogen bonds that hold the fold together; extreme pH can denature).
step: Predict and sketch how adding (a) a competitive inhibitor and (b) a noncompetitive inhibitor would alter a curve of rate against substrate concentration, stating the effect on Vmax and Km in each case.
deliverable: A table of all computed rates (umol/min) for the time course, temperature series and pH series, with the Q10 calculation shown.
deliverable: Two labeled graphs (rate versus temperature, rate versus pH) with the optimum marked.
deliverable: A written explanation (one page) of the shapes of both curves and the inhibitor predictions.
rubric: Rates are computed correctly: 5 umol/min for the time course; 1.6, 3.2, 6.0, 10.4, 6.0 and 0.8 umol/min for the temperature series; 1.2, 4.0, 8.0, 10.0, 6.8 and 2.4 umol/min for the pH series.
rubric: Graphs have labeled axes with units, the optimum is identified (40 C and pH 7), and the shape (rise then sharp fall for temperature; bell shape for pH) is drawn accurately.
rubric: The temperature explanation distinguishes the rising phase (kinetics) from the falling phase (denaturation), and the Q10 for 20 to 30 C is about 1.9.
rubric: The pH explanation refers to ionization of side chains and disruption of protein structure rather than saying only that the enzyme "does not like" the pH.
rubric: The inhibitor predictions are right: competitive raises apparent Km with Vmax unchanged; noncompetitive lowers Vmax with Km unchanged.
tutor: Why does the rate of an enzyme-catalyzed reaction fall above the optimum temperature when heating usually speeds up chemical reactions?
tutor: Which amino acid side chains could change charge between pH 6 and pH 9, and how might that affect an active site?
tutor: How could I tell from a Lineweaver-Burk plot whether an unknown inhibitor is competitive or noncompetitive?
@review
### Week by week
- **Week 1, biological molecules:** polymers form by dehydration synthesis (releases water) and break by hydrolysis; a chain of $n$ units has $n-1$ bonds. Know the four protein structure levels and Chargaff's rules.
- **Week 2, cells:** prokaryote versus eukaryote; organelle functions; surface area to volume ratio $=6/L$ for a cube; organelle-disease pairs (lysosome and Tay-Sachs disease, mitochondrion and MELAS, peroxisome and Zellweger syndrome).
- **Week 3, membranes:** fluid mosaic model; passive versus active transport; $\Pi = iMRT$; tonicity; Nernst equation; Na$^+$/K$^+$ ATPase (3 Na$^+$ out, 2 K$^+$ in).
- **Week 4, enzymes:** $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$; enzymes lower activation energy only; $v = V_{\max}[S]/(K_m+[S])$; competitive inhibitors raise apparent $K_m$; noncompetitive inhibitors lower $V_{\max}$.
- **Week 5, respiration:** glycolysis (cytosol), pyruvate oxidation and citric acid cycle (matrix), electron transport and ATP synthase (inner membrane); about 32 ATP per glucose with 2.5 and 1.5 ATP per NADH and FADH$_2$.
- **Week 6, photosynthesis:** light reactions make ATP, NADPH and O$_2$ (from water); the Calvin cycle uses 3 ATP and 2 NADPH per CO$_2$.
- **Week 7, signaling:** receptors, second messengers, amplification, termination; occupancy $=[L]/(K_d+[L])$.
- **Week 8, cell cycle:** G$_1$, S, G$_2$, M; cyclin-CDK; Rb and p53; the RTK-Ras-Raf-MEK-ERK pathway; oncogenes versus tumor suppressors.
- **Week 9, meiosis:** homologs separate in meiosis I and chromatids in meiosis II; $2^n$ gametes; nondisjunction.
- **Week 10, inheritance:** Mendel's laws, product and sum rules, X-linked pedigrees; RF $=$ recombinants over total.
- **Week 11, replication:** semiconservative, 5' to 3', primers, Okazaki fragments, proofreading and repair.
- **Week 12, gene expression:** transcription, processing, translation, mutation types, lac operon.

### Self-check
Can you, without notes: draw a Michaelis-Menten curve with and without each inhibitor; list the NADH, FADH$_2$ and ATP made at each stage of glucose oxidation; trace a growth factor to cyclin D; sketch meiosis I and II and show where nondisjunction could occur; and translate an mRNA and classify a mutation?

### Common errors
- Mixing up chromosome number with chromatid number.
- Saying enzymes change $\Delta G$ or equilibrium.
- Forgetting two pyruvates per glucose in the ATP count.
- Writing the RF as parental over total.
- Reading the template strand as the mRNA.
- Forgetting the stop codon when converting between coding length and protein length.
@exam num
prompt: An enzyme has $V_{\max} = 120\ \mu\text{mol/min}$ and $K_m = 3$ mM. A competitive inhibitor is present at 6 $\mu$M with $K_i = 2\ \mu$M. What is the reaction rate at $[S] = 9$ mM, in $\mu$mol/min? Round to one decimal place.
answer: 51.4
check: 120 * 9 / (3 * (1 + 6 / 2) + 9)
tol: 1%
unit: µmol/min
hint: A competitive inhibitor changes the apparent $K_m$ by the factor $1 + [I]/K_i$.
hint: Apparent $K_m = 3(1 + 3) = 12$ mM.
solution: Apparent $K_m = 3(1 + 6/2) = 12$ mM, and $V_{\max}$ is unchanged. $v = 120(9)/(12 + 9) = 1080/21 \approx 51.4\ \mu\text{mol/min}$, compared with $90\ \mu\text{mol/min}$ without inhibitor.

prompt: A human cell must copy $6.4\times10^{9}$ base pairs of DNA during an S phase of 8 hours. Each replication fork copies 50 nucleotides per second. What is the minimum average number of forks that must be active at the same time? Round to the nearest whole number.
answer: 4444
check: 6.4e9 / (50 * 8 * 3600)
tol: 1%
hint: Find how much DNA one fork can copy during S phase.
hint: One fork copies $50 \times 8 \times 3600 = 1.44\times10^{6}$ bp.
solution: One fork copies $50 \times 28{,}800 = 1.44\times10^{6}$ bp in 8 hours. The number of forks is $6.4\times10^{9}/1.44\times10^{6} \approx 4444$, so thousands of origins are needed, which is why eukaryotes have many.
@exam self
prompt: A child has a mutation in a nuclear gene for a subunit of a respiratory chain complex. The mRNA reads 5'-AUG GAG AAA UUU CUG-3' near the start of the coding sequence and the mutation changes the third codon AAA to UAA. (a) Translate normal and mutant sequences and classify the mutation. (b) Explain the effect on oxidative phosphorylation, naming the organelle and the structure involved. (c) Predict the cell's ATP production and metabolic response if the cell lacks functional oxidative phosphorylation, naming the fermentation product.
hint: Translate the first four codons, then consider where the new stop codon appears.
solution: (a) Normal: Met-Glu-Lys-Phe-Leu. Mutant: Met-Glu-stop, a nonsense mutation giving a 2-residue peptide that cannot function. (b) The subunit is missing, so the respiratory chain complex in the inner mitochondrial membrane cannot assemble or pump protons; the proton gradient across the inner membrane falls and ATP synthase makes little ATP. (c) The cell depends on glycolysis for 2 ATP per glucose instead of about 32; NADH is regenerated by converting pyruvate to lactate, so lactate accumulates (lactic acidosis), and tissues with high energy demand (brain, muscle, heart) are most affected.
rubric: Correctly translates both sequences and identifies a nonsense mutation.
rubric: Identifies the inner mitochondrial membrane and the loss of the proton gradient and ATP synthesis.
rubric: Estimates ATP from glycolysis alone (2 per glucose) and names lactate as the fermentation product.
