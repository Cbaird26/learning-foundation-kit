@article
DNA replication is **semiconservative**: each new double helix has one old (template) strand and one newly made strand. The **Meselson-Stahl** experiment showed this: bacteria grown in $^{15}$N-heavy medium and then moved to $^{14}$N medium gave DNA of intermediate density after one generation, and half intermediate, half light after two. A conservative model would have given only heavy and light bands in generation one.

### Initiation
Replication begins at an **origin of replication**. Bacteria have one origin (*oriC*); a human cell has thousands of origins to finish copying its genome during S phase. **Helicase** unwinds the helix, producing a **replication bubble** with two **replication forks**; **single-strand binding proteins** keep strands apart, and **topoisomerase** relieves the supercoiling ahead of the fork by cutting and rejoining DNA (fluoroquinolones target bacterial topoisomerase II, gyrase).

### Synthesis at the fork
**DNA polymerase** adds nucleotides only to a free **3' hydroxyl** group, so it synthesizes only in the **5' to 3'** direction and cannot start a chain alone. **Primase**, an RNA polymerase, lays down a short **RNA primer**.
- **Leading strand:** synthesized continuously toward the fork, needing one primer.
- **Lagging strand:** synthesized away from the fork in short **Okazaki fragments** (100 to 200 nucleotides in eukaryotes; 1000 to 2000 in bacteria), each needing its own primer.
- In *E. coli*, DNA polymerase III is the main replicative enzyme; polymerase I removes RNA primers and fills gaps with DNA; **DNA ligase** seals the nicks. Eukaryotes use polymerase $\delta$ and $\epsilon$ for elongation and polymerase $\alpha$ with primase for priming. A **sliding clamp** keeps the polymerase on the template.

### Accuracy
Raw base selection by the polymerase errs about once per $10^{5}$ bases. Two systems improve this:
1. **Proofreading** by the polymerase's 3' to 5' exonuclease activity, improving accuracy about 100-fold.
2. **Mismatch repair** (MutS/MutL in bacteria; MSH/MLH in humans) recognizes distortions after replication, improving accuracy about 100-fold more. Inherited defects cause **Lynch syndrome** (hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer).

Overall the error rate is about $10^{-9}$ to $10^{-10}$ per base per replication.

### Repair of damage
- **Nucleotide excision repair** removes bulky lesions such as UV-induced thymine dimers; defects cause **xeroderma pigmentosum**.
- **Base excision repair** removes damaged single bases (deamination, oxidation).
- **Double-strand breaks** are repaired by homologous recombination (BRCA1 and BRCA2 are involved) or nonhomologous end joining.

### The end-replication problem
Linear chromosomes cannot be fully copied at the lagging strand's 5' end, so ends would shorten each cycle. **Telomeres** (repeats of TTAGGG in humans) buffer this loss, and **telomerase**, a ribonucleoprotein reverse transcriptase with its own RNA template, extends them in germ cells, stem cells and most cancer cells; most somatic cells lack it, which limits their divisions.

@example
title: Time to replicate a chromosome
problem: A yeast chromosome of 1.2 million base pairs has 4 origins evenly spaced, so each origin is responsible for one quarter. Forks move at 50 nucleotides per second in both directions from each origin. How long does replication take, assuming all origins fire at once?
step: Each origin handles $1.2\times10^{6}/4 = 3.0\times10^{5}$ bp.
step: The two forks from an origin each travel half of that, $1.5\times10^{5}$ bp.
step: Time $= 1.5\times10^{5}\ \text{bp} / 50\ \text{bp/s} = 3000$ s, or 50 minutes.
@example
title: Writing the new strand
problem: A template strand reads 3'-ATGCCGTA-5'. What is the sequence of the newly made complementary strand, written 5' to 3'?
step: Pair each base: A with T, T with A, G with C, C with G, giving the complement written along the template as 5'-TACGGCAT-3' (reading the template from its 3' end gives the new strand from its 5' end).
step: The new strand is antiparallel to the template, which is why the answer is read from the 5' end: 5'-TACGGCAT-3'.
@mistakes
- Saying DNA polymerase can synthesize 3' to 5'; it adds nucleotides to the 3' end only, so chains grow 5' to 3'.
- Forgetting that the lagging strand needs many primers while the leading strand needs one per fork.
- Confusing helicase (unwinds) with topoisomerase (relieves supercoils) or with ligase (seals nicks).
- Assuming the primer is DNA; the primer is RNA and is later replaced with DNA.
