@article
Sexually reproducing organisms alternate between **haploid** ($n$) and **diploid** ($2n$) states. In humans, $2n = 46$ and $n = 23$. Gametes are made by **meiosis**: one round of DNA replication followed by **two** divisions, giving four genetically distinct haploid cells. Fertilization restores the diploid number.

### Meiosis I: homologs separate
After S phase, each chromosome consists of two sister chromatids.
- **Prophase I:** homologous chromosomes pair (synapsis) to form a **bivalent**, held by the synaptonemal complex. **Crossing over** swaps segments between non-sister chromatids; the points of contact are **chiasmata**.
- **Metaphase I:** bivalents line up at the plate, each pair oriented randomly (independent assortment).
- **Anaphase I:** homologs separate; sister chromatids stay together (centromeres do not split).
- **Telophase I:** two haploid cells, each chromosome still having two chromatids. Meiosis I is the **reductional** division.

### Meiosis II: sister chromatids separate
Meiosis II resembles mitosis: chromosomes line up, and in anaphase II sister chromatids separate. The result is four haploid cells with unreplicated chromosomes. There is no DNA replication between the divisions.

### Sources of variation
1. **Independent assortment:** with $n$ chromosome pairs, there are $2^n$ possible gamete combinations even without crossing over. For humans, $2^{23} \approx 8.4$ million.
2. **Crossing over:** produces recombinant chromosomes, enormously increasing diversity.
3. **Random fertilization:** any sperm can fuse with any egg, so the number of zygote combinations is $(2^n)^2$ (ignoring crossovers).

### Mitosis versus meiosis
| Feature | Mitosis | Meiosis |
|---|---|---|
| Divisions | 1 | 2 |
| Daughter cells | 2, diploid | 4, haploid |
| Homologous pairing | No | Yes (prophase I) |
| Crossing over | No (rare) | Yes |
| Genetic result | Identical | Different |
| Function | Growth, repair | Gametes |

### Nondisjunction
**Nondisjunction** is failure of chromosomes or chromatids to separate. It produces **aneuploid** gametes (an extra or missing chromosome).
- In **meiosis I**, a homologous pair fails to separate: all four gametes are abnormal (two $n+1$, two $n-1$).
- In **meiosis II**, sister chromatids fail to separate: two gametes are normal, one is $n+1$ and one is $n-1$.

Fertilization of an $n+1$ gamete gives a **trisomy** ($2n+1$) and of an $n-1$ gamete gives a **monosomy** ($2n-1$). Examples: **Down syndrome** (trisomy 21, 47,XX,+21 or 47,XY,+21; risk rises with maternal age because oocytes remain arrested in prophase I for decades; most cases arise from maternal meiosis I error), **Edwards syndrome** (trisomy 18), **Patau syndrome** (trisomy 13), **Turner syndrome** (45,X, the only viable human monosomy) and **Klinefelter syndrome** (47,XXY; it can arise when a sperm carrying both X and Y, from meiosis I nondisjunction in the father, fuses with a normal egg). Most other aneuploidies cause early miscarriage. A small proportion of Down syndrome results from a Robertsonian translocation of chromosome 21 onto another acrocentric chromosome.

@example
title: Tracking chromosomes and chromatids through meiosis
problem: A diploid cell has $2n = 8$. How many chromosomes and chromatids does it have in prophase I, at the end of meiosis I, and at the end of meiosis II?
step: After S phase, there are 8 chromosomes, each with 2 chromatids: 8 chromosomes and 16 chromatids in prophase I.
step: Meiosis I separates homologs, so each daughter cell has 4 chromosomes, each with 2 chromatids: 4 chromosomes and 8 chromatids.
step: Meiosis II separates sister chromatids, so each of the four cells has 4 chromosomes and 4 chromatids.
@example
title: Nondisjunction in meiosis I versus meiosis II
problem: For one chromosome pair, what gametes result from nondisjunction at (a) meiosis I and (b) meiosis II?
step: (a) At anaphase I both homologs go to the same pole. One daughter cell gets both homologs and the other gets none. Meiosis II then separates their chromatids normally, so the four gametes are $n+1, n+1, n-1, n-1$.
step: (b) Meiosis I is normal, so each daughter cell has one homolog (two chromatids). In one cell, the chromatids fail to separate at anaphase II. That cell gives $n+1$ and $n-1$; the other cell gives two normal gametes.
step: So meiosis I errors leave no normal gametes, while meiosis II errors leave half normal.
@mistakes
- Saying homologs separate in meiosis II; homologs separate in meiosis I and sister chromatids in meiosis II.
- Counting chromosome number by chromatids: a replicated chromosome with two chromatids is still one chromosome.
- Assuming DNA replicates between meiosis I and meiosis II; it does not.
- Treating trisomy as an error in the egg only; nondisjunction can occur in either parent's meiosis.
