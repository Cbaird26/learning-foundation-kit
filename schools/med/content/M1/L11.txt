@article
The neck is a narrow passage that carries the airway, the esophagus, the great vessels and nerves between the head and the thorax. Its structures are packed close together, so small injuries have large effects.

### Triangles

The **sternocleidomastoid (SCM)** divides each side into an **anterior triangle** (midline, mandible and SCM) and a **posterior triangle** (SCM, trapezius and clavicle).

- **Anterior triangle**: divided by the digastric and omohyoid muscles into the **submental**, **submandibular**, **muscular** and **carotid** triangles. The carotid triangle contains the common carotid bifurcation, the carotid sheath and the hypoglossal and vagus nerves.
- **Posterior triangle**: contains the **spinal accessory nerve (CN XI)** superficially, the roots and trunks of the brachial plexus, the subclavian artery, and cervical lymph nodes. The accessory nerve is vulnerable to lymph node biopsy: injury causes weakness of trapezius (shoulder drop, difficulty abducting above horizontal).

The **SCM** is supplied by CN XI (with C2-C3 proprioception), turns the head to the opposite side and tilts it to the same side.

### Fascial layers

The **investing layer** surrounds the neck and encloses SCM and trapezius; the **pretracheal layer** surrounds the thyroid, trachea and esophagus; the **prevertebral layer** surrounds the vertebral column and its muscles; and the **carotid sheath** encloses the common and internal carotid arteries, internal jugular vein and vagus nerve. An infection in the **retropharyngeal space**, between the buccopharyngeal and prevertebral fascia, can spread downward into the **posterior mediastinum**.

### Thyroid and parathyroids

The **thyroid** has two lobes joined by an **isthmus** overlying the second to fourth tracheal rings. It is supplied by the **superior thyroid artery** (first branch of the external carotid) and **inferior thyroid artery** (from the thyrocervical trunk), and moves up with swallowing because it lies within the pretracheal fascia. The **recurrent laryngeal nerve** runs in the tracheoesophageal groove near the inferior thyroid artery; the **external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve** accompanies the superior thyroid artery. At thyroidectomy, the surgeon must preserve both: injury to a recurrent nerve causes **hoarseness** (unilateral), or **stridor** if both are injured; injury to the external branch lowers vocal pitch (cricothyroid weakness). The four **parathyroid glands** lie posterior to the thyroid; their removal or devascularization causes **hypocalcemia** (perioral tingling, tetany, positive Chvostek and Trousseau signs).

The **right recurrent laryngeal nerve** loops around the right subclavian artery, and the **left** around the aortic arch (beside the ligamentum arteriosum), so a left-sided thoracic mass can affect it.

### Larynx and airway

The larynx has **thyroid**, **cricoid** (the only complete ring), **epiglottis**, **arytenoid**, corniculate and cuneiform cartilages. All intrinsic muscles are supplied by the recurrent laryngeal nerve except the **cricothyroid** (external branch of the superior laryngeal). The **posterior cricoarytenoid** is the only muscle that abducts the vocal cords. In an emergency "can't intubate, can't oxygenate" situation, the **cricothyroid membrane**, between the thyroid and cricoid cartilages, is entered (cricothyrotomy). A **tracheostomy** is placed through the 2nd to 4th tracheal rings, below the isthmus or through it.

Vertebral level landmarks: hyoid **C3**, thyroid cartilage **C4-C5** (the common carotid bifurcates at the upper border of the thyroid cartilage, C4), cricoid **C6** (where the larynx becomes trachea and the pharynx becomes the esophagus).

### Carotid arteries

The **common carotid** divides into the **internal** and **external** carotid arteries. The **internal carotid has no branches in the neck**. The external carotid gives, in order: **S**uperior thyroid, **A**scending pharyngeal, **L**ingual, **F**acial, **O**ccipital, **P**osterior auricular, then terminates as the **M**axillary and **S**uperficial temporal. The **carotid sinus** (baroreceptor) and **carotid body** (chemoreceptor) are innervated by the glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX). **Carotid stenosis** is measured as
$$\% \text{ stenosis} = \left(1 - \frac{\text{narrowest diameter}}{\text{normal distal ICA diameter}}\right) \times 100$$
(the NASCET method).

### Veins and central access

The **internal jugular vein** lies lateral to the common carotid in the carotid sheath. For cannulation, the needle enters at the apex of the triangle formed by the sternal and clavicular heads of the SCM, aimed toward the ipsilateral nipple, ideally under ultrasound guidance. The right side is preferred because it is a straighter path to the superior vena cava. The **thoracic duct** enters the left venous angle (junction of the left internal jugular and subclavian veins), so left-sided procedures risk a chyle leak; left supraclavicular nodes (Virchow's node) can reveal abdominal malignancy.

### Scalenes, plexus and sympathetic chain

The **subclavian artery** and the brachial plexus pass between the **anterior and middle scalene** muscles, while the subclavian vein passes in front of the anterior scalene, and the **phrenic nerve (C3-C5)** descends on its anterior surface. The **cervical sympathetic chain** has a **superior cervical ganglion**. Interruption of the sympathetic supply to the head (for example by an apical lung (Pancoast) tumor or carotid dissection) causes **Horner syndrome**: ptosis, miosis and anhidrosis on the same side.

@example
title: Hoarseness after thyroidectomy
problem: A 44-year-old woman has a hoarse, breathy voice immediately after total thyroidectomy for a goiter, but no stridor. Explain the anatomy and risk.
step: The recurrent laryngeal nerve ascends in the tracheoesophageal groove, close to the inferior thyroid artery and the posterior surface of the thyroid lobes.
step: Injury, traction or ligation of one nerve paralyzes the ipsilateral intrinsic laryngeal muscles (except cricothyroid), leaving that vocal cord in a paramedian position.
step: Hoarseness without stridor suggests unilateral injury; bilateral injury would cause airway obstruction.
step: The surgeon identifies the nerve and ligates the inferior thyroid artery near the gland, and should also check calcium for parathyroid injury.

@mistakes
- Saying the internal carotid artery gives branches in the neck.
- Confusing the cricothyroid membrane (emergency airway) with the thyrohyoid membrane.
- Forgetting that the recurrent laryngeal nerve supplies all intrinsic muscles except cricothyroid.
- Thinking the subclavian vein passes behind the anterior scalene; the artery does.
