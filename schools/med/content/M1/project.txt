@project
title: Clinical anatomy portfolio
overview: You will build a portfolio explaining the anatomy behind ten common injuries or procedures. For each entry you trace the structures involved from surface to target, predict what goes wrong, and link each finding to a named structure.
goal: Convert regional anatomy into clinical reasoning by explaining why each injury or procedure produces its signs or risks.
goal: Practice precise anatomical language: relations, layers, levels, nerve roots and vessels.
goal: Show how imaging and surface landmarks guide safe procedures.
step: Choose ten entries from this list (at least one from each of the head and neck, thorax, abdomen and pelvis, and limbs): (1) lumbar puncture at L4-L5; (2) thoracentesis or chest tube placement; (3) tension pneumothorax needle decompression; (4) right internal jugular central venous line; (5) femoral neck fracture; (6) epidural hematoma at the pterion; (7) appendicitis with referred pain; (8) inguinal hernia repair (direct versus indirect); (9) brachial plexus upper trunk injury; (10) common fibular nerve injury at the fibular neck; (11) thyroidectomy complications; (12) straddle injury of the bulbar urethra; (13) occlusion of the left anterior descending coronary artery; (14) cavernous sinus thrombosis from a facial infection.
step: For each entry write a two-sentence clinical scenario that you invent (for example "A 24-year-old man falls astride a bicycle bar and cannot pass urine"). Do not use real patients.
step: Write the anatomy in order: surface landmarks, layers or structures crossed from superficial to deep, the target structure, and neighbors at risk. Name vertebral levels, rib spaces, nerve roots and vessels.
step: For each entry predict the expected signs or complications and explain each with a named structure (for example "wrist drop because the radial nerve is damaged at the spiral groove while triceps branches leave earlier").
step: Describe one imaging view that would show the problem (radiograph, CT, MRI or ultrasound) and what you would see, using the density, intensity and echo rules from week 1.
step: Add one quantitative item to at least three entries: percent stenosis, an index such as ABI, McBurney's point distance, a Glasgow Coma Scale total or a Hounsfield value.
step: Review the portfolio against the rubric, correct vague statements such as "near" or "around", and add a one-page summary table listing each entry, its key structure and its key sign.
deliverable: A ten-entry portfolio, each entry 150 to 250 words with scenario, anatomy, prediction and imaging.
deliverable: A one-page summary table of the ten entries with key structure, level or root, and key sign.
deliverable: A short list of the three entries you found hardest and the facts you now check before using them.
rubric: Anatomy is correct and specific: layers, levels, roots and vessels are named and right.
rubric: Each predicted sign is linked to a named structure and mechanism, not just a diagnosis label.
rubric: Entries cover head and neck, thorax, abdomen and pelvis, and limbs.
rubric: Imaging descriptions use correct directions and signal or density terms.
rubric: Calculations are correct, with units and the formula stated.
safety: This is an invented teaching exercise, not medical advice. Do not apply any procedure description to a real patient; real procedures require training, supervision and current references.
tutor: Quiz me on the layers a needle crosses for my chosen procedure and tell me which one I skipped.
tutor: Give me a new injury scenario and ask me to predict the deficit before telling me the nerve.

### Week by week

1. **Terms and imaging**: anatomical position; sagittal, coronal and transverse planes; proximal and distal; CT numbers $\text{HU} = 1000(\mu - \mu_{\text{water}})/\mu_{\text{water}}$; T1 fat bright, T2 fluid bright.
2. **Back**: 33 vertebrae, 31 spinal nerve pairs; cord ends at L1-L2, dural sac at S2; lumbar puncture layers; posterolateral disc herniation compresses the traversing root.
3. **Thorax wall and lungs**: neurovascular bundle under the rib's lower border; diaphragm T8, T10, T12; right main bronchus wider and more vertical; pleura two ribs below lung.
4. **Heart**: right ventricle anterior, left atrium posterior; LAD (anterior wall), LCx (lateral), RCA (inferior, AV node); percent stenosis and $r^4$.
5. **Abdominal wall**: rectus sheath above and below the arcuate line; inguinal canal; direct (medial) versus indirect (lateral) hernias; SAD PUCKER; Morison's pouch.
6. **Abdominal organs**: celiac T12, SMA L1, IMA L3; portal anastomoses; Calot's triangle; appendix T10 then right iliac fossa; gallbladder T7-T9 and right shoulder via the phrenic nerve.
7. **Pelvis and perineum**: pelvic brim; pudendal nerve S2-S4 around the ischial spine; Colles' fascia and urine extravasation; pectinate line; ureter under uterine artery.
8. **Upper limb**: brachial plexus roots to branches; Erb, Klumpke, radial, median, ulnar, axillary injuries; carpal tunnel; scaphoid.
9. **Lower limb**: femoral head supply from the medial circumflex femoral artery; Trendelenburg; foot drop from the common fibular nerve; NAVEL; ABI.
10. **Head**: SCALP; pterion and middle meningeal artery; epidural versus subdural; foramina; cavernous sinus.
11. **Neck**: triangles; thyroid and recurrent laryngeal nerve; carotid branches; central line anatomy; Horner syndrome.
12. **Cranial nerves**: functions, exits and lesion localization.

### Self-check

- Can you list the layers of a lumbar puncture without notes?
- Can you name the territory and ECG leads for each coronary artery?
- Can you explain why appendicitis pain moves and why gallbladder pain reaches the right shoulder?
- Can you predict the deficit for each brachial plexus injury?
- Can you localize a cranial nerve lesion from a set of signs?

### Common errors

- Mixing left and right on imaging or when naming relations.
- Placing a needle below, not above, a rib.
- Reversing direct and indirect hernia relations to the inferior epigastric vessels.
- Reversing the uvula and tongue deviation rules.
- Treating visceral pain as localized.

@exam mc
prompt: A 28-year-old man with fever and neck stiffness has a lumbar puncture at L4-L5 in the midline. Which sequence correctly lists the structures crossed before CSF is obtained?
choice: Skin, ligamentum flavum, supraspinous ligament, interspinous ligament, epidural space, dura, subarachnoid space
choice*: Skin, subcutaneous tissue, supraspinous ligament, interspinous ligament, ligamentum flavum, epidural space, dura and arachnoid, subarachnoid space
choice: Skin, supraspinous ligament, ligamentum flavum, interspinous ligament, posterior longitudinal ligament, subarachnoid space
choice: Skin, subcutaneous tissue, interspinous ligament, supraspinous ligament, epidural space, ligamentum flavum, dura, subarachnoid space
hint: Go from the spinous process tips inward: first the ligament over the tips, then between them, then between the laminae.
solution: The needle crosses skin, subcutaneous tissue, supraspinous ligament, interspinous ligament, ligamentum flavum, epidural space, dura and arachnoid, then reaches the subarachnoid space. The other sequences misorder the ligaments or include the posterior longitudinal ligament, which lies anterior to the dural sac.

prompt: An angiogram shows a coronary artery with a reference diameter of 3.5 mm and a minimal luminal diameter of 1.4 mm at a lesion. Assuming the same pressure gradient and flow proportional to the fourth power of the radius, what fraction of the normal flow remains through the lesion? Give a decimal to four places.
answer: 0.0256
check: (1.4 / 3.5) ** 4
tol: 0.0005
hint: Radius scales with diameter, so the ratio of radii is the ratio of diameters.
hint: Divide 1.4 by 3.5 and raise the result to the fourth power.
solution: The radius ratio is $1.4/3.5 = 0.4$; flow ratio is $0.4^4 = 0.0256$. This corresponds to a percent stenosis of 60%, and shows how sharply flow falls with narrowing.

@exam self
prompt: A 65-year-old man needs a right internal jugular central venous catheter. Describe the surface landmarks, the layers and relations of the vein, the route the catheter takes to the heart, and at least three structures at risk with the complication each could cause.
solution: Landmarks: the apex of the triangle between the sternal and clavicular heads of the sternocleidomastoid, aimed toward the ipsilateral nipple, under ultrasound. The needle passes skin, subcutaneous tissue, platysma and the investing fascia, then enters the internal jugular vein lateral to the common carotid artery within the carotid sheath. The catheter passes through the brachiocephalic vein into the superior vena cava, with the tip at the junction with the right atrium; the right side is preferred because the path is nearly straight. At risk: the carotid artery (arterial puncture, hematoma), the pleural dome (pneumothorax), the thoracic duct on the left side (chyle leak) and the vagus nerve or phrenic nerve (nerve injury).
rubric: Gives correct landmarks and the relation of the vein lateral to the carotid artery in the carotid sheath.
rubric: Describes the route through the brachiocephalic vein to the superior vena cava.
rubric: Names at least three structures at risk with the corresponding complication.
hint: Move from surface to vein, then from vein to heart.
