@article
The heart lies in the **middle mediastinum**, enclosed by the pericardium. Learn it as a pump with four chambers, four valves, its own blood supply and an electrical wiring system.

### The mediastinum

The mediastinum is the central compartment between the two pleural cavities. A plane from the sternal angle to the T4-T5 disc divides it into **superior** (thymus remnant, great vessels, trachea, esophagus, thoracic duct) and **inferior**. The inferior mediastinum has three parts: **anterior** (fat, nodes, thymus in children), **middle** (heart and pericardium, roots of great vessels, phrenic nerves) and **posterior** (descending aorta, esophagus, azygos system, thoracic duct, sympathetic trunks, vagal plexus).

### Pericardium

The **fibrous pericardium** is a tough outer sac; the **serous pericardium** has a parietal layer lining it and a visceral layer (epicardium) on the heart. Fluid in the pericardial cavity compresses the thin-walled chambers: **cardiac tamponade** produces hypotension, jugular venous distension and muffled heart sounds (Beck's triad). Pericardiocentesis is performed from the subxiphoid approach, aiming toward the left shoulder.

### Chambers and valves

| Chamber | Key features |
|---|---|
| Right atrium | Forms the right border; pectinate muscles anterior to the crista terminalis; fossa ovalis (remnant of the foramen ovale) on the septum; SA node near the SVC opening; AV node in the triangle of Koch |
| Right ventricle | Forms most of the anterior surface; trabeculae carneae; moderator band (septomarginal trabecula) carries the right bundle branch; tricuspid valve with chordae and papillary muscles |
| Left atrium | Forms most of the posterior surface (base); smooth wall except for the auricle; lies directly anterior to the esophagus |
| Left ventricle | Forms the apex and most of the left border; thickest wall; bicuspid (mitral) valve and aortic valve |

Valve auscultation sites differ from the true valve positions: **aortic** (right 2nd intercostal space, parasternal), **pulmonary** (left 2nd space), **tricuspid** (left lower sternal border, 4th-5th space), **mitral** (apex, 5th space, midclavicular line).

### Conduction system

Impulses arise at the **SA node**, cross the atria to the **AV node**, enter the **bundle of His**, divide into **right and left bundle branches** and spread by **Purkinje fibers**. Sympathetic fibers (T1-T4) speed the heart; the vagus (parasympathetic) slows it.

### Coronary arteries

The **left main coronary artery** arises from the left aortic sinus and divides into the **left anterior descending (LAD)** and **left circumflex (LCx)**. The **right coronary artery (RCA)** arises from the right aortic sinus.

- **LAD**: anterior wall of the left ventricle, the anterior two-thirds of the interventricular septum, and the apex. Occlusion produces ST changes in the anterior leads (V1-V4).
- **LCx**: lateral wall of the left ventricle (obtuse marginal branches); occlusion affects I, aVL, V5-V6.
- **RCA**: right atrium and ventricle (acute marginal branch), the SA node artery in about 60% of people, and the AV node artery in about 90%. In the common **right-dominant** circulation (about 85%) it gives the **posterior descending artery (PDA)**, supplying the inferior wall and the posterior third of the septum. Occlusion affects II, III, aVF, and may cause bradycardia and AV block.

**Dominance** is defined by which artery gives the PDA: RCA in most people, LCx in about 8%, and both (codominant) in the rest. Veins drain mainly via the **great cardiac vein**, **middle cardiac vein** and **small cardiac vein** into the **coronary sinus**, which opens into the right atrium.

### Flow through a narrowing

Percent diameter stenosis is
$$\% \text{ stenosis} = \left(1 - \frac{d_{\text{min}}}{d_{\text{ref}}}\right) \times 100.$$
Poiseuille's relation says that, for a fixed pressure gradient, flow through a tube is proportional to $r^4$. Halving the radius therefore cuts flow to $1/16$, which is why moderate-looking narrowings can become critical at high demand.

### Great vessels

The aortic arch gives, from right to left, the **brachiocephalic trunk**, **left common carotid** and **left subclavian arteries**. The left recurrent laryngeal nerve loops under the arch beside the ligamentum arteriosum, so an aneurysm of the arch can cause hoarseness.

@example
title: Percent stenosis of a coronary lesion
problem: An angiogram shows a reference diameter of 4.0 mm in a coronary artery and a minimal luminal diameter of 1.0 mm at a lesion. What is the percent diameter stenosis, and what fraction of flow remains if radius alone changes and pressure gradient is unchanged?
step: Percent stenosis is $(1 - d_{\text{min}}/d_{\text{ref}}) \times 100$.
step: Substitute: $(1 - 1.0/4.0) \times 100 = 75\%$.
step: Radius is proportional to diameter, so $r$ falls to one quarter. Flow scales as $r^4$: $(1/4)^4 = 1/256$.
step: Check: a lesion this tight would reduce flow drastically, which is why it causes symptoms at rest or minimal exertion.

@mistakes
- Equating the auscultation sites with the actual valve locations.
- Forgetting that the RCA usually supplies the AV node, so inferior MI often causes AV block.
- Calling the LCx "posterior" territory in every patient; the PDA comes from the RCA in most people.
- Thinking the left atrium is anterior; it forms the posterior surface and touches the esophagus.
