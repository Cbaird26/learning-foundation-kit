@article
The lower limb supports weight and drives locomotion. Anatomy here is organized by compartment, each with one dominant nerve and one dominant artery.

### The hip joint and femoral head blood supply

The hip is a ball-and-socket joint between the femoral head and the acetabulum, stabilized by a strong capsule and the iliofemoral ligament. The femoral head and neck receive most of their blood from the **medial circumflex femoral artery** (a branch of the profunda femoris), whose **retinacular branches** run up the neck along the capsule, with a smaller contribution from the lateral circumflex femoral artery. In the adult the **artery of the ligament of the head (ligamentum teres)**, from the obturator artery, is a minor supply.

A displaced **intracapsular (femoral neck) fracture** can tear the retinacular vessels, so the femoral head may undergo **avascular necrosis**; **extracapsular (intertrochanteric)** fractures have a much better blood supply. A displaced femoral neck fracture classically produces a **shortened and externally rotated** leg, because the iliopsoas and external rotators of the hip now pull the distal fragment. A **posterior hip dislocation** gives a shortened, adducted and **internally rotated** leg, and may damage the sciatic nerve.

### Gluteal region and the sciatic nerve

The **gluteus maximus** (inferior gluteal nerve L5-S2) extends the hip. **Gluteus medius and minimus** (superior gluteal nerve L4-S1) abduct the hip and keep the pelvis level during walking. With a superior gluteal nerve lesion, standing on the affected leg makes the pelvis drop toward the **opposite (unsupported) side**: a positive **Trendelenburg sign**. The patient may lean the trunk toward the affected side to compensate.

The **sciatic nerve (L4-S3)** leaves the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen **below piriformis**, descends in the posterior thigh and divides into the **tibial** and **common fibular (peroneal)** nerves. Intramuscular injections therefore go into the **upper outer quadrant** of the gluteal region (or the ventrogluteal site).

### Thigh compartments

| Compartment | Muscles | Nerve | Main action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anterior | Quadriceps, sartorius, iliopsoas, pectineus | Femoral (L2-L4) | Knee extension, hip flexion |
| Medial | Adductors, gracilis | Obturator (L2-L4) | Hip adduction |
| Posterior | Hamstrings | Sciatic (tibial part; short head of biceps via common fibular) | Hip extension, knee flexion |

### Femoral triangle

Its borders are the **inguinal ligament** superiorly, **sartorius** laterally and **adductor longus** medially. From lateral to medial it contains the femoral **N**erve, **A**rtery, **V**ein, an **E**mpty space (femoral canal) and **L**ymphatics: **NAVEL**. The femoral pulse is felt at the **mid-inguinal point** (halfway between the ASIS and the pubic symphysis). For central venous or femoral venous access, aim just **medial to the pulse**. The great saphenous vein passes anterior to the medial malleolus, ascends the medial leg and thigh and joins the femoral vein at the **saphenofemoral junction** in the femoral triangle; it is used for coronary bypass grafts.

### Knee and leg

The **ACL** prevents anterior translation of the tibia on the femur (anterior drawer test), the **PCL** prevents posterior translation, and the **MCL** resists valgus stress. The "unhappy triad" is ACL, MCL and medial meniscus injury. The **popliteal fossa** contains, from superficial to deep, the tibial nerve, popliteal vein and popliteal artery.

The leg has four compartments:

- **Anterior**: dorsiflexors, deep fibular nerve, anterior tibial artery.
- **Lateral**: fibularis longus and brevis (eversion), superficial fibular nerve.
- **Superficial posterior**: gastrocnemius, soleus (plantarflexion), tibial nerve.
- **Deep posterior**: tibialis posterior, flexors (inversion, toe flexion), tibial nerve, posterior tibial artery.

**Compartment syndrome** (after tibial fracture or crush injury) causes pain out of proportion, tense swelling and pain on passive stretch; pulses are often preserved until late.

### Nerve injuries

The **common fibular nerve** winds around the **neck of the fibula**, where a fracture, tight cast or crossing the legs injures it. Result: **foot drop** (loss of dorsiflexion and eversion; high-stepping gait) and sensory loss over the lateral leg and dorsum of the foot. **Tibial nerve** injury impairs plantarflexion and inversion and sensation on the sole. The **ankle-brachial index (ABI)** is ankle systolic pressure divided by brachial systolic pressure; an ABI of 0.90 or less indicates peripheral arterial disease.

### Lumbar and sacral reflexes

Patellar reflex L3-L4 (femoral nerve); Achilles reflex S1-S2 (tibial nerve). The L4 dermatome is the medial leg, L5 the dorsum of the foot and S1 the lateral foot.

@example
title: Why is a displaced femoral neck fracture dangerous?
problem: An 82-year-old woman falls and cannot bear weight; her right leg is shortened and externally rotated. Radiograph shows a displaced fracture of the femoral neck inside the capsule. Explain the complication risk.
step: The femoral head and neck are supplied mainly by retinacular branches of the medial circumflex femoral artery, running along the neck under the capsule.
step: A displaced intracapsular fracture tears these retinacular vessels.
step: Only the small artery of the ligamentum teres remains in an adult, so the head may become ischemic: avascular necrosis.
step: This is why displaced femoral neck fractures in older adults are often treated by hemiarthroplasty or total hip replacement, while intertrochanteric fractures (good supply) are fixed.

@mistakes
- Saying the artery of the ligamentum teres is the main supply of the adult femoral head.
- Reversing the Trendelenburg sign: the pelvis drops on the side opposite the weak gluteus medius.
- Putting the femoral vein lateral to the artery; it is medial.
- Attributing foot drop to a tibial nerve lesion; it is the common fibular nerve.
