@article
The head combines a protective vault (the neurocranium), a facial skeleton and a rich set of vessels, nerves and spaces. Clinically, three structures dominate: the scalp, the meninges and the cranial base foramina.

### The skull

The **neurocranium** comprises the **frontal**, two **parietal**, two **temporal**, **occipital**, **sphenoid** and **ethmoid** bones. The **pterion** is the H-shaped junction of the frontal, parietal, squamous temporal and greater wing of the sphenoid, about two finger-breadths above the zygomatic arch. The bone here is thin, and the **anterior branch of the middle meningeal artery** runs deep to it. The main sutures are the **coronal**, **sagittal** and **lambdoid**.

### The scalp: SCALP

**S**kin, **C**onnective tissue (dense, vascular), **A**poneurosis (galea aponeurotica), **L**oose areolar tissue, **P**ericranium. Because the vessels lie in the dense connective tissue layer and are held open by it, scalp lacerations **bleed profusely**. The loose areolar layer is the **danger area**: infection can spread widely and passes through **emissary veins** to the intracranial venous sinuses. Arteries come from the internal carotid (supraorbital, supratrochlear) and external carotid (superficial temporal, posterior auricular, occipital). Sensory supply comes from the trigeminal nerve (V1 supraorbital and supratrochlear, V3 auriculotemporal) and the greater occipital nerve (C2).

### Meninges and hematomas

Three layers cover the brain: **dura mater** (periosteal and meningeal layers), **arachnoid** and **pia**. The dural venous sinuses lie between the layers.

| Bleed | Source | Shape on CT | Key facts |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Epidural** hematoma | Middle meningeal artery (arterial), often at pterion after temporal bone fracture | Biconvex (lens-shaped), does not cross sutures | Brief loss of consciousness, then a **lucid interval**, then deterioration; uncal herniation with a blown ipsilateral pupil (CN III compression) |
| **Subdural** hematoma | Bridging veins (venous) | Crescent-shaped, crosses sutures, not dural reflections | Older adults, alcohol use, anticoagulants; slow bleed |
| **Subarachnoid** hemorrhage | Ruptured berry aneurysm (often at circle of Willis branch points) | Blood in the sulci and cisterns | Thunderclap headache |

The **middle meningeal artery** arises from the **maxillary artery**, enters the skull through the **foramen spinosum** and runs in a groove inside the skull. The epidural space exists only after the dura is stripped from the bone by the expanding hematoma, which explains the lens shape and the limit at the sutures, where dura is firmly attached.

### Skull base foramina

| Foramen | Main contents |
|---|---|

| Optic canal | CN II, ophthalmic artery |
| Superior orbital fissure | CN III, IV, V1, VI, ophthalmic veins |
| Foramen rotundum | CN V2 |
| Foramen ovale | CN V3, accessory meningeal artery |
| Foramen spinosum | Middle meningeal artery |
| Carotid canal | Internal carotid artery |
| Internal acoustic meatus | CN VII, CN VIII |
| Jugular foramen | CN IX, X, XI; internal jugular vein |
| Hypoglossal canal | CN XII |
| Foramen magnum | Medulla, vertebral arteries, spinal roots of CN XI |

### Cavernous sinus and facial veins

The **cavernous sinus** lies beside the sella turcica. The **internal carotid artery** and **CN VI** pass through the sinus; **CN III, IV, V1 and V2** run in its lateral wall. The **facial vein** connects through the **ophthalmic veins** to the cavernous sinus, and these veins have no valves, so infections of the "danger triangle" (upper lip, nose) can cause **cavernous sinus thrombosis**: fever, periorbital edema, ophthalmoplegia and sensory loss.

### Face

The muscles of facial expression are supplied by the **facial nerve (CN VII)**, which passes through the **parotid gland** and divides into temporal, zygomatic, buccal, marginal mandibular and cervical branches. Facial skin sensation is by V1, V2 and V3. The **orbital floor** is thin, so a blow to the eye can cause a **blowout fracture** with entrapment of the **inferior rectus** (upward gaze limited) and numbness of the cheek and upper lip from **infraorbital nerve (V2)** injury. Signs of a **basilar skull fracture** include **Battle sign** (mastoid bruising), **raccoon eyes** and CSF leak from the nose or ear.

### Glasgow Coma Scale

The GCS sums three components: **eye opening** (1-4), **verbal response** (1-5) and **motor response** (1-6), for a total of 3-15.

@example
title: An epidural hematoma
problem: A 19-year-old man is struck on the side of the head, loses consciousness briefly, wakes and talks normally, then becomes drowsy 2 hours later with a dilated right pupil. CT shows a lens-shaped collection under a right temporal fracture. Explain the anatomy.
step: The fracture at the pterion tears the anterior branch of the middle meningeal artery, which lies in a groove on the inner bone surface.
step: Arterial blood strips the dura off bone, giving a biconvex shape limited by the sutures.
step: The lucid interval is the time during which the hematoma expands.
step: Rising pressure pushes the medial temporal lobe (uncus) over the tentorium, compressing CN III, so the ipsilateral pupil dilates.

@mistakes
- Calling a subdural hematoma lens-shaped; it is crescent-shaped and crosses sutures.
- Saying the middle meningeal artery enters through the foramen ovale; it is the foramen spinosum.
- Forgetting that scalp bleeding is profuse because vessels are held open in the connective tissue layer.
- Thinking CN VI runs in the lateral wall of the cavernous sinus; it runs through the sinus beside the carotid.
