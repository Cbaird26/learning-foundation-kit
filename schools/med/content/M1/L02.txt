@article
The back is a stack of bones, joints and muscles protecting the spinal cord and its roots. Its clinical importance is huge: lumbar puncture, epidurals, disc herniation and spinal cord injury all depend on a few simple relationships.

### The vertebral column

There are **33 vertebrae**: 7 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral (fused into the sacrum) and 4 coccygeal (fused). A typical vertebra has a **body** anteriorly, a **vertebral arch** (pedicles and laminae) enclosing the **vertebral foramen**, a **spinous process** posteriorly, two transverse processes, and articular processes forming zygapophyseal (facet) joints. Between adjacent bodies lies an **intervertebral disc**: a central gelatinous **nucleus pulposus** surrounded by a fibrous **anulus fibrosus**. Posterior to each disc the **posterior longitudinal ligament** is weak laterally, so discs herniate **posterolaterally**, toward the nerve roots.

### Spinal nerves

There are **31 pairs**: 8 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral and 1 coccygeal. Cervical nerves C1-C7 exit **above** the vertebra of the same number; C8 exits between C7 and T1; from T1 downward each nerve exits **below** its vertebra. Each nerve forms from a dorsal (sensory) and ventral (motor) root, and divides into a **posterior ramus** (back muscles, skin of the back) and an **anterior ramus** (everything else, and the plexuses).

### Cord and cauda equina

The spinal cord is shorter than the vertebral canal because the column grows faster during development. In adults the cord ends as the **conus medullaris** at about **L1-L2** (L3 in neonates). The **dural sac** with its CSF extends further, to about **S2**. Below the conus, lumbar and sacral roots descend as the **cauda equina**, surrounded by CSF, and the pial **filum terminale** anchors the cord to the coccyx. A needle in the lower lumbar subarachnoid space therefore meets mobile roots that tend to be pushed aside, not the cord.

### Meninges and spaces

From outside in: **epidural space** (fat and the internal vertebral venous plexus), **dura mater**, a potential **subdural space**, **arachnoid mater**, **subarachnoid space** (CSF) and **pia mater**. The epidural space exists only in the spinal canal because the cranial dura fuses with the periosteum of the skull.

### Anatomy of a lumbar puncture

The patient is usually placed in lateral decubitus or sitting, with the spine flexed to open the interspinous gaps. The **supracristal (intercristal) line** joining the iliac crests crosses the L4 spinous process or the L4-L5 interspace. The needle enters at **L3-L4 or L4-L5**, below the conus. In the midline the needle passes, in order: skin, subcutaneous tissue, **supraspinous ligament**, **interspinous ligament**, **ligamentum flavum** (a distinct "give" is felt), epidural space, dura and arachnoid together, and then the subarachnoid space, where CSF flows. Contraindications include signs of raised intracranial pressure with mass effect, local skin infection and uncorrected coagulopathy.

### Blood supply and tracts

The cord is supplied by one **anterior spinal artery** (anterior two-thirds) and two **posterior spinal arteries**, reinforced by segmental radicular arteries. The largest, the **artery of Adamkiewicz** (arteria radicularis magna), usually arises on the left between about T9 and L2 and supplies the lower cord. Anterior spinal artery occlusion spares the dorsal columns but causes bilateral loss of motor function and pain and temperature sensation below the lesion.

Key tracts: the **dorsal columns** carry fine touch and proprioception and cross in the medulla; the **spinothalamic tract** carries pain and temperature and crosses within a segment or two of entry; the **lateral corticospinal tract** carries voluntary motor output and crosses in the medullary pyramids. A hemisection (Brown-Sequard) therefore causes ipsilateral weakness and loss of vibration and position sense, and contralateral loss of pain and temperature below the level.

### Disc herniation

At L4-L5, a posterolateral herniation usually compresses the **traversing L5 root** (the L4 root has already exited above the disc at that level). At L5-S1 it compresses S1. A large central herniation can compress the whole cauda equina: **saddle anesthesia, urinary retention or incontinence and bilateral leg weakness** form a surgical emergency.

@example
title: Layers crossed in a lumbar puncture
problem: List, in order, the structures a midline lumbar puncture needle crosses to reach CSF at L4-L5, and explain why the site is safe in an adult.
step: Skin, subcutaneous fat, then the supraspinous ligament joining the spinous process tips.
step: Next, the interspinous ligament between adjacent spinous processes, then the ligamentum flavum joining adjacent laminae.
step: Past the ligamentum flavum is the epidural space (fat and veins), then dura and arachnoid, and finally the subarachnoid space.
step: The adult cord ends at about L1-L2, so L4-L5 is below it. The needle meets only floating cauda equina roots.

@mistakes
- Saying the cord ends at the bottom of the vertebral canal; it ends at about L1-L2, while the dural sac reaches S2.
- Placing the ligamentum flavum anterior to the dura: it lies posterior to the epidural space, between laminae.
- Assuming an L4-L5 disc herniation compresses the L4 root; the traversing L5 root is the usual victim of a posterolateral herniation.
- Forgetting that C8 exists although there are only seven cervical vertebrae.

@practice self
prompt: Explain to a patient, in plain language, which tissue layers a lumbar puncture needle crosses and why the needle is placed below the end of the spinal cord.
solution: The needle goes through the skin and fat, then three firm bands (two ligaments between the bones of the spine and the ligamentum flavum). It passes a space with fat and veins, then the two membranes (dura and arachnoid) that hold the fluid, and reaches the CSF. In adults the spinal cord ends around L1-L2, so at L3-L4 or L4-L5 the needle meets only loose nerve roots that float in the fluid and usually move aside.
