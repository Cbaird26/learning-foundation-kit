@article
Twelve pairs of cranial nerves carry the head and neck's sensory and motor traffic. The most useful way to learn them is by **function**, **brainstem level** and **foramen**, because lesions are localized from the pattern of signs.

### Overview

| Nerve | Type | Main function | Skull exit |
|---|---|---|---|
| I Olfactory | Sensory | Smell | Cribriform plate |
| II Optic | Sensory | Vision; afferent limb of pupillary light reflex | Optic canal |
| III Oculomotor | Motor and parasympathetic | Superior, inferior, medial rectus, inferior oblique, levator palpebrae; pupil constriction and accommodation | Superior orbital fissure |
| IV Trochlear | Motor | Superior oblique | Superior orbital fissure |
| V Trigeminal | Mixed | Facial sensation (V1, V2, V3); mastication (V3) | V1 superior orbital fissure; V2 foramen rotundum; V3 foramen ovale |
| VI Abducens | Motor | Lateral rectus | Superior orbital fissure |
| VII Facial | Mixed | Facial expression; taste from anterior two-thirds of tongue; lacrimal and salivary glands; stapedius | Internal acoustic meatus, then stylomastoid foramen |
| VIII Vestibulocochlear | Sensory | Hearing and balance | Internal acoustic meatus |
| IX Glossopharyngeal | Mixed | Taste and sensation from posterior third of tongue; stylopharyngeus; parotid secretion; carotid sinus and body | Jugular foramen |
| X Vagus | Mixed | Palate, pharynx, larynx; parasympathetic to thorax and abdomen | Jugular foramen |
| XI Accessory | Motor | Sternocleidomastoid and trapezius | Jugular foramen |
| XII Hypoglossal | Motor | Tongue muscles | Hypoglossal canal |

Brainstem levels: **midbrain** (III, IV), **pons** (V, VI, VII, VIII), **medulla** (IX, X, XII; XI from the upper cervical cord and nucleus ambiguus). Parasympathetic fibers travel with III, VII, IX and X.

### Eye movements

Remember **LR6 SO4 AO3**: the lateral rectus is supplied by VI, the superior oblique by IV and all the rest by III. **CN III palsy** produces a "down and out" eye (unopposed lateral rectus and superior oblique), ptosis and, when compressed, a dilated pupil, because parasympathetic fibers lie on the outside of the nerve. Compressive causes (a posterior communicating artery aneurysm, uncal herniation) therefore affect the pupil early; ischemic causes (diabetes) often **spare the pupil**. **CN IV palsy** gives vertical diplopia worst when looking down (reading, stairs) and a head tilt away from the affected side. **CN VI palsy** gives horizontal diplopia with the affected eye turned in; because of its long course it can be affected by raised intracranial pressure.

### Trigeminal nerve

V1 (ophthalmic), V2 (maxillary) and V3 (mandibular) carry facial sensation; V3 also carries motor fibers to the muscles of mastication. On opening the mouth, the jaw deviates **toward the side of a V3 motor lesion**. The afferent limb of the corneal reflex is V1; the efferent limb is the facial nerve. **Trigeminal neuralgia** causes brief lancinating facial pain, most often in V2 or V3.

### Facial nerve

Facial nerve fibers also carry taste from the anterior two-thirds of the tongue (via the chorda tympani), parasympathetic fibers to the lacrimal, submandibular and sublingual glands, and motor fibers to the stapedius. A **lower motor neuron lesion** (for example **Bell palsy**) paralyzes the whole ipsilateral face, including the forehead. An **upper motor neuron lesion** (for example stroke) causes weakness of the contralateral lower face with the forehead spared, because the part of the facial nucleus supplying the forehead receives bilateral cortical input. Bell palsy is usually treated with early corticosteroids, and the eye is protected from drying. A lesion proximal to the stapedius branch causes hyperacusis.

### Vestibulocochlear nerve

**Weber and Rinne tests** distinguish hearing loss types. In **conductive** loss, Weber lateralizes to the affected ear and Rinne is abnormal (bone conduction louder than air) in that ear. In **sensorineural** loss, Weber lateralizes to the better ear and Rinne remains normal (air conduction louder than bone, though both reduced). A **vestibular schwannoma** in the cerebellopontine angle causes unilateral sensorineural hearing loss and tinnitus, with later involvement of CN VII and V.

### Glossopharyngeal, vagus, accessory and hypoglossal

CN IX is the afferent limb and CN X the efferent limb of the gag reflex. With a **unilateral vagus lesion**, the uvula deviates **away** from the side of the lesion, because the intact side pulls the uvula up. A lesion of **CN XI** weakens the ipsilateral shoulder shrug (trapezius) and head turning toward the opposite side (SCM). With a unilateral **CN XII** lesion, the tongue deviates **toward the side of the lesion**, because the intact genioglossus pushes it across.

### Localizing a lesion

Combine four rules: (1) the nerve's function; (2) which neighbors are involved; (3) whether signs are ipsilateral or contralateral; (4) whether the lesion is brainstem (cranial nerve signs plus crossed long-tract signs), cavernous sinus (III, IV, V1, V2, VI together), cerebellopontine angle (VII, VIII, and later V), jugular foramen (IX, X, XI) or peripheral. **Weber syndrome** (medial midbrain) gives an ipsilateral CN III palsy and contralateral hemiparesis. **Lateral medullary (Wallenberg) syndrome** (PICA or vertebral artery) causes ipsilateral facial pain and temperature loss, contralateral body pain and temperature loss, Horner syndrome, hoarseness and dysphagia, vertigo and ipsilateral ataxia.

@example
title: Localizing a cranial nerve lesion
problem: A 61-year-old man has a left drooping eyelid, the left eye turned down and out, a dilated left pupil and a right hemiparesis. Where is the lesion?
step: Ptosis, the down-and-out eye and the dilated pupil indicate a left CN III palsy, including the parasympathetic fibers.
step: A cranial nerve palsy plus contralateral hemiparesis suggests a lesion in the brainstem where the nerve emerges near the corticospinal tract.
step: CN III arises in the midbrain; the corticospinal fibers in the cerebral peduncle have not yet crossed (they cross in the medulla), so a left midbrain lesion causes a right-sided weakness.
step: The diagnosis is Weber syndrome from a left midbrain lesion, for instance a PCA branch infarct.

@mistakes
- Saying the uvula deviates toward the side of a vagus lesion; it deviates away.
- Saying the tongue deviates away from the side of a hypoglossal lesion; it deviates toward it.
- Treating Bell palsy as upper motor neuron because only one side is weak; the forehead is also involved.
- Forgetting that CN VI has a long intracranial course and may be affected by raised pressure.

@practice self
prompt: A 57-year-old woman has progressive right hearing loss, tinnitus, right facial weakness and reduced right corneal reflex. Localize the lesion and list the nerves involved with a reason for each sign.
solution: The lesion is in the right cerebellopontine angle, most likely a vestibular schwannoma. CN VIII causes sensorineural hearing loss and tinnitus. CN VII causes facial weakness. CN V (V1 afferent limb) causes a reduced corneal reflex when the mass enlarges. CN VII and VIII leave the pontomedullary junction and enter the internal acoustic meatus together, and CN V lies just above them at the pons.
