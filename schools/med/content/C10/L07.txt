@article
Bacteria change which genes they express within minutes. Related genes are often grouped into an **operon**: one promoter drives a single **polycistronic mRNA** that encodes several proteins, so they are expressed together.

### The lac operon: an inducible system

The *lac* operon lets *Escherichia coli* use lactose. Its structural genes are *lacZ* (beta-galactosidase, which cleaves lactose and makes allolactose), *lacY* (lactose permease) and *lacA* (transacetylase). Upstream are the promoter, the **operator** (the repressor's binding site) and, separately, the regulatory gene *lacI*, which encodes the **Lac repressor**.

- **No lactose**: the repressor binds the operator and blocks RNA polymerase. Expression is very low.
- **Lactose present**: a little lactose is converted to **allolactose**, the **inducer**, which binds the repressor and releases it from the operator. This is **negative control**: the default is OFF unless the repressor is removed. The laboratory analog IPTG induces without being metabolized.

There is also **positive control** by the **catabolite activator protein (CAP)**. When glucose is low, adenylyl cyclase makes **cAMP**; cAMP binds CAP, and the cAMP-CAP complex binds upstream of the promoter and helps RNA polymerase bind. High glucose lowers cAMP, so CAP does not activate. Thus the operon is strongly expressed only when **lactose is present AND glucose is absent**:

| Glucose | Lactose | Repressor | cAMP-CAP | Expression |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| High | Absent | Bound | Low | Off |
| High | Present | Released | Low | Low (basal) |
| Low | Absent | Bound | High | Off |
| Low | Present | Released | High | High |

This explains **diauxic growth**: *E. coli* grown on glucose and lactose uses glucose first, pauses, and then switches to lactose.

### Mutations and complementation tests

- ***lacI$^-$***: no functional repressor, so the operon is **constitutive** (always on). In a partial diploid (merodiploid) with a wild-type *lacI$^+$* copy, the repressor is a diffusible protein and acts **in trans**, restoring regulation. *lacI$^-$* is recessive.
- ***lacI$^s$*** (super-repressor): the repressor cannot bind allolactose, so the operon is always off. It is **dominant**, because the mutant repressor still binds operators on both chromosomes.
- ***lacO$^c$***: an operator mutation that the repressor cannot bind. It acts only **in cis**: it affects only the genes on the same DNA molecule.

The key logic: a diffusible product (repressor) works in trans; a DNA site (operator, promoter) works only in cis.

### The trp operon: a repressible system

The *trp* operon makes the enzymes for tryptophan biosynthesis. Its repressor is made inactive; **tryptophan acts as a corepressor**, binding the repressor and enabling it to bind the operator. High tryptophan turns the operon off. A second mechanism, **attenuation**, uses a leader peptide that contains tryptophan codons: when tryptophan is plentiful, the ribosome translates the leader quickly, a terminator hairpin forms, and transcription stops early. Low tryptophan stalls the ribosome, an antiterminator forms, and transcription continues. The trp system shows how coupling of transcription and translation, possible in bacteria, can regulate expression.

@example
title: Predicting lac expression
problem: Cells are grown in medium with lactose but no glucose. Predict the state of repressor, cAMP and operon expression.
step: Lactose is present, so allolactose forms and binds the repressor, which releases the operator.
step: Glucose is absent, so adenylyl cyclase is active, cAMP is high, and the cAMP-CAP complex activates the promoter.
step: Both conditions favor transcription, so the operon is strongly expressed (beta-galactosidase and permease are made).

@example
title: A partial diploid with a super-repressor
problem: A merodiploid has the genotype $lacI^s\,lacO^+\,lacZ^+ / lacI^+\,lacO^+\,lacZ^+$. Is beta-galactosidase made in the presence of lactose?
step: The $lacI^s$ gene makes a repressor that cannot bind allolactose.
step: This protein is diffusible and acts in trans: it binds the operators on both DNA molecules and cannot be released.
step: The wild-type repressor is released normally but cannot overcome the super-repressor. Result: no induction, so beta-galactosidase is not made even with lactose; the mutation is dominant.

@mistakes
- Saying that lactose itself binds the repressor; the inducer is allolactose.
- Treating the operator like a protein; it is a DNA site, so its mutations act only in cis.
- Forgetting positive control: lactose alone does not give high expression if glucose is also present.
- Describing the trp repressor as active alone; it needs tryptophan as a corepressor.

@practice self
prompt: Explain why *E. coli* growing in a medium with both glucose and lactose uses glucose first, and describe what happens to the lac operon when glucose is exhausted.
solution: With high glucose, cAMP levels are low, so CAP is not activated and RNA polymerase binds the lac promoter poorly; expression stays low even when allolactose has removed the repressor. The cells therefore grow on glucose. When glucose is exhausted, adenylyl cyclase becomes active and cAMP rises. The cAMP-CAP complex binds near the promoter and strongly stimulates transcription. Because lactose is present, allolactose has released the repressor, so the operon is induced and the cells resume growth on lactose after a lag, producing the biphasic growth curve called diauxie.
rubric: Links high glucose to low cAMP and inactive CAP.
rubric: States that allolactose removes the repressor but CAP is still needed for high expression.
rubric: Explains that low glucose raises cAMP and induces the operon.
hint: Consider both the repressor and the activator.
