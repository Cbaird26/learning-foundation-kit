@article
**Transcription** copies one strand of DNA into RNA. The strand read by RNA polymerase is the **template** (antisense) strand; the RNA has the same sequence as the other, **coding** (sense) strand, with $U$ in place of $T$. RNA polymerase reads the template $3'\to5'$ and builds RNA $5'\to3'$. For example, a template $3'\text{-TACGGA-}5'$ gives the mRNA $5'\text{-AUGCCU-}3'$.

### Initiation, elongation and termination

In bacteria, a single RNA polymerase core enzyme binds a **sigma factor**, which recognizes the promoter elements at about $-10$ (the Pribnow box, TATAAT) and $-35$. Termination uses either a hairpin followed by a run of $U$ (intrinsic) or the helicase Rho. Rifampin inhibits bacterial RNA polymerase and is a first-line antituberculosis drug.

In eukaryotes three polymerases make different RNAs: **RNA polymerase I** makes most rRNA, **II** makes mRNA, snRNA and some microRNAs, and **III** makes tRNA and 5S rRNA. Pol II needs **general transcription factors** (TFIID binds the TATA box, about $-25$ to $-30$) to form a preinitiation complex. **Enhancers**, which can be far away and in either orientation, recruit activators. Alpha-amanitin, the death-cap mushroom toxin, inhibits RNA polymerase II.

### Processing of eukaryotic pre-mRNA

Primary transcripts (**pre-mRNA**) are modified in the nucleus:

1. **5' cap**: a 7-methylguanosine linked $5'$-to-$5'$ is added early; it protects the RNA and helps ribosome recruitment.
2. **Splicing**: **introns** are removed and **exons** joined by the **spliceosome**, made of snRNPs (U1, U2, U4, U5, U6). U1 binds the **5' donor site** (consensus GU at the intron start), and U2 binds the branch point adenine, which attacks the donor site to form a lariat. The $3'$ acceptor site ends with AG. The GU and AG are invariant. Anti-Smith antibodies against snRNP proteins are specific for lupus.
3. **Cleavage and polyadenylation**: signal AAUAAA is recognized, the RNA is cut downstream, and about $200$ adenines are added. The tail stabilizes mRNA and promotes translation.

**Alternative splicing** lets one gene encode several proteins by including or skipping exons. Most human multi-exon genes are alternatively spliced.

### Splice-site mutations

A mutation in an invariant GU or AG usually abolishes recognition of that site. The spliceosome may then:

- **skip the exon** (exon skipping), joining the neighboring exons;
- **retain the intron** (intron retention);
- **use a nearby cryptic splice site**, adding or removing a few nucleotides.

If the skipped exon's length is a multiple of $3$, the frame is preserved (in-frame deletion). If not, the outcome is a **frameshift**, often with a premature stop codon. Such mRNAs are usually destroyed by **nonsense-mediated decay**. Beta-thalassemia is a classic example of splicing mutations in the $\beta$-globin gene.

### Gene structure and sizes

In humans the average exon is only about $150$ nucleotides long, while introns can run for thousands. The mature mRNA length (excluding cap and tail) is the sum of exon lengths, including the untranslated regions in the first and last exon.

@example
title: Effect of a donor splice-site mutation
problem: A patient has a $G\to A$ change at the first base of the donor site (GU becomes AU) of the intron that follows exon 3. Exon 3 is 90 nucleotides long; exon 4 is 100 nucleotides long. Predict the likely consequence if the spliceosome skips exon 3, and again if it skips exon 4 instead.
step: The GU of the donor site is invariant; changing it prevents U1 from defining the end of exon 3, so exon 3 is often skipped or the intron is retained.
step: Skipping exon 3 removes $90$ nucleotides. Since $90/3=30$, the frame is preserved, and the protein loses $30$ amino acids but remains in frame.
step: If exon 4 were skipped, $100$ nucleotides would be lost. Since $100/3$ is not an integer ($100=3\times33+1$), the frame shifts, and the downstream codons are read in the wrong frame. A premature stop is likely, so nonsense-mediated decay would reduce mRNA levels.

@example
title: Length of a mature mRNA
problem: A gene has exons of 150, 90 and 210 nucleotides separated by introns of 1200 and 3400 nucleotides. What is the length of the spliced mRNA before adding the cap and poly(A) tail?
step: Introns are removed, so only exons remain.
step: Add the exons: $150+90+210=450$ nucleotides.

@mistakes
- Writing the mRNA sequence as the complement of the coding strand; the mRNA matches the coding strand and complements the template.
- Reading the template strand $5'\to3'$ when deriving the RNA.
- Saying the poly(A) tail is encoded by DNA; it is added after transcription by poly(A) polymerase.
- Assuming every splice-site mutation causes a frameshift; the effect depends on the length of the skipped exon.

@practice self
prompt: Describe the roles of the 5' cap, the poly(A) tail and splicing in producing a mature eukaryotic mRNA, and say why each is important for gene expression.
solution: The 5' cap, a 7-methylguanosine linked through a 5'-to-5' triphosphate, protects the mRNA from 5' exonucleases and is recognized by the cap-binding complex that recruits ribosomes. The poly(A) tail, added after cleavage at the AAUAAA signal, stabilizes the RNA, aids nuclear export and enhances translation through poly(A)-binding protein. Splicing by the spliceosome removes introns and joins exons, so that the coding region is continuous; it also allows alternative splicing to produce different proteins from one gene and marks the RNA for export. Together these modifications distinguish mature mRNA and control its stability, export and translation.
