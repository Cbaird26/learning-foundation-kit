@article
**Population genetics** asks how allele frequencies behave in a group over time. For one gene with two alleles, let $p$ be the frequency of $A$ and $q$ the frequency of $a$, so $p+q=1$.

### Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium

If a large population mates at random and nothing else changes allele frequencies, genotype frequencies after one generation are

$$p^2\ (AA),\qquad 2pq\ (Aa),\qquad q^2\ (aa),\qquad p^2+2pq+q^2=1,$$

and they stay constant in later generations. The assumptions are: random mating, no selection, no new mutation, no migration (gene flow), and a very large population (no genetic drift). Equilibrium is reached after **one generation** of random mating for an autosomal gene. It is a null model: deviation from it signals that one of the assumptions fails, or that genotypes were miscalled.

Counting alleles from genotype counts: with $N$ people, $p=\frac{2n_{AA}+n_{Aa}}{2N}$.

### Using Hardy-Weinberg in medicine

For a rare autosomal recessive disease, the incidence is $q^2$, so $q=\sqrt{\text{incidence}}$. Because $q$ is small, $p\approx1$ and the **carrier frequency** is $2pq\approx2q$. Most carriers are healthy: for $q=0.02$, carriers outnumber affected people $0.0392$ to $0.0004$, about $98$ to $1$. To estimate the recurrence risk to the offspring of an unaffected sibling of a patient and an unrelated partner, multiply the conditional carrier probability of the sibling ($\frac{2}{3}$), the partner's population carrier frequency ($2pq$), and $\frac{1}{4}$.

For an **X-linked recessive** trait, male incidence is $q$ (males have one X) and female incidence is $q^2$. Females are carriers with frequency $2pq$.

### Forces that change allele frequency

- **Mutation** introduces new alleles but slowly.
- **Natural selection** changes frequencies according to fitness. **Heterozygote advantage** (balancing selection) can maintain a harmful recessive allele: sickle-cell trait protects against severe *Plasmodium falciparum* malaria, so the $HbS$ allele is common in malaria-endemic regions.
- **Genetic drift**: random sampling changes frequencies, especially in small populations. A **founder effect** occurs when a small group founds a new population, and a **bottleneck** when a population is sharply reduced. Examples include a high carrier frequency for Tay-Sachs disease in Ashkenazi Jewish and French Canadian populations.
- **Migration (gene flow)** mixes populations and makes them more similar.
- **Non-random mating**: **assortative mating** and **consanguinity** (mating between relatives) change genotype frequencies without changing allele frequencies, increasing homozygosity and so recessive disease. First cousins share $\frac{1}{8}$ of their genes, and their offspring have an inbreeding coefficient $F=\frac{1}{16}$.

### Linkage disequilibrium and haplotypes

Alleles at nearby loci are inherited together more often than expected (**linkage disequilibrium**), because there has been little recombination between them. This is the basis for genome-wide association studies, covered in the next topic.

### Limits of the model

Hardy-Weinberg proportions apply to a given generation; they say nothing about evolution toward a goal. They also do not hold for X-linked loci in the same way and need adjustment for non-random mating. Testing the model uses a chi-square test comparing observed with expected genotype counts, with one degree of freedom for two alleles.

@example
title: Carrier frequency from incidence
problem: An autosomal recessive disease affects 1 in 2500 newborns in a population in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium. What is the carrier frequency, and what is the risk that an unaffected sister of a patient and an unrelated partner have an affected child?
step: The incidence $q^2=\frac{1}{2500}=0.0004$, so $q=0.02$ and $p=0.98$.
step: The carrier frequency is $2pq=2(0.98)(0.02)=0.0392$, about $1$ in $26$.
step: The sister has carrier probability $\frac{2}{3}$. Risk of an affected child $=\frac{2}{3}\times0.0392\times\frac{1}{4}\approx0.0065$, about $1$ in $150$.

@example
title: Allele frequency from genotype counts
problem: In a sample of 500 people, 180 are $AA$, 250 are $Aa$ and 70 are $aa$. Find $p$ and $q$.
step: Count alleles: $A$ alleles $=2(180)+250=610$; total alleles $=2(500)=1000$.
step: $p=\frac{610}{1000}=0.61$ and $q=1-0.61=0.39$.

@mistakes
- Using the incidence as $q$ instead of $q^2$ for a recessive disease.
- Forgetting that the carrier frequency is $2pq$, not $q^2$ or $q$.
- Applying the $\frac{2}{3}$ conditional carrier probability to an unrelated partner; it applies only to an unaffected sibling of a patient.
- Believing that dominant alleles must become common with time; frequencies stay constant under Hardy-Weinberg.

@practice self
prompt: A genetic counselor tells a couple who are first cousins that their risk of having a child with an autosomal recessive condition is higher than average. Explain why consanguinity raises risk, and give the coefficient of inbreeding for the children of first cousins.
solution: Relatives share alleles inherited from common ancestors. First cousins share one set of grandparents and on average one eighth of their alleles. Each rare recessive allele carried by one cousin has a higher chance of also being carried by the other, so the children have more chance of being homozygous by descent for it. The inbreeding coefficient of their children is $F = \frac{1}{16}$, meaning that at a random locus there is a one in sixteen chance that the two alleles are identical by descent. The absolute extra risk is modest in general terms (the baseline risk for birth defects roughly doubles, from about 2 to 3 percent to about 4 to 6 percent), but is much greater for rare recessive diseases in particular families.

hint: Think about why two related parents are likely to carry the same rare allele.
