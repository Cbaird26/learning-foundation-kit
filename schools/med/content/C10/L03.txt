@article
A normal human somatic cell is **diploid**, with $46$ chromosomes: $22$ pairs of autosomes and two sex chromosomes. A gamete is **haploid**, with $23$. **Euploid** means a whole number of complete haploid sets; **aneuploid** means one or a few chromosomes too many or too few.

### Chromosome structure

Each chromosome has a **centromere** dividing a short arm (**p**) from a long arm (**q**), and telomeres at the ends. By centromere position, chromosomes are **metacentric** (central), **submetacentric**, or **acrocentric** (near one end). In humans, chromosomes $13$, $14$, $15$, $21$ and $22$ are acrocentric. After S phase each chromosome has two **sister chromatids**, so a cell with $46$ chromosomes has $92$ chromatids in G2.

A **karyotype** is an ordered picture of the chromosomes from a cell arrested in metaphase and banded, typically with Giemsa (G-banding). Notation lists the total number, the sex chromosomes, then abnormalities: $46,XY$ is a normal male; $47,XX,+21$ is a female with trisomy $21$; $45,X$ is Turner syndrome; $47,XXY$ is Klinefelter syndrome; $46,XY,t(9;22)(q34;q11.2)$ shows a translocation between the long arms of chromosomes $9$ and $22$ (the Philadelphia chromosome, fusing *BCR* and *ABL1*).

### Nondisjunction

**Nondisjunction** is failure of chromosomes to separate properly. In **meiosis I** the homologs fail to separate: all four gametes are abnormal (two with an extra chromosome, two missing one). In **meiosis II** sister chromatids fail to separate: two gametes are normal, one has an extra chromosome and one lacks it. After fertilization by a normal gamete, the extra-chromosome gamete gives a **trisomy** and the missing-chromosome gamete gives a **monosomy**. Most autosomal aneuploidies are lethal in utero. Survivors include trisomies $21$ (Down), $18$ (Edwards) and $13$ (Patau), and sex chromosome aneuploidies, which are tolerated partly because of **X inactivation**: in each cell, all X chromosomes but one are silenced, forming a **Barr body**. A cell therefore has (number of X chromosomes $-1$) Barr bodies. Most trisomy $21$ arises from maternal meiosis I errors, and risk rises with maternal age.

Aneuploidy can also be **mosaic**: two or more cell lines in one person, arising from a postzygotic error.

### Structural rearrangements

- **Deletion** (loss of a segment, e.g. $5p$ deletion in cri-du-chat syndrome) and **duplication**.
- **Inversion**: a segment is reversed. *Paracentric* inversions exclude the centromere, *pericentric* inversions include it. Carriers are usually healthy, but crossing over within the inversion loop can produce unbalanced gametes.
- **Reciprocal translocation**: two nonhomologous chromosomes exchange segments.
- **Robertsonian translocation**: two acrocentric chromosomes fuse at the centromeres, losing the short arms. A carrier has $45$ chromosomes but is phenotypically normal, since the short arms of acrocentrics carry redundant genes. A carrier of a $14;21$ Robertsonian translocation can have a child with translocation Down syndrome.

A **balanced** rearrangement keeps all genetic material and usually causes no disease in the carrier but risks unbalanced gametes; an **unbalanced** one gains or loses material.

### Which test?

A karyotype finds large changes (about $5$ to $10$ Mb or more), including balanced translocations. **FISH** (fluorescence in situ hybridization) uses labeled probes to detect specific loci or rapidly count a chromosome. A **chromosomal microarray** detects small copy-number variants (deletions and duplications) genome-wide, but cannot detect balanced rearrangements or triploidy in some designs.

@example
title: Reading a karyotype
problem: A newborn has a karyotype $47,XX,+21$ in all 20 cells examined. Her mother's karyotype is $46,XX$. Describe the likely mechanism, how many Barr bodies a cell has, and how many chromatids a cell has in G2.
step: The karyotype shows a trisomy of chromosome $21$ with a normal parental karyotype, so the likely cause is meiotic nondisjunction, most often in the maternal meiosis I.
step: There are two X chromosomes, so one is inactivated: $2-1=1$ Barr body.
step: In G2, each of the $47$ chromosomes has two sister chromatids: $47\times2=94$ chromatids.

@mistakes
- Believing a meiosis II nondisjunction affects all four gametes; it affects only two of them.
- Treating a Robertsonian translocation carrier as having missing genetic material and being clinically affected.
- Counting Barr bodies as equal to the number of X chromosomes instead of that number minus one.
- Expecting a chromosomal microarray to detect a balanced translocation.

@practice self
prompt: A 24-year-old woman has had three first-trimester miscarriages and one child with translocation Down syndrome. Her karyotype is $45,XX,rob(14;21)(q10;q10)$. Explain how she is clinically healthy, how her child could have Down syndrome, and what testing you would offer.
solution: Her karyotype has only $45$ chromosomes because chromosomes $14$ and $21$ have fused at their centromeres, but she has two copies of the long-arm material of both, so she is genetically balanced and healthy. During meiosis the fused chromosome can segregate with a normal $21$ into the same gamete: an egg with the fused $14;21$ chromosome plus a normal chromosome $21$ yields, after fertilization, three copies of $21$ long-arm material, giving translocation Down syndrome; other segregation patterns yield monosomy $21$ or trisomy/monosomy $14$ and miscarriage. Counseling should explain the elevated risk of unbalanced offspring and offer prenatal diagnosis by chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis with karyotype, and testing of the partner and relatives; options include preimplantation genetic testing.

solution: After meiosis I, one daughter has both homologs and the other has neither. Meiosis II then splits sister chromatids normally, so all four products are abnormal: $\frac{4}{4}=1$.
