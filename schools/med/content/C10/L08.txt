In eukaryotes, DNA is wrapped around **histone** octamers to form **nucleosomes**, packaged as **chromatin**. Regulation therefore has an extra layer compared with bacteria: a gene must first be made accessible, and then be activated.

### Cis-regulatory elements and transcription factors

A **promoter** lies at the transcription start site and recruits the general transcription factors and RNA polymerase II. **Enhancers** are short DNA elements that can lie tens of kilobases away, upstream, downstream or within introns, and work in either orientation. **Silencers** repress. **Transcription factors** bind these elements through **DNA-binding domains** (zinc finger, helix-turn-helix, leucine zipper, helix-loop-helix) and recruit **coactivators** such as the Mediator complex, which contact the promoter by DNA looping. Combinations of factors give **combinatorial control**: a modest number of factors can generate many distinct patterns of expression. Mutations in enhancers can cause disease without altering any protein.

### Chromatin states

- **Euchromatin** is open, gene-rich and active. **Heterochromatin** is compact and silent.
- **Histone acetylation** by histone acetyltransferases (HATs) neutralizes the positive charge of lysines, loosening DNA and promoting transcription; **histone deacetylases (HDACs)** reverse it.
- **Histone methylation** can mark either state: H3K4me3 marks active promoters, while H3K27me3 (placed by Polycomb) and H3K9me3 mark repressed chromatin.
- **ATP-dependent chromatin remodelers** (SWI/SNF) slide or eject nucleosomes. SWI/SNF components are mutated in many cancers.

### DNA methylation

DNA methyltransferases (**DNMTs**) add a methyl group to cytosine at the 5 position in **CpG** dinucleotides. **CpG islands** are CpG-rich stretches near promoters; they are normally unmethylated when the gene is active. Methylation of a promoter CpG island recruits methyl-binding proteins and HDACs, producing silencing. **DNMT1** copies the methylation pattern onto the new strand after replication (maintenance methylation), so the mark is heritable through cell division. Because methylated cytosine deaminates to thymine, CpG sites are mutation hotspots (see the earlier topic on mutation). In cancer, tumor suppressor promoters (for example *MLH1* and *CDKN2A*) may be silenced by hypermethylation, while global hypomethylation destabilizes the genome. Drugs such as azacitidine inhibit DNMTs and are used in myelodysplastic syndromes.

### Imprinting and X inactivation

In **genomic imprinting**, a small set of genes is expressed from only one parental allele, set by methylation in the germline. Two classic diseases arise from the same region, chromosome $15q11$-$q13$:

- **Prader-Willi syndrome**: loss of the paternal copy (deletion of paternal $15q11$-$q13$ or maternal uniparental disomy). Features: neonatal hypotonia, hyperphagia leading to obesity, intellectual disability, hypogonadism.
- **Angelman syndrome**: loss of the maternal copy (maternal deletion, paternal uniparental disomy, or *UBE3A* mutation). Features: severe developmental delay, absent speech, ataxia, happy demeanor with seizures.

**X inactivation**: early in female development, one X in each cell is randomly silenced by the lncRNA *XIST*, which coats the chromosome, and methylation and histone marks maintain the silencing. Females are therefore **mosaics**. Skewed X inactivation explains why some carriers of X-linked recessive disease have symptoms.

### Comparison with bacteria

Bacterial genes in operons are usually controlled by a few repressors and activators on one promoter, and transcription and translation are coupled. In eukaryotes, nuclear compartmentalization, chromatin, combinatorial factors, RNA processing and microRNA control add layers of regulation.

@example
title: Interpreting promoter methylation
problem: A colon tumor shows dense methylation of the *MLH1* promoter CpG island and absent MLH1 protein, but no *MLH1* coding mutation. Explain.
step: Methylated CpG islands recruit methyl-binding proteins and HDACs, which compact chromatin and block transcription factor binding.
step: The gene is transcriptionally silenced although its sequence is normal, which is an epigenetic "second hit".
step: Loss of MLH1 impairs mismatch repair and causes microsatellite instability, mimicking Lynch syndrome in a sporadic tumor.

@example
title: Imprinting disorder diagnosis
problem: An infant has profound hypotonia and feeding difficulty. Testing shows two maternal copies of chromosome 15 and no paternal copy. Which disorder is it, and why?
step: Only the paternal alleles at $15q11$-$q13$ are normally active in the relevant genes; the maternal copies are silenced by imprinting.
step: Two maternal copies (maternal uniparental disomy) means no active allele from this region.
step: This is Prader-Willi syndrome, which later presents with hyperphagia and obesity. Angelman syndrome would result from loss of the maternal contribution.

@mistakes
- Saying that methylation of a promoter CpG island activates the gene; it generally silences it.
- Mixing up Prader-Willi (paternal loss) and Angelman syndrome (maternal loss).
- Assuming enhancers must be adjacent to promoters and act in only one orientation.
- Treating epigenetic marks as permanent DNA sequence changes; they are potentially reversible.

@practice self
prompt: Describe how DNA methylation at a promoter CpG island silences a gene, and how this is maintained through cell division. Then explain why this mechanism can be a target of cancer therapy.
solution: DNMTs add methyl groups to cytosines in CpG dinucleotides. Methyl-CpG-binding proteins recognize these marks and recruit HDACs and chromatin remodelers, which deacetylate histones and compact the chromatin, blocking transcription factors and RNA polymerase. After DNA replication, the new strand is unmethylated opposite the methylated parental strand (hemimethylated DNA); DNMT1 recognizes hemimethylated CpG and methylates the new strand, so the pattern is copied to daughter cells. In cancer, tumor suppressor promoters may be hypermethylated and silenced although their sequence is normal. Because methylation is reversible (unlike mutation), DNMT inhibitors such as azacitidine can demethylate and reactivate silenced genes, and HDAC inhibitors act similarly.
