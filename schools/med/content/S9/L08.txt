@article
The most useful bedside question in weakness is: **upper or lower motor neuron?** Upper motor neurons (cortex through corticospinal tract) modulate lower motor neurons; lower motor neurons (anterior horn cell, root, nerve) drive the muscle.

| Feature | Upper motor neuron | Lower motor neuron |
|---|---|---|
| Tone | Increased (spasticity, clasp-knife) | Decreased (flaccid) |
| Reflexes | Brisk | Reduced or absent |
| Atrophy | Mild (disuse) | Marked |
| Fasciculations | Absent | Present |
| Babinski sign | Extensor (upgoing) | Absent |
| Pattern | Pyramidal distribution | Nerve, root or segmental |

After an acute upper motor neuron lesion there may be initial flaccid "spinal shock" before spasticity develops.

### Multiple sclerosis (MS)

Immune-mediated demyelination of the central nervous system, with onset between 20 and 40 years, more often in women; risk factors include Epstein-Barr virus infection, low vitamin D, smoking and northern latitude. **Relapsing-remitting** disease is most common. Manifestations: **optic neuritis** (painful monocular vision loss with a relative afferent pupillary defect), **internuclear ophthalmoplegia** (lesion of the medial longitudinal fasciculus: impaired adduction on the side of the lesion with abducting nystagmus of the other eye), sensory loss, spastic weakness, ataxia, bladder dysfunction, Lhermitte sign (electric sensation on neck flexion) and the Uhthoff phenomenon (worsening with heat).

Diagnosis (McDonald criteria) requires dissemination in space and in time on MRI or clinically: T2 lesions in periventricular, juxtacortical, infratentorial and spinal cord regions (Dawson fingers) and CSF-specific **oligoclonal bands**. Acute relapse: **intravenous methylprednisolone** 1 g daily for 3 to 5 days. Disease-modifying therapies include interferon beta, glatiramer acetate, dimethyl fumarate, teriflunomide, fingolimod, natalizumab (risk of progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy from JC virus; test for anti-JCV antibody) and anti-CD20 antibodies (ocrelizumab). **Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder** (anti-aquaporin-4 antibody) causes optic neuritis and longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis (3 or more vertebral segments) and is treated differently from MS.

### Guillain-Barre syndrome (GBS)

Acute inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy, often 1 to 3 weeks after infection (*Campylobacter jejuni*, cytomegalovirus, Zika) through molecular mimicry. Ascending, symmetric weakness with **areflexia**, mild sensory symptoms and autonomic instability (labile blood pressure, arrhythmias). CSF shows **albuminocytologic dissociation** (high protein, normal cells), and nerve conduction studies show demyelination. Treat with **IVIG or plasma exchange**; steroids do not help. Monitor forced vital capacity (FVC) and negative inspiratory force: consider intubation when FVC falls below 20 mL/kg, negative inspiratory force is weaker than $-30$ cmH$_2$O, or bulbar dysfunction or rapid decline occurs.

### Neuromuscular junction disease

- **Myasthenia gravis:** autoantibodies to the acetylcholine receptor (or MuSK) cause **fatigable weakness** of ocular (ptosis, diplopia), bulbar and proximal muscles that worsens at day's end. Associated with thymoma or thymic hyperplasia. Tests: acetylcholine receptor antibodies, ice-pack test, repetitive nerve stimulation (decrement), CT of the chest. Treat with **pyridostigmine**, immunosuppression, thymectomy; crisis needs IVIG or plasma exchange. Avoid fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, magnesium and beta-blockers.
- **Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome:** antibodies to presynaptic P/Q-type calcium channels, often with **small cell lung cancer**; proximal weakness that **improves with repeated effort**, reduced reflexes, and dry mouth; treat the cancer and give 3,4-diaminopyridine.
- **Botulism:** descending paralysis, dilated pupils, from inhibited acetylcholine release.

### Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS)

Combined upper and lower motor neuron degeneration without sensory loss, with muscle wasting, fasciculations, spasticity and brisk reflexes, sparing eye movements and sphincters. **Riluzole** (reduces glutamate release) and edaravone modestly slow progression; care is multidisciplinary with noninvasive ventilation.

title: Respiratory monitoring in Guillain-Barre syndrome
problem: A 75-kg man with Guillain-Barre syndrome has a forced vital capacity of 1.8 L. Does this meet the criterion for intubation by FVC alone?
step: Convert to mL: 1.8 L is 1800 mL.
step: Divide by weight: $1800/75 = 24$ mL/kg.
step: The threshold is below 20 mL/kg, so he does not meet it, but with progression repeat measurement every few hours in a monitored unit.

@mistakes
- Calling fasciculations and atrophy upper motor neuron signs; they indicate lower motor neuron disease.
- Giving corticosteroids for GBS; IVIG or plasma exchange is the treatment.
- Giving a fluoroquinolone to a patient with myasthenia gravis.
- Attributing worsening of MS symptoms in heat to a new relapse rather than the Uhthoff phenomenon.

@practice self
prompt: Compare the findings of an upper motor neuron lesion and a lower motor neuron lesion in a patient with weak legs, then explain why ALS shows both.
solution: Upper motor neuron lesions cause spastic weakness, brisk reflexes, an extensor plantar response and little atrophy. Lower motor neuron lesions cause flaccid weakness, reduced or absent reflexes, marked atrophy and fasciculations. ALS degenerates both corticospinal neurons and anterior horn cells, so the same patient can have brisk reflexes and spasticity together with fasciculations and wasting, without sensory loss.
hint: Give tone, reflexes, atrophy, fasciculations and plantar response for each.
rubric: Gives tone, reflexes and plantar response correctly for each type.
rubric: Includes atrophy and fasciculations for lower motor neuron disease.
rubric: Explains ALS as combined neuron loss with preserved sensation.
