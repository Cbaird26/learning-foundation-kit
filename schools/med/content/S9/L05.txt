@article
Headache is among the most common complaints in medicine, and almost all of it is benign. The skill is to recognize the small fraction that threatens life or vision. Brain parenchyma has no pain receptors; pain arises from the meninges, large vessels, venous sinuses, cranial nerves and scalp structures, which is why a mass causes pain only when it stretches or displaces them.

### Primary headaches

**Migraine** is an episodic disorder with unilateral, throbbing pain lasting 4 to 72 hours, nausea, photophobia and phonophobia, worsened by activity. About a third have an **aura** (visual scotoma or scintillations, sensory or speech symptoms) lasting 5 to 60 minutes. Mechanism: cortical spreading depression and activation of the trigeminovascular system with release of calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP).

- **Acute treatment:** NSAIDs and acetaminophen for mild attacks; **triptans** (sumatriptan, 5-HT1B/1D agonists) with an antiemetic for moderate to severe attacks. Triptans are contraindicated in coronary disease, prior stroke, uncontrolled hypertension, hemiplegic or brainstem migraine, and with recent ergot use. Gepants (CGRP receptor antagonists) are an alternative.
- **Prevention** (for frequent or disabling attacks): propranolol, topiramate, amitriptyline, valproate (avoid in pregnancy), or CGRP monoclonal antibodies such as erenumab. Estrogen-containing contraceptives are avoided in migraine with aura because of stroke risk.
- **Medication-overuse headache:** frequent use of analgesics (more than 15 days a month for simple analgesics, more than 10 days for triptans or opioids) perpetuates daily headache; treat by withdrawal and prevention.

**Tension-type headache** is bilateral, pressing or band-like, mild to moderate, without nausea or worsening on exertion. Treat with NSAIDs; amitriptyline for chronic cases.

**Cluster headache** (trigeminal autonomic cephalalgia) is severe unilateral periorbital pain lasting 15 to 180 minutes up to several times daily, in bouts, with ipsilateral lacrimation, conjunctival injection, rhinorrhea, ptosis or miosis. Patients are restless and pace, unlike migraine patients who lie still. It is commoner in men. Acute treatment is **100% oxygen** by non-rebreather mask at 12 to 15 L/min or subcutaneous sumatriptan; prevention is **verapamil** (monitor the ECG for PR prolongation) with a short corticosteroid bridge.

### Red flags: when to investigate

The mnemonic **SNOOP** captures them: Systemic symptoms (fever, weight loss) or secondary risk (HIV, cancer); Neurologic deficit or altered consciousness; Onset sudden (thunderclap); Older age at onset (over 50); Pattern change or progression, or positional or Valsalva-provoked headache; Papilledema, pregnancy or postpartum, painful eye, post-traumatic, precipitated by exertion. A **thunderclap headache** (peak intensity within a minute) is subarachnoid hemorrhage until proven otherwise; also consider cerebral venous thrombosis, arterial dissection, reversible vasoconstriction syndrome, pituitary apoplexy and hypertensive emergency. Evaluation is non-contrast CT (within 6 hours, highly sensitive), then lumbar puncture for xanthochromia if CT is negative.

### Important secondary causes

- **Giant cell arteritis (GCA):** age over 50, new temporal headache, jaw claudication, scalp tenderness, polymyalgia rheumatica, visual loss; ESR and CRP are raised. Start **high-dose prednisone immediately** to prevent permanent blindness (arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy); temporal artery biopsy follows and can still be positive after steroids begin. A reference upper limit for ESR is age/2 in men and $(\text{age} + 10)/2$ in women (mm/h).
- **Idiopathic intracranial hypertension:** obese young women, daily headache, transient visual obscurations, pulsatile tinnitus, **papilledema**, normal imaging and lumbar puncture opening pressure above 25 cm H$_2$O with normal CSF. Treat with weight loss and the carbonic anhydrase inhibitor **acetazolamide**; urgent procedures for threatened vision.
- **Post-lumbar-puncture headache:** orthostatic, relieved by lying flat; treat with fluids and caffeine, then an epidural blood patch.
- **Trigeminal neuralgia:** brief, electric, unilateral facial pain triggered by touch; carbamazepine is first-line; vascular compression of the nerve root is common.
- **Others:** meningitis, brain tumor, carbon monoxide poisoning, acute angle-closure glaucoma, sinusitis.

@example
title: Treating suspected giant cell arteritis
problem: A 76-year-old woman has 3 weeks of new right temporal headache, pain on chewing and brief blurring of vision in the right eye. ESR is 88 mm/h. What is the first action?
step: Recognize the syndrome: age over 50, headache, jaw claudication and visual symptoms with a high ESR point to giant cell arteritis.
step: The risk is irreversible blindness in the other eye within days, so treat before confirming.
step: Start high-dose glucocorticoid (prednisone, typically 40 to 60 mg daily as a study dose to be verified against current references before clinical use, or intravenous methylprednisolone if vision is already lost) without waiting for biopsy.
step: Arrange temporal artery biopsy, ideally within 2 weeks of starting steroids, and add imaging as available.

@mistakes
- Waiting for biopsy before steroids in suspected giant cell arteritis.
- Giving a triptan to a patient with hemiplegic migraine or coronary disease.
- Missing medication-overuse headache because the patient reports using over-the-counter pills.
- Treating a thunderclap headache as migraine without CT and lumbar puncture.
