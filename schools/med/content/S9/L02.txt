@article
Every neurological symptom is ultimately a change in how neurons generate and transmit electrical signals. This lesson gives you the mechanisms behind antiseizure drugs, anesthetics, demyelination and many toxins. It also connects to the stroke map from the previous week: ischemia fails when the sodium-potassium pump runs out of ATP.

### The resting potential

At rest a neuron sits near $-70$ mV. Two things set this: unequal ion concentrations maintained by the Na$^+$/K$^+$ ATPase (3 Na$^+$ out, 2 K$^+$ in per ATP) and a membrane that is far more permeable to K$^+$ than to anything else at rest. The potential at which one ion is at electrochemical equilibrium is given by the **Nernst equation**, which at 37 °C for a monovalent ion is

$$E_{\text{ion}} = \frac{61.5}{z}\,\log_{10}\frac{[\text{ion}]_{\text{out}}}{[\text{ion}]_{\text{in}}} \text{ mV},$$

where $z$ is the charge. With $[\text{K}^+]_{\text{out}} = 4$ and $[\text{K}^+]_{\text{in}} = 140$ mEq/L, $E_K \approx -95$ mV. Resting potential lies between $E_K$ and $E_{Na}$ (about $+65$ mV) but much closer to $E_K$.

Clinical consequence: **hyperkalemia** raises $[\text{K}^+]_{\text{out}}$, making $E_K$ less negative and **depolarizing** the cell. Persistent depolarization inactivates Na$^+$ channels, so excitable tissue (heart, muscle) fails to fire normally. Hypokalemia hyperpolarizes and causes weakness.

### The action potential

1. **Depolarization:** a stimulus opens voltage-gated Na$^+$ channels; Na$^+$ rushes in.
2. **Repolarization:** Na$^+$ channels inactivate and voltage-gated K$^+$ channels open.
3. **Hyperpolarization:** K$^+$ channels close slowly, so the potential briefly dips toward $E_K$.
4. The **refractory period** (inactivated Na$^+$ channels) ensures one-way propagation.

**Local anesthetics** (lidocaine) and the sodium-channel antiseizure drugs (phenytoin, carbamazepine, lamotrigine) block Na$^+$ channels in a use-dependent way: rapidly firing neurons are blocked preferentially. **Tetrodotoxin** blocks the channel completely.

### Myelin and conduction

Myelin, made by oligodendrocytes in the CNS and Schwann cells in the periphery, insulates the axon and increases the **length constant**, so current spreads farther before decaying. The action potential regenerates only at the nodes of Ranvier (**saltatory conduction**), increasing speed several-fold. Demyelination (multiple sclerosis, Guillain-Barre syndrome) slows or blocks conduction, producing conduction block, delayed evoked potentials and symptoms that worsen with heat.

### The synapse

An action potential opens presynaptic voltage-gated Ca$^{2+}$ channels; Ca$^{2+}$ entry triggers vesicle fusion (SNARE proteins) and neurotransmitter release. Botulinum toxin cleaves SNARE proteins and tetanus toxin blocks inhibitory release in the spinal cord.

### Neurotransmitters

| Transmitter | Main action and receptors | Clinical relevance |
|---|---|---|
| Glutamate | Excitatory; AMPA, kainate, NMDA | Excitotoxicity in stroke; memantine blocks NMDA; riluzole reduces glutamate release |
| GABA | Main inhibitory transmitter of the brain; GABA-A (Cl$^-$ channel), GABA-B | **Benzodiazepines** increase frequency of Cl$^-$ channel opening; **barbiturates** increase duration |
| Glycine | Inhibitory in spinal cord and brainstem | Blocked by strychnine |
| Acetylcholine | Nicotinic (ion channel, neuromuscular junction) and muscarinic (G-protein) | Myasthenia gravis, Alzheimer disease, anticholinergic toxicity |
| Dopamine | D1 (stimulatory), D2 (inhibitory) | Parkinson disease, psychosis, prolactin inhibition |
| Serotonin | Mostly G-protein receptors | Migraine (triptans), depression, serotonin syndrome |
| Norepinephrine | $\alpha$ and $\beta$ receptors | Arousal, locus coeruleus |

Termination of signaling comes from reuptake, enzymatic breakdown (acetylcholinesterase, monoamine oxidase, COMT) or diffusion.

@example
title: Calculating an equilibrium potential
problem: A neuron has $[\text{K}^+]_{\text{out}} = 4$ mEq/L and $[\text{K}^+]_{\text{in}} = 140$ mEq/L at 37 °C. Calculate $E_K$.
step: Write the Nernst equation for a monovalent cation: $E = 61.5\,\log_{10}\frac{[\text{out}]}{[\text{in}]}$.
step: Substitute the concentrations: $E_K = 61.5\,\log_{10}\frac{4}{140} = 61.5\,\log_{10}(0.02857)$.
step: $\log_{10}(0.02857) \approx -1.544$, so $E_K \approx 61.5 \times (-1.544) \approx -95$ mV.
step: The negative sign means K$^+$ would flow out until the inside is about 95 mV negative, which is why the resting potential is close to this value.

@mistakes
- Using the wrong sign for anions: chloride has $z = -1$, so $E_{Cl} = -61.5\,\log_{10}\frac{[\text{Cl}]_{\text{out}}}{[\text{Cl}]_{\text{in}}}$.
- Saying hyperkalemia hyperpolarizes cells; raising extracellular K$^+$ makes $E_K$ less negative and depolarizes.
- Confusing benzodiazepines (more frequent channel opening) with barbiturates (longer opening).
- Calling the neuron's resting potential equal to $E_{Na}$ because Na$^+$ is abundant outside; at rest the membrane is mostly permeable to K$^+$.

@practice num
prompt: In severe hyperkalemia the extracellular potassium rises to 8 mEq/L while intracellular potassium stays at 140 mEq/L. Calculate $E_K$ in mV at 37 °C using $E = 61.5\,\log_{10}([\text{out}]/[\text{in}])$. Round to the nearest mV.
answer: -76
check: 61.5 * math.log10(8 / 140)
unit: mV
tol: 1
hint: Substitute $8$ for the outside concentration and $140$ for the inside.
hint: $\log_{10}(8/140) \approx -1.243$.
solution: $E_K = 61.5\,\log_{10}(8/140) \approx 61.5 \times (-1.243) \approx -76$ mV, less negative than $-95$ mV, so the cell is partly depolarized.

@practice self
prompt: Explain, in terms of ion channels, why phenytoin and carbamazepine reduce seizures without causing general sedation to the same degree as a benzodiazepine.
hint: Think about which neurons fire fastest during a seizure and which channel states the drugs prefer.
solution: Phenytoin and carbamazepine bind voltage-gated sodium channels in the inactivated state and slow their recovery. The block is use-dependent: neurons firing at the high frequencies of a seizure have more channels in the inactivated state and are blocked preferentially, while neurons firing at normal rates are mostly spared. Benzodiazepines instead enhance GABA-A receptor inhibition across the brain, which suppresses seizures but also produces sedation and respiratory depression.
rubric: States that these drugs block sodium channels in the inactivated state.
rubric: Explains use-dependence and the preference for rapidly firing neurons.
rubric: Contrasts with the global inhibitory enhancement of benzodiazepines.
