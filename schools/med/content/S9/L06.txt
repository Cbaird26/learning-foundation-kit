@article
The skull is a rigid box containing brain (about 80%), blood (about 10%) and cerebrospinal fluid (about 10%). The **Monro-Kellie doctrine** says that the sum of these volumes is constant: when a mass or swelling adds volume, CSF and venous blood are first displaced, and after compensation is exhausted pressure rises steeply. Normal intracranial pressure (ICP) in an adult lying supine is about 7 to 15 mmHg; treatment is usually started above 22 mmHg.

### Assessment of head injury

The **Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)** adds three scores: **eye opening** (4 spontaneous, 3 to voice, 2 to pain, 1 none), **verbal** (5 oriented, 4 confused, 3 inappropriate words, 2 incomprehensible sounds, 1 none) and **motor** (6 obeys, 5 localizes, 4 withdraws, 3 flexion posturing, 2 extension posturing, 1 none). The range is 3 to 15: **mild** injury is 13 to 15, **moderate** 9 to 12, **severe** 8 or below, for which securing the airway is the priority. Always follow ABCDE, protect the cervical spine and treat hypotension and hypoxia, which worsen secondary injury.

Imaging: the **Canadian CT head rule** (for minor head injury with GCS 13 to 15) recommends CT if any of these high-risk features is present: GCS below 15 at 2 hours, suspected open or depressed skull fracture, signs of basal skull fracture, two or more vomiting episodes, or age 65 or older; medium-risk features are amnesia before impact of 30 minutes or more and a dangerous mechanism. Anticoagulated patients usually need CT.

### Patterns of injury

| Lesion | Mechanism | CT and course |
|---|---|---|
| **Epidural hematoma** | Temporal bone fracture tears the middle meningeal artery (pterion) | Biconvex (lens-shaped), does not cross sutures; lucid interval then rapid deterioration; surgical emergency |
| **Subdural hematoma** | Tearing of bridging veins (elderly, alcohol use, anticoagulants, infants with abusive injury) | Crescent shaped, crosses sutures but not the midline falx; acute (hyperdense) or chronic (hypodense) |
| **Subarachnoid hemorrhage** | Traumatic or aneurysmal | Blood in sulci and cisterns; nimodipine for aneurysmal |
| **Cerebral contusion** | Coup and contrecoup injury at the frontal and temporal poles | Hemorrhagic foci that may enlarge |
| **Diffuse axonal injury** | Rotational shear of white matter | CT often normal; MRI shows small lesions; coma from the time of injury |

**Skull base fracture:** periorbital ecchymoses (raccoon eyes), mastoid ecchymosis (Battle sign), hemotympanum and CSF rhinorrhea or otorrhea; do not place a nasogastric tube through the nose. **Concussion** is a transient functional disturbance without structural CT changes; recovery is graded and repeated injury risks second-impact syndrome.

### Raised intracranial pressure

Causes include mass lesions, hemorrhage, edema, hydrocephalus, and venous thrombosis. Symptoms are headache (worse lying down or on waking), vomiting, papilledema, and decreased consciousness. **Cushing triad** (hypertension with widened pulse pressure, bradycardia, irregular respirations) signals brainstem compression.

**Herniation syndromes:** *uncal* (medial temporal lobe through the tentorium) compresses CN III, causing an ipsilateral blown pupil, and the cerebral peduncle, causing contralateral hemiparesis (the **Kernohan notch** phenomenon gives false-localizing ipsilateral weakness); *subfalcine* (cingulate gyrus under the falx) compresses the ACA; *tonsillar* (cerebellar tonsils through the foramen magnum) causes respiratory arrest.

### Perfusion pressure and treatment

$$\text{CPP} = \text{MAP} - \text{ICP},$$

with a target of about 60 to 70 mmHg in severe traumatic brain injury. Management is stepwise: head of bed at 30 degrees, neutral neck position, treat hypoxia, hypotension, fever, hypoglycemia and seizures; analgesia and sedation; osmotic therapy with **mannitol** (0.25 to 1 g/kg, a typical adult dose for study, to be checked against current references before any clinical use) or **3% hypertonic saline** (target serum sodium of 145 to 155 mEq/L); CSF drainage by external ventricular drain; brief **hyperventilation** (PaCO$_2$ 30 to 35 mmHg) as a temporizing bridge only because vasoconstriction can cause ischemia; and in refractory cases barbiturate coma or **decompressive craniectomy**. Corticosteroids do not help traumatic brain injury and increase mortality, although dexamethasone is used for the vasogenic edema of tumors. Epidural and large subdural hematomas with mass effect require surgical evacuation.

@example
title: Cerebral perfusion pressure
problem: A patient with a severe head injury has a blood pressure of 140/80 mmHg and an ICP of 30 mmHg. Calculate the CPP and interpret it.
step: MAP $= \frac{140 + 2(80)}{3} = \frac{300}{3} = 100$ mmHg.
step: CPP $= 100 - 30 = 70$ mmHg.
step: This lies at the upper end of the 60 to 70 mmHg target, but the ICP itself is raised above 22 mmHg and needs treatment.

@mistakes
- Treating a lucid interval as reassurance; epidural hematomas can deteriorate within hours.
- Calling hyperventilation a sustained therapy; prolonged hypocapnia worsens ischemia.
- Mistaking a crescent-shaped subdural collection for an epidural hematoma.
- Doing a lumbar puncture in a patient with a suspected mass or raised ICP before imaging.

solution: Red flags are a progressive pattern, positional (worse supine) headache, morning vomiting suggesting raised ICP, and age over 50. Examine for papilledema, focal deficits and gaze palsies, and obtain urgent imaging (CT, then MRI with contrast) to look for a mass, hemorrhage, venous thrombosis or hydrocephalus.
