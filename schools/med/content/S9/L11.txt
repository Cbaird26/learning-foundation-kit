Vision depends on light focused by the cornea and lens onto the retina, where photoreceptors (rods for dim light, cones for color and acuity, concentrated in the macula and fovea) transduce it. Retinal ganglion cell axons form the optic nerve.

### The visual pathway and field defects

The lens inverts the image, so the nasal retina sees the temporal field and the temporal retina sees the nasal field. Fibers from the **nasal** retinas cross at the chiasm. Follow the pathway: retina, optic nerve, chiasm, optic tract, lateral geniculate nucleus, optic radiations, primary visual cortex (calcarine fissure, occipital lobe).

| Lesion | Field defect |
|---|---|
| Retina or optic nerve | Monocular vision loss (amaurosis fugax from carotid or retinal emboli) |
| Optic chiasm (pituitary adenoma, craniopharyngioma) | **Bitemporal hemianopia** |
| Optic tract | Contralateral homonymous hemianopia (incongruent) |
| Temporal radiations (Meyer loop) | Contralateral superior quadrantanopia ("pie in the sky") |
| Parietal radiations | Contralateral inferior quadrantanopia ("pie on the floor") |
| Occipital cortex (PCA stroke) | Contralateral homonymous hemianopia with **macular sparing** |

The rule: lesions before the chiasm cause monocular loss; at the chiasm, bitemporal; after the chiasm, homonymous (the same side of space in both eyes).

### Pupils

- **Relative afferent pupillary defect (Marcus Gunn):** on the swinging flashlight test the affected pupil dilates when light moves to it, because its afferent signal is weaker; optic neuritis, ischemic optic neuropathy or severe retinal disease.
- **Oculomotor (III) palsy:** eye down and out, ptosis, dilated pupil if compressive (posterior communicating aneurysm); a pupil-sparing palsy suggests ischemia from diabetes.
- **Horner syndrome:** ptosis, miosis and anhidrosis from interruption of the sympathetic pathway (hypothalamus to brainstem to spinal cord T1 to the superior cervical ganglion and carotid plexus). Causes: lateral medullary stroke, Pancoast tumor, carotid dissection.

### Common diseases

- **Open-angle glaucoma:** elevated pressure (normal 10 to 21 mmHg) with optic nerve cupping and arcuate field loss, usually asymptomatic. Treatment lowers intraocular pressure: prostaglandin analogues (latanoprost, increase uveoscleral outflow), beta-blockers (timolol, reduce aqueous production), alpha-agonists (brimonidine), carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (dorzolamide).
- **Acute angle-closure glaucoma:** painful red eye, halos, nausea, mid-dilated fixed pupil and a hard eye. Emergency: acetazolamide, topical timolol and pilocarpine, then laser peripheral iridotomy. Avoid mydriatics.
- **Central retinal artery occlusion:** sudden painless monocular blindness with a cherry-red spot; urgent evaluation for embolic source and for giant cell arteritis.
- **Central retinal vein occlusion:** "blood and thunder" retina with hemorrhages and edema.
- **Retinal detachment:** flashes, floaters and a curtain-like loss; surgical emergency.
- **Age-related macular degeneration:** central vision loss; the wet form is treated with intravitreal anti-VEGF (ranibizumab, aflibercept).
- **Diabetic retinopathy:** microaneurysms, then neovascularization; control glucose, laser and anti-VEGF.
- **Cataract:** lens opacity; steroids, diabetes and age are risk factors; treated with lens replacement.

### Refractive errors and lens power

Lens power in diopters is $P = 1/f$ with focal length $f$ in meters. In **myopia** the image focuses in front of the retina; correct with a concave (negative) lens whose focal length equals the far point. **Hyperopia** requires a convex lens; **presbyopia** (loss of accommodation after about 40 years) needs reading glasses; astigmatism needs a cylindrical lens.

@example
title: Correcting myopia
problem: A myopic patient cannot see clearly beyond 0.5 m (far point). What lens power corrects the distance vision?
step: The lens must form a virtual image of distant objects at the far point, so $f = -0.5$ m.
step: $P = 1/f = 1/(-0.5) = -2$ diopters.
step: A concave lens of $-2.0$ D corrects the myopia.

@mistakes
- Calling bitemporal hemianopia a retinal problem; it is a chiasm lesion.
- Dilating the pupils of a patient with possible angle-closure glaucoma.
- Treating painless sudden monocular vision loss as benign without checking for giant cell arteritis or emboli.
- Forgetting that the lens inverts the image, so retinal and field positions are opposite.
