### What the liver does

The liver synthesizes albumin and clotting factors (all except factor VIII, which is also made by endothelium) and bile acids, converts ammonia to urea, metabolizes drugs through cytochrome P450 and conjugation, stores glycogen and makes glucose, and clears bilirubin. Hepatocytes in **zone 3** (around the central vein) have the lowest oxygen and the most P450 activity, so they are injured first by ischemia, acetaminophen and alcohol.

### Bilirubin metabolism

Senescent red cells release heme. Heme is converted to biliverdin and then to **unconjugated (indirect) bilirubin**, which is lipid soluble, carried on albumin and not filtered by the kidney. Hepatocytes take it up, and **UDP-glucuronosyltransferase (UGT1A1)** conjugates it to glucuronic acid. **Conjugated (direct) bilirubin** is water soluble and is excreted into bile through the canalicular transporter MRP2. Gut bacteria convert it to urobilinogen; most becomes **stercobilin** (brown stool), a portion is reabsorbed and excreted by the kidney as **urobilin** (yellow urine), and the remainder recirculates in the enterohepatic circulation.

- **Unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia:** hemolysis, **Gilbert syndrome** (reduced UGT1A1; benign; mild jaundice with fasting, illness or stress; normal liver tests otherwise), Crigler-Najjar syndrome (severe), and physiologic neonatal jaundice. Bilirubin is **absent from urine** (acholuric).
- **Conjugated hyperbilirubinemia:** biliary obstruction, intrahepatic cholestasis, hepatocellular disease, **Dubin-Johnson** (MRP2 defect, black liver) and Rotor syndrome. Bilirubin appears in urine (dark urine), and with obstruction the stool is pale.

### The liver panel

| Test | What it reflects |
|---|---|
| ALT | Hepatocellular injury (more specific to liver) |
| AST | Hepatocellular injury; also muscle, heart, red cells |
| Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) | Cholestasis; also bone, placenta; confirmed as hepatic by elevated **GGT** |
| Total and direct bilirubin | Excretory function and cholestasis |
| Albumin | Chronic synthetic function (half-life about 3 weeks); also reduced by nephrotic syndrome and inflammation |
| INR (prothrombin time) | Acute synthetic function (factor VII half-life of hours); corrects with vitamin K in cholestasis and malabsorption but not in severe hepatocellular failure |

### Patterns and the R factor

Divide each enzyme by its upper limit of normal (ULN):

$$R = \frac{\text{ALT} / \text{ALT}_{ULN}}{\text{ALP} / \text{ALP}_{ULN}}$$

**R of 5 or more:** hepatocellular. **R of 2 or less:** cholestatic. **Between 2 and 5:** mixed. R is widely used in drug-induced liver injury.

- **Hepatocellular, ALT in the thousands:** acute viral hepatitis, **ischemic hepatitis** ("shock liver"), acetaminophen toxicity, autoimmune hepatitis, toxins.
- **Hepatocellular, AST:ALT above 2:1 with AST rarely above 300:** alcohol-associated hepatitis (pyridoxine deficiency, and mitochondrial AST).
- **Cholestatic:** biliary obstruction (stones, tumors), primary biliary cholangitis (antimitochondrial antibodies, middle-aged women), primary sclerosing cholangitis, drugs, infiltrative disease. First imaging is **right upper quadrant ultrasound** for duct dilation.
- **Mild ALT elevation:** metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease is the most common cause, followed by alcohol, medications, viral hepatitis and hemochromatosis.

### MELD score

The **Model for End-Stage Liver Disease** predicts short-term mortality in cirrhosis and ranks liver transplant candidates. Its components are **bilirubin, INR and creatinine** (the version used for allocation also adds sodium, MELD-Na; newer versions add sex and albumin). The original equation, with each value set to a minimum of 1.0 and creatinine capped at 4.0 mg/dL, is

$$\text{MELD} = 3.78\ln(\text{bilirubin}) + 11.2\ln(\text{INR}) + 9.57\ln(\text{creatinine}) + 6.43.$$

@example
title: Classify the pattern
problem: A patient has ALT 900 U/L (ULN 40), ALP 150 U/L (ULN 120), total bilirubin 3.0 mg/dL. Calculate R and classify the pattern.
step: ALT is 900 / 40 = 22.5 times ULN.
step: ALP is 150 / 120 = 1.25 times ULN.
step: R = 22.5 / 1.25 = 18, well above 5: hepatocellular injury. With ALT this high consider viral hepatitis, ischemia and acetaminophen.

@example
title: Gilbert syndrome or something else?
problem: A 20-year-old has mild jaundice during a viral illness. Total bilirubin is 2.8 mg/dL, direct 0.3 mg/dL; ALT, AST, ALP and hemoglobin are normal and urine bilirubin is negative. Explain.
step: Bilirubin is mainly unconjugated (indirect 2.5 of 2.8 mg/dL).
step: Normal hemoglobin and liver tests argue against hemolysis and liver disease.
step: Reduced UGT1A1 activity (Gilbert syndrome) is unmasked by stress. No treatment is needed; reassure.

@mistakes
- Dividing by absolute enzyme values rather than the upper limit of normal when calculating R.
- Treating AST:ALT above 2 with AST in the thousands as alcohol-associated: alcohol rarely pushes AST above about 300.
- Using albumin as a marker of acute liver failure: it has a half-life of about 3 weeks.
- Expecting urine bilirubin in Gilbert syndrome: unconjugated bilirubin is not filtered.
