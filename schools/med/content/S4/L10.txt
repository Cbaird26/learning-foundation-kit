### Gallstone disease

**Cholesterol stones** (about 80%) form when bile is supersaturated with cholesterol, nucleation occurs and the gallbladder empties poorly. Risk factors are female sex, obesity, age over 40, fertility (pregnancy, estrogen), rapid weight loss, ileal disease and ceftriaxone. **Black pigment stones** accompany hemolysis (sickle cell disease, spherocytosis) and cirrhosis; **brown stones** accompany biliary infection.

| Condition | Mechanism | Key features | Management |
|---|---|---|---|
| Biliary colic | Transient cystic duct obstruction | Postprandial RUQ or epigastric pain lasting 30 minutes to hours; no fever; normal labs | Elective cholecystectomy |
| Acute cholecystitis | Persistent cystic duct obstruction and inflammation | Pain over 6 hours, fever, leukocytosis, Murphy sign | NPO, fluids, antibiotics, early laparoscopic cholecystectomy |
| Choledocholithiasis | Stone in common bile duct | Elevated ALP, GGT, bilirubin; dilated duct | Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) for stone removal |
| Cholangitis | Obstruction plus bacterial infection | **Charcot triad** (fever, RUQ pain, jaundice); **Reynolds pentad** adds hypotension and confusion | IV antibiotics and urgent biliary decompression |

Ultrasound is the first test for gallbladder disease: stones, wall thickening above 3 mm, pericholecystic fluid and a sonographic Murphy sign. If ultrasound is equivocal, a **HIDA scan** shows nonfilling of the gallbladder in cholecystitis. MRCP or endoscopic ultrasound evaluates the common bile duct. **Acalculous cholecystitis** occurs in critically ill patients. **Gallstone ileus** is a stone passing through a cholecystoenteric fistula and obstructing the terminal ileum (pneumobilia, small-bowel obstruction). A low HIDA gallbladder ejection fraction (below about 35 to 40%, depending on the laboratory) supports biliary dyskinesia in a patient with biliary pain and no stones. A **porcelain gallbladder** is linked to cancer. **Primary sclerosing cholangitis** and **primary biliary cholangitis** are cholestatic autoimmune diseases covered in the liver lessons.

### Acute pancreatitis

Premature activation of trypsinogen in acinar cells causes autodigestion. Causes: **gallstones** and **alcohol** (most common), hypertriglyceridemia (usually above 1,000 mg/dL), hypercalcemia, ERCP, drugs (for example azathioprine, valproate, GLP-1 receptor agonists, thiazides), trauma, IgG4-related autoimmune disease, scorpion venom, and infections.

**Revised Atlanta criteria:** diagnosis requires two of three: characteristic epigastric pain that radiates to the back, serum **lipase (or amylase) above three times the upper limit of normal**, and characteristic imaging. Lipase is more specific than amylase. Imaging is not required at presentation if the diagnosis is clear. **Severity:** mild (no organ failure), moderately severe (transient organ failure under 48 hours or local complications), severe (persistent organ failure over 48 hours).

**Management** is supportive: early **goal-directed IV fluids** with lactated Ringer's (a moderate approach is favored over aggressive: for study, a bolus of 10 mL/kg if hypovolemic, then about 1.5 mL/kg per hour), analgesia, and **early oral feeding** as tolerated. Prophylactic antibiotics are not recommended. ERCP within 24 hours only for concomitant cholangitis; otherwise no urgent ERCP for gallstone pancreatitis. Perform **cholecystectomy during the same admission** for mild gallstone pancreatitis. Treat hypertriglyceridemia with insulin infusion or apheresis in severe cases.

Complications: **pseudocyst** (more than 4 weeks, encapsulated), **necrosis** (infected necrosis is managed with antibiotics and delayed step-up drainage), ARDS, shock, hypocalcemia, splenic vein thrombosis and fluid collections.

### Chronic pancreatitis and pancreatic cancer

**Chronic pancreatitis** (alcohol, smoking, genetic) gives chronic pain, calcifications, exocrine insufficiency (steatorrhea, treated with pancreatic enzymes) and endocrine insufficiency (diabetes). **Pancreatic adenocarcinoma** arises from ductal cells (KRAS mutation), mostly in the head: painless jaundice, weight loss, **Courvoisier sign** (palpable nontender gallbladder), and migratory thrombophlebitis (Trousseau). Risks include smoking, chronic pancreatitis, new-onset diabetes in older adults and BRCA2. Use pancreas-protocol CT; CA 19-9 monitors response but does not screen. Resection (Whipple procedure) is the only cure for localized disease.

### Complications of cirrhosis in one plan

Think of cirrhosis as portal hypertension plus failing synthesis, and work through the organ systems. **Ascites:** sodium restriction, spironolactone and furosemide, paracentesis with albumin. **SBP:** ceftriaxone and albumin, then prophylaxis. **Varices:** beta-blocker or banding for prophylaxis; octreotide, ceftriaxone and endoscopic banding for bleeding. **Hepatorenal syndrome:** stop diuretics, give albumin, add terlipressin or norepinephrine. **Encephalopathy:** treat precipitants, lactulose, rifaximin. **HCC:** ultrasound every 6 months. **Definitive:** transplant evaluation, with MELD or Child-Pugh guiding timing.

@example
title: Diagnosing acute pancreatitis
problem: A 45-year-old man has severe epigastric pain radiating to the back and vomiting after heavy drinking. Lipase is 540 U/L (upper limit 60). Does he meet criteria?
step: Pain is characteristic. Lipase is 540 / 60 = 9 times the upper limit, well over 3 times.
step: Two of three criteria are met: no imaging is needed to confirm.
step: Give lactated Ringer's, analgesia and early oral feeding; look for gallstones and triglycerides; avoid prophylactic antibiotics.

@example
title: Cholangitis recognized
problem: A 70-year-old woman has fever, RUQ pain, jaundice, a blood pressure of 85/50 mm Hg and confusion. Ultrasound shows a dilated common bile duct with a stone. What is the plan?
step: Fever, pain and jaundice make Charcot triad; hypotension and confusion make Reynolds pentad: severe cholangitis with sepsis.
step: Resuscitate, draw blood cultures and start broad-spectrum IV antibiotics.
step: Urgent biliary decompression by ERCP with sphincterotomy and stone extraction. Do not wait for elective surgery.

@mistakes
- Waiting for imaging or amylase to treat suspected cholangitis: decompression is urgent.
- Giving prophylactic antibiotics in acute pancreatitis: they are not recommended.
- Using aggressive fluid loading in pancreatitis without monitoring: moderate resuscitation is favored.
- Using CA 19-9 as a screening test for pancreatic cancer.

@practice self
prompt: Explain the major complications of cirrhosis and the management of each.
hint: Group the complications by portal hypertension (ascites, varices, SBP, hepatorenal syndrome), encephalopathy and cancer.
hint: For each give one diagnostic point and one treatment.
solution: Ascites is diagnosed by paracentesis; SAAG 1.1 or more indicates portal hypertension. Treatment is sodium restriction, spironolactone with furosemide, large-volume paracentesis with albumin, and TIPS or transplant for refractory disease. SBP is diagnosed by ascitic PMN of 250 per microliter or more and treated with ceftriaxone and albumin, followed by prophylaxis. Varices are prevented with nonselective beta-blocker or banding; bleeding is treated with resuscitation, octreotide, ceftriaxone and band ligation within 12 hours. Hepatorenal syndrome is AKI without another cause that does not respond to albumin; treat with albumin plus terlipressin or norepinephrine, and transplant. Hepatic encephalopathy is treated by correcting precipitants and giving lactulose with rifaximin to prevent recurrence. HCC surveillance is ultrasound every 6 months. All patients are assessed for transplant using MELD, avoiding NSAIDs, and vaccinated.
