@article
Almost every GI disease you will meet this year can be traced back to a few anatomic ideas: what layer is involved, which artery feeds the segment, which vein drains it, and how the tube was built in the embryo. This lesson gives you that scaffold.

### The wall of the gut

From lumen outward the tube has four layers: **mucosa** (epithelium, lamina propria, muscularis mucosae), **submucosa**, **muscularis externa** (inner circular, outer longitudinal smooth muscle) and **serosa** (visceral peritoneum; the esophagus and retroperitoneal segments have an adventitia instead). Two plexuses form the enteric nervous system. The **submucosal (Meissner) plexus** regulates secretion and local blood flow. The **myenteric (Auerbach) plexus** lies between the two muscle layers and regulates motility. Parasympathetic input comes from the vagus nerve (to the transverse colon) and pelvic splanchnic nerves S2 to S4 (beyond); sympathetic input arrives via splanchnic nerves and slows motility.

### Three embryologic gut segments

| Segment | Extent | Artery | Approximate level |
|---|---|---|---|
| Foregut | Distal esophagus to the second part of the duodenum (proximal to the ampulla), plus liver, gallbladder, pancreas | Celiac trunk | T12 |
| Midgut | Duodenum distal to the ampulla to the proximal two thirds of the transverse colon | Superior mesenteric artery (SMA) | L1 |
| Hindgut | Distal third of the transverse colon to the anal canal above the dentate line | Inferior mesenteric artery (IMA) | L3 |

The **splenic flexure** and the **rectosigmoid junction** lie at the borders between territories and are **watershed areas**: they are the segments most vulnerable to ischemic colitis during hypotension.

### Rotation of the midgut

During weeks 6 to 10 the midgut elongates faster than the abdominal cavity grows, so it herniates into the umbilical cord. It rotates 90 degrees counterclockwise (seen from the front) around the SMA while herniated, returns to the abdomen during week 10, and rotates another 180 degrees, for a total of **270 degrees**. Failure of this process gives **malrotation**. The cecum then sits near the midline or upper abdomen and peritoneal (Ladd) bands cross the duodenum. The narrow mesenteric base can twist: **midgut volvulus** presents with bilious vomiting in an infant and is a surgical emergency (upper GI series shows a corkscrew duodenum).

### Congenital anomalies to know

- **Duodenal atresia**: failure of recanalization of the solid-cord stage; bilious (or nonbilious if proximal to the ampulla) vomiting, "double bubble" sign, associated with Down syndrome and polyhydramnios.
- **Esophageal atresia with tracheoesophageal fistula**: most common type is a proximal blind pouch with a distal fistula between trachea and lower esophagus. Infants drool, choke with feeds, and have gas in the stomach.
- **Gastroschisis**: bowel herniates through a defect lateral (usually right) to an intact cord, with no covering sac, and few associated anomalies. **Omphalocele**: bowel (and often liver) herniates into the base of the cord inside a membrane; it is strongly associated with other anomalies, including trisomies 13 and 18 and Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome.
- **Meckel diverticulum**: a persistent remnant of the vitelline (omphalomesenteric) duct in the ileum, the "rule of 2s" (about 2% of people, 2 feet from the ileocecal valve, 2 inches long, often presents before age 2, may contain ectopic gastric or pancreatic tissue). Ectopic gastric mucosa causes painless rectal bleeding; a technetium-99m pertechnetate scan detects it.
- **Hirschsprung disease**: neural crest cells fail to migrate into the distal bowel, so the rectum (and a variable length of colon) lacks ganglion cells in both plexuses. Newborn with delayed meconium (over 48 hours), distension, and a tight empty rectum with explosive stool after examination. RET mutations are common; diagnosis is by rectal suction biopsy.
- **Annular pancreas** (ventral bud wraps the duodenum) can cause duodenal obstruction.

### Venous drainage and portosystemic collaterals

Venous blood from the gut, pancreas and spleen reaches the liver through the **portal vein** (formed by the splenic and superior mesenteric veins). When portal pressure rises, blood finds low-resistance paths back to the systemic veins:

1. **Left gastric to esophageal veins to azygos**: esophageal and gastric varices.
2. **Superior rectal (portal) to middle and inferior rectal (systemic)**: anorectal varices and hemorrhoids.
3. **Paraumbilical to superficial epigastric veins**: caput medusae.
4. **Retroperitoneal** connections of colonic veins to systemic veins.

### The dentate line

Above the **dentate line** the anal canal is hindgut-derived, with visceral innervation (painless), drainage to the portal system via the superior rectal vein, lymph to internal iliac and inferior mesenteric nodes, and columnar epithelium (internal hemorrhoids, adenocarcinoma). Below it, innervation is somatic by the inferior rectal nerve (painful), venous drainage is systemic, lymph goes to the **superficial inguinal nodes**, and the epithelium is squamous (external hemorrhoids, squamous cell carcinoma).

@example
title: Why does a patient with cirrhosis bleed from the esophagus and the rectum?
problem: A 60-year-old man with alcohol-associated cirrhosis vomits bright red blood. Endoscopy shows dilated veins in the distal esophagus. He also has large anorectal varices. Explain how one disease creates both.
step: Cirrhosis distorts liver architecture and raises resistance to flow through the sinusoids, so pressure in the portal vein rises (portal hypertension).
step: Veins that connect portal and systemic circulations have no valves and carry little flow normally. As portal pressure climbs, flow reverses through them.
step: The left gastric vein drains into submucosal esophageal veins that connect to the azygos vein, so the lower esophagus fills with high-pressure blood and forms varices.
step: The superior rectal vein is portal, and the middle and inferior rectal veins are systemic. Rising pressure dilates the rectal plexus and gives anorectal varices. Both lesions are thin-walled veins under pressure, so both can bleed.

@example
title: A newborn who vomits bile
problem: A term newborn with Down syndrome vomits bilious fluid on day 1. An abdominal radiograph shows two gas bubbles and no distal gas. What is the diagnosis and what failed in development?
step: Two air-filled bubbles (stomach and proximal duodenum) with no gas beyond is the double bubble sign: complete obstruction at the duodenum.
step: Duodenal atresia results from failure of the solid epithelial cord of the duodenum to recanalize during weeks 8 to 10.
step: Down syndrome is associated with duodenal atresia (and Hirschsprung disease), and the mother may have had polyhydramnios because the fetus could not swallow and absorb amniotic fluid efficiently.

@mistakes
- Saying the myenteric plexus controls secretion: it is the submucosal plexus that regulates secretion and blood flow; the myenteric plexus controls motility.
- Calling the splenic flexure hindgut territory only: it is a watershed between the SMA and IMA, which is why it is prone to ischemia.
- Mixing lymphatic drainage across the dentate line: above goes to internal iliac nodes, below goes to superficial inguinal nodes.
- Confusing omphalocele (membrane-covered, midline, many associated anomalies) with gastroschisis (no membrane, right of the cord, few anomalies).
