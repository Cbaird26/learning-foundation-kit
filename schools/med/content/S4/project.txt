overview: You will interpret four supplied liver panels, calculate R factors, Child-Pugh and MELD scores, and then plan the initial management of two upper GI bleeds step by step. The workshop turns the course's mechanisms into the decisions you will make on call. All patients are invented and the doses are typical adult doses for study only.
goal: Classify a liver test pattern as hepatocellular, cholestatic or mixed, propose a differential diagnosis and choose the next test.
goal: Calculate the R factor, Child-Pugh class and MELD score from supplied data and explain what each means.
goal: Plan the initial management of an upper GI bleed in order, including the variceal bleeding additions.
step: Read the supplied data. Upper limits of normal (ULN) are ALT 40 U/L and ALP 120 U/L.
- Panel A (34-year-old woman, 2 days of malaise and dark urine, no alcohol): ALT 1,450 U/L, AST 1,200 U/L, ALP 110 U/L, total bilirubin 4.2 mg/dL (direct 2.9), INR 1.3, albumin 3.8 g/dL.
- Panel B (51-year-old man, heavy daily alcohol use): ALT 100 U/L, AST 240 U/L, ALP 100 U/L, GGT 400 U/L (elevated), total bilirubin 3.0 mg/dL, INR 1.4, albumin 3.2 g/dL.
- Panel C (57-year-old woman, itching for 6 months): ALT 60 U/L, AST 55 U/L, ALP 640 U/L, GGT 520 U/L, total bilirubin 6.8 mg/dL (direct 5.9), INR 1.1, albumin 3.9 g/dL.
- Panel D (62-year-old man with cirrhosis): total bilirubin 3.4 mg/dL, INR 2.1, albumin 2.7 g/dL, creatinine 1.8 mg/dL, sodium 134 mEq/L, moderate ascites, grade 1 to 2 encephalopathy.
- Bleed 1: a 64-year-old man taking naproxen and low-dose aspirin has had black stools for 2 days and feels dizzy. Heart rate 118 per minute, blood pressure 92/58 mm Hg, hemoglobin 7.4 g/dL (baseline 13), BUN 62 mg/dL, creatinine 1.0 mg/dL, INR 1.1.
- Bleed 2: a 55-year-old woman with known cirrhosis from hepatitis C vomits bright red blood. Heart rate 124 per minute, blood pressure 86/50 mm Hg, hemoglobin 7.0 g/dL, platelets 70,000 per microliter, INR 1.8.
step: For Panels A, B and C compute the R factor from ALT and ALP as multiples of ULN, classify each pattern and write a ranked differential with one reason per diagnosis (for example viral hepatitis, ischemic or toxic injury, autoimmune hepatitis, alcohol-associated hepatitis, primary biliary cholangitis, choledocholithiasis).
step: For each panel name the next one or two tests (for example hepatitis serologies and acetaminophen level; right upper quadrant ultrasound and antimitochondrial antibodies) and say why each discriminates between your top diagnoses.
step: For Panel D compute the Child-Pugh points and class and the original MELD score with the formula 3.78 ln(bilirubin) + 11.2 ln(INR) + 9.57 ln(creatinine) + 6.43. State what a high MELD means and which complications you would look for in this patient.
step: For Bleed 1, calculate the shock index and the BUN to creatinine ratio, then write the ordered initial management: airway, access, fluids, transfusion decision, medications, endoscopy timing and later steps (H. pylori testing, NSAID and aspirin plan).
step: For Bleed 2, write the same plan and mark every addition because varices are suspected (vasoactive drug, antibiotic, endoscopy within 12 hours, restrictive transfusion and the reason). List the complications you would watch for in the next 48 hours.
step: Finish with a one-page summary table: panel or bleed, key calculation, pattern or diagnosis, and first three actions.

### Week by week

- **Week 1, anatomy and embryology:** gut layers and plexuses (myenteric for motility, submucosal for secretion), celiac/SMA/IMA territories, midgut rotation of 270 degrees, malrotation, atresias, gastroschisis versus omphalocele, Meckel, Hirschsprung, portosystemic anastomoses, dentate line.
- **Week 2, physiology:** slow waves, migrating motor complex, gastrin/CCK/secretin, parietal cell regulation by acetylcholine, gastrin and histamine, somatostatin feedback, alkaline tide, absorption sites.
- **Week 3, esophagus and stomach:** achalasia, GERD and alarm features, Barrett, eosinophilic esophagitis, Mallory-Weiss versus Boerhaave, gastritis types, gastric cancer.
- **Week 4, ulcer and bleeding:** H. pylori, NSAIDs, Zollinger-Ellison, complications, first-hour management, Forrest classification, variceal bundle.
- **Week 5, malabsorption:** mechanisms, fecal fat, D-xylose, osmotic gap, celiac, SIBO, Whipple, obstruction, mesenteric ischemia.
- **Week 6, IBD:** Crohn versus ulcerative colitis, extraintestinal disease, toxic megacolon, treatment ladder, screening before biologics.
- **Week 7, colon:** diverticular disease, polyps, adenoma-carcinoma sequence, Lynch, FAP, screening from age 45, family-history rule.
- **Week 8, liver tests:** bilirubin metabolism, enzymes, R factor, MELD.
- **Week 9, hepatitis and cirrhosis:** HBV serology, other chronic liver diseases, SAAG, PMN count, Child-Pugh, complications.
- **Week 10, gallbladder and pancreas:** cholecystitis, cholangitis, Atlanta criteria, supportive care, pancreatic cancer.
- **Week 11, infections:** syndromes, pathogens, ORS, C. difficile, when not to use antibiotics.
- **Week 12, pharmacology:** PPI, H2 blockers, prokinetics, antiemetics, antidiarrheals, IBD drugs, infusion calculations.

### Self-check

- Can you draw acid secretion with three stimuli and the somatostatin brake?
- Can you compute R, SAAG, PMN count, osmotic gap, shock index and MELD without notes?
- Can you list the first seven actions in an upper GI bleed?
- Can you match each diarrhea syndrome to a treatment, including the two situations where antibiotics are harmful?

### Common errors

- Confusing the plexuses, or saying gastrin acts mainly on the parietal cell.
- Using serology to prove H. pylori cure.
- Forgetting that Crohn disease is transmural and can involve any part of the gut, and that 5-ASA does not treat it.
- Using raw enzyme values instead of multiples of the upper limit of normal in the R factor.
- Judging ascites by transudate versus exudate instead of the SAAG.
- Giving antibiotics or loperamide for STEC.

prompt: A 58-year-old man with cirrhosis presents with fever, abdominal pain and mild confusion. Serum albumin is 2.9 g/dL. Paracentesis shows ascitic albumin 1.3 g/dL, total WBC 900 per microliter with 50% neutrophils, and a negative Gram stain. His blood pressure is 100/60 mm Hg and creatinine is 1.1 mg/dL. What is the best management?
choice*: IV ceftriaxone with IV albumin
choice: IV furosemide and spironolactone
choice: Lactulose alone
choice: Nonselective beta-blocker and repeat paracentesis in 1 week
choice: Large-volume paracentesis without antibiotics
hint: Calculate the PMN count and compare it with the threshold.
hint: PMN = 450 per microliter, above 250; SAAG = 1.6, consistent with portal hypertension.
solution: PMN count is 900 times 0.5 = 450 per microliter, above 250, so he has spontaneous bacterial peritonitis. SAAG is 2.9 - 1.3 = 1.6 g/dL, consistent with cirrhosis. Treat with ceftriaxone and albumin and consider long-term prophylaxis. Diuretics worsen kidney injury and lactulose alone treats only the encephalopathy.

@exam self
prompt: A 56-year-old man with alcohol-associated cirrhosis presents with hematemesis, heart rate 126 per minute, blood pressure 84/48 mm Hg and hemoglobin 7.2 g/dL. Outline the first 24 hours of management, including which findings would make you think of each cirrhosis complication over the next days and how you would treat them.
hint: Start with resuscitation and airway protection, then the variceal bundle.
hint: Then consider infection, kidney injury, encephalopathy and alcohol withdrawal.
solution: Assess the airway and protect it (intubation if vomiting blood with altered mental status). Place two large-bore IV lines, give judicious crystalloid and send CBC, chemistry, INR, liver tests, lactate and crossmatch. Transfuse red cells restrictively to a hemoglobin of 7 to 8 g/dL, avoiding overtransfusion because it raises portal pressure. Start octreotide and IV ceftriaxone, and an IV PPI until varices are confirmed. Perform endoscopy within 12 hours, with band ligation for esophageal varices. Failure or early high-risk bleeding leads to TIPS or balloon tamponade as a bridge. Over the next days watch for spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (diagnostic paracentesis; ceftriaxone and albumin), hepatorenal syndrome (stop diuretics, give albumin and terlipressin or norepinephrine), hepatic encephalopathy (lactulose, rifaximin), and alcohol withdrawal (benzodiazepines, thiamine). Start a nonselective beta-blocker with banding for secondary prophylaxis, and assess for transplant and alcohol use treatment.
rubric: Resuscitates before endoscopy and protects the airway.
rubric: Includes octreotide, ceftriaxone, restrictive transfusion and endoscopic band ligation within 12 hours.
rubric: Names SBP, hepatorenal syndrome and encephalopathy with a treatment for each.
rubric: Plans secondary prophylaxis and transplant evaluation.
