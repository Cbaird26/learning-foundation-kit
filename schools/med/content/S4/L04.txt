@article
### How ulcers form

A peptic ulcer is a mucosal break that penetrates the muscularis mucosae. It forms when aggressive factors (acid, pepsin, H. pylori) overwhelm mucosal defense (mucus, bicarbonate, blood flow, **prostaglandins**).

- **H. pylori** is a spiral gram-negative organism that survives in acid by producing **urease**, which turns urea into ammonia. Virulence factors include CagA and VacA. It causes most duodenal ulcers and many gastric ulcers.
- **NSAIDs** inhibit COX-1, reducing protective prostaglandins, and cause both gastric and duodenal ulcers.
- **Zollinger-Ellison syndrome** (gastrinoma, often in the duodenum or pancreas, associated with MEN1 in about a quarter of cases) produces massive acid output, with multiple or refractory ulcers in unusual sites (distal duodenum, jejunum), diarrhea and fasting hypergastrinemia. Gastrin is elevated with gastric pH below 2; if gastrin is equivocal a **secretin stimulation test** shows a paradoxical rise in gastrin (an increase of 120 pg/mL or more).

**Duodenal ulcer** pain often occurs 2 to 5 hours after meals or at night and is relieved by food; **gastric ulcer** pain is often worsened by eating with weight loss. Gastric ulcers can be malignant, so they are biopsied, whereas duodenal ulcers almost never are.

### H. pylori testing and treatment

Noninvasive tests for active infection are the **urea breath test** and **stool antigen test**. Both require stopping PPIs for 2 weeks and antibiotics for 4 weeks to avoid false negatives. Serology shows only past exposure, so it is not used to confirm eradication. Biopsy-based tests include rapid urease test and histology. Current U.S. guidance favors **bismuth quadruple therapy for 14 days** (PPI, bismuth, tetracycline and metronidazole) as first-line, because clarithromycin resistance is common; clarithromycin triple therapy is now reserved for infections shown to be clarithromycin-susceptible, and rifabutin triple therapy or vonoprazan with amoxicillin (14 days) are alternatives for patients without penicillin allergy (ACG 2024). Eradication is confirmed with a urea breath test or stool antigen test at least 4 weeks after treatment. For NSAID ulcers, stop the NSAID if possible and give a PPI, and give a PPI for patients who must continue NSAIDs or aspirin.

### Complications

- **Bleeding:** most common. Posterior duodenal ulcers erode the gastroduodenal artery.
- **Perforation:** anterior duodenal ulcers; sudden severe epigastric pain with a rigid abdomen and free air under the diaphragm on upright chest radiograph.
- **Gastric outlet obstruction:** early satiety, nonbilious vomiting of undigested food, succussion splash and hypochloremic hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis.

### Upper GI bleeding: the first hour

Upper GI bleeding arises proximal to the ligament of Treitz. Presentations are hematemesis, coffee-ground emesis, **melena** (black tarry stool), or hematochezia when bleeding is brisk. A BUN-to-creatinine ratio above 30 favors an upper source because blood proteins are digested and absorbed. The most common cause is **peptic ulcer disease**; others are varices, erosive esophagitis and gastritis, Mallory-Weiss tear, Dieulafoy lesion, angiodysplasia and malignancy.

**Initial management** follows resuscitation before diagnosis:
1. Assess airway, breathing, circulation; intubate if consciousness or airway is compromised.
2. Place **two large-bore IV lines** and give crystalloid for hypotension; send CBC, chemistry, INR, liver tests, lactate and **type and crossmatch**.
3. Use a **restrictive transfusion threshold**: transfuse for hemoglobin below **7 g/dL** (below 8 in cardiovascular disease), except with hemodynamic instability. One unit of red cells raises hemoglobin by about 1 g/dL in an adult. Avoid overtransfusion in suspected varices because it raises portal pressure.
4. Risk stratify: the **shock index** (heart rate divided by systolic pressure, normally about 0.5 to 0.7) and the **Glasgow-Blatchford score** (BUN, hemoglobin, systolic pressure, pulse, melena, syncope, hepatic disease, cardiac failure); very low scores identify patients safe for outpatient care.
5. Give an **IV PPI**, hold anticoagulants and NSAIDs, and consider **erythromycin** before endoscopy to clear blood.
6. Perform **endoscopy within 24 hours** of presentation, once resuscitated; within **12 hours** if variceal bleeding is suspected.
7. **If varices are suspected** (known cirrhosis, stigmata): add a vasoactive drug (**octreotide**), **antibiotic prophylaxis (ceftriaxone)**, and endoscopic **band ligation**.

At endoscopy, ulcers are graded by the **Forrest classification**: Ia spurting, Ib oozing, IIa nonbleeding visible vessel, IIb adherent clot, IIc flat pigmented spot, III clean base. High-risk lesions (Ia, Ib, IIa; clot removal for IIb) receive endoscopic hemostasis (clips, thermal coagulation or injection), followed by high-dose PPI for about 72 hours. Rebleeding after therapy is managed with repeat endoscopy, then interventional radiology embolization or surgery.

@example
title: First hour for a bleeding patient
problem: A 66-year-old man taking naproxen for arthritis presents with melena and dizziness. Heart rate is 122 per minute, blood pressure 88/50 mm Hg, hemoglobin 8.1 g/dL. What are the first steps?
step: He is unstable (tachycardia and hypotension; shock index 122/88 = 1.39). Secure the airway if needed, place two large-bore IV lines, and begin crystalloid. Send type and crossmatch, CBC, chemistry, INR and lactate.
step: Hemoglobin 8.1 is above the restrictive threshold of 7, but he is in shock, so give blood for hemodynamic instability while resuscitating.
step: Give an IV PPI, stop naproxen, and arrange endoscopy within 24 hours after stabilization.
step: At endoscopy, a visible vessel in the ulcer base (Forrest IIa) would be treated with endoscopic hemostasis, then a high-dose PPI for about 72 hours. Test for H. pylori and treat if positive.

@example
title: Which test confirms eradication?
problem: A patient with a duodenal ulcer finishes 14 days of bismuth quadruple therapy. He has been taking a PPI daily since. When and how should eradication be tested?
step: Wait at least 4 weeks after antibiotics.
step: Stop the PPI for 2 weeks before testing; otherwise the breath and stool tests may give false negatives.
step: Use a urea breath test or stool antigen test. Serology stays positive after cure and is not suitable.

@mistakes
- Using H. pylori serology to prove cure: antibodies persist long after eradication.
- Transfusing every patient with hemoglobin below 10 g/dL: use a restrictive threshold (7, or 8 with cardiovascular disease) unless unstable.
- Forgetting that a gastric ulcer needs biopsy to exclude cancer while a duodenal ulcer rarely does.
- Delaying resuscitation for endoscopy: stabilize first, then endoscope.
