### Common colonic disorders

- **Diverticulosis:** acquired outpouchings (false diverticula: mucosa and submucosa herniating through the muscle where vasa recta penetrate) mostly in the sigmoid colon, associated with low-fiber diet and age. Usually silent. It is a common cause of **painless hematochezia** because the vasa recta at the diverticular neck can rupture.
- **Diverticulitis:** inflammation of a diverticulum with left lower quadrant pain, fever, leukocytosis. **CT of the abdomen and pelvis with contrast** is the diagnostic test. Uncomplicated disease in an otherwise well patient may be managed with outpatient observation or short-course antibiotics; complicated disease (abscess, perforation, fistula, obstruction) needs admission, antibiotics, and drainage or surgery. Avoid colonoscopy during an acute episode; perform it after recovery if no recent colonoscopy.
- **Ischemic colitis:** older patient with hypotension or vascular disease; abdominal pain followed by bloody diarrhea, usually at the watershed areas (splenic flexure, rectosigmoid). Treat supportively; surgery for gangrene.
- **Volvulus:** sigmoid volvulus in older, constipated or institutionalized patients: "coffee bean" dilated loop on radiograph; sigmoidoscopic detorsion first. Cecal volvulus is less common and usually needs surgery.
- **Angiodysplasia:** vascular ectasias in the right colon of older adults with painless bleeding, associated with aortic stenosis (Heyde syndrome).
- **Anorectal:** internal hemorrhoids (above the dentate line; painless bleeding), external hemorrhoids (below; painful when thrombosed), **anal fissure** (posterior midline; severe pain with defecation; treated with fiber, topical nifedipine or nitroglycerin, or sphincterotomy).
- **Irritable bowel syndrome:** a functional disorder diagnosed by symptom criteria (Rome IV: recurrent abdominal pain at least once a week related to defecation with change in stool frequency or form) in the absence of alarm features.

### Polyps and the adenoma-carcinoma sequence

Most colorectal cancers arise from **adenomatous polyps**. Histology: tubular, tubulovillous, villous (highest malignant potential; larger polyps and high-grade dysplasia also raise risk). Sessile serrated lesions are a second precursor pathway (BRAF mutation, CpG island methylation). Hamartomatous polyps (Peutz-Jeghers with STK11 mutation; juvenile polyps) are mainly a risk in syndromes.

In the **chromosomal instability pathway** (about 80%): **APC** loss, then **KRAS** activation, then **TP53** loss, along with loss of 18q (SMAD4 and DCC). In the **microsatellite instability (MSI) pathway**, loss of DNA mismatch repair (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2) allows replication errors to accumulate.

### Hereditary syndromes

- **Lynch syndrome (hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer):** autosomal dominant germline mismatch repair mutation; early-onset, right-sided colon cancer; also endometrial, ovarian, gastric and urinary tract cancers. Tumors are tested for MSI or loss of mismatch repair protein staining.
- **Familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP):** germline APC mutation; hundreds to thousands of colonic adenomas in adolescence; essentially 100% cancer risk without colectomy. Gardner syndrome adds osteomas, desmoid tumors and epidermoid cysts.

### Screening and surveillance

For **average-risk adults, screening runs from age 45 to 75** (USPSTF 2021; the American Cancer Society agrees). Options include **colonoscopy every 10 years**, **annual FIT**, multitarget stool DNA-FIT (every 1 to 3 years), CT colonography every 5 years and flexible sigmoidoscopy every 5 years. A **positive stool test requires follow-up colonoscopy**, not a repeat stool test. For individuals with a first-degree relative diagnosed with colorectal cancer before age 60, colonoscopy begins at **age 40 or 10 years before the youngest relative's diagnosis, whichever is earlier**, repeated about every 5 years. Surveillance after polypectomy is individualized by number, size and histology (intervals range from 1 to 10 years).

### Colorectal cancer

**Right-sided** tumors bleed slowly and present with **iron deficiency anemia** and weight loss; **left-sided** tumors present with a change in bowel habit, narrowed stools, hematochezia or obstruction. Streptococcus gallolyticus (bovis) bacteremia or endocarditis should prompt colonoscopy. Staging uses TNM: stage I confined to the bowel wall and stage II through it, both without nodes; stage III with node metastases, stage IV distant (most often liver, via portal blood). Treatment is resection; stage III colon cancer receives adjuvant chemotherapy (for example FOLFOX or CAPOX); rectal cancer often receives neoadjuvant chemoradiation; MSI-high tumors respond to checkpoint inhibitors. **CEA** is used for monitoring recurrence, not screening.

@example
title: Positive FIT in a 47-year-old
problem: A healthy 47-year-old with no family history has a positive FIT. What is the next step?
step: Positive stool-based screening indicates that a precancerous or cancerous lesion may be present.
step: Order a colonoscopy; do not repeat the FIT, and do not substitute another stool test.
step: Colonoscopy allows removal of polyps and biopsy of masses.

@example
title: When should the child of a patient with colon cancer start screening?
problem: A 30-year-old woman's father was diagnosed with colon cancer at 52. When should she start colonoscopy?
step: One first-degree relative diagnosed before age 60 triggers early screening.
step: Candidate start ages are 40 and (52 - 10) = 42. Take the earlier one: 40.
step: Repeat colonoscopy about every 5 years. If the father had been diagnosed at 46, she would start at 36.

@mistakes
- Obtaining colonoscopy during acute diverticulitis, which risks perforation.
- Repeating FIT after a positive result instead of proceeding to colonoscopy.
- Using CEA to screen asymptomatic patients: it is for monitoring known disease.
- Forgetting that right-sided cancers present with anemia rather than obstruction.

choice*: Rupture of the vasa recta at the neck of a diverticulum
