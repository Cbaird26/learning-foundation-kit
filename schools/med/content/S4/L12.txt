All doses here are typical adult doses for study. Check a current reference before any clinical use.

### Reducing acid

- **Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs)** (omeprazole, esomeprazole, lansoprazole, pantoprazole, rabeprazole): prodrugs activated in the acid of the canaliculus that **irreversibly** bind the H+/K+ ATPase. Take **30 to 60 minutes before a meal**, because only active pumps are inhibited. Used for GERD, ulcers, bleeding, Zollinger-Ellison and H. pylori regimens. Adverse effects with long use: *C. difficile* and enteric infection, pneumonia, hypomagnesemia, vitamin B12 and iron deficiency, fracture risk, acute interstitial nephritis, rebound acid hypersecretion. Omeprazole and esomeprazole inhibit CYP2C19, reducing activation of clopidogrel. Potassium-competitive acid blockers (vonoprazan) are a newer class.
- **H2 blockers** (famotidine): block histamine at the parietal cell; less potent, tolerance develops. **Cimetidine** inhibits cytochrome P450 and has antiandrogen effects (gynecomastia). Famotidine requires renal dose adjustment.
- **Antacids:** calcium carbonate (constipation, hypercalcemia), aluminum hydroxide (constipation, hypophosphatemia), magnesium hydroxide (diarrhea); neutralize acid quickly and briefly.
- **Sucralfate:** polymerizes in acid to coat ulcer bases; needs an acidic pH and binds other drugs. **Misoprostol:** PGE1 analog that raises mucus and reduces acid; causes diarrhea and **is contraindicated in pregnancy** (uterine contractions). **Bismuth** coats ulcers and has anti-H. pylori activity.

### Motility and nausea

- **Metoclopramide:** D2 antagonist and 5-HT4 agonist, increasing gastric emptying (diabetic gastroparesis). Adverse: extrapyramidal effects, **tardive dyskinesia (boxed warning)**, restlessness, hyperprolactinemia. Contraindicated in bowel obstruction.
- **Erythromycin:** motilin agonist, used before endoscopy in bleeding and for gastroparesis (tachyphylaxis).
- **Ondansetron:** 5-HT3 antagonist for chemotherapy and postoperative nausea; adverse: **QT prolongation**, constipation, headache. Others: prochlorperazine and promethazine (dopamine antagonists, sedation), aprepitant (NK1 antagonist), scopolamine (motion sickness).

### Diarrhea and constipation

- **Loperamide:** peripheral mu-opioid agonist; it does not cross the blood-brain barrier at usual doses; high doses cause cardiac toxicity. Avoid in bloody diarrhea, high fever or C. difficile. **Diphenoxylate-atropine** crosses more.
- **Alosetron** (5-HT3 antagonist for severe IBS-D in women) can cause ischemic colitis and constipation.
- **Laxatives:** bulk (psyllium), osmotic (polyethylene glycol, lactulose, magnesium salts), stimulant (senna, bisacodyl), secretagogues (lubiprostone, a chloride channel activator; linaclotide, a guanylate cyclase C agonist), and peripherally acting mu-opioid antagonists (methylnaltrexone) for opioid-induced constipation.

### IBD, liver and biliary drugs

- **Mesalamine** (5-ASA): topical anti-inflammatory; adverse: interstitial nephritis. **Sulfasalazine** adds sulfapyridine toxicity (rash, hemolysis in G6PD deficiency, folate deficiency, reversible male infertility).
- **Azathioprine and 6-mercaptopurine:** antimetabolites; check **TPMT** activity before use (low activity risks myelosuppression); allopurinol raises levels.
- **Infliximab:** chimeric monoclonal antibody against TNF-alpha; typical induction 5 mg/kg IV at 0, 2 and 6 weeks; risks include TB reactivation, infection, hepatitis B reactivation and anti-drug antibodies. **Adalimumab** is a human anti-TNF. **Vedolizumab** blocks alpha-4 beta-7 integrin (gut selective). **Ustekinumab** blocks IL-12 and IL-23. **Tofacitinib** and **upadacitinib** are JAK inhibitors.
- **Lactulose** and **rifaximin** for hepatic encephalopathy; **octreotide** (somatostatin analog) for variceal bleeding (typical 50 microgram bolus then 50 micrograms per hour); **propranolol, nadolol and carvedilol** for varices.
- **Ursodiol** (hydrophilic bile acid) for primary biliary cholangitis and dissolving small cholesterol stones; **cholestyramine** (bile acid sequestrant) for pruritus and bile acid diarrhea (can raise triglycerides and bind other drugs); **N-acetylcysteine** replenishes glutathione in acetaminophen overdose (NAPQI toxicity).
- **Pancreatic enzyme replacement** with meals in pancreatic insufficiency.

### Choosing treatment for infectious diarrhea

Always rehydrate. Then match drug to syndrome: afebrile watery diarrhea needs fluids with or without loperamide; febrile or bloody (non-STEC) dysentery needs azithromycin; traveler's diarrhea of moderate or severe degree needs azithromycin; **C. difficile** needs fidaxomicin or oral vancomycin; **STEC** needs supportive care only; giardiasis needs tinidazole or metronidazole; amebiasis needs metronidazole then paromomycin; cholera needs rehydration plus single-dose doxycycline or azithromycin.

@example
title: Weight-based dosing
problem: A 70 kg patient with Crohn disease is to receive infliximab at 5 mg/kg. What is the dose?
step: Dose = weight times dose per kilogram.
step: 70 kg times 5 mg/kg = 350 mg.
step: Doses are rounded to vial size and given as an IV infusion over about 2 hours; screen for TB and hepatitis B first.

@example
title: Choosing therapy
problem: A 26-year-old woman returns from India with 6 watery stools per day, cramping and a temperature of 38.6 C, without blood. What is the treatment?
step: Moderate to severe traveler's diarrhea with fever: antibiotic therapy is indicated.
step: Choose azithromycin (typical adult dose 1 g once or 500 mg daily for 3 days); fluoroquinolone resistance is common in South Asia.
step: Provide oral rehydration; loperamide is acceptable only with antibiotics and without bloody stool.

@mistakes
- Taking a PPI at bedtime or with no relation to meals: it works best 30 to 60 minutes before eating.
- Giving metoclopramide to a patient with suspected bowel obstruction.
- Using misoprostol in women who could be pregnant.
- Forgetting TPMT testing before azathioprine, and TB or hepatitis B screening before anti-TNF drugs.

hint: Decide first whether antibiotics are indicated at all.
hint: For each case, state the fluid plan and whether loperamide is acceptable.
solution: (1) Oral rehydration with or without loperamide; no antibiotics, because it is probably viral or toxin-mediated and self-limited. (2) Supportive care with fluids and monitoring for HUS; no antibiotics and no loperamide, because STEC antibiotics raise HUS risk. (3) Fidaxomicin (or oral vancomycin) for C. difficile; stop clindamycin if possible, avoid antimotility drugs, use contact precautions. (4) Rehydration and azithromycin for invasive traveler's diarrhea; avoid loperamide because of fever and blood; check stool testing and adjust.
rubric: States that case 1 needs fluids without antibiotics.
rubric: Avoids antibiotics and antimotility drugs in STEC and explains the HUS risk.
rubric: Gives fidaxomicin or oral vancomycin for C. difficile.
rubric: Gives azithromycin for febrile traveler's dysentery and rehydrates in every case.
