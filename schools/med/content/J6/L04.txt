@article
A **chemical reaction** rearranges atoms. We write it as an **equation** with the **reactants** (what you start with) on the left, an arrow, and the **products** (what you end with) on the right:

$$\text{reactants} \longrightarrow \text{products}$$

### The law of conservation of mass

In a chemical reaction, atoms are not created or destroyed. They are only regrouped. So the total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products. This is the **law of conservation of mass**:

$$\text{mass of reactants} = \text{mass of products}$$

### Balancing equations

Because atoms are conserved, each element must appear the same number of times on both sides. We fix an equation by changing **coefficients** (the big numbers in front of formulas). We never change the small subscripts, because that would change the substance.

Hydrogen and oxygen make water. Starting with $\mathrm{H_2 + O_2 \to H_2O}$, the left has $2$ oxygen atoms and the right has $1$. Put a $2$ in front of water: $\mathrm{H_2 + O_2 \to 2H_2O}$. Now oxygen balances ($2$ and $2$) but hydrogen does not ($2$ and $4$). Put a $2$ in front of $\mathrm{H_2}$:

$$\mathrm{2H_2 + O_2 \to 2H_2O}$$

Count: $4$ H and $2$ O on each side. It is balanced.

### The baking soda and vinegar reaction

Baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) reacts with the acetic acid in vinegar and produces carbon dioxide gas, water and a salt. In an **open** cup the gas drifts away, so the scale shows a drop in mass. That does not break the law: the missing mass is in the gas that left. In a **sealed bag**, the gas stays inside and inflates the bag, so the total mass before and after is the same (within the scale's precision). Keep the bag, baking soda, vinegar and gas all on the scale to show conservation of mass.

Be careful of buoyancy: a swelling bag displaces more air and can make the reading change by a tiny amount, so give a tolerance such as $\pm 0.1\ \mathrm{g}$ when comparing readings.

@example
title: Finding an unknown mass
problem: In a sealed bag, $5.0\ \mathrm{g}$ of baking soda reacts with $50.0\ \mathrm{g}$ of vinegar. The bag itself has a mass of $3.0\ \mathrm{g}$. What total mass should the scale read after the reaction?
step: Add everything that starts in the system: $5.0 + 50.0 + 3.0 = 58.0\ \mathrm{g}$.
step: The bag is sealed, so nothing leaves. Conservation of mass says the mass after equals the mass before.
step: The scale should read $58.0\ \mathrm{g}$, within about $0.1\ \mathrm{g}$.

@mistakes
- Changing subscripts to balance an equation. Only coefficients may be changed.
- Saying that mass was destroyed when an open reaction loses mass. The gas product escaped.
- Forgetting to include the container in the total mass.
- Balancing only some of the elements, such as oxygen, and not checking the others.

answer: 4
check: 1 * 2 + 2 * 1
hint: Count oxygen in $\mathrm{CO_2}$ and in each $\mathrm{H_2O}$.
hint: $\mathrm{CO_2}$ has $2$ oxygen atoms, and $2\mathrm{H_2O}$ has $2$ more.
solution: $\mathrm{CO_2}$ has $2$ O and $2\mathrm{H_2O}$ has $2$ O, so there are $4$ oxygen atoms. This matches $2\mathrm{O_2}$ on the left.

choice: The baking soda and vinegar became lighter when they mixed.

@practice self
prompt: Describe how you would show that mass is conserved when baking soda and vinegar react. Include the materials, what you would measure and when, how you would handle the gas, and what result you expect.
hint: Decide what must stay inside the system, and measure the whole system before and after mixing.
solution: Put a small amount of vinegar in a zip-top bag (in a cup inside the bag) and baking soda in a corner or small container, keeping them apart, then seal the bag. Measure the mass of the whole sealed setup with a kitchen scale. Mix the two by tipping them together without opening the bag, then measure again. The gas inflates the bag but stays inside, so the two readings should match within the scale's precision (about 0.1 g). Repeat the test in an open container to show that mass seems to be lost when the gas escapes.
rubric: Keeps reactants separated until after the first mass reading.
rubric: Keeps the bag sealed and weighs the whole system before and after.
rubric: Predicts equal masses within the scale's precision and explains the role of the gas.
rubric: Uses only safe kitchen materials with an adult nearby (and the bag is not overfilled).

@quiz num
prompt: Balance $\mathrm{Fe + O_2 \to Fe_2O_3}$ using the smallest whole-number coefficients. What is the coefficient in front of $\mathrm{O_2}$?
answer: 3
check: 2 * 3 / 2
hint: $\mathrm{Fe_2O_3}$ has $3$ oxygen atoms. To avoid halves, use two of these.
hint: Two $\mathrm{Fe_2O_3}$ units contain $6$ oxygen atoms, which is $3$ molecules of $\mathrm{O_2}$.
solution: $\mathrm{4Fe + 3O_2 \to 2Fe_2O_3}$ has $4$ Fe and $6$ O on both sides, so the coefficient of $\mathrm{O_2}$ is $3$.
