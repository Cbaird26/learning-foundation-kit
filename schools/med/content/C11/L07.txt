@article
**Motivation** is the set of forces that start, direct and sustain behavior. **Personality** is the stable pattern of thoughts, feelings and behaviors that distinguishes individuals.

### Theories of motivation

- **Instinct theory**: behavior arises from innate programs; it explains fixed patterns but not flexible behavior.
- **Drive-reduction theory** (Hull): a physiological need creates a drive (such as hunger) and behavior reduces it, restoring **homeostasis**. It cannot explain behaviors that increase arousal.
- **Arousal theory**: people seek an optimal arousal level, consistent with the Yerkes-Dodson relationship.
- **Incentive theory**: external rewards pull behavior.
- **Maslow's hierarchy of needs**: physiological, safety, belonging, esteem and self-actualization, with lower needs usually pressing first. It is influential but its strict ordering has weak empirical support.
- **Self-determination theory**: people need **autonomy**, **competence** and **relatedness**. **Intrinsic motivation** (doing something for its own sake) can be undermined by excessive external rewards (the **overjustification effect**).

Hunger is regulated by the hypothalamus: the **lateral hypothalamus** promotes eating and the **ventromedial hypothalamus** signals satiety. **Ghrelin** (stomach) increases hunger, while **leptin** (adipose tissue) and **insulin** signal energy stores.

### Motivation and health behavior

Several models describe why people change health behaviors. The **Health Belief Model** emphasizes perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits, barriers and cues to action. **Self-efficacy** (Bandura), the belief in one's ability to carry out a behavior, strongly predicts change. The **theory of planned behavior** links attitudes, subjective norms and perceived control to intention and action. The **transtheoretical model** describes stages: **precontemplation**, **contemplation**, **preparation**, **action** and **maintenance** (relapse can occur at any point). Matching counseling to the stage, as in **motivational interviewing**, respects autonomy and avoids confrontation.

### Theories of personality

- **Psychoanalytic** (Freud): the **id** (pleasure principle), **ego** (reality principle) and **superego** (moral standards) conflict, and the ego uses **defense mechanisms** such as repression, denial, projection, displacement, reaction formation, rationalization and sublimation. Evidence for many specific claims is limited, but defense mechanisms remain a common clinical vocabulary.
- **Humanistic** (Rogers, Maslow): emphasis on the **self-concept**, growth and **unconditional positive regard**.
- **Trait**: stable dimensions. The **Big Five** (OCEAN) are **openness**, **conscientiousness**, **extraversion**, **agreeableness** and **neuroticism**. Conscientiousness predicts better health behavior and longevity.
- **Social-cognitive** (Bandura): **reciprocal determinism** among person, behavior and environment, with **self-efficacy** and **locus of control** (internal versus external) as key variables.
- **Biological**: temperament appears early, and twin studies estimate heritability.

**Assessment** includes self-report inventories such as the **MMPI**, and projective tests (Rorschach, Thematic Apperception Test), which have lower reliability and validity.

### Estimating heritability from twins

Monozygotic (MZ) twins share nearly all segregating genes and dizygotic (DZ) twins share about half. Falconer's approximation uses the twin correlations $r_{MZ}$ and $r_{DZ}$ of a trait:

$$h^2 \approx 2\,(r_{MZ} - r_{DZ}), \qquad c^2 \approx 2 r_{DZ} - r_{MZ}, \qquad e^2 \approx 1 - r_{MZ}$$

where $h^2$ is the share of variance attributable to genetic differences, $c^2$ to shared environment and $e^2$ to nonshared environment plus measurement error. **Heritability** describes variation in a population, not how much of an individual's trait is "caused by" genes, and it can change with the environment.

@example
title: Heritability from twin correlations
problem: In a twin study, the correlation of a personality trait is $r_{MZ} = 0.60$ for identical twins and $r_{DZ} = 0.35$ for fraternal twins. Estimate $h^2$, $c^2$ and $e^2$.
step: Heritability: $h^2 = 2(0.60 - 0.35) = 0.50$.
step: Shared environment: $c^2 = 2(0.35) - 0.60 = 0.10$.
step: Nonshared environment: $e^2 = 1 - 0.60 = 0.40$.
step: Check: $h^2 + c^2 + e^2 = 1.00$. About half of the variation in this population is attributable to genetic differences.

@example
title: Matching a stage of change
problem: A 52-year-old with hypertension says, "I know my blood pressure is high and I've been thinking I should walk after dinner, but I haven't started and I'm not ready to commit." Which stage of change is this, and what is an appropriate response?
step: He is aware of the problem and considering change but has not committed: this is contemplation.
step: He is ambivalent, so exploring pros and cons and his own reasons, as in motivational interviewing, fits better than giving instructions.
step: Eliciting a small, specific plan can build self-efficacy and move him toward preparation.

@mistakes
- Treating Maslow's levels as a rigid ladder that all people climb in order.
- Interpreting heritability as the percentage of an individual's trait caused by genes.
- Confusing intrinsic motivation with reinforcement from external rewards.
- Assuming a patient in precontemplation just needs more information; confrontation often backfires.

@practice self
prompt: A student says, "I used to love drawing, but since the program started paying me for each drawing I do it less and I enjoy it less." Name the effect and explain it using self-determination theory. Suggest how a clinician could use this idea in designing incentives for medication adherence.
solution: This is the overjustification effect: an external reward for an already intrinsically motivated activity shifts the perceived reason for acting from enjoyment to payment, reducing intrinsic motivation. In self-determination theory the reward feels controlling and reduces autonomy, so the need for autonomy is less satisfied. For adherence, incentives work best when they are small, support competence (such as feedback on progress) and do not replace the patient's own reasons; pairing them with shared goal setting and choice preserves autonomy.
rubric: Names the overjustification effect.
rubric: Links it to autonomy and perceived control.
rubric: Gives a reasonable clinical application that preserves autonomy or intrinsic motivation.
hint: Ask what changed about why the student draws.
hint: Think about which of the three basic needs a controlling reward threatens.
