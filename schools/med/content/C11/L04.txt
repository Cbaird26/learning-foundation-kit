@article
**Cognition** covers thinking, knowing, remembering, judging and problem solving.

### Cognitive development

Piaget proposed that children build **schemas** (mental frameworks). **Assimilation** fits new information into an existing schema; **accommodation** changes the schema to fit new information. His four stages are:

| Stage | Approximate age | Hallmarks |
|---|---|---|
| Sensorimotor | 0 to 2 years | Learning through senses and actions; **object permanence** develops |
| Preoperational | 2 to 7 years | Symbolic thought, language, **egocentrism**; lacks **conservation** |
| Concrete operational | 7 to 11 years | Conservation, logical thought about concrete objects |
| Formal operational | 12 years onward | Abstract and hypothetical reasoning |

Vygotsky emphasized the **zone of proximal development** and social scaffolding, and later researchers found that children's abilities appear earlier than Piaget thought.

### Problem solving, heuristics and biases

An **algorithm** guarantees a solution; a **heuristic** is a fast rule of thumb. Heuristics are efficient but produce predictable errors:

- **Availability**: judging likelihood by how easily examples come to mind.
- **Representativeness**: judging by resemblance to a prototype while ignoring base rates.
- **Anchoring**: relying too much on an initial value.
- **Confirmation bias**: seeking and favoring evidence that supports an existing belief.
- **Framing**: choices differ when the same outcome is described as a gain or a loss.
- **Functional fixedness** and **mental set**: inability to see new uses or to change a successful approach.

In medicine, **premature closure** (stopping the search after the first plausible diagnosis) combines anchoring and confirmation bias. Dual-process models describe fast intuitive (System 1) and slow analytic (System 2) thinking; deliberate checklists and differential diagnoses force System 2 reasoning.

### Intelligence

Spearman proposed a general factor **g**; Gardner proposed multiple intelligences; Sternberg proposed analytic, creative and practical intelligence. **Fluid intelligence** (novel reasoning) peaks earlier and declines with age; **crystallized intelligence** (accumulated knowledge) holds up. The original **ratio IQ** is

$$\text{IQ} = \frac{\text{mental age}}{\text{chronological age}} \times 100.$$

Modern tests (Wechsler, Stanford-Binet) use **deviation IQ**, with mean 100 and standard deviation 15, so a **z-score** is $z = (\text{IQ} - 100)/15$. Tests must show **reliability** (consistency) and **validity** (measuring what they claim) and be **standardized** on a representative sample. Both genes and environment contribute to individual differences, and average scores have risen over time (the Flynn effect), which points to environmental influences such as schooling and nutrition.

### Language

Language has **phonemes** (sound units), **morphemes** (smallest meaning units), **syntax** (word order rules), **semantics** (meaning) and **pragmatics** (use in context). Children babble at about 6 months, say a first word at about 12 months, and combine words at about 2 years. Skinner explained language by reinforcement, Chomsky proposed an innate **language acquisition device**, and current views combine both. The **critical period** hypothesis holds that language is learned most readily in early life. The **Sapir-Whorf** (linguistic relativity) hypothesis, in its weak form, says language influences thought. **Broca's area** (left inferior frontal) is linked to production, and **Wernicke's area** (left superior temporal) to comprehension.

### Applying cognition to studying

Memory research from the last lesson gives a concrete plan. **Spaced retrieval** means testing yourself on material at expanding intervals, for example 1 day, 3 days, 7 days and 14 days after first learning it. Each successful retrieval increases memory strength $S$, so the next gap can be longer.

@example
title: Ratio IQ
problem: A child with a chronological age of 8 years performs like the average 10-year-old (mental age 10). What is the ratio IQ?
step: Write the formula: $\text{IQ} = (\text{MA}/\text{CA}) \times 100$.
step: Substitute: $\text{IQ} = (10/8) \times 100$.
step: Evaluate: $\text{IQ} = 125$.

@example
title: Spotting a heuristic in a vignette
problem: After caring for three patients with pulmonary embolism this week, a resident orders CT angiography for a 24-year-old with mild pleuritic chest pain and a low pretest probability. Which heuristic is most likely, and what is the corrective step?
step: Recent, vivid cases come easily to mind, so the resident overestimates the probability of pulmonary embolism: the availability heuristic.
step: The corrective step is to estimate the pretest probability from base rates and a validated tool such as the Wells score before ordering imaging.
step: Using explicit criteria shifts reasoning from System 1 to System 2.

@mistakes
- Confusing assimilation (fitting new data into an old schema) with accommodation (changing the schema).
- Using ratio IQ for adults; mental age does not rise indefinitely, so deviation IQ is used.
- Assuming heuristics are always wrong; they are usually efficient but biased in predictable situations.
- Blaming a single cause for intelligence differences; genes and environment interact.

@practice self
prompt: You have an anatomy exam in 28 days and 4 topic lists to learn. Design a spaced-retrieval study plan: state the retrieval schedule (day gaps), what each session consists of, and how you will use mistakes. Explain, using memory principles, why this beats rereading the night before.
solution: Plan: Learn each list on days 1 to 4. Test yourself from memory (flashcards or blank-page recall) the next day, then at gaps of about 3, 7 and 14 days, finishing with a cumulative test around day 24 and light review the day before. Each session starts with free recall, then you check answers and mark errors; missed items return in the next session at a shorter gap, and items answered correctly move to a longer gap. Why it works: retrieval practice (testing effect) strengthens traces more than rereading, spacing (spacing effect) lets some forgetting occur so that each retrieval is effortful and increases memory strength $S$, and varied contexts support retrieval cues. Cramming gives short-lived recall that decays quickly.
rubric: Gives a concrete schedule with increasing gaps before the exam.
rubric: Each session uses active recall with feedback and handling of errors.
rubric: Names the testing effect and spacing effect and explains them.
rubric: Contrasts with rereading or massed study.
hint: Count back from day 28 and choose gaps that grow.
hint: Decide what you do at each session, and what happens to items you missed.
