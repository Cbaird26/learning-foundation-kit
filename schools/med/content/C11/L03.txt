@article
Memory is the set of processes that **encode**, **store** and **retrieve** information. The information-processing model (Atkinson and Shiffrin) describes three stores, and later work refined it.

### Stores and capacity

**Sensory memory** holds a brief, high-capacity trace: **iconic** (visual, about a second) and **echoic** (auditory, a few seconds). Attention moves selected information into **working memory**, which holds and manipulates information for roughly 15 to 30 seconds without rehearsal. Classic estimates give a capacity of about seven items ($7 \pm 2$); later work suggests about four **chunks**. A **chunk** is a meaningful unit, so grouping digits into familiar numbers expands what you can hold. Baddeley's model of working memory has a **central executive** that directs attention, a **phonological loop** (verbal rehearsal), a **visuospatial sketchpad** and an **episodic buffer** that binds information from different sources.

**Long-term memory** has no known capacity limit. It divides into:

- **Explicit (declarative)** memory, consciously recalled: **episodic** (personal events) and **semantic** (facts and concepts). The hippocampus and medial temporal lobe are critical for forming new explicit memories; they are eventually stored widely in the cortex.
- **Implicit (nondeclarative)** memory, expressed through performance: **procedural** (skills, relying on the basal ganglia and cerebellum), **priming**, and conditioned responses (the amygdala for conditioned emotional responses, the cerebellum for motor ones).

The amygdala also strengthens memories of emotionally arousing events.

### Encoding and retrieval

Craik and Lockhart's **levels of processing** shows that deeper, meaning-based (semantic) encoding produces better memory than shallow, structural or phonemic encoding. **Elaborative rehearsal** and **mnemonics** (method of loci, acronyms) exploit this. Retrieval is easier when cues match encoding: the **encoding specificity principle**, **context-dependent** and **state-dependent** memory. The **serial position effect** shows better recall for early items (**primacy**, more rehearsal and transfer to long-term memory) and late items (**recency**, still in working memory).

### Consolidation and neural basis

**Consolidation** stabilizes new memories, in part during sleep. At the cellular level, **long-term potentiation** (LTP), a lasting strengthening of synapses after repeated stimulation involving NMDA receptors, is the leading model of learning.

### Forgetting and errors

Ebbinghaus's **forgetting curve** shows steep early loss that levels off; a common model is exponential, $R = e^{-t/S}$, where $R$ is retention, $t$ is time since learning and $S$ is memory strength. Rehearsal and retrieval practice increase $S$. Causes of forgetting include failure to encode, decay, **interference** (**proactive**: old information disrupts new; **retroactive**: new disrupts old) and retrieval failure (tip-of-the-tongue). Memory is reconstructive: the **misinformation effect** (Loftus) shows that post-event suggestions can alter recall, and **source monitoring** errors confuse where a memory came from.

### Amnesia and disease

**Anterograde amnesia** is the inability to form new explicit memories, classically after bilateral hippocampal damage, with procedural learning spared. **Retrograde amnesia** is loss of memories from before the injury. **Korsakoff syndrome** (thiamine deficiency, often with alcohol use disorder) causes anterograde amnesia and confabulation. **Alzheimer disease** begins with episodic memory loss and medial temporal degeneration.

### Retrieval as a learning tool

Testing yourself is more effective than rereading (the **testing effect**), and spacing practice over time beats massing it (the **spacing effect**). You will use both in the next lesson's study plan.

@example
title: Using chunking to estimate span
problem: A student can hold about 4 chunks in working memory. She is given the digits 8 0 0 5 5 5 1 2 3 4. If she groups them as 800, 555 and 1234, how many chunks is that, and could she in principle hold more digits if each chunk were 4 digits?
step: The grouped string is $800\,|\,555\,|\,1234$, which is $3$ chunks.
step: With a limit of $4$ chunks she can hold the whole string with room for one more chunk.
step: If each chunk were $4$ digits, she could hold $4 \times 4 = 16$ digits.
step: The limit is on chunks, not digits, which is why meaningful grouping helps.

@example
title: Retention from an exponential forgetting model
problem: Suppose retention follows $R = e^{-t/S}$ with strength $S = 5$ days. What fraction is retained after $t = 10$ days without review?
step: Substitute the values: $R = e^{-10/5} = e^{-2}$.
step: Evaluate: $e^{-2} \approx 0.135$.
step: About $13.5\%$ remains. Retrieval practice would raise $S$ and slow this decline.

@mistakes
- Equating working memory with a passive store; it actively manipulates information.
- Calling procedural memory "explicit" because the person can describe the skill.
- Assuming that rereading creates strong memory; retrieval practice is far more effective.
- Treating confident memories as accurate; confidence and accuracy are only loosely related.

@practice self
prompt: A 62-year-old man has bilateral hippocampal damage after an anoxic injury. He converses normally and remembers his childhood, but cannot recall meeting his physician ten minutes ago. solution: He has anterograde amnesia: he cannot form new explicit (episodic) memories because the hippocampus is needed for encoding and consolidation. His childhood memories were consolidated earlier and are stored in the cortex, so retrograde memory is largely spared. His working memory and conversation are intact.
