@project
title: Social determinants analysis of two neighborhoods
overview: You will compare the health outcomes of two invented neighborhoods, Eastbank and Westfield, using the data supplied below. You will compute disparity measures, propose mechanisms drawn from psychology and sociology, and state clearly what the data cannot show. The result is a short analytic report of about 800 to 1200 words with a table.
goal: Compute and interpret relative risk, risk difference, excess cases and life expectancy gaps from supplied data.
goal: Link at least three social determinants to specific health outcomes through named material, psychosocial, behavioral or access pathways.
goal: Evaluate the limits of the evidence, including confounding, reverse causation and the ecological fallacy.
goal: Communicate findings neutrally, in respectful person-first language, without assigning blame to residents.
step: Read the supplied data below (all values are invented for this exercise). Eastbank: 24,000 adults; median household income \$38,000; 14% of adults with a bachelor's degree; 19% uninsured; 24% of households below the poverty line; 82% of homes built before 1978; 71% of residents more than one mile from a full-service grocery store; 8% tree canopy; life expectancy 74.2 years; adult diabetes prevalence 13.5%; hypertension prevalence 38%; adult smoking 22%; childhood asthma emergency visits 21 per 1000 children; infant mortality 8.4 per 1000 live births. Westfield: 30,000 adults; median household income \$96,000; 52% with a bachelor's degree; 4% uninsured; 5% of households below the poverty line; 35% of homes built before 1978; 12% of residents more than one mile from a full-service grocery store; 31% tree canopy; life expectancy 81.6 years; adult diabetes prevalence 6.5%; hypertension prevalence 27%; adult smoking 9%; childhood asthma emergency visits 7 per 1000 children; infant mortality 3.6 per 1000 live births.
step: Build a table with both neighborhoods side by side and compute, for each health outcome, the relative risk (Eastbank over Westfield) and the risk difference. Compute the life expectancy gap in years.
step: Compute the number of excess adult diabetes cases and excess adult smokers in Eastbank relative to what would be expected at Westfield's rates (use Eastbank's 24,000 adults).
step: Choose three determinants from the data (for example housing age, grocery access, insurance or education). For each, write the pathway to a health outcome in three sentences, naming whether it is material, psychosocial, behavioral or access related, and citing the supplied numbers.
step: Add one paragraph on psychological mechanisms: how chronic stress (allostatic load), health behavior models (such as self-efficacy and the Health Belief Model) and attribution errors by clinicians could help sustain the disparities.
step: Write a limitations section covering confounding, reverse causation, the ecological fallacy, the lack of individual data, and the fact that the data are cross-sectional and invented.
step: Finish with two proposals for further data or study designs that would test your proposed mechanisms, and proofread for neutral, person-first language.
deliverable: A one-page table of measures, relative risks, risk differences, and the life expectancy gap, with the arithmetic shown.
deliverable: A written report of about 800 to 1200 words covering three determinants, mechanisms and limitations.
deliverable: A two-minute spoken explanation of one major idea, given without notes.
rubric: All calculations are correct and clearly labeled with units.
rubric: At least three determinants are linked to outcomes by plausible, named pathways, citing the supplied data.
rubric: Limitations are specific (confounding, reverse causation, ecological fallacy) and not generic.
rubric: Psychological and sociological concepts are used accurately and by standard names.
rubric: Tone is neutral and respectful, with no blame of residents and no claim of proven causation.

### Week by week
- **Weeks 1 to 2.** Sensation turns energy into neural signals (transduction); perception organizes them. Weber's law: $\Delta I / I = k$. Signal detection separates sensitivity from criterion using hits, misses, false alarms and correct rejections. Classical conditioning pairs a CS with a UCS; operant conditioning uses reinforcement and punishment, and the four schedules (variable ratio is most resistant to extinction).
- **Weeks 3 to 4.** Sensory, working and long-term memory; explicit (episodic, semantic) versus implicit; forgetting $R = e^{-t/S}$; spacing and testing effects. Piaget's stages, heuristics and biases, ratio and deviation IQ, language areas.
- **Weeks 5 to 6.** EEG and sleep stages, 90-minute cycles, sleep disorders, drug classes and mechanisms. Emotion theories; the SAM and HPA axes (CRH, ACTH, cortisol); general adaptation syndrome; allostatic load.
- **Weeks 7 to 8.** Motivation theories, self-efficacy, the transtheoretical model, personality theories and the Big Five, Falconer heritability. Major psychological disorders, treatments, and predictive values of screening tests.
- **Weeks 9 to 10.** Attribution errors, dissonance, Asch and Milgram, group processes, bystander effect, prejudice. Erikson, Kohlberg, attachment, socialization, identity concepts, and conformity and attribution errors in clinical teams.
- **Weeks 11 to 12.** Sociological perspectives, stratification, demographic rates (CBR, CDR, IMR, dependency ratio, rule of 70), social determinants, the social gradient, fundamental cause theory, relative risk and excess cases.

### Common errors
- Confusing negative reinforcement with punishment, and the UCR with the CR.
- Using the false-alarm rate denominator incorrectly.
- Reading heritability as an individual's genetic share.
- Treating screening sensitivity as predictive value.
- Describing disparities as proof of one cause, or blaming individuals for constraints.

@exam num
prompt: A neighborhood clinic screens for depression using a questionnaire with sensitivity $0.88$ and specificity $0.88$. In its patient population, $14\%$ have depression. What is the probability that a patient with a positive screen has depression? Round to two decimal places.
answer: 0.54
check: (0.88 * 0.14) / (0.88 * 0.14 + (1 - 0.88) * (1 - 0.14))
tol: 0.01
hint: Imagine $1000$ patients and count true and false positives.
hint: $140$ have depression, so true positives are $0.88 \times 140$; false positives come from the other $860$.
solution: True positives $= 123.2$; false positives $= 0.12 \times 860 = 103.2$. PPV $= 123.2/226.4 \approx 0.54$.

@exam self
prompt: A 6-year-old from a neighborhood with older housing and high stress is seen repeatedly for asthma. His mother misses two appointments, and a trainee writes "mother noncompliant, does not care." (a) Name the attribution error and propose a better approach. (b) Describe two pathways, one material and one psychosocial, linking the neighborhood to his asthma. (c) Explain how a team could keep a trainee from simply conforming to a senior clinician's label.
solution: (a) The trainee commits the fundamental attribution error by explaining missed visits through disposition. A better approach is to ask about barriers such as work hours, transportation and cost, and to plan around them. (b) Material: older housing with mold, pests or poor ventilation raises allergen and irritant exposure, and traffic or industrial air pollution irritates airways. Psychosocial: chronic stress for the family activates the HPA axis and sympathetic system, and allostatic load may worsen airway inflammation and affect parental coping. (c) Normative and informational pressure can make the trainee adopt the label; the team can invite dissent, use structured case reviews that list situational factors, and make it routine for any member to raise concerns.
rubric: Names the fundamental attribution error and proposes inquiry about situational barriers.
rubric: Gives one correct material and one correct psychosocial pathway.
rubric: Describes a team practice that reduces conformity to an unexamined label.
hint: Separate the three parts and answer each in a few sentences.
hint: For part (b), think of what in the home exposes the airways and what chronic stress does to the body.
