@article
An **emotion** has three components: a physiological response (autonomic arousal), a cognitive appraisal and a behavioral expression. Emotion theories differ in what comes first.

### Theories of emotion

- **James-Lange**: the body responds first and the perceived arousal pattern is the emotion ("I am afraid because I tremble").
- **Cannon-Bard**: the thalamus sends signals simultaneously to the cortex (experience) and the body (arousal).
- **Schachter-Singer two-factor**: arousal plus a cognitive label produces the emotion; the same arousal can become fear or excitement depending on context.
- **Lazarus appraisal theory**: appraisal of the situation precedes and shapes emotion. **Zajonc** argued that some emotional reactions occur before conscious thought.

### Neural circuits

The **amygdala** evaluates threat and drives fear responses, including conditioned fear; the **prefrontal cortex** regulates emotion (ventromedial prefrontal damage impairs emotional decision making); the **insula** supports awareness of bodily states and disgust; the **hypothalamus** coordinates autonomic and endocrine output. Studies of facial expressions (Ekman) identified expressions of basic emotions recognized across cultures, while **display rules** govern when expressing them is acceptable.

### Stress and the stress response

**Stress** is the response to demands appraised as taxing resources. A **stressor** can be physical, psychological, acute or chronic. Lazarus and Folkman's model includes **primary appraisal** (is this a threat?) and **secondary appraisal** (can I cope?). Selye's **general adaptation syndrome** has three stages: **alarm** (mobilization), **resistance** (adaptation with elevated hormones) and **exhaustion** (depleted reserves and illness).

Two systems mediate the response:

1. **Sympathetic-adrenal-medullary (SAM) axis (fast)**: hypothalamus activates the sympathetic nervous system and the adrenal medulla releases epinephrine and norepinephrine, increasing heart rate, blood pressure, glucose and airway diameter ("fight or flight").
2. **Hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis (slower)**: the hypothalamus releases **CRH**, which stimulates the anterior pituitary to release **ACTH**, which stimulates the adrenal cortex to release **cortisol**. Cortisol raises glucose, mobilizes energy, suppresses inflammation and immune function, and feeds back to inhibit CRH and ACTH.

### Health effects

Acute stress is adaptive. Chronic activation causes **allostatic load**, the cumulative wear from repeated or prolonged activation. Consequences include hypertension and cardiovascular disease, hyperglycemia and insulin resistance, visceral fat deposition, impaired immune function and slower wound healing, gastrointestinal symptoms, sleep disruption, and increased risk of depression and anxiety. Prolonged cortisol exposure can reduce hippocampal volume and impair memory. Behaviors such as smoking, alcohol use and poor diet add indirect effects. The **Yerkes-Dodson law** describes an inverted-U relationship between arousal and performance: moderate arousal is best for most tasks, and the peak is lower for complex tasks.

### Coping and moderators

**Problem-focused coping** acts on the stressor; **emotion-focused coping** manages the emotional response. Social support, a sense of control, exercise, mindfulness and adequate sleep buffer stress. Hostility is more strongly linked to coronary disease than a general Type A pattern. Life-event scales (the Holmes and Rahe Social Readjustment Rating Scale) add up life-change units, but the appraisal of an event matters as much as its count.

@example
title: Following the HPA axis
problem: Give the sequence of hormones in the HPA axis and the feedback step, and predict what happens to ACTH if a patient takes high-dose prednisone (a glucocorticoid) for months.
step: Hypothalamus secretes CRH, which stimulates the anterior pituitary.
step: The anterior pituitary secretes ACTH, which stimulates the adrenal cortex to secrete cortisol.
step: Cortisol acts by negative feedback on the hypothalamus and pituitary.
step: Exogenous glucocorticoid acts like cortisol, so CRH and ACTH are suppressed and the adrenal cortex atrophies; stopping the drug abruptly can cause adrenal insufficiency.

@example
title: Adding stress points on a life-event scale
problem: A study uses a simplified scale on which divorce is 73 points, change in work responsibilities is 29 and moving is 20. A patient has experienced all three in one year. What is the total score, and what is the main limitation of using it to predict illness?
step: Sum the points: $73 + 29 + 20 = 122$.
step: The score counts events but not how the person appraises them or what resources the person has.
step: So similar scores can correspond to very different stress and health outcomes.

@mistakes
- Saying cortisol is released by the adrenal medulla; cortisol comes from the adrenal cortex and epinephrine from the medulla.
- Assuming that stress is always harmful; moderate arousal can improve performance.
- Confusing James-Lange (body first) with Cannon-Bard (simultaneous).
- Treating the number of life events as a precise predictor of illness.

solution: Within seconds, the amygdala and hypothalamus activate the sympathetic nervous system, and the adrenal medulla releases epinephrine and norepinephrine (SAM axis), raising heart rate, blood pressure, glucose and breathing.
