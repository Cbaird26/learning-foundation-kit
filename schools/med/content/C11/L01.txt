@article
**Sensation** is the detection of physical energy by sensory receptors and its conversion into neural signals. **Perception** is the brain's organization and interpretation of those signals into meaningful experience. The conversion step is called **transduction**: photoreceptors turn light into a change in membrane potential, hair cells turn vibration into a receptor potential, and mechanoreceptors in skin turn deformation into action potentials.

### Thresholds and Weber's law

The **absolute threshold** is the minimum stimulus intensity detected 50% of the time. The **difference threshold**, or **just-noticeable difference** (JND), is the smallest change in a stimulus that is detected 50% of the time. **Weber's law** says the JND is a constant fraction of the starting intensity:

$$\frac{\Delta I}{I} = k$$

where $I$ is the standard intensity, $\Delta I$ is the JND and $k$ is the Weber fraction for that sense. If you notice the difference between 50 g and 52 g, then $k = 2/50 = 0.04$, and at a standard of 200 g you need a change of about 8 g.

### Signal detection theory

A radiologist looking for a faint nodule does not simply "have a threshold". Detection depends on **sensitivity** (how well signal is separated from noise) and on the **response criterion** (how much evidence the observer demands before saying "yes"). On every trial the signal is present or absent and the observer says yes or no, giving four outcomes:

| | Says "yes" | Says "no" |
|---|---|---|
| Signal present | Hit | Miss |
| Signal absent | False alarm | Correct rejection |

The **hit rate** is hits divided by all signal-present trials; the **false-alarm rate** is false alarms divided by all signal-absent trials. A lenient criterion raises both hits and false alarms; a strict criterion lowers both. Changing the criterion moves you along the same curve, whereas better sensitivity (often called $d'$) raises hits without raising false alarms. Motivation, expected prevalence and the cost of errors all shift the criterion, which is why screening programs report both sensitivity and specificity.

### Senses in brief

- **Vision.** Light is focused on the retina. Rods (dim light, peripheral, no color) and cones (bright light, fovea, color) transduce it. Color vision uses three cone types (trichromatic theory), and downstream cells compare signals in opposing pairs (opponent-process theory), which explains afterimages. Signals travel through the optic nerve and optic chiasm to the lateral geniculate nucleus and primary visual cortex in the occipital lobe.
- **Hearing.** Sound waves vibrate the tympanic membrane and the ossicles, then the fluid of the cochlea. Hair cells on the basilar membrane transduce the motion; frequency is coded by place along the membrane and by firing rate.
- **Somatosensation.** Touch, temperature, pain and proprioception travel via the dorsal column and spinothalamic pathways to the somatosensory cortex.
- **Chemical senses.** Olfaction bypasses the thalamus on its way to the cortex; taste has five basic qualities (sweet, sour, salty, bitter, umami).

### Organizing perception

**Bottom-up processing** builds a percept from the features of the stimulus; **top-down processing** uses expectations, context and knowledge. The **Gestalt principles** (proximity, similarity, closure, continuity, figure-ground) describe how we group features into wholes. **Depth perception** uses binocular cues such as retinal disparity and monocular cues such as linear perspective, interposition and texture gradient. **Sensory adaptation** is the reduced response to an unchanging stimulus, which is why you stop noticing a smell.

@example
title: Weber's law with a changed standard
problem: A person can just detect the difference between a 40 g standard weight and a 42 g weight. Using Weber's law, how large a change is needed to be just noticeable when the standard is 300 g?
step: Compute the Weber fraction from the first measurement: $k = \Delta I / I = 2/40 = 0.05$.
step: Weber's law says the same fraction applies at the new standard: $\Delta I = k I$.
step: Substitute: $\Delta I = 0.05 \times 300 = 15$ g.
step: Interpret: heavier standards require proportionally larger changes, so the JND grows with intensity.

@example
title: Hits and false alarms in a detection task
problem: In 50 trials with a faint tone present, a listener says "yes" on 40. In 50 trials with no tone, the listener says "yes" on 5. Find the hit rate and the false-alarm rate, and decide whether the criterion looks strict or lenient.
step: Hits are the "yes" responses on signal-present trials: $40$. Hit rate $= 40/50 = 0.80$.
step: False alarms are "yes" responses on signal-absent trials: $5$. False-alarm rate $= 5/50 = 0.10$.
step: Hit rate is high and false alarms are low, which indicates good sensitivity.
step: Because the false-alarm rate (10%) is lower than the miss rate (20%), the listener errs toward "no", so the criterion is slightly strict rather than lenient.

@mistakes
- Treating the absolute threshold as a fixed wall; it is a probabilistic point (50% detection) that shifts with attention and motivation.
- Computing the false-alarm rate with all trials in the denominator instead of only signal-absent trials.
- Confusing a change in criterion with a change in sensitivity: a more willing observer gains hits only by also gaining false alarms.
- Mixing up sensation (receptor and transduction) with perception (interpretation).

@practice self
prompt: A radiologist is told that a missed tumor is far more costly than an unnecessary follow-up scan. Using signal detection theory, explain how this knowledge should shift the response criterion and what it does to hits, misses, false alarms and correct rejections. Then state whether the shift changes sensitivity.
solution: The radiologist should adopt a more lenient criterion, saying "abnormal" on weaker evidence. Hits increase and misses decrease, but false alarms increase and correct rejections decrease. Sensitivity ($d'$) is unchanged because the radiologist is moving along the same curve, not separating signal from noise better. The extra false alarms are the accepted price of avoiding costly misses, which is why screening tests with high sensitivity are followed by confirmatory testing.
