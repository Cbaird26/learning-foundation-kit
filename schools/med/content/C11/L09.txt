@article
**Social psychology** studies how thoughts, feelings and behavior are shaped by other people and by the situation.

### Social cognition and attribution

**Attributions** are explanations for behavior. A **dispositional** (internal) attribution points to traits; a **situational** (external) attribution points to circumstances. Systematic errors include:

- **Fundamental attribution error**: overweighting disposition and underweighting situation when explaining other people's behavior.
- **Actor-observer bias**: attributing our own behavior to the situation but others' to disposition.
- **Self-serving bias**: crediting success to ourselves and failure to circumstances.
- **Just-world hypothesis**: the belief that people get what they deserve, leading to victim blaming.

A **self-fulfilling prophecy** arises when an expectation causes behavior that makes it true (the Pygmalion effect). **Schemas** and **stereotypes** speed processing but can bias it.

### Attitudes and persuasion

Attitudes have cognitive, affective and behavioral components. **Cognitive dissonance** (Festinger) is the discomfort of holding conflicting cognitions, reduced by changing the attitude, the behavior or the interpretation; in the classic experiment, people paid little to tell a lie shifted their own attitude more than those paid a lot. The **elaboration likelihood model** distinguishes the **central route** (careful argument evaluation, durable change) from the **peripheral route** (cues such as source attractiveness). Compliance techniques include the **foot-in-the-door** (small request first) and **door-in-the-face** (large request first, then a smaller one).

### Conformity and obedience

**Conformity** is adjusting behavior to a group. In Asch's line-judgment studies, about one third of responses on critical trials conformed to a unanimous incorrect majority and about three quarters of participants conformed at least once. Conformity falls sharply when even one other person dissents, and rises with group unanimity (and size, up to about 3 to 5 confederates). **Normative influence** (wanting to be accepted) and **informational influence** (assuming the group knows better, as in Sherif's autokinetic effect) are the two mechanisms. **Obedience** to authority was studied by Milgram: in the baseline study about 65% of participants delivered the maximum shock when told to continue by an experimenter; obedience fell when the authority was absent, was less legitimate or when peers refused. These studies established situational power and shaped modern research ethics.

### Group processes

- **Social facilitation**: the presence of others improves performance on simple or well-learned tasks and impairs performance on difficult ones.
- **Social loafing**: individuals exert less effort in groups when contributions are not identifiable.
- **Deindividuation**: loss of self-awareness in a crowd or when anonymous.
- **Groupthink**: a cohesive group suppresses dissent, promoting poor decisions; remedies include a devil's advocate and independent subgroups.
- **Group polarization**: discussion strengthens the group's initial leaning.

### Helping, aggression and prejudice

The **bystander effect** (Darley and Latané) is that the presence of others reduces each person's chance of helping, mainly through **diffusion of responsibility** and pluralistic ignorance. Altruism, reciprocity, empathy and social norms promote helping. Aggression is explained by biological factors, frustration and learned models. **Prejudice** is an attitude, a **stereotype** is a belief about a group, and **discrimination** is behavior. In-group favoritism appears even in arbitrary groups (Tajfel's minimal-group studies). **Implicit bias** measures show automatic associations, though how well they predict individual behavior is debated. Prejudice can be reduced by equal-status, cooperative contact toward shared goals (the **contact hypothesis**). **Stereotype threat** can lower performance when a person fears confirming a negative stereotype.

### A bystander model

If each of $n$ bystanders independently helps with probability $p$, the chance that nobody helps is $(1-p)^n$. Real bystanders are not independent, and individual helping falls as $n$ grows, which makes the problem worse.

@example
title: Probability that no one helps
problem: In a model, each of 3 bystanders independently helps with probability $0.3$. What is the probability that nobody helps?
step: The chance that one person does not help is $1 - 0.3 = 0.7$.
step: For three independent people multiply: $0.7^3$.
step: $0.7^3 = 0.343$, so about a one in three chance that no one helps.
step: In real emergencies individual helping drops as group size grows (diffusion of responsibility), so this model understates the problem.

@example
title: Identifying an attribution error
problem: A resident notes that a patient arrived 40 minutes late and writes, "unreliable, doesn't care about her health." The patient had taken two buses after her shift was extended. Name the error and a better practice.
step: The resident attributes lateness to disposition (traits) without considering the situation: the fundamental attribution error.
step: The resident would probably explain his own lateness by traffic: an actor-observer difference.
step: A better practice is to ask about barriers (transportation, work, caregiving) before inferring motivation.
step: This improves the accuracy of the history and the therapeutic relationship.

@mistakes
- Saying that Milgram's participants were "sadistic"; most showed distress, and situational features strongly shaped obedience.
- Treating prejudice (attitude), stereotype (belief) and discrimination (behavior) as synonyms.
- Using the fundamental attribution error to describe how we explain our own behavior.
- Assuming that conformity is always irrational; informational influence is often reasonable.

@practice self
prompt: Explain the difference between normative and informational social influence, and illustrate each with an example from a medical team's rounds.
solution: Normative influence is conforming to be accepted or avoid disapproval, even if you privately disagree. For example, a student sees an error in a dosing plan but says nothing because the whole team and the attending seem to agree. Informational influence is conforming because you assume others have better information, particularly when the situation is ambiguous. For example, a new intern is unsure how to interpret an unusual electrocardiogram and adopts the interpretation of the group. Remedies include inviting dissent explicitly and a culture in which any member can stop the line.
rubric: Defines normative influence as motivated by acceptance.
rubric: Defines informational influence as motivated by accuracy under uncertainty.
rubric: Gives a distinct and plausible team example of each.
hint: Ask in each case whether the person privately agrees.
hint: Think of speaking up in front of a senior team.
