@article
**Social determinants of health** are the conditions in which people are born, grow, work, live and age, and the wider systems that shape them. A **health disparity** is a difference in health outcomes between groups. A **health inequity** is a disparity that is judged avoidable and unfair, arising from differences in social conditions. Health care matters, but estimates suggest that medical care explains a minority of the variation in population health compared with social and behavioral factors.

### Domains

The Healthy People 2030 framework groups determinants into five domains: **economic stability** (income, employment, housing and food security), **education access and quality** (including health literacy), **health care access and quality** (insurance, regional supply of clinicians), **neighborhood and built environment** (housing quality, air and water, safe places to walk, grocery access) and **social and community context** (social support, discrimination, incarceration, civic participation).

### The social gradient

Health improves step by step with socioeconomic position, not only at the poverty line. The Whitehall studies of British civil servants found that mortality was higher in lower employment grades even among people who all had stable jobs and health coverage, which suggests that status, control over work and stress contribute. Link and Phelan's **fundamental cause theory** argues that SES persistently affects health because it provides flexible resources (money, knowledge, power, prestige, social connection) that people use to avoid risks and to benefit from new treatments, so the link persists even when specific diseases and mechanisms change.

### Pathways

- **Material pathways**: crowded or poor-quality housing, lead exposure, air pollution, unsafe workplaces, unaffordable food or medication.
- **Psychosocial pathways**: chronic stress activates the HPA axis and sympathetic system, producing allostatic load; low control and social isolation increase risk.
- **Behavioral pathways**: smoking, diet and physical activity are shaped by price, marketing, safety and culture.
- **Access pathways**: coverage, transportation, language, health literacy and trust in the system.

### Race, racism and gender

Race is a social category, and differences in outcomes across racial and ethnic groups in the United States (for example in maternal mortality, infant mortality and life expectancy) are documented by public health agencies. Researchers study **structural racism** (laws, policies and institutional practices that distribute resources unequally, such as historical residential segregation and lending practices), **interpersonal discrimination** and its effects through stress (the **weathering hypothesis**), and differences in neighborhood conditions and care. Disparities remain after adjustment for income and insurance in many studies, though the size depends on the outcome and data. Gender also matters: women live longer on average but report more illness, while men have higher mortality from injury and many chronic diseases, and gender norms shape help-seeking and caregiving burden. **Intersectionality** reminds us that identities combine. Immigration status, disability and sexual and gender minority status are further factors in access to care. Policy responses are debated; the descriptive evidence is the subject here.

### Comparing populations

Suppose a condition occurs with risk $R_1$ in an exposed or disadvantaged group and $R_0$ in a reference group. Then:

$$\text{RR} = \frac{R_1}{R_0}, \qquad \text{risk difference} = R_1 - R_0, \qquad \text{excess cases} = (R_1 - R_0)\times N_1$$

where $N_1$ is the size of the first group. The **relative risk** (RR) says how many times greater the risk is; the **risk difference** shows absolute burden. An RR of 2.0 is more important when the baseline is common than when it is rare. These are associations: confounding by other factors and **ecological fallacy** (drawing individual conclusions from group data) limit causal claims, and the direction of causation can run both ways (illness can reduce income).

@example
title: Comparing two neighborhoods
problem: Adult diabetes prevalence is 14% in Neighborhood A (20,000 adults) and 7% in Neighborhood B. Find the relative risk, the risk difference and the number of excess cases in A.
step: $\text{RR} = 0.14/0.07 = 2.0$.
step: Risk difference: $0.14 - 0.07 = 0.07$, or 7 percentage points.
step: Excess cases: $0.07 \times 20{,}000 = 1400$.
step: Interpretation: if A had B's prevalence, about 1400 fewer adults would have diabetes, assuming the difference is attributable to factors that could be changed; this is a model, not a proof of cause.

@example
title: Using the social gradient
problem: In a cohort, 10-year mortality is 8% in the lowest-income quintile, 6% in the middle quintile and 4% in the highest. Is there a gradient, and what is the RR of the lowest versus highest?
step: Mortality falls steadily as income rises, which is a gradient.
step: $\text{RR} = 8/4 = 2.0$.
step: The middle group also has higher mortality than the top group, showing that the effect is not only a poverty-threshold effect.

@mistakes
- Treating an observed disparity as proof of one specific cause.
- Reading a high relative risk as a large absolute burden when the baseline rate is very low.
- Using the ecological fallacy to attribute a neighborhood's average to each of its residents.
- Describing disparities as differences in "choices" alone, ignoring constraints.

@practice self
prompt: Pick three social determinants of health (for example, housing quality, education, and neighborhood food access). For each, state one plausible mechanism by which it affects a named health outcome, and one limitation in interpreting an observed association.
solution: Housing quality: older housing with peeling lead paint exposes children to lead, impairing neurodevelopment; limitation: housing age is correlated with income, so confounding is possible. Education: higher education is associated with greater health literacy and better jobs and coverage, lowering cardiovascular risk; limitation: reverse causation, as childhood illness can reduce schooling. Neighborhood food access: distance from grocery stores limits fresh food, contributing to obesity and diabetes; limitation: ecological fallacy, as neighborhood averages do not describe every resident, and people with resources can shop elsewhere.
