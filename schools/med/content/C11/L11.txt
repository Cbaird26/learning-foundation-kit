@article
**Sociology** studies how social structures shape behavior and life chances. Where psychology often looks at the individual, sociology looks at groups, institutions and patterns across a population.

### Theoretical perspectives

- **Functionalism** (Durkheim, Parsons): society is a system of interdependent parts, each serving a function that maintains stability. Parsons's **sick role** has four elements: the sick person is exempt from normal duties, is not blamed for the illness, should want to get well and should seek competent help.
- **Conflict theory** (Marx, Weber): society is shaped by competition over resources, power and status, producing inequality. Weber distinguished **class** (economic position), **status** (prestige) and **power**.
- **Symbolic interactionism** (Mead, Blumer): people build meaning through everyday interaction with symbols; this perspective examines doctor-patient communication and the labeling of illness.
- **Social constructionism**: many categories, such as illness definitions or race, are products of social agreement as well as biology. **Medicalization** is the process by which a human condition becomes defined as a medical problem.
- **Rational choice** and **exchange theory**: people weigh costs and benefits. **Feminist theory** examines gender inequality.

### Institutions and culture

**Social institutions** are stable structures that meet societal needs: the family, education, religion, government, the economy and the health care system. **Culture** consists of shared beliefs, values, symbols and practices. **Norms** are rules of behavior (**folkways** are mild conventions, **mores** are strongly held, and **laws** are formal). **Values** are broad ideals. **Subcultures** differ from the dominant culture, **assimilation** is absorption into a dominant culture, and **multiculturalism** values the coexistence of cultures. **Cultural relativism** evaluates practices within their own context; **ethnocentrism** judges others by one's own standards. **Anomie** (Durkheim) describes normlessness, and **social capital** is the resources available through social networks.

### Stratification and mobility

**Social stratification** is a ranking of people by wealth, income, education, occupation and prestige. A **caste** system is rigid and ascribed at birth; a **class** system allows some **social mobility** (change in position, within a generation or across generations). **Socioeconomic status** (SES) combines income, education and occupation. **Poverty** can be defined in absolute terms (below a threshold) or relative terms (compared with others).

### Demography

**Demography** studies the size, distribution and composition of populations. Key measures:

$$\text{CBR} = \frac{\text{births}}{\text{mid-year population}}\times 1000, \qquad \text{CDR} = \frac{\text{deaths}}{\text{mid-year population}}\times 1000$$

$$\text{Rate of natural increase} = \frac{\text{CBR} - \text{CDR}}{10}\ \text{percent per year}$$

$$\text{IMR} = \frac{\text{deaths under age 1}}{\text{live births}}\times 1000$$

The **total fertility rate** is the average number of children per woman, and replacement level is about 2.1 in low-mortality countries. A population that grows at $r$ percent per year doubles in about $70/r$ years (the **rule of 70**). The **dependency ratio** compares people typically not in the workforce with those who are:

$$\text{Dependency ratio} = \frac{\text{population under 15} + \text{population 65 and over}}{\text{population aged 15 to 64}}\times 100.$$

The **demographic transition** has stages: high birth and death rates (stable, small population); falling death rates with high birth rates (rapid growth); falling birth rates (growth slows); and low birth and death rates (stable or declining). **Population pyramids** display age and sex structure, and aging societies have large older cohorts and growing demand for chronic disease care. **Migration** (immigration and emigration), urbanization and **life expectancy** also shape health systems. Differences in life expectancy and infant mortality among groups are among the clearest signs of unequal health.

@example
title: Birth, death and growth rates
problem: A country has a mid-year population of $2{,}000{,}000$, with $30{,}000$ births and $18{,}000$ deaths in a year. Find the CBR, CDR, annual rate of natural increase and approximate doubling time.
step: $\text{CBR} = 30{,}000/2{,}000{,}000 \times 1000 = 15$ per 1000.
step: $\text{CDR} = 18{,}000/2{,}000{,}000 \times 1000 = 9$ per 1000.
step: Natural increase: $(15 - 9)/10 = 0.6\%$ per year.
step: Rule of 70: $70/0.6 \approx 117$ years (ignoring migration).

@example
title: A dependency ratio
problem: In a region, 20% of the population is under 15 and 12% is 65 or older. What is the dependency ratio?
step: Dependents: $20\% + 12\% = 32\%$.
step: Working-age share: $100\% - 32\% = 68\%$.
step: Ratio: $32/68 \times 100 \approx 47.1$ dependents per 100 working-age people.

@mistakes
- Mixing up the CDR (all deaths per 1000 people) with the infant mortality rate (infant deaths per 1000 live births).
- Dividing by 100 instead of 1000 when finding CBR and CDR.
- Treating a social construct as "not real"; constructs can have real consequences.
- Using the sick role as a description of all illness; it fits acute illness better than chronic illness.

@practice self
prompt: A hospital finds that patients from lower-income neighborhoods are admitted for preventable conditions more often. Describe how a functionalist and a conflict theorist might each interpret this finding, and name one data source that would help distinguish the explanations.
solution: A functionalist might see the health system as a set of institutions that usually serve society but that has gaps, such as access to primary care, which can be repaired to restore the system's balance; the pattern is a dysfunction to correct. A conflict theorist would emphasize unequal distribution of resources and power, so that groups with fewer resources receive less preventive care and face more exposures, and the pattern reflects structural inequality that benefits some groups. Useful data would include insurance status, primary-care supply by neighborhood, travel time to clinics, or income-stratified rates of preventable admissions over time.
rubric: Gives a coherent functionalist interpretation.
rubric: Gives a coherent conflict-theory interpretation emphasizing resources and power.
rubric: Names a relevant data source or measure.
hint: Ask whether each theory sees the pattern as a malfunction or as a product of unequal power.
hint: Think of measures such as insurance coverage or clinic distance.
