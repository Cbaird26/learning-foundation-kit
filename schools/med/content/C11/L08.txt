@article
A **psychological disorder** is a pattern of thoughts, feelings or behaviors that causes significant distress or impairment and is not an expected cultural response. The **DSM-5-TR** defines disorders by criteria, and the ICD-11 is used worldwide. Explanations combine **biological**, **psychological** and **social** factors (the biopsychosocial model); the **diathesis-stress model** says a vulnerability produces illness when stress exceeds a threshold.

### Major categories

- **Anxiety disorders**: **generalized anxiety disorder** (excessive worry for at least 6 months), **panic disorder** (recurrent unexpected panic attacks with persistent worry about them), **specific phobia**, **social anxiety disorder** and **agoraphobia**. The amygdala is hyperreactive and GABA and serotonin systems are involved.
- **Obsessive-compulsive and related disorders**: **obsessions** (intrusive thoughts) and **compulsions** (repetitive behaviors to reduce distress); cortico-striatal-thalamic circuits are implicated.
- **Trauma-related**: **PTSD** has intrusion, avoidance, negative changes in mood and cognition, and hyperarousal for more than a month after a traumatic event; acute stress disorder lasts 3 days to 1 month.
- **Depressive disorders**: **major depressive disorder** needs at least 5 of 9 symptoms (including depressed mood or anhedonia) for 2 weeks; **persistent depressive disorder** lasts at least 2 years. Monoamine, HPA-axis, inflammatory and cognitive (negative triad, learned helplessness) accounts all contribute.
- **Bipolar disorders**: **bipolar I** requires a **manic** episode (at least 1 week of elevated or irritable mood with increased energy, or any duration if hospitalization is needed); **bipolar II** has hypomania (at least 4 days) and major depression.
- **Schizophrenia spectrum**: **positive symptoms** (hallucinations, delusions, disorganized speech) and **negative symptoms** (flat affect, alogia, avolition); continuous signs for at least 6 months. The dopamine hypothesis is supported by effects of antipsychotics (D2 blockade), and glutamatergic and developmental factors also contribute. Onset is typically in late adolescence to early adulthood.
- **Personality disorders**: enduring maladaptive patterns in three clusters: A (odd: paranoid, schizoid, schizotypal), B (dramatic: antisocial, borderline, histrionic, narcissistic), C (anxious: avoidant, dependent, obsessive-compulsive personality disorder).
- **Neurodevelopmental**: **ADHD** (inattention or hyperactivity-impulsivity before age 12) and **autism spectrum disorder** (deficits in social communication plus restricted, repetitive behaviors).
- **Others**: eating disorders (anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, binge-eating disorder), somatic symptom disorders and dissociative disorders.

### Treatment

**Psychotherapies** include **cognitive behavioral therapy** (CBT; first-line for anxiety, depression and insomnia), exposure therapy, psychodynamic therapy, humanistic therapy and dialectical behavior therapy. **Medications** include SSRIs (for depression and anxiety), lithium or other mood stabilizers (bipolar disorder), antipsychotics and benzodiazepines (short-term use only). **Electroconvulsive therapy** is highly effective for severe or treatment-resistant depression. Stigma delays treatment, and person-first language ("a person with schizophrenia") is standard.

### Screening statistics

Screening questionnaires report **sensitivity** and **specificity**, but clinicians need the **positive predictive value** (PPV): the probability that a person with a positive screen has the disorder, which depends on prevalence:

$$\text{PPV} = \frac{\text{sens}\times \text{prev}}{\text{sens}\times\text{prev} + (1-\text{spec})(1-\text{prev})}.$$

A positive screen in a low-prevalence population is often a false positive.

### Motivation and health behavior

Recall the lesson on motivation. When a patient does not follow treatment, different models point to different interventions: the **Health Belief Model** to perceived threat and barriers, **self-efficacy** to building small successes, **self-determination theory** to supporting autonomy, the **transtheoretical model** to matching advice to stage, and **operant** theory to changing consequences. Depression and anxiety are themselves common reasons for nonadherence.

@example
title: Positive predictive value of a depression screen
problem: A questionnaire has sensitivity $0.88$ and specificity $0.88$. In a primary care clinic where $10\%$ of patients have major depression, what is the PPV?
step: Take $1000$ patients: $100$ have depression and $900$ do not.
step: True positives: $0.88 \times 100 = 88$. False positives: $(1 - 0.88) \times 900 = 108$.
step: PPV $= 88/(88 + 108) = 88/196 \approx 0.449$.
step: Fewer than half of positive screens are true cases, so a positive screen is followed by a diagnostic interview.

@example
title: Distinguishing panic disorder from generalized anxiety disorder
problem: A 29-year-old has had four sudden episodes of chest tightness, palpitations, sweating and fear of dying that peak in 10 minutes, and now avoids driving for fear of another. The ECG and thyroid tests are normal. What is the diagnosis?
step: Abrupt peaks of fear with somatic symptoms are panic attacks.
step: Recurrent unexpected attacks with persistent worry about more attacks and behavioral change (avoiding driving) define panic disorder.
step: Generalized anxiety disorder instead involves continuous excessive worry over many areas for at least 6 months without discrete peaks.
step: First-line treatment is CBT, an SSRI, or both.

@mistakes
- Equating schizophrenia with "split personality" (dissociative identity disorder).
- Treating a high-sensitivity screening test as a diagnosis, ignoring prevalence and PPV.
- Confusing hypomania (no marked impairment, no psychosis, at least 4 days) with mania.
- Assuming that people with psychiatric disorders are generally violent; they are more often victims than perpetrators.

@practice self
prompt: A 48-year-old man with hypertension has stopped taking his daily antihypertensive. He says he feels fine, thinks the tablets are unnecessary, is tired of being told what to do, and doubts that he could remember daily doses. Compare how the Health Belief Model, self-determination theory and self-efficacy would each interpret his nonadherence, and give one intervention for each.
solution: Health Belief Model: he has low perceived susceptibility and severity because hypertension is asymptomatic; intervention: personalized risk information, home blood pressure readings and a clear cue to action. Self-determination theory: his resistance reflects a threat to autonomy; intervention: shared decision making, offering choices of medication or timing, and eliciting his own reasons for treatment. Self-efficacy: he doubts that he can take medication daily; intervention: a simple routine (a pillbox, a link to brushing teeth), and a goal of a small number of successful days to build confidence. These models are complementary, so a clinician can combine them by first asking what concerns him.

rubric: Notes that models are complementary or that the patient's reasons should be elicited.

hint: Perceived risk, autonomy and confidence are the key constructs.
