@article
**Identity** is a person's sense of who they are, made up of a **personal identity** (traits, values) and **social identities** (group memberships). **Socialization** is the lifelong process by which a person learns the norms, values and roles of a society.

### Self and identity

Cooley's **looking-glass self** says we form our self-concept by imagining how others see us. Mead described the **I** (spontaneous self) and the **me** (internalized expectations of others) and the importance of taking the role of others in play. Self-concept includes **self-esteem** (evaluation of one's worth) and **self-efficacy**. Goffman's **dramaturgical** approach treats social life as **impression management**: front stage and back stage behavior. **Social identity theory** (Tajfel and Turner) holds that group membership contributes to self-esteem and drives in-group favoritism.

### Developmental frameworks

**Erikson's psychosocial stages** each pose a crisis: trust versus mistrust (infancy), autonomy versus shame and doubt (toddler), initiative versus guilt (preschool), industry versus inferiority (school age), identity versus role confusion (adolescence), intimacy versus isolation (young adulthood), generativity versus stagnation (middle adulthood) and integrity versus despair (late adulthood). Marcia described identity statuses based on exploration and commitment: diffusion, foreclosure, moratorium and achievement.

**Kohlberg's moral development** has three levels: **preconventional** (obedience and self-interest), **conventional** (social approval and law and order) and **postconventional** (social contract and universal principles). Critics note that it reflects a particular justice orientation and that moral reasoning may not predict behavior.

**Attachment**: Bowlby proposed an evolved bond between infant and caregiver; Harlow's monkey studies showed that contact comfort matters more than feeding. In Ainsworth's Strange Situation, infants show **secure** attachment (distressed on separation, easily soothed on reunion), **avoidant**, **ambivalent (resistant)** or **disorganized** attachment. About two thirds of infants in typical U.S. samples are secure. Baumrind's parenting styles are **authoritative** (warm with firm limits, linked to good outcomes), **authoritarian**, **permissive** and **neglectful**, although culture and temperament modify their effects.

### Agents and processes of socialization

The main **agents** are family, peers, school, mass and social media, religion and work. **Anticipatory socialization** prepares someone for a future role, as in premedical advising. **Resocialization** replaces old norms, as in a **total institution** (Goffman) such as a prison or military boot camp. In medicine, the "hidden curriculum" socializes trainees into the professional identity, including attitudes toward patients and hierarchy.

### Concepts of identity

- **Sex** refers to biological attributes; **gender identity** is a person's internal sense of their gender; **gender roles** are social expectations; **sexual orientation** is a pattern of attraction. These are distinct dimensions.
- **Race** is a social category with no biological basis as discrete human groups, though racial categories have real social consequences; **ethnicity** refers to shared culture, ancestry and language. Identity can combine several categories, an idea called **intersectionality** (Crenshaw), which explains how overlapping categories shape experience.
- **Stigma** (Goffman) is a mark that discredits a person. **Labeling theory** describes how labels shape self-concept and behavior.

### Social psychology applied to clinical settings

**Conformity**: trainees and nurses may defer to a senior clinician's wrong plan (normative influence) or accept a group's reading of an ambiguous test (informational influence). Hierarchy, group cohesion and **groupthink** sustain errors. Safety practices such as checklists, time-outs, "stop the line" authority and explicit invitations to disagree reduce conformity pressure.

**Attribution errors**: the fundamental attribution error leads clinicians to label patients as "noncompliant" or "difficult" without examining barriers. The **self-serving bias** can make a clinician explain good outcomes by skill and bad ones by patient factors. **Implicit bias** may affect pain assessment and treatment decisions, and awareness and structured protocols reduce its impact.

title: Percentage of securely attached infants
problem: In a Strange Situation study of 80 infants, 52 are classified as secure. What percentage is this, and what percentage are classified insecure (avoidant, ambivalent or disorganized)?
step: Secure share: $52/80 = 0.65$, or $65\%$.
step: Insecure infants: $80 - 52 = 28$.
step: Insecure share: $28/80 = 35\%$, which equals $100\% - 65\%$.

@mistakes
- Treating insecure attachment as a disorder; it is a risk factor, not a diagnosis.
- Using "sex" and "gender" as synonyms.
- Confusing a social role (a set of expected behaviors) with a status (the position).
- Assuming that Kohlberg's stages describe what a person will do rather than how they reason.

prompt: In a surgical team, a nurse notices that the wrong side is marked for a procedure, but the senior surgeon and the rest of the team appear confident. (a) Explain how conformity could prevent her from speaking up. (b) Describe two practices that reduce this risk. (c) Later, the surgeon says of a patient who missed an appointment, "he's irresponsible." Name the attribution error and a better approach.
solution: (a) Normative influence (fear of disapproval and of challenging authority) and informational influence (assuming others must know better) both push toward silence, and the unanimous appearance of the group increases pressure. (b) A pre-procedure time-out in which every team member must verify the site, and explicit norms that anyone can stop the procedure. Inviting dissent from the team leader also helps. (c) The fundamental attribution error: attributing the missed appointment to disposition. A better approach is to ask about situational barriers such as transportation, work or caregiving, and then to problem solve.
