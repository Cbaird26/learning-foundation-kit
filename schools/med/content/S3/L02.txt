@article
The **glomerular filtration rate (GFR)** is the volume of plasma filtered per minute by all nephrons: normally about 125 mL/min (180 L/day), or roughly 90 to 120 mL/min/1.73 m$^2$ when indexed to body surface area. It is the best single measure of kidney function, and nearly every renal drug dose and staging system depends on it.

### Starling forces

Filtration is driven by hydrostatic and oncotic pressures across the glomerular capillary:

$$\text{GFR} = K_f\,[(P_{GC} - P_{BS}) - (\pi_{GC} - \pi_{BS})]$$

where $P_{GC}$ is glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure (about 50 mmHg), $P_{BS}$ is Bowman space pressure (about 10 mmHg), $\pi_{GC}$ is glomerular capillary oncotic pressure (about 25 to 35 mmHg, rising along the capillary as protein-free fluid leaves), and $\pi_{BS}$ is essentially zero. $K_f$ is the filtration coefficient: surface area times permeability. Net pressure is therefore only about 10 mmHg, but $K_f$ is large, so volume is large.

### Clearance

The **clearance** of a substance is the plasma volume completely cleared of it per unit time:

$$C = \frac{U \times V}{P}$$

with $U$ the urine concentration, $V$ the urine flow rate and $P$ the plasma concentration. A marker that is freely filtered and neither reabsorbed, secreted nor metabolized has a clearance equal to GFR. **Inulin** meets this definition and is the reference standard, but it is used in research only. Interpretation of clearance of other substances: clearance greater than GFR means net tubular secretion; clearance less than GFR means net reabsorption; glucose is normally zero. **Para-aminohippurate (PAH)** is filtered and almost completely secreted, so its clearance estimates effective renal plasma flow (about 90% extraction).

### Creatinine in practice

**Creatinine** comes from muscle metabolism, is freely filtered, and is slightly secreted by the proximal tubule, so creatinine clearance overestimates GFR by about 10 to 20%. Trimethoprim and cimetidine block creatinine secretion and raise serum creatinine without changing GFR. Muscle mass, diet and some supplements affect it too. Cystatin C is an alternative marker, and current laboratories report an estimated GFR from the CKD-EPI 2021 equation, which does not use race.

At steady state, creatinine production equals excretion, so $P_{Cr} \propto 1/\text{GFR}$: a halving of GFR doubles serum creatinine. A rise from 1.0 to 2.0 mg/dL means a 50% loss of GFR, but a change from 5.0 to 6.0 means a small further loss. After an acute insult, serum creatinine lags behind true GFR.

The **Cockcroft-Gault** equation estimates creatinine clearance for drug dosing:

$$\text{CrCl} = \frac{(140 - \text{age}) \times \text{weight (kg)}}{72 \times P_{Cr}\ (\text{mg/dL})} \times (0.85 \text{ if female}).$$

### Filtration fraction and hemodynamics

The **filtration fraction** is the fraction of plasma filtered:

$$\text{FF} = \frac{\text{GFR}}{\text{RPF}} \approx \frac{125}{625} = 0.2.$$

Autoregulation (the myogenic response and tubuloglomerular feedback, in which a high NaCl load at the macula densa constricts the afferent arteriole) keeps GFR nearly constant for mean arterial pressures of about 80 to 180 mmHg. Outside those limits, GFR follows pressure.

| Change | GFR | RPF | FF |
|---|---|---|---|
| Afferent arteriole constriction | decreases | decreases | unchanged |
| Efferent arteriole constriction (angiotensin II) | increases | decreases | increases |
| Efferent arteriole dilation (ACE inhibitor or ARB) | decreases | increases | decreases |
| Ureteral obstruction (raised $P_{BS}$) | decreases | unchanged | decreases |
| Raised plasma protein concentration | decreases | unchanged | decreases |

NSAIDs block vasodilatory prostaglandins at the afferent arteriole; ACE inhibitors and ARBs remove efferent constriction. Together with volume depletion (for example from a diuretic) this combination can precipitate acute kidney injury.

@example
title: Cockcroft-Gault estimate
problem: A 70-year-old man weighs 70 kg and has a serum creatinine of 1.4 mg/dL. Estimate his creatinine clearance.
step: Write the formula for a man: $\text{CrCl} = \frac{(140 - \text{age}) \times \text{weight}}{72 \times P_{Cr}}$.
step: Substitute: $\frac{(140 - 70) \times 70}{72 \times 1.4} = \frac{4900}{100.8}$.
step: Divide: about 48.6 mL/min. No sex correction is needed for a man.
step: This is a moderately reduced clearance, and drugs cleared by the kidney may need adjustment.

@example
title: Filtration fraction from inulin and PAH
problem: Plasma inulin is 2 mg/dL, urine inulin is 240 mg/dL, plasma PAH is 0.5 mg/dL, urine PAH is 300 mg/dL, and urine flow is 1 mL/min. Treat the PAH clearance as renal plasma flow. Calculate GFR, RPF and FF.
step: GFR equals inulin clearance: $\frac{240 \times 1}{2} = 120$ mL/min.
step: RPF equals PAH clearance: $\frac{300 \times 1}{0.5} = 600$ mL/min.
step: Filtration fraction: $\text{FF} = \frac{120}{600} = 0.20$, so 20% of plasma entering the glomerulus is filtered.

@mistakes
- Forgetting the 0.85 factor for women in Cockcroft-Gault, or using it for men.
- Mixing units in the clearance equation: urine and plasma concentrations must use the same units and V must be in mL/min to give mL/min.
- Assuming that efferent constriction lowers GFR; moderate constriction raises GFR because it raises glomerular capillary pressure, while RPF falls.
- Treating any rise in creatinine as an equal loss of GFR: the relationship is hyperbolic.

prompt: A patient has an obstructing ureteral stone. Bowman space pressure is 14 mmHg, glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure is 46 mmHg, mean glomerular capillary oncotic pressure is 28 mmHg, and $K_f$ is 12.5 mL/min per mmHg. Calculate the GFR in mL/min.
answer: 50
check: 12.5 * ((46 - 14) - 28)
tol: 1%
unit: mL/min
hint: Net filtration pressure equals hydrostatic gradient minus oncotic gradient.
hint: Net pressure is $(46 - 14) - 28$ mmHg; multiply by $K_f$.
solution: Net pressure is $(46 - 14) - 28 = 4$ mmHg, and $\text{GFR} = 12.5 \times 4 = 50$ mL/min.
