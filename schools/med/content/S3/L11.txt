@article
Urinary tract infections are among the most common bacterial infections, and urinary stones affect about one in ten people over a lifetime. They interact: stones obstruct and harbor bacteria, and some bacteria make stones.

### Cystitis and pyelonephritis

Most infections are **ascending**. The usual organism is *Escherichia coli* (about 80% of uncomplicated cases); *Staphylococcus saprophyticus* is common in young sexually active women, while *Klebsiella*, *Proteus*, *Enterococcus* and *Pseudomonas* appear with obstruction, catheters and hospital exposure.

**Cystitis** causes dysuria, frequency, urgency and suprapubic pain without fever or flank pain. The urinalysis shows **leukocyte esterase** (pyuria) and often **nitrite** (made by gram-negative bacteria that reduce nitrate; *Enterococcus* and *S. saprophyticus* are nitrite-negative). In a typical woman with symptoms, empirical therapy without culture is acceptable. First-line options for uncomplicated cystitis: **nitrofurantoin** for 5 days, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for 3 days where local resistance is low, or single-dose **fosfomycin**; fluoroquinolones are reserved for other situations because of toxicity.

**Pyelonephritis** adds fever, flank pain, costovertebral angle tenderness, nausea and **white blood cell casts**. Obtain a urine culture; treat with an agent that achieves tissue levels, such as ceftriaxone or a fluoroquinolone according to local resistance, for 7 to 14 days. Image if there is no improvement in 48 to 72 hours or if obstruction or abscess is suspected. Complications include perinephric abscess, emphysematous pyelonephritis (diabetes), and papillary necrosis (diabetes, sickle cell disease, analgesic use, obstruction). **Infection with obstruction** (a stone with fever) is an emergency requiring urgent decompression by stent or nephrostomy, with antibiotics.

### Asymptomatic bacteriuria

Bacteria in a clean-catch culture without symptoms should be **treated only in pregnancy** (reduces pyelonephritis; screen at 12 to 16 weeks) and **before urologic procedures that cause mucosal bleeding**. Do not treat in elderly or institutionalized patients, catheterized patients or diabetics.

### Special situations

Men with UTI are considered complicated: look for prostatitis and obstruction. Children with febrile UTI may have vesicoureteral reflux, assessed by ultrasound and a voiding cystourethrogram. Pregnancy: avoid fluoroquinolones and tetracyclines.

### Kidney stones

Stones present with colicky flank pain radiating to the groin, nausea and hematuria. **Noncontrast CT** is the best test; ultrasound is used in pregnancy and children. Stones lodge at the ureteropelvic junction, the pelvic brim and the ureterovesical junction.

| Stone | Frequency | Urine | Crystal | Radiograph | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Calcium oxalate | Most common (about 80% are calcium) | Acidic | Envelope or dumbbell | Radiopaque | Hypercalciuria, hyperoxaluria (Crohn disease, ethylene glycol), hypocitraturia |
| Calcium phosphate | Common | Alkaline | Wedge-shaped | Radiopaque | Type 1 RTA, hyperparathyroidism |
| Struvite (magnesium ammonium phosphate) | 10 to 15% | Alkaline (above 7) | Coffin-lid | Radiopaque | Urease-producing bacteria (*Proteus*); staghorn calculi |
| Uric acid | About 5 to 10% | Acidic | Rhomboid or rosette | **Radiolucent** | Gout, tumor lysis, high protein intake |
| Cystine | Rare | Acidic | Hexagonal | Faintly radiopaque | Inherited defect of dibasic amino acid transport; positive cyanide-nitroprusside test |

### Management of colic and prevention

Analgesia with an NSAID such as ketorolac, hydration as tolerated and antiemetics. Small stones (under about 5 mm) usually pass; an alpha-blocker (tamsulosin) is sometimes used for distal stones of 5 to 10 mm, though the evidence is mixed. Larger or stuck stones need shock wave lithotripsy, ureteroscopy or percutaneous nephrolithotomy (large or staghorn).

Prevention for calcium stones: **high fluid intake** to a urine output above 2.5 L/day, **thiazide diuretics** for hypercalciuria (they reduce urinary calcium), **potassium citrate** for hypocitraturia, **normal dietary calcium** (restriction increases oxalate absorption), reduced sodium and animal protein. Uric acid stones: alkalinize urine to pH 6 to 6.5 with potassium citrate and allopurinol if needed. Struvite: surgical removal and antibiotics. Cystine: fluids, alkalinization and tiopronin.

@example
title: Matching a stone to its cause
problem: A woman has recurrent *Proteus* UTIs and a large branching stone filling the renal pelvis on imaging. Urine pH is 7.8. Identify the stone and plan management.
step: Alkaline urine plus a urease-producing organism points to struvite, which forms when urease splits urea to ammonia and raises urine pH.
step: The branching shape is a staghorn calculus; crystals are coffin-lid shaped.
step: Management: surgical removal (percutaneous nephrolithotomy) and antibiotics, since the stone harbors the bacteria; medical dissolution alone is not enough.

@mistakes
- Treating asymptomatic bacteriuria in an elderly woman or a catheterized patient.
- Using fluoroquinolones as first-line for uncomplicated cystitis.
- Restricting dietary calcium to prevent calcium oxalate stones.
- Attempting to pass a stone in a febrile patient with obstruction instead of urgent decompression.
- Assuming all stones are radiopaque: uric acid stones are not.
