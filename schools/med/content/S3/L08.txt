@article
**Acute kidney injury (AKI)** is a rapid decline in GFR, recognized by a rise in creatinine or a fall in urine output. In hospitalized patients it is common and strongly linked to mortality. The practical job is to find which of three compartments has failed: blood supply (prerenal), the kidney itself (intrinsic) or the drainage system (postrenal).

### KDIGO definition and stages

AKI is present with any of: **creatinine rise of 0.3 mg/dL or more within 48 hours**, **creatinine at least 1.5 times baseline within 7 days**, or **urine output below 0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 hours**.

| Stage | Creatinine | Urine output |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1.5 to 1.9 times baseline, or rise of 0.3 mg/dL | Under 0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 to 12 h |
| 2 | 2.0 to 2.9 times baseline | Under 0.5 mL/kg/h for 12 h or more |
| 3 | 3 times baseline, or 4.0 mg/dL or above, or dialysis started | Under 0.3 mL/kg/h for 24 h or more, or anuria for 12 h |

### Prerenal azotemia

Reduced renal perfusion without tubular damage: hypovolemia (bleeding, vomiting, diarrhea), low cardiac output, cirrhosis, and drugs that impair autoregulation (NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors, ARBs). The tubule is intact and avidly retains sodium and water: **FE$_{Na}$ under 1%**, urine sodium under 20 mEq/L, urine osmolality above 500 mOsm/kg, a bland sediment (hyaline casts), and a **BUN to creatinine ratio above 20** (urea is reabsorbed with water). Fractional excretion of urea,

$$\text{FE}_{urea} = \frac{U_{urea} \times P_{Cr}}{P_{urea} \times U_{Cr}} \times 100,$$

is under 35% in prerenal states and is useful when diuretics make FE$_{Na}$ unreliable. Treat the cause: balanced crystalloid for hypovolemia, stop offending drugs, optimize cardiac output.

Hepatorenal syndrome is a functional prerenal state in advanced cirrhosis (splanchnic vasodilation, renal vasoconstriction, low urine sodium, bland sediment, no response to fluids). Management includes stopping diuretics and NSAIDs, IV albumin and a vasoconstrictor such as terlipressin or norepinephrine.

### Intrinsic AKI

- **Acute tubular necrosis (ATN)** is the most common cause in hospitalized patients: ischemic (prolonged hypotension) or toxic (aminoglycosides, contrast, cisplatin, amphotericin B, myoglobin from rhabdomyolysis). Findings: **muddy brown granular casts**, FE$_{Na}$ over 2%, urine osmolality near 300 (isosthenuria), urine sodium over 40, and BUN to creatinine ratio under 20. Recovery over 1 to 3 weeks often shows a polyuric phase.
- **Acute interstitial nephritis (AIN)**: drug hypersensitivity (penicillins, cephalosporins, proton pump inhibitors, NSAIDs, sulfonamides). Fever, rash and eosinophilia occur only in a minority; urine shows sterile pyuria and **white blood cell casts**. Stop the drug; steroids are sometimes used.
- **Rhabdomyolysis**: dark urine, dipstick positive for blood without red cells, markedly raised creatine kinase, hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hyperuricemia and early hypocalcemia. Treat with large volumes of IV isotonic fluid.
- Glomerulonephritis (red blood cell casts), vasculitis, atheroembolic disease after catheterization (eosinophilia, livedo reticularis) and thrombotic microangiopathy also appear here.

### Postrenal AKI

Obstruction at any level: benign prostatic hyperplasia, stones, tumors, neurogenic bladder, retroperitoneal fibrosis. To cause AKI both kidneys, or a solitary kidney, must be blocked. Check a bladder scan and **renal ultrasound for hydronephrosis**. Relieve the obstruction with a catheter, stent or nephrostomy; after relief, a post-obstructive diuresis can cause volume and electrolyte losses.

### Indications for urgent dialysis

Remember the vowels: **A**cidosis refractory to treatment, **E**lectrolytes (refractory hyperkalemia), **I**ntoxication (lithium, toxic alcohols, salicylates), **O**verload of volume resistant to diuretics, **U**remic complications (pericarditis, encephalopathy, bleeding).

### Supportive care

Use balanced crystalloid to correct hypovolemia without overloading, avoid nephrotoxins and contrast where possible, adjust drug doses, treat hyperkalemia, monitor urine output and weight, and look for the cause: history, drug list, urinalysis with microscopy, bladder scan and ultrasound.

@example
title: Prerenal azotemia versus ATN
problem: After three days of diarrhea, a patient's creatinine is 3.1 mg/dL (baseline 1.0). Urine sodium is 10 mEq/L, plasma sodium 138, urine creatinine 80 mg/dL. The sediment is bland. Calculate FE$_{Na}$ and classify.
step: Apply the formula: $\frac{10 \times 3.1}{138 \times 80} \times 100$.
step: The numerator is 31 and the denominator is 11040, giving 0.28%, which is under 1%.
step: A low FE$_{Na}$ with a bland sediment after volume loss is prerenal azotemia; stage 3 by creatinine (3.1 times baseline). Give IV isotonic crystalloid and recheck.

@example
title: Staging by urine output
problem: An 80 kg man has urine output of 30 mL/h for 14 hours. Which stage by urine output?
step: The threshold of 0.5 mL/kg/h for this man is 40 mL/h.
step: His 30 mL/h is 0.375 mL/kg/h, which is below 0.5, for more than 12 hours, so stage 2 by urine output.
step: Stage by creatinine too; the higher stage determines the AKI stage.

@mistakes
- Using FE$_{Na}$ during diuretic therapy: use fractional excretion of urea instead.
- Treating every low-urine-output patient with fluids without checking for obstruction or volume overload.
- Expecting eosinophilia or rash in every case of AIN: the classic triad is uncommon.
- Missing a postrenal cause because the ultrasound was not done.

@practice self
prompt: A 72-year-old man is admitted with creatinine 3.5 mg/dL (baseline 1.1). He has benign prostatic hyperplasia, takes ibuprofen and ramipril, and had three days of poor intake. List a differential diagnosis for his AKI by category and the first investigations you would order.
solution: Prerenal (volume depletion with NSAID and ACE inhibitor impairing autoregulation); postrenal (bladder outlet obstruction from BPH); intrinsic (ATN from prolonged prerenal state, AIN from NSAID). First investigations: bladder scan and renal ultrasound for hydronephrosis, urinalysis with microscopy, urine sodium, urea and creatinine for FE indices, potassium and bicarbonate, ECG if potassium is high. Stop ibuprofen and ramipril, give fluid if hypovolemic, and relieve obstruction with a urinary catheter if the bladder is distended.
