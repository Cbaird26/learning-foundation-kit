@article
Acid–base diagnosis is a procedure, not a pattern: determine the primary disorder, check compensation with the formulas from week 6, calculate the anion gap, and then ask whether a second or third disorder is hiding.

### Anion gap metabolic acidosis

A raised gap means an unmeasured acid anion has replaced bicarbonate. Mnemonic causes: **methanol, uremia, diabetic (and alcoholic and starvation) ketoacidosis, propylene glycol, isoniazid or iron, lactic acidosis, ethylene glycol, salicylates**.

- **Lactic acidosis**: type A from hypoperfusion or hypoxia; type B from metformin, thiamine deficiency or liver failure. Treat the cause.
- **Diabetic ketoacidosis**: insulin deficiency makes beta-hydroxybutyrate and acetoacetate; the nitroprusside test detects acetoacetate but not beta-hydroxybutyrate. Treat with IV fluids, then insulin once potassium is at least 3.3 mEq/L, and replace potassium, since total body potassium is depleted even when the plasma level is normal or high.
- **Toxic alcohols**: methanol (visual disturbance, optic neuropathy, formic acid) and ethylene glycol (calcium oxalate crystals, kidney injury, hypocalcemia). Both raise the osmolal gap early. Treat with **fomepizole**, which blocks alcohol dehydrogenase, plus hemodialysis for severe acidosis, kidney injury or end-organ effects.
- **Salicylate toxicity**: direct stimulation of the respiratory center causes respiratory alkalosis plus AG metabolic acidosis, with tinnitus. Treat with IV sodium bicarbonate to alkalinize blood and urine, and with dialysis if severe.

### The osmolal gap

$$\text{osmolal gap} = \text{measured osmolality} - \text{calculated osmolality}$$

where calculated osmolality is $2\,\text{Na} + \text{glucose}/18 + \text{BUN}/2.8$ (add ethanol/4.6 if ethanol is present). A normal gap is under about 10 mOsm/kg. A higher gap points to unmeasured osmoles: toxic alcohols, mannitol, and ethanol. With late presentation the parent alcohol may have already been metabolized, so the gap can be normal when the anion gap is high.

### Non-anion gap (hyperchloremic) metabolic acidosis

Causes are bicarbonate loss (diarrhea, ureteral diversion, acetazolamide) and renal acid excretion defects (renal tubular acidosis, RTA), plus large-volume saline. The **urine anion gap** ($U_{Na} + U_K - U_{Cl}$) estimates ammonium excretion: it is **negative** when the kidney excretes plenty of NH$_4^+$ (diarrhea) and **positive** when it cannot (RTA).

| RTA | Defect | Urine pH | Potassium | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Type 1 (distal) | Alpha-intercalated H$^+$ secretion fails | Above 5.5 | Low | Calcium phosphate stones, nephrocalcinosis; Sjögren, amphotericin B |
| Type 2 (proximal) | Bicarbonate reabsorption fails (Fanconi) | Below 5.5 once bicarbonate is low | Low | Myeloma, tenofovir, acetazolamide; needs large alkali doses |
| Type 4 | Hypoaldosteronism or resistance | Below 5.5 | **High** | Diabetic nephropathy, ACE inhibitors, spironolactone, trimethoprim |

### Metabolic alkalosis

Generation: loss of gastric acid (vomiting, nasogastric suction), contraction alkalosis from diuretics, or mineralocorticoid excess. Maintenance: volume contraction, chloride depletion, hypokalemia and aldosterone. **Urine chloride** divides the causes:

- **Chloride-responsive** (urine Cl under 20 mEq/L): vomiting, remote diuretic use, post-hypercapnia. Treat with IV isotonic saline and potassium.
- **Chloride-resistant** (urine Cl over 20): with hypertension, primary hyperaldosteronism, Cushing syndrome, licorice excess; without hypertension, Bartter and Gitelman syndromes and active diuretic use.

### Respiratory disorders

Acidosis from hypoventilation: COPD, opioids, neuromuscular disease, obesity hypoventilation. Alkalosis from hyperventilation: anxiety, pulmonary embolism, pregnancy, high altitude, salicylates, sepsis.

### Mixed disorders and the delta ratio

In pure AG acidosis, each mEq/L rise in AG should be matched by about a 1 mEq/L fall in bicarbonate, using normal values of 12 and 24:

$$\text{delta ratio} = \frac{\text{AG} - 12}{24 - [\text{HCO}_3^-]}.$$

A ratio of **1 to 2** is a pure AG acidosis; **below 1** suggests an additional non-AG acidosis; **above 2** suggests an additional metabolic alkalosis (or a high baseline bicarbonate). Winter's formula tests for a second respiratory disorder.

@example
title: Diabetic ketoacidosis, full interpretation
problem: A patient has Na 134, Cl 96, HCO$_3^-$ 8 mEq/L, glucose 520 mg/dL, pH 7.20 and $P_{CO_2}$ 21 mmHg. Interpret fully.
step: pH is acidemic with a low bicarbonate: primary metabolic acidosis.
step: Winter's formula gives $1.5 \times 8 + 8 = 20 \pm 2$ mmHg, so the expected $P_{CO_2}$ is 18 to 22; the measured 21 is appropriate compensation.
step: The anion gap is $134 - (96 + 8) = 30$ mEq/L, elevated: ketoacids in DKA.
step: The delta ratio is $(30 - 12)/(24 - 8) = 18/16 \approx 1.1$, which fits a pure AG acidosis with no additional alkalosis or non-AG acidosis.

@mistakes
- Using the delta ratio without first calculating the anion gap, or calling a gap of 14 enough to apply it.
- Forgetting that fomepizole, not ethanol, is the preferred antidote for toxic alcohols and that dialysis may still be needed.
- Calling type 1 and type 4 RTA the same because both have a urine pH above 5.5: type 4 usually has acidic urine and high potassium.
- Treating chloride-resistant alkalosis with saline when the cause is mineralocorticoid excess.
