@article
About 98% of body potassium is inside cells, so the normal plasma level of 3.5 to 5.0 mEq/L is a tiny window onto a huge reservoir. The ratio of intracellular to extracellular potassium sets the resting membrane potential, which is why small changes cause arrhythmia and weakness.

### Hyperkalemia

Causes fall into four groups: **reduced excretion** (acute or chronic kidney disease, hypoaldosteronism, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, spironolactone, trimethoprim, NSAIDs, heparin), **shift out of cells** (acidemia, insulin deficiency, beta-blockers, digoxin toxicity, hyperosmolality), **increased release** (rhabdomyolysis, tumor lysis, hemolysis, massive tissue injury) and **pseudohyperkalemia** (hemolyzed sample, severe leukocytosis or thrombocytosis). Always ask whether it is real before treating.

The ECG changes progress with the level: **peaked T waves**, then PR prolongation and P wave flattening, then **QRS widening**, a sine-wave pattern, ventricular fibrillation or asystole. ECG changes, not the number alone, decide urgency.

Treat in this order:

1. **Stabilize the membrane** with IV calcium (calcium gluconate), which acts in minutes but lowers nothing. A typical adult dose for study is 1 g (10 mL of 10%) given over several minutes and repeated if ECG changes persist; check current references.
2. **Shift potassium into cells**: regular insulin with dextrose (typically 10 units IV with 25 g of dextrose, with glucose monitoring; lower insulin doses are used in kidney failure to reduce hypoglycemia), nebulized albuterol in high dose, and sodium bicarbonate when there is metabolic acidosis (its benefit is modest otherwise). These act within 15 to 30 minutes for about 1 to 2 hours.
3. **Remove potassium** from the body: loop diuretic if the patient makes urine, potassium binders (sodium zirconium cyclosilicate, patiromer; sodium polystyrene sulfonate is less favored), and hemodialysis for severe or refractory cases or kidney failure.

Then stop contributing drugs, look for the cause and recheck potassium and glucose.

### Hypokalemia

Causes: GI loss (diarrhea, vomiting via alkalosis and aldosterone), renal loss (loop and thiazide diuretics, hyperaldosteronism, Bartter and Gitelman syndromes, renal tubular acidosis), and shift into cells (insulin, beta-agonists, alkalemia, refeeding). The ECG shows flattened T waves, U waves and ST depression; effects include weakness, ileus, rhabdomyolysis and arrhythmias. Replace orally when possible; intravenous potassium chloride is typically limited to about 10 mEq/h by peripheral line unless monitored. **Check magnesium**: hypomagnesemia causes renal potassium wasting and makes hypokalemia refractory until magnesium is replaced.

### Calcium

Total calcium is normally 8.5 to 10.5 mg/dL, and about 40% is bound to albumin. Correct for low albumin:

$$\text{corrected Ca} = \text{total Ca} + 0.8\,(4.0 - \text{albumin in g/dL}).$$

Ionized calcium is the active fraction (about 4.5 to 5.3 mg/dL, 1.1 to 1.3 mmol/L) and is measured directly when the correction is doubtful. **PTH** raises calcium (bone resorption, distal tubule reabsorption, activation of vitamin D) and lowers phosphate by phosphaturia.

**Hypercalcemia** presents with polyuria (nephrogenic diabetes insipidus), constipation, nausea, confusion, stones and a short QT interval. Measure PTH. High or inappropriately normal PTH means primary hyperparathyroidism (adenoma; low phosphate); suppressed PTH means malignancy (PTHrP from squamous cancers, osteolytic metastases, myeloma), vitamin D excess (granulomatous disease making calcitriol), thiazides or milk-alkali syndrome. Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia has a very low urine calcium clearance. Treat with **IV isotonic saline** first, then calcitonin for fast short-term effect, bisphosphonates (zoledronic acid) or denosumab, glucocorticoids for vitamin D-mediated disease, and dialysis in extreme cases; loop diuretics only after volume is restored.

**Hypocalcemia** causes paresthesias, tetany (Chvostek and Trousseau signs), QT prolongation, seizures. Causes: hypoparathyroidism (often after neck surgery), vitamin D deficiency, chronic kidney disease, hypomagnesemia (impaired PTH release and action), pancreatitis, citrate in transfusions and rhabdomyolysis. Treat with IV calcium for symptoms, then oral calcium and vitamin D.

### Phosphate

Hyperphosphatemia occurs mainly in kidney failure, tumor lysis and rhabdomyolysis; it is treated with dietary restriction and binders taken with meals (sevelamer, calcium acetate). Hypophosphatemia occurs with refeeding, alcohol use, recovery from DKA, and hyperparathyroidism, and causes weakness, hemolysis and respiratory failure. FGF23 lowers phosphate and calcitriol.

@example
title: Hyperkalemia with ECG changes
problem: A 58-year-old man with end-stage kidney disease missed dialysis and now has potassium 7.1 mEq/L, peaked T waves and a widened QRS. What is the correct sequence of treatment?
step: The ECG shows the heart is at risk, so first give IV calcium gluconate to stabilize the membrane. It does not lower potassium.
step: Next shift potassium into cells with regular insulin and dextrose, and add nebulized albuterol; if he is acidemic, bicarbonate can help.
step: Last, remove potassium from the body: he has kidney failure, so urgent hemodialysis is the definitive therapy; binders help bridge.
step: Recheck potassium and glucose in 1 to 2 hours, because the shift is temporary and hypoglycemia is common.

@example
title: Calcium corrected for albumin
problem: A patient with cirrhosis has total calcium 8.0 mg/dL and albumin 2.5 g/dL. Is she hypocalcemic?
step: Albumin is 1.5 g/dL below 4.0.
step: Corrected calcium equals $8.0 + 0.8 \times 1.5 = 9.2$ mg/dL, which is normal.
step: The low total calcium is explained by hypoalbuminemia, so no calcium replacement is needed.

@mistakes
- Treating hyperkalemia with insulin first when the ECG is abnormal: calcium protects the heart first.
- Giving calcium as if it lowered potassium; it only buys time.
- Replacing potassium in a patient with low magnesium without replacing the magnesium.
- Starting a loop diuretic in hypercalcemia before volume repletion, which worsens the dehydration.
