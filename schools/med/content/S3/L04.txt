@article
Sodium disorders are **water** disorders. Serum sodium concentration reflects total body water relative to sodium and potassium, not the amount of sodium alone. Both hyponatremia and hypernatremia are dangerous mainly through their effect on brain cell volume.

### Osmolality and ADH

Plasma osmolality is estimated by

$$P_{osm} \approx 2\,[\text{Na}^+] + \frac{\text{glucose}}{18} + \frac{\text{BUN}}{2.8},$$

with normal about 275 to 295 mOsm/kg. Urea crosses cell membranes freely, so only sodium and glucose are **effective** osmoles that move water. **ADH** (vasopressin) is released when osmoreceptors sense a rise of only 1 to 2% in osmolality, and also by a large (10 to 15%) fall in effective circulating volume, nausea, pain and some drugs. Thirst is the other defense. ADH acts on V2 receptors to insert aquaporin-2 channels in the collecting duct.

### Hyponatremia: three questions

1. **Serum osmolality.** *High* (hyperglycemia, mannitol): water has left cells; correct sodium by about 1.6 mEq/L for each 100 mg/dL of glucose above 100 (some references use 2.4). *Normal* (pseudohyponatremia from severe hyperlipidemia or hyperproteinemia, with older flame-photometry or indirect electrode methods). *Low*: true hypotonic hyponatremia, the common case.
2. **Urine osmolality.** Below about 100 mOsm/kg means ADH is appropriately suppressed: primary polydipsia, or low solute intake (beer potomania, "tea and toast"). Above 100 means ADH is active.
3. **Volume status and urine sodium.**

| Volume status | Examples | Urine Na |
|---|---|---|
| Hypovolemic | Vomiting, diarrhea (extrarenal); diuretics, salt wasting, adrenal insufficiency (renal) | Under 20 extrarenal; over 20 renal |
| Euvolemic | SIADH, hypothyroidism, glucocorticoid deficiency | Over 30 to 40 in SIADH |
| Hypervolemic | Heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome; advanced kidney disease | Under 20 in HF and cirrhosis; over 20 in kidney disease |

**SIADH** is a diagnosis of exclusion: hypotonic hyponatremia, inappropriately concentrated urine (over 100), euvolemia, urine sodium over 30 to 40, and normal thyroid, adrenal and kidney function with no diuretic. Causes: small cell lung cancer, CNS disease, pulmonary disease, and drugs (SSRIs, carbamazepine, cyclophosphamide).

### Treatment of hyponatremia

Severe symptoms (seizure, coma, vomiting with somnolence) call for a bolus of hypertonic (3%) saline, typically 100 to 150 mL over 10 to 20 minutes, repeated as needed for a rise of 4 to 6 mEq/L (a typical adult regimen for study; check current references). Chronic asymptomatic hyponatremia is treated by the cause: fluid restriction for SIADH, salt and loop diuretic or urea, or tolvaptan in selected cases; isotonic saline for hypovolemia.

**Correction limit.** Brain cells adapt to chronic hyponatremia by losing osmolytes. If sodium rises too fast, the brain shrinks, causing **osmotic demyelination syndrome** (central pontine myelinolysis): days later, dysarthria, dysphagia, quadriparesis and a locked-in state. Limit correction to no more than 8 mEq/L in 24 hours in high-risk patients (alcohol use, malnutrition, liver disease, hypokalemia, sodium of 105 or below) and no more than 10 to 12 in anyone. If the patient overcorrects (for example after volume repletion shuts off ADH and a water diuresis follows), relowering with desmopressin and dextrose water is an option.

The **Adrogue-Madias** estimate predicts the change in serum sodium per liter of infusate:

$$\Delta\text{Na} = \frac{\text{infusate Na} + \text{infusate K} - \text{serum Na}}{\text{TBW} + 1}.$$

Total body water (TBW) is about 0.6 of body weight in men and 0.5 in women (lower in the elderly). Isotonic saline has 154 mEq/L sodium; 3% saline has 513 mEq/L.

### Hypernatremia

Hypernatremia nearly always means water loss in a patient who cannot drink enough. Causes: insensible or GI losses, osmotic diuresis, and **diabetes insipidus (DI)**: central (ADH deficiency; head trauma, surgery, tumors) or nephrogenic (lithium, hypercalcemia, hypokalemia, V2 receptor mutations). Urine osmolality under 300 mOsm/kg with high urine volume in a hypernatremic patient suggests DI; a water deprivation test followed by desmopressin separates central (urine osmolality rises) from nephrogenic (it does not). Treat nephrogenic DI by removing the cause and using a thiazide, amiloride (lithium) or an NSAID.

The **free water deficit** is

$$\text{deficit (L)} = \text{TBW} \times \left(\frac{\text{Na}}{140} - 1\right).$$

Replace with oral water or dextrose 5% in water, and lower sodium at no more than about 10 to 12 mEq/L per day to avoid cerebral edema. Hypovolemic patients need isotonic fluid first.

@example
title: Working up hyponatremia
problem: A 66-year-old man with a lung mass has a serum sodium of 118 mEq/L. He looks euvolemic. Serum osmolality is 250 mOsm/kg, urine osmolality is 480 mOsm/kg and urine sodium is 55 mEq/L. His thyroid and adrenal tests are normal and he takes no diuretics. What is the diagnosis and the first-line approach?
step: Serum osmolality is low, so this is true hypotonic hyponatremia (not hyperglycemia or pseudohyponatremia).
step: Urine osmolality of 480 is well above 100, so ADH is active and polydipsia alone is excluded.
step: He is euvolemic with a urine sodium above 30; with normal thyroid, adrenal and kidney function and no diuretic this is SIADH, here probably from small cell lung cancer.
step: With mild or chronic symptoms, use fluid restriction and treat the cause; reserve hypertonic saline for severe neurologic symptoms, and limit correction to 8 mEq/L per 24 hours if risk factors are present.

@example
title: Free water deficit in a woman
problem: An 80 kg woman has a serum sodium of 152 mEq/L. Estimate her free water deficit using TBW = 0.5 of body weight.
step: TBW is $0.5 \times 80 = 40$ L.
step: Apply the formula: $40 \times (152/140 - 1) = 40 \times 0.0857$.
step: The deficit is about 3.4 L, to be replaced over 48 to 72 hours with oral water or dextrose 5% in water, in addition to ongoing losses.

@mistakes
- Correcting chronic hyponatremia quickly: too rapid a rise risks osmotic demyelination, which appears days later.
- Calling urine osmolality of 480 appropriate in a hyponatremic patient: with low serum osmolality, ADH should be off and urine below 100.
- Using the glucose correction in the wrong direction: hyperglycemia lowers measured sodium, so corrected sodium is higher than measured.
- Treating hypovolemic hyponatremia with fluid restriction instead of isotonic saline.

@practice self
prompt: A 58-year-old man with alcohol use disorder and poor nutrition is admitted with sodium 108 mEq/L and mild confusion; he is hypovolemic. After isotonic saline his urine output rises to 400 mL/h and sodium reaches 122 mEq/L within 20 hours. Explain the risk and write the steps you would take.
solution: The sodium rise is about 14 mEq/L in 20 hours, which exceeds the limit of 8 mEq/L per 24 hours for a high-risk patient (alcohol use, malnutrition). Volume repletion switched off ADH, causing a water diuresis and overcorrection, which risks osmotic demyelination. Stop saline, check sodium every 1 to 2 hours, replace urine losses with dextrose water as needed, and consider desmopressin to stop the water diuresis and relower sodium to stay within 8 mEq/L from the starting value, in consultation with a nephrologist. Counsel and monitor for dysarthria, dysphagia and weakness.

@quiz num
prompt: A 60 kg man (TBW 0.6 of body weight) has a serum sodium of 118 mEq/L. Using the Adrogue-Madias formula, by how much is his sodium predicted to rise, in mEq/L, after 1 L of 3% saline (513 mEq/L of sodium)? Round to one decimal place.
answer: 10.7
check: (513 - 118) / (0.6 * 60 + 1)
tol: 0.1
unit: mEq/L
hint: TBW is 36 L; the denominator is TBW plus 1.
hint: The numerator is infusate sodium minus serum sodium.
solution: $\Delta\text{Na} = (513 - 118)/(36 + 1) = 395/37 = 10.7$ mEq/L per liter. This is why only small boluses of hypertonic saline are given at a time.
