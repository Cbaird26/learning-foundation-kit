@article
The body makes about 15,000 mmol of CO$_2$ and 50 to 100 mEq of fixed acid every day, yet blood pH stays between 7.35 and 7.45. Three lines of defense do this: buffers (instant), the lungs (minutes to hours) and the kidneys (days).

### Henderson-Hasselbalch

The bicarbonate buffer system, $\text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_2\text{CO}_3 \rightleftharpoons \text{H}^+ + \text{HCO}_3^-$, is described by

$$\text{pH} = 6.1 + \log_{10}\frac{[\text{HCO}_3^-]}{0.03 \times P_{CO_2}}.$$

With $\text{HCO}_3^- = 24$ mEq/L and $P_{CO_2} = 40$ mmHg, the pH is 7.40. A practical version is $[\text{H}^+]\ (\text{nEq/L}) = 24 \times P_{CO_2}/[\text{HCO}_3^-]$, and 40 nEq/L corresponds to pH 7.40. The **lungs** control CO$_2$ (the denominator) and the **kidneys** control bicarbonate (the numerator). Other buffers are hemoglobin, phosphate, proteins and bone.

### The kidney's job

- **Reabsorb all filtered bicarbonate.** About 4,300 mEq (24 mEq/L $\times$ 180 L) are filtered each day; roughly 80 to 90% is reclaimed in the proximal tubule by Na$^+$/H$^+$ exchange and carbonic anhydrase, and the rest in the thick ascending limb and collecting duct.
- **Excrete the daily acid load** and **generate new bicarbonate** by secreting H$^+$ in the collecting duct (alpha-intercalated cells, via H$^+$-ATPase) buffered by phosphate (**titratable acid**) and by **ammonium** (NH$_4^+$) made from glutamine in the proximal tubule. Ammoniagenesis rises greatly in chronic acidosis and is the main adaptive response.
- Urine pH reaches about 4.5 to 5.0 at its lowest.

### Compensation rules

Each primary disorder has an expected compensatory change. If the measured value falls outside the expected range, a second disorder is present.

| Primary disorder | Expected compensation |
|---|---|
| Metabolic acidosis | **Winter's formula**: $P_{CO_2} = 1.5\,[\text{HCO}_3^-] + 8 \pm 2$ |
| Metabolic alkalosis | $P_{CO_2}$ rises about 0.7 mmHg per 1 mEq/L rise in HCO$_3^-$ |
| Acute respiratory acidosis | HCO$_3^-$ rises about 1 mEq/L per 10 mmHg rise in $P_{CO_2}$ |
| Chronic respiratory acidosis | HCO$_3^-$ rises about 3.5 mEq/L per 10 mmHg rise |
| Acute respiratory alkalosis | HCO$_3^-$ falls about 2 mEq/L per 10 mmHg fall |
| Chronic respiratory alkalosis | HCO$_3^-$ falls about 4 to 5 mEq/L per 10 mmHg fall |

Renal compensation takes 3 to 5 days, which is why chronic is distinguished from acute.

### Anion gap

$$\text{AG} = [\text{Na}^+] - ([\text{Cl}^-] + [\text{HCO}_3^-]).$$

The normal gap is about 8 to 12 mEq/L with modern ion-selective electrodes (older references say 12 $\pm$ 4); check your laboratory's range. It represents unmeasured anions, mostly albumin, so correct for hypoalbuminemia by adding **2.5 mEq/L for each 1 g/dL that albumin is below 4.0**. An elevated gap means added unmeasured acid anions (lactate, ketoacids, toxins, uremic anions); a normal gap acidosis has a rise in chloride matching the fall in bicarbonate (hyperchloremic).

### A stepwise approach

1. Is the pH acidemic (below 7.35) or alkalemic (above 7.45)?
2. Which is the primary process: low HCO$_3^-$ or high $P_{CO_2}$ in acidemia; the reverse in alkalemia?
3. Is the compensation appropriate (Winter's, etc.)?
4. Calculate the anion gap, corrected for albumin.
5. If the gap is high, compare the change in gap with the change in bicarbonate (week 7).

### Acid–base and potassium

Acidemia pushes potassium out of cells as H$^+$ enters. The effect is larger with mineral acid (hyperchloremic acidosis) than with organic acids such as lactate or ketoacids. Alkalemia has the opposite effect, and correction of acidosis can drop potassium abruptly, so potassium must be checked during treatment of DKA and with bicarbonate.

@example
title: Interpreting a blood gas with an anion gap
problem: A hypotensive patient has pH 7.24, HCO$_3^-$ 10 mEq/L, $P_{CO_2}$ 24 mmHg, sodium 140 and chloride 98 mEq/L. Interpret.
step: pH is below 7.35 and bicarbonate is low, so this is metabolic acidosis.
step: Winter's formula gives $1.5 \times 10 + 8 = 23 \pm 2$ mmHg, so the expected $P_{CO_2}$ is 21 to 25. The measured value of 24 is within range: appropriate respiratory compensation.
step: The anion gap is $140 - (98 + 10) = 32$ mEq/L, markedly elevated. In a hypotensive patient, lactic acidosis is the leading cause (other causes: ketoacids, toxic alcohols, uremia).

@example
title: Henderson-Hasselbalch at normal values
problem: Confirm that bicarbonate 24 mEq/L with $P_{CO_2}$ 40 mmHg gives a pH of about 7.40.
step: Dissolved CO$_2$ is $0.03 \times 40 = 1.2$ mmol/L.
step: The ratio is $24/1.2 = 20$, and $\log_{10} 20 = 1.30$.
step: $\text{pH} = 6.1 + 1.30 = 7.40$. A ratio of 20:1 defines normal pH.

@mistakes
- Using Winter's formula for a respiratory disorder or alkalosis; it applies to metabolic acidosis only.
- Forgetting to correct the anion gap for low albumin, which hides a true elevated gap.
- Treating a normal pH as normal: a mixed disorder (for example metabolic acidosis with respiratory alkalosis) can leave pH near 7.40.
- Calling all hyperkalemia during acidosis "true total body excess": much of it is a shift and can reverse with treatment.
