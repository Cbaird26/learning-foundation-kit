@article
Springs, pendulums, alternating current, sound waves and tides all repeat in time. The simplest model, **simple harmonic motion**, is a sinusoid in the time variable $t$:

$$x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \varphi) + D.$$

### Reading the parameters

- **Amplitude** $A > 0$: the largest displacement from the equilibrium level $D$.
- **Angular frequency** $\omega$ (radians per second): the angle $\omega t$ advances by $\omega$ every second.
- **Period** $T = \dfrac{2\pi}{\omega}$ (seconds per cycle) and **frequency** $f = \dfrac{1}{T} = \dfrac{\omega}{2\pi}$ (cycles per second, or hertz). So $\omega = 2\pi f$.
- **Phase** $\varphi$ (radians): it sets where in its cycle the motion is at $t = 0$. A maximum occurs where $\omega t + \varphi = 0$, that is at $t = -\varphi/\omega$; adding whole periods $2\pi/\omega$ gives the others, so for $\varphi > 0$ the first maximum after $t = 0$ is one period later. A positive $\varphi$ moves the graph to the **left**; a negative $\varphi$ delays it.

To fit data, take $A = \dfrac{\max - \min}{2}$ and $D = \dfrac{\max + \min}{2}$, measure $T$ from the time between successive peaks, and then find $\varphi$ from the time of one peak.

### Phasors: the complex view

Euler's formula lets us write a cosine as the real part of a rotating complex number:

$$A\cos(\omega t + \varphi) = \operatorname{Re}\left(Ae^{i(\omega t + \varphi)}\right) = \operatorname{Re}\left(Z\,e^{i\omega t}\right), \qquad Z = Ae^{i\varphi}.$$

The complex number $Z$ is the **phasor**. Its modulus is the amplitude and its argument is the phase: $|Z| = A$ and $\arg Z = \varphi$. Multiplying by $e^{i\omega t}$ spins $Z$ counterclockwise at $\omega$ rad/s, and the oscillation is the shadow of that uniform circular motion on the real axis. The unit circle, the complex plane and oscillation are one idea.

Varying the amplitude stretches the circle; varying the phase starts the point at a different angle. Neither changes the period.

### Adding sinusoids of the same frequency

Adding two oscillations with the same $\omega$ means adding their phasors, because taking the real part is linear: the sum is $\operatorname{Re}\left((Z_1 + Z_2)e^{i\omega t}\right)$. The result is again a sinusoid of the same frequency, with amplitude $|Z_1 + Z_2|$. This is why amplitudes do **not** simply add. In real form,

$$a\cos\omega t + b\sin\omega t = R\cos(\omega t - \delta), \qquad R = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}, \quad \delta = \operatorname{atan2}(b, a).$$

```python
import numpy as np
A, w, phi = 2.0, 3.0, np.pi/3
t = np.linspace(0, 2*np.pi/w, 7)          # one period
x = A*np.cos(w*t + phi)
z = (A*np.exp(1j*phi)) * np.exp(1j*w*t)   # phasor times rotation
print(np.allclose(x, z.real))             # True
```

### Solving for times

"When does the displacement equal $c$?" is a trigonometric equation. Solve $\cos(\omega t + \varphi) = (c - D)/A$ for the angle, as in week 6, and then solve for $t$. Each period contains two such times when $|c - D| < A$, one when $c - D = \pm A$, and none when $|c - D| > A$.

@example
title: Reading a spring model
problem: A mass on a spring has displacement $x(t) = 0.05\cos\left(8\pi t - \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$ metres, with $t$ in seconds. Find the amplitude, angular frequency, frequency, period and phase, and the time of the first maximum.
step: Compare with $A\cos(\omega t + \varphi)$: the amplitude is $A = 0.05$ m, the angular frequency is $\omega = 8\pi$ rad/s and the phase is $\varphi = -\frac{\pi}{2}$.
step: The period is $T = \frac{2\pi}{8\pi} = \frac{1}{4}$ s, so the frequency is $f = \frac{1}{T} = 4$ Hz.
step: The first maximum occurs when $8\pi t - \frac{\pi}{2} = 0$, so $t = \frac{1}{16}$ s $= 0.0625$ s. The negative phase delays the peak.
step: Check: a phase of $-\frac{\pi}{2}$ is a quarter of a cycle of delay, and a quarter of the period is $\frac{1}{16}$ s. Also, $x(0) = 0.05\cos\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = 0$, so the mass starts at equilibrium.

@example
title: Combining two sinusoids with phasors
problem: Write $3\cos\omega t + 4\sin\omega t$ as a single cosine $R\cos(\omega t + \varphi)$.
step: Write the sine as a cosine: $4\sin\omega t = 4\cos\left(\omega t - \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$.
step: The phasors are $Z_1 = 3$ and $Z_2 = 4e^{-i\pi/2} = -4i$. Their sum is $Z = 3 - 4i$.
step: The modulus is $R = |Z| = 5$ and the argument is $\varphi = \operatorname{atan2}(-4, 3) \approx -0.927$ rad, in Quadrant IV.
step: So $3\cos\omega t + 4\sin\omega t = 5\cos(\omega t - 0.927)$. Check at $t = 0$: the left side is $3$ and the right side is $5\cos(0.927) \approx 5(0.6) = 3$.

@mistakes
- Confusing $\omega$ (radians per second) with $f$ (hertz). They differ by a factor of $2\pi$: a $50$ Hz supply has $\omega = 100\pi \approx 314$ rad/s.
- Reading the time shift as $\varphi$ instead of $-\varphi/\omega$. In $A\cos(\omega t + \varphi)$ a peak is at $t = -\varphi/\omega$, and the others are whole periods away.
- Adding amplitudes of sinusoids that are out of phase. The amplitude of the sum is $|Z_1 + Z_2|$, not $A_1 + A_2$.
- Taking the amplitude to be the maximum value when there is a midline $D \ne 0$. It is $\frac{\max - \min}{2}$.

@practice self
prompt: (a) Show that $\operatorname{Re}\left(Ze^{i\omega t}\right) = A\cos(\omega t + \varphi)$ when $Z = Ae^{i\varphi}$. (b) The signal $x(t) = 2\cos\left(3t + \frac{\pi}{3}\right)$ has a phasor $Z$. Give $Z$, then give the phasor and the real formula for the signal obtained by tripling the amplitude and decreasing the phase by $\frac{\pi}{2}$. (c) Describe in words how each change moves the graph: the height of the peaks, the period and the times of the peaks.
hint: For part (a), multiply $Ae^{i\varphi}$ by $e^{i\omega t}$ using the rule for adding exponents, then use Euler's formula and take the real part.
hint: For part (c), the peaks of $A\cos(\omega t + \varphi)$ occur when $\omega t + \varphi = 2\pi k$. Compare the peak times before and after the phase change.
solution: (a) $Ze^{i\omega t} = Ae^{i\varphi}e^{i\omega t} = Ae^{i(\omega t + \varphi)} = A\cos(\omega t + \varphi) + iA\sin(\omega t + \varphi)$, whose real part is $A\cos(\omega t + \varphi)$. (b) $Z = 2e^{i\pi/3}$. Tripling the amplitude gives modulus $6$, and decreasing the phase gives $\frac{\pi}{3} - \frac{\pi}{2} = -\frac{\pi}{6}$, so the new phasor is $Z' = 6e^{-i\pi/6}$ and the new signal is $x'(t) = 6\cos\left(3t - \frac{\pi}{6}\right)$. (c) The peaks are now at height $6$ instead of $2$ (the graph is stretched vertically by $3$), but the period $\frac{2\pi}{3}$ is unchanged because $\omega$ did not change. The old peaks were at $3t + \frac{\pi}{3} = 2\pi k$, that is $t = -\frac{\pi}{9} + \frac{2\pi k}{3}$. The new peaks are at $3t - \frac{\pi}{6} = 2\pi k$, that is $t = \frac{\pi}{18} + \frac{2\pi k}{3}$. Each peak is later by $\frac{\pi}{6}$, so the graph slides right by $\frac{\pi/2}{3} = \frac{\pi}{6}$.
rubric: Part (a) combines the exponentials, applies Euler's formula and identifies the real part correctly.
rubric: Part (b) gives $Z = 2e^{i\pi/3}$, the new phasor $6e^{-i\pi/6}$ and the signal $6\cos\left(3t - \frac{\pi}{6}\right)$.
rubric: Part (c) says that the amplitude change scales the peak height only and leaves the period alone, and that the phase decrease delays the graph by $\frac{\pi}{6}$ without changing its shape.
