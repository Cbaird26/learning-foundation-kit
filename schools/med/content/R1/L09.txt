@article
Good antibiotic use rests on four questions: **What is the syndrome and likely source? Which organisms cause it? What do local resistance and patient factors (allergies, kidney function, recent antibiotics) allow? What is the narrowest effective regimen and the shortest effective duration?** Always send cultures before antibiotics when this does not delay therapy in a sick patient, and reassess at 48 to 72 hours.

### Spectrum in practice

- **Gram-positive including MRSA:** vancomycin, linezolid, daptomycin (not for pneumonia, inactivated by surfactant). For methicillin-susceptible *S. aureus* (MSSA), cefazolin or an antistaphylococcal penicillin (nafcillin, oxacillin) is better than vancomycin.
- **Streptococci:** penicillin, ampicillin, ceftriaxone.
- **Gram-negative rods:** ceftriaxone for community organisms; for *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*: cefepime, piperacillin-tazobactam, meropenem, ciprofloxacin. ESBL producers need a carbapenem.
- **Anaerobes:** metronidazole, ampicillin-sulbactam, piperacillin-tazobactam, carbapenems.
- **Atypicals:** macrolides, doxycycline, fluoroquinolones.

### Syndromes

**Skin and soft tissue:** non-purulent cellulitis is usually streptococcal (cephalexin or cefazolin); purulent infections or abscesses need drainage and MRSA coverage if systemic signs are present. **Urinary tract:** uncomplicated cystitis in women: nitrofurantoin for 5 days or trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for 3 days; pyelonephritis needs a longer course (a fluoroquinolone, or ceftriaxone then oral therapy; about 7 days for many). Do not treat asymptomatic bacteriuria except in pregnancy or before urologic procedures with mucosal bleeding.

**S. aureus bacteremia** is never treated as a contaminant. Obtain repeat blood cultures every 24 to 48 hours until clear, remove or drain the source, perform echocardiography (transesophageal if high risk), and give IV therapy (cefazolin or nafcillin for MSSA; vancomycin or daptomycin for MRSA) for at least 14 days after the first negative culture, longer for endocarditis, metastatic foci or persistent bacteremia. Consultation with infectious disease lowers mortality. **Infective endocarditis** is suspected with bacteremia, new murmur, emboli or fever with risk factors (injection drug use, prosthetic valve); use the modified Duke criteria.

**Bacterial meningitis:** fever, headache, neck stiffness and altered mental status. Do not delay antibiotics. Give **dexamethasone** with or before the first antibiotic dose, and empiric **vancomycin plus ceftriaxone**, adding **ampicillin** for *Listeria* if the patient is older than 50 or immunocompromised. Obtain blood cultures first; a CT before lumbar puncture is needed only for specific features (focal deficit, papilledema, severe altered mentation, immunocompromise, seizure), and antibiotics are not delayed for it.

***C. difficile*:** diarrhea after antibiotics. Stop the inciting antibiotic when possible. Treat with **oral fidaxomicin** (preferred) or oral vancomycin for 10 days; severe or fulminant disease (hypotension, ileus, megacolon) needs high-dose oral vancomycin with intravenous metronidazole. Never give antimotility agents in active disease.

### Dosing and allergies

Dose by weight, kidney function and site. For vancomycin, a **loading dose of 20-25 mg/kg** (actual body weight) is commonly given in serious infection, followed by dosing guided by area under the curve (target AUC 400-600 mg·h/L) or levels. Infusion rate in mL/h equals volume divided by time in hours.

About 10% of patients report penicillin allergy but fewer than 1 in 10 of them are truly allergic. Ask what happened, when, and the treatment required. A remote benign rash or gastrointestinal upset is low risk and these patients can often receive penicillins after evaluation or direct oral challenge. Cross-reactivity between penicillins and later-generation cephalosporins with dissimilar side chains is low (about 1-2% or less). True IgE-mediated reactions (anaphylaxis, angioedema) or severe cutaneous reactions (Stevens-Johnson syndrome) mean avoidance.

### Stewardship: the 48-hour time-out

At 48-72 hours ask: Is infection confirmed? Do cultures allow narrowing (vancomycin and piperacillin-tazobactam to cefazolin for MSSA)? Can the patient switch from IV to oral therapy with good bioavailability (fluoroquinolones, doxycycline, linezolid, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, metronidazole)? What is the stop date? Shorter courses (5-7 days) are often as effective for pneumonia, cellulitis and pyelonephritis.

**Writing a consult question:** state the syndrome, what you have done, and a specific question ("Please advise on duration and need for TEE for MSSA bacteremia"), with allergies, renal function and cultures.

@example
title: Vancomycin loading dose
problem: A 70 kg patient with sepsis and suspected MRSA is to receive a vancomycin loading dose of 25 mg/kg. How many milligrams?
step: Dose equals dose per kilogram times weight.
step: $25 \times 70 = 1750$ mg.
step: Give over an appropriate time (not faster than about 1 g/h) and then use AUC- or level-guided maintenance.

@example
title: De-escalation at 48 hours
problem: A patient with cellulitis and bacteremia has been on vancomycin and piperacillin-tazobactam. Blood cultures at 48 hours grow methicillin-susceptible S. aureus. How should therapy change?
step: The organism and susceptibility are known, so broad coverage is no longer needed.
step: Narrow to cefazolin (or nafcillin), which is more effective than vancomycin for MSSA.
step: Repeat blood cultures, search for a source and endocarditis, and plan at least 14 days from the first negative culture.

@mistakes
- Treating a single positive blood culture for S. aureus as a contaminant.
- Continuing vancomycin for MSSA after susceptibility results return.
- Delaying meningitis treatment for CT or lumbar puncture, or omitting dexamethasone and ampicillin when indicated.
- Giving loperamide for C. difficile colitis, or oral vancomycin only when the disease is fulminant.
