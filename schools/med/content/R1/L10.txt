@article
**Delirium** is an acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness with an additional change in cognition (memory, orientation, language, perception) that is caused by a medical condition, medication or withdrawal. It is a medical emergency: it signals brain dysfunction, raises mortality and risk of long-term cognitive decline, and is under-recognized, especially the **hypoactive** form (quiet, drowsy, withdrawn). **Hyperactive** delirium (agitation, hallucinations) is obvious; many patients are mixed.

### Recognition

The **Confusion Assessment Method (CAM)** is positive when there is (1) acute onset with a fluctuating course **and** (2) inattention (for example, cannot recite the months backward), **and** either (3) disorganized thinking or (4) altered level of consciousness. In contrast, **dementia** is chronic and progressive, with preserved alertness until late; but dementia is the biggest risk factor for delirium, and the two coexist. Other risk factors: older age, sensory impairment, prior stroke, polypharmacy, surgery, infection, hospitalization, and sleep deprivation. Depression and primary psychiatric illness do not usually produce inattention and fluctuating consciousness.

### The work-up

Start with life threats and reversible causes. A reliable mnemonic is **DELIRIUMS**:

| Letter | Causes |
|---|---|
| **D** | Drugs (anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, opioids, sedatives, steroids, antihistamines) |
| **E** | Electrolytes and endocrine (sodium, calcium, glucose, thyroid, adrenal) |
| **L** | Lack of drugs: withdrawal from alcohol, benzodiazepines, opioids |
| **I** | Infection (urinary, pneumonia, meningitis, sepsis) |
| **R** | Reduced sensory input (lost glasses or hearing aids), restraints |
| **I** | Intracranial (stroke, hemorrhage, seizure, mass) |
| **U** | Urinary retention and fecal impaction |
| **M** | Myocardial (infarction, heart failure), metabolic (uremia, hepatic encephalopathy, hypoxia, hypercapnia) |
| **S** | Sleep deprivation, psychosocial |

1. **Vital signs, oxygen saturation and a fingerstick glucose immediately.** Hypoglycemia is rapidly fatal and easily treated.
2. **Medication review** against the Beers list: stop or reduce offending drugs.
3. **Examination**: temperature, volume status, focal neurologic deficits, meningismus, asterixis, nystagmus and ophthalmoplegia, bladder distension, rectal examination, skin and signs of trauma.
4. **Labs**: BMP including sodium and BUN, calcium with albumin, glucose, CBC, liver tests, TSH when suggested, B12, arterial or venous blood gas, urinalysis and culture if urinary symptoms or no other cause, drug screen as appropriate. Ammonia is not a reliable test for hepatic encephalopathy.
5. **ECG and chest radiograph.**
6. **Head CT** for new focal deficits, trauma, anticoagulation with a fall, or unexplained cause; **lumbar puncture** if fever with headache or meningismus; **EEG** for suspected nonconvulsive seizure.

Corrected calcium (for hypoalbuminemia) and serum osmolality are useful:

$$\text{corrected Ca} = \text{Ca} + 0.8 \times (4.0 - \text{albumin}), \qquad \text{Osm} = 2\,\text{Na} + \frac{\text{glucose}}{18} + \frac{\text{BUN}}{2.8}.$$

Hypercalcemia (confusion, constipation, polyuria) is commonly from malignancy or primary hyperparathyroidism. In an alcohol-dependent patient with confusion, ataxia or ophthalmoplegia (**Wernicke encephalopathy**), give **intravenous thiamine before glucose**. Alcohol withdrawal (tremor, tachycardia, then seizures at about 6-48 hours or delirium tremens at about 48-96 hours after the last drink) is managed with symptom-triggered benzodiazepines (CIWA-Ar), thiamine and supportive care.

### Management

Treat the cause. **Nonpharmacologic measures** are first-line for everyone: reorientation, glasses and hearing aids, daytime light and sleep protection at night, early mobilization, hydration, avoiding catheters, restraints and unnecessary night-time vital signs, and family presence. **Antipsychotics** (for example haloperidol 0.5-1 mg, typical dose for study) are reserved for severe agitation that endangers the patient or others; they do not shorten delirium. **Avoid benzodiazepines** except for alcohol or sedative-hypnotic withdrawal, where they are the treatment. Educate the family and document delirium as a diagnosis, because it affects later care.

@example
title: Corrected calcium
problem: A confused patient with cancer has total calcium of 9.6 mg/dL and albumin of 2.4 g/dL. What is the corrected calcium and what does it mean?
step: Albumin deficit is $4.0 - 2.4 = 1.6$ g/dL.
step: Correction is $0.8 \times 1.6 = 1.28$ mg/dL.
step: Corrected calcium is $9.6 + 1.28 \approx 10.9$ mg/dL, which is elevated (the reference range is about 8.5-10.5).
step: The apparently normal total calcium hides hypercalcemia; measure ionized calcium if in doubt and look for malignancy.

@example
title: Applying the CAM
problem: An 84-year-old woman, 2 days after hip surgery, was alert yesterday. Today the nurse reports she is sleepy and drifting in and out, cannot count backward from 20 and gives answers unrelated to the questions. Is the CAM positive?
step: Acute change with a fluctuating course: present.
step: Inattention: present (cannot count backward).
step: Disorganized thinking: present.
step: All required features are present, so the CAM is positive for delirium (hypoactive subtype). Start the work-up.

@mistakes
- Attributing acute confusion in an older patient to dementia or "sundowning" without a work-up.
- Ordering only a urinalysis and treating asymptomatic bacteriuria while missing medication, hypoxia or hypoglycemia as the cause.
- Giving benzodiazepines or diphenhydramine for sleep or agitation, which worsens delirium.
- Giving glucose before thiamine to a patient at risk for Wernicke encephalopathy.
