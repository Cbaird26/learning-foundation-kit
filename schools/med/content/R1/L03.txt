@article
Dyspnea is a symptom with a short list of dangerous causes: **cardiac** (heart failure, ischemia, arrhythmia, valvular disease, tamponade), **pulmonary** (COPD or asthma exacerbation, pneumonia, pneumothorax, interstitial disease), **vascular** (pulmonary embolism, pulmonary hypertension), and **other** (anemia, metabolic acidosis, anxiety, neuromuscular weakness, obesity). History separates them: orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea and edema favor heart failure; wheeze and a smoking history favor COPD; pleuritic pain, immobilization or cancer favor pulmonary embolism; fever and sputum favor pneumonia.

### Pathophysiology of heart failure

Heart failure is a clinical syndrome in which the heart cannot supply tissues with adequate output at normal filling pressures. In **systolic dysfunction (HFrEF)** contractility falls and the ventricle dilates. In **diastolic dysfunction (HFpEF)** the ventricle is stiff and fills poorly despite normal ejection. Neurohormonal activation (sympathetic nervous system, renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system) first compensates but then drives remodeling, sodium retention and progression. This is why the drugs that block those systems save lives.

**Ejection fraction (EF)** is stroke volume divided by end-diastolic volume:

$$\text{EF} = \frac{\text{EDV} - \text{ESV}}{\text{EDV}} \times 100\%.$$

HFrEF: EF 40% or less. HFmrEF: 41-49%. HFpEF: 50% or more.

### Recognition

Findings: jugular venous distention, an S3 gallop, crackles, displaced apex beat, hepatomegaly and peripheral edema. **BNP** above 100 pg/mL or NT-proBNP above age-adjusted cutoffs (about 450, 900 and 1800 pg/mL for ages under 50, 50-75 and over 75) supports the diagnosis. A normal BNP makes acute heart failure unlikely; obesity lowers natriuretic peptides, while kidney failure and age raise them. Sacubitril increases BNP but not NT-proBNP. Order ECG, chest radiograph (cardiomegaly, cephalization, Kerley B lines, effusions), troponin, electrolytes and creatinine, and **echocardiography** to measure EF.

Look for the precipitant: ischemia, arrhythmia such as atrial fibrillation, uncontrolled hypertension, medication or sodium non-adherence, infection, NSAIDs, thyroid disease or valve disease.

### The A-a gradient

For hypoxemia, the alveolar-arterial gradient separates hypoventilation (normal gradient) from V/Q mismatch, shunt or diffusion defect (high gradient). On room air at sea level:

$$P_A\text{O}_2 = 0.21 \times (760 - 47) - \frac{P_a\text{CO}_2}{0.8}, \qquad \text{A-a} = P_A\text{O}_2 - P_a\text{O}_2.$$

The normal gradient is about $\frac{\text{age}}{4} + 4$ mm Hg. Pulmonary edema widens it.

### Acute decompensated heart failure

Assess the profile: **warm or cold** (perfusion) and **wet or dry** (congestion). Most patients are **warm and wet**: treat with **intravenous loop diuretics**. A typical approach is an IV dose of 1 to 2.5 times the home oral daily dose, given in divided doses (oral furosemide is about 50% bioavailable). Assess response in hours (urine output, spot urine sodium) and escalate if inadequate; aim for steady net negative balance and daily weights, and replace potassium and magnesium. Add noninvasive ventilation for respiratory distress and nitrates for pulmonary edema with hypertension. **Cold and wet** patients with hypoperfusion may need inotropes and ICU care. Continue GDMT unless there is shock, symptomatic hypotension, severe kidney injury or hyperkalemia.

### Guideline-directed medical therapy for HFrEF

Four pillars reduce mortality and hospitalization: an **ARNI** (sacubitril-valsartan; ACE inhibitor or ARB if not tolerated), an **evidence-based beta-blocker** (carvedilol, metoprolol succinate, bisoprolol), a **mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist** (spironolactone, eplerenone), and an **SGLT2 inhibitor** (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin). Avoid non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers, NSAIDs and thiazolidinediones in HFrEF. Digoxin lowers hospitalization but not mortality. SGLT2 inhibitors also improve outcomes in HFpEF. ICD placement is considered with EF 35% or less despite 3 months of therapy.

@example
title: Calculating ejection fraction
problem: Echocardiography shows an end-diastolic volume of 130 mL and an end-systolic volume of 91 mL. What is the ejection fraction and which category applies?
step: Stroke volume is $\text{EDV} - \text{ESV} = 130 - 91 = 39$ mL.
step: $\text{EF} = 39 / 130 = 0.30$, or 30%.
step: EF of 40% or less is HFrEF.

@example
title: A-a gradient in a hypoxemic patient
problem: A 40-year-old patient breathing room air at sea level has $P_a\text{CO}_2 = 50$ mm Hg and $P_a\text{O}_2 = 60$ mm Hg. Calculate the A-a gradient and interpret it.
step: $P_A\text{O}_2 = 0.21 \times 713 - 50/0.8 = 149.7 - 62.5 = 87.2$ mm Hg.
step: A-a gradient $= 87.2 - 60 = 27.2$ mm Hg.
step: Normal for age 40 is about $40/4 + 4 = 14$ mm Hg. The gradient is elevated, so hypoventilation alone does not explain the hypoxemia; there is also V/Q mismatch or shunt.

@mistakes
- Treating a high BNP as diagnostic of heart failure; it also rises with kidney disease, PE, atrial fibrillation and age.
- Using a non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker for rate or blood pressure control in HFrEF.
- Stopping a beta-blocker in every decompensation; stop it only for shock or symptomatic bradycardia or low output.
- Giving too little loop diuretic IV, forgetting that the IV route is about twice as potent as the oral dose.
