### Approach to anemia

Anemia is a hemoglobin below about 13.5 g/dL in men and 12 g/dL in women. Start with the **mean corpuscular volume (MCV)** and the **reticulocyte count**. The corrected reticulocyte count is

$$\text{corrected retic} = \text{retic \%} \times \frac{\text{Hct}}{45}.$$

A low corrected count (below about 2%) means underproduction; a high count means blood loss or hemolysis.

**Microcytic (MCV below 80 fL):** iron deficiency, thalassemia trait, anemia of chronic disease (often normocytic), sideroblastic anemia, lead poisoning. In **iron deficiency**, ferritin is low (below about 30 ng/mL), transferrin saturation is below 20%, and total iron-binding capacity (TIBC) is high. In **anemia of chronic disease**, ferritin is normal or high, TIBC low. In thalassemia, the red cell count is high for the degree of anemia and iron studies are normal. In an adult man or postmenopausal woman, iron deficiency means GI blood loss until proven otherwise: endoscopy and colonoscopy.

**Normocytic:** acute blood loss, hemolysis, chronic kidney disease (low erythropoietin), anemia of chronic disease, marrow failure.

**Macrocytic (MCV above 100 fL):** vitamin B12 or folate deficiency, alcohol, hypothyroidism, liver disease, myelodysplastic syndrome, drugs (methotrexate, hydroxyurea). B12 deficiency (pernicious anemia, ileal disease, metformin, strict veganism) raises methylmalonic acid and homocysteine and causes neurologic findings (paresthesias, posterior column loss); folate deficiency raises only homocysteine. Giving folate alone to a B12-deficient patient can worsen neurologic disease.

**Hemolysis:** elevated LDH and indirect bilirubin, low haptoglobin, high reticulocytes, schistocytes (microangiopathic) or spherocytes. A positive direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test indicates autoimmune hemolysis. Transfuse red cells for hemoglobin below 7 g/dL, below 8 g/dL with coronary disease or orthopedic surgery, or for symptomatic instability.

### Venous thromboembolism

Virchow's triad: stasis, hypercoagulability, endothelial injury. Risk factors: surgery, immobilization, cancer, pregnancy, estrogen, inherited thrombophilia, prior VTE. **DVT** presents with unilateral leg swelling, pain and warmth; diagnose by compression ultrasound. **PE** presents with dyspnea, pleuritic pain, tachycardia, hypoxemia, and sometimes syncope, right heart strain on ECG or echo, and elevated troponin or BNP.

**Wells score for PE**: clinical signs of DVT (3), PE the most likely diagnosis (3), heart rate above 100 (1.5), immobilization or surgery in the past 4 weeks (1.5), previous VTE (1.5), hemoptysis (1), cancer (1). A score of 4 or less is "PE unlikely": check **D-dimer** (negative excludes); above 4 is "PE likely": go directly to **CT pulmonary angiography** (V/Q scan if contrast or radiation is a concern, such as renal failure or pregnancy). D-dimer is not useful when the pretest probability is high, or in hospitalized or postoperative patients.

**Treatment** (typical adult regimens for study): for most patients a direct oral anticoagulant (apixaban or rivaroxaban, with a loading phase) or low molecular weight heparin (enoxaparin 1 mg/kg every 12 hours) transitioning to oral therapy. Unfractionated heparin is preferred when thrombolysis may be needed, in severe renal failure or high bleeding risk because it is reversible. **Hemodynamically unstable (massive) PE** needs systemic thrombolysis (alteplase) unless contraindicated, or catheter or surgical embolectomy. An IVC filter is considered only when anticoagulation is contraindicated. Duration is at least 3 months; provoked VTE with a transient risk factor is treated for 3 months, while unprovoked or cancer-associated VTE usually warrants extended therapy. Antiphospholipid syndrome is treated with warfarin. For **heparin-induced thrombocytopenia** (platelets fall by more than 50% after 5-10 days of heparin, with thrombosis), stop all heparin and use a non-heparin anticoagulant such as argatroban.

### Variceal bleed in the first hour: the checklist

Airway and access; octreotide; ceftriaxone; restrictive transfusion (hemoglobin 7-8 g/dL); endoscopy with banding within 12 hours; hold beta-blockers; plan TIPS if refractory.

@example
title: Wells score
problem: A 35-year-old woman 10 days after knee surgery has a heart rate of 108/min and dyspnea. She has no signs of DVT and no hemoptysis or cancer. PE is the most likely diagnosis in the physician's judgment. Calculate the Wells score and choose the test.
step: Surgery in the past 4 weeks: 1.5. Heart rate above 100: 1.5. PE most likely: 3.
step: Total $1.5 + 1.5 + 3 = 6$, so PE is likely (above 4).
step: Go directly to CT pulmonary angiography; a D-dimer would not safely exclude PE.

@example
title: Interpreting iron studies
problem: A 62-year-old man has hemoglobin 9.5 g/dL, MCV 72 fL, ferritin 8 ng/mL, transferrin saturation 6%, TIBC elevated. What is the diagnosis and the next step?
step: Microcytic anemia with low ferritin and high TIBC indicates iron deficiency.
step: In an older man this suggests chronic GI blood loss (colon cancer, ulcer, angiodysplasia).
step: Replace iron and evaluate with upper endoscopy and colonoscopy.

@mistakes
- Using D-dimer in a patient who is already "PE likely," or in the hospitalized patient where it is nearly always positive.
- Replacing iron in an older man with iron deficiency without looking for a GI source.
- Treating macrocytic anemia with folate without checking B12.
- Withholding anticoagulation because of a creatinine elevation instead of switching to unfractionated heparin or adjusting the agent.

@practice self
prompt: A 55-year-old man with alcohol-related cirrhosis presents with vomiting a large volume of blood. Blood pressure is 90/55 mm Hg and heart rate 125/min. List, in order, the actions you take in the first hour and give the reason for each.
hint: Prioritize airway and access, then drugs, endoscopy and rescue, and give a reason each time.
solution: 1) Airway and breathing: intubate if altered or actively vomiting blood. 2) Two large-bore IVs, type and cross, labs. 3) Resuscitate with limited crystalloid and transfuse to a hemoglobin of 7-8 g/dL only, because over-transfusion raises portal pressure. 4) Octreotide to reduce portal inflow. 5) Ceftriaxone to prevent infection and reduce mortality. 6) IV PPI. 7) Endoscopy within 12 hours with band ligation. 8) Hold beta-blockers and diuretics. 9) If bleeding persists, balloon tamponade as a bridge and TIPS. 10) Lactulose and monitoring for encephalopathy and withdrawal.
rubric: Order of priorities follows airway, access, resuscitation, drug therapy and endoscopy.
rubric: A reason is given for each step.
rubric: Restrictive transfusion, octreotide, antibiotics and banding are included.
rubric: Rescue options are named.
