@article
**Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP)** is infection of the lung parenchyma acquired outside hospital. The commonest bacterial cause is **Streptococcus pneumoniae**; others include *Haemophilus influenzae*, atypical organisms (*Mycoplasma*, *Chlamydophila*, *Legionella*) and respiratory viruses. Presentation is fever, cough, sputum, pleuritic pain and dyspnea with crackles or bronchial breath sounds; the chest radiograph shows a new infiltrate. Check oxygen saturation, blood counts, chemistry and a chest radiograph; in hospitalized patients obtain blood cultures and sputum culture in severe disease, and Legionella and pneumococcal urinary antigens when severe.

### Site of care: CURB-65

One point each for **C**onfusion, **U**rea (BUN above 19 mg/dL), **R**espiratory rate 30 or more, **B**lood pressure (systolic under 90 or diastolic 60 or less), and age **65** or older. Scores of 0-1 usually permit outpatient care, 2 suggests hospital admission, and 3 or more suggests severe pneumonia requiring consideration of intensive care. Clinical judgment and the IDSA/ATS severe-pneumonia criteria also guide the decision.

### Empiric antibiotics (IDSA/ATS approach, typical adult regimens for study)

- **Outpatient, healthy:** amoxicillin, or doxycycline, or a macrolide where local pneumococcal macrolide resistance is below 25%.
- **Outpatient with comorbidities** (chronic heart, lung, liver or kidney disease, diabetes, alcoholism, cancer, asplenia): a beta-lactam plus a macrolide or doxycycline, or a respiratory fluoroquinolone (levofloxacin, moxifloxacin).
- **Hospitalized, non-severe:** a beta-lactam (ceftriaxone, ampicillin-sulbactam) plus a macrolide, or a respiratory fluoroquinolone alone.
- **Severe (ICU):** a beta-lactam plus a macrolide, or a beta-lactam plus a fluoroquinolone.
- Add MRSA coverage (vancomycin or linezolid) or antipseudomonal coverage only with risk factors such as prior isolation of the organism or recent hospitalization with IV antibiotics. Aspiration alone does not require anaerobic coverage unless abscess or empyema is suspected.

Treat at least 5 days and until clinically stable (afebrile 48 to 72 hours, stable vital signs, improving oxygenation). Corticosteroids are suggested for severe CAP in current guidance; vaccinate against influenza and pneumococcus. Failure to improve in 48-72 hours should prompt review for complications (parapneumonic effusion, empyema, abscess), resistant organisms and alternative diagnoses such as pulmonary embolism or heart failure.

### Sepsis

**Sepsis** is life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, operationalized as an acute rise of 2 or more points in the SOFA score. **qSOFA** (respiratory rate 22 or more, altered mentation, systolic pressure 100 mm Hg or less; 2 or more positive) is a bedside prompt, not a diagnostic criterion. **Septic shock** is sepsis with vasopressor requirement to keep mean arterial pressure (MAP) at or above 65 mm Hg and lactate above 2 mmol/L despite adequate fluid resuscitation.

First-hour actions (Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2021): measure lactate; obtain blood cultures before antibiotics if this causes no substantial delay; give broad-spectrum antibiotics **within 1 hour** for shock or a high probability of sepsis; suggest **at least 30 mL/kg of crystalloid** within the first 3 hours for sepsis-induced hypoperfusion or septic shock (balanced solutions are preferred over saline; this is a weak recommendation based on low-quality evidence, so individualize the volume and reassess after each bolus; older bundles also used a lactate of 4 mmol/L or more as a trigger); use **norepinephrine** as the first-line vasopressor, aiming for MAP 65 or more; add vasopressin if needed and consider hydrocortisone for persistent vasopressor need; identify and control the source (drain abscess, remove infected device, relieve obstruction). Reassess perfusion with capillary refill, urine output and lactate trend.

@example
title: Calculating CURB-65
problem: A 70-year-old man is brought in with new confusion, a respiratory rate of 26/min, BUN 14 mg/dL and blood pressure 118/70 mm Hg. Calculate CURB-65 and suggest the site of care.
step: Confusion: 1 point. Respiratory rate 26 is below 30: 0. BUN 14 is not above 19: 0. Blood pressure is normal: 0. Age 70 is 65 or older: 1.
step: Total is 2.
step: A score of 2 suggests hospital admission. Because he is confused, also assess oxygenation and for severe criteria.

@example
title: Choosing antibiotics
problem: A 52-year-old woman with COPD and type 2 diabetes has CAP and is admitted to the ward with stable blood pressure and oxygen saturation 90% on room air. She has no prior MRSA or Pseudomonas isolation and no recent IV antibiotics. Select empiric therapy.
step: She is hospitalized without severe criteria and without risk factors for resistant organisms.
step: The standard options are a beta-lactam such as ceftriaxone plus a macrolide, or a respiratory fluoroquinolone alone.
step: MRSA or antipseudomonal agents are not needed because the risk factors are absent.

@mistakes
- Delaying antibiotics for imaging or cultures in a patient with septic shock.
- Adding vancomycin and piperacillin-tazobactam to every pneumonia without risk factors for MRSA or Pseudomonas.
- Treating qSOFA as a diagnostic test for sepsis or ignoring a normal qSOFA in a patient who looks unwell.
- Using vasopressors before any volume in hypotension from hypovolemia, or giving large volumes to a patient with severe heart failure without monitoring response.

Home medications: furosemide 40 mg orally daily, carvedilol 12.5 mg twice daily, lisinopril 10 mg daily, spironolactone 25 mg daily. hint: Decide warm or wet first, then choose the diuretic route and dose from the home dose.
solution: Assessment: acute decompensated HFrEF, warm and wet profile, with unknown precipitant (check ECG for ischemia or atrial fibrillation, troponin, infection, adherence, sodium intake, NSAIDs). Plan: admit to telemetry. Oxygen to keep saturation at or above 92%, BiPAP only if distress. IV loop diuretic, for example furosemide 40-100 mg IV per day in divided doses, such as 40 mg IV twice daily (1 to 2.5 times the home oral daily dose; study values, verify before clinical use), reassess urine output and symptoms in 2-6 hours and double the dose if inadequate.
