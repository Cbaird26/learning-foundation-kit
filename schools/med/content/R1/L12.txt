@article
Discharge is the highest-risk handoff in a hospital stay. About one in five Medicare patients is readmitted within 30 days, and medication discrepancies, pending results, and unclear follow-up are common reasons. Start discharge planning on admission.

### Readiness

A patient is ready when the acute problem is stable or improving, oral medications and diet are tolerated, oxygen and monitoring needs can be met at the destination, the patient can mobilize safely, and follow-up is arranged. Choose the destination with the team: home, home with services (home health, nursing, therapy), skilled nursing facility, inpatient rehabilitation, or hospice for patients whose goals are comfort. (Medicare Part A coverage of skilled nursing generally requires a qualifying inpatient stay of 3 midnights; check the current rule.) Reassess goals of care and code status.

### Medication reconciliation at discharge

Compare **every** pre-admission medication with the inpatient list and the new plan. Assign each: **continue**, **change** (dose or frequency), **new**, **stop** or **hold until** a stated condition, and give the reason. Typical high-risk issues: new anticoagulants with interacting antiplatelets or NSAIDs, insulin and sulfonylureas with changed intake, held antihypertensives or diuretics after kidney injury (state when to resume), antibiotics (stop date), steroid tapers, opioids with a bowel regimen, duplicate therapies, and medications that cannot be afforded. Verify the pharmacy has them and use teach-back so the patient states the plan in their own words.

### Readmission risk: LACE index

| Component | Points |
|---|---|
| **L**ength of stay | under 1 day 0; 1 day 1; 2 days 2; 3 days 3; 4-6 days 4; 7-13 days 5; 14 or more days 7 |
| **A**cuity (emergent admission) | 3 |
| **C**omorbidity (Charlson score) | 0 = 0; 1 = 1; 2 = 2; 3 = 3; 4 or more = 5 |
| **E**mergency department visits in the previous 6 months | 1 point each, up to 4 |

A score of 10 or more is high risk. High-risk patients benefit from early follow-up, a call within 48-72 hours, and pharmacist involvement. For heart failure, a visit within 7 days of discharge is recommended.

### The discharge summary

It is written for the next clinician (primary care, skilled nursing facility) and should be completed at or within days of discharge. Contents:

1. Dates, attending, and discharge destination.
2. **Principal and secondary diagnoses**, procedures, consults.
3. Brief presentation, then a **problem-based hospital course** with key results.
4. **Condition at discharge** and functional and cognitive status; code status.
5. **Medication reconciliation**: new, changed, stopped, continued, with reasons.
6. **Pending results** and who is responsible for them.
7. **Follow-up** appointments and tests with timing, and specific tasks for the primary care physician ("repeat BMP in 1 week; decide on restarting lisinopril").
8. Patient instructions: diet, activity, symptoms that should prompt return, who to call.

Make the first lines carry the message: diagnosis, key changes, and the single most important follow-up task. Avoid copying large chunks of the chart.

@example
title: LACE index
problem: A patient was admitted emergently and stayed 5 days. His Charlson comorbidity score is 2 and he had 1 ED visit in the previous 6 months. Calculate the LACE index.
step: Length of stay of 5 days is in the 4-6 range: 4 points.
step: Emergent admission: 3 points. Charlson score 2: 2 points. One ED visit: 1 point.
step: Total $4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 10$, which is high risk.

@example
title: Reconciling a medication list
problem: A patient admitted with AKI took lisinopril, ibuprofen, metformin and atorvastatin at home. Creatinine returned from 2.4 to 1.3 mg/dL (baseline 1.1). How would you reconcile at discharge?
step: Atorvastatin: continue (no change).
step: Ibuprofen: stop, because NSAIDs contributed to kidney injury; suggest acetaminophen.
step: Lisinopril and metformin: hold or resume depending on renal recovery. With creatinine near baseline you may resume metformin if eGFR is adequate and lisinopril after a repeat BMP in about a week.
step: Document each decision, the reason and the follow-up test in the summary.

@mistakes
- Discharging without telling the primary care physician about pending results or held medications.
- Listing a medication list without reasons, so the next clinician cannot tell whether a missing drug was stopped on purpose.
- Failing to arrange follow-up within 7 days for heart failure or other high-risk patients.
- Omitting code status, functional status or stop dates for antibiotics and steroids.

prompt: A 74-year-old woman is being discharged on hospital day 4 after community-acquired pneumonia complicated by acute kidney injury. Hospital course: treated with ceftriaxone and azithromycin, now improved and on room air. Creatinine peaked at 2.1 mg/dL and is now 1.2 mg/dL (baseline 1.0). Blood cultures negative. Lisinopril and hydrochlorothiazide were held for hypotension and AKI; her blood pressure is now 138/80 mm Hg. Home medications: lisinopril 20 mg daily, hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg daily, metformin 1000 mg twice daily, atorvastatin 40 mg daily, ibuprofen 400 mg as needed for knee pain. She is to complete amoxicillin-clavulanate for 3 more days. Write the medication reconciliation and the follow-up section of a discharge summary.
hint: Give every drug one label and one reason, then state tests, dates and who acts.
solution: Medications. NEW: amoxicillin-clavulanate 875/125 mg twice daily for 3 more days (stop date given). CONTINUE: atorvastatin 40 mg daily. HELD, resume after repeat BMP in 1 week if creatinine is at baseline and blood pressure is above goal: lisinopril 20 mg daily and hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg daily (restart one at a time, lisinopril first). METFORMIN 1000 mg twice daily: resume if eGFR is 45 or higher on the follow-up BMP (current eGFR adequate; reason for holding was AKI). STOP: ibuprofen (NSAID contributed to AKI); use acetaminophen instead for knee pain. Follow-up: primary care within 7 days with BMP; blood pressure check and resumption of antihypertensives; chest imaging only if symptoms persist or lung cancer risk is high (routine follow-up radiograph is not recommended after clinical recovery); pneumococcal and influenza vaccination status review; return precautions for fever, worsening dyspnea, decreased urine output or confusion. Pending results: none. Teach-back done with the patient.
rubric: Each home medication is labeled new, continued, held, changed or stopped with a reason.
rubric: Conditions and timing for restarting held drugs are specific (test, date, order of restarting).
rubric: NSAID avoidance after AKI is explicit, with an alternative analgesic.
rubric: Follow-up includes a visit with labs, return precautions and a statement about pending results.
