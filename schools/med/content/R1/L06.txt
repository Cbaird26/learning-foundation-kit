@article
Hyperglycemia in hospitalized patients is common and harmful: it impairs immune function, wound healing and outcomes. Hypoglycemia is also harmful. The standard target for most hospitalized patients, critically ill or not, is a **glucose of 140-180 mg/dL** (7.8-10.0 mmol/L). Start insulin when glucose is persistently 180 mg/dL or higher.

### Home drugs on admission

Hold **metformin** (risk of lactic acidosis with AKI, hypoperfusion or contrast), **SGLT2 inhibitors** (risk of euglycemic ketoacidosis, volume depletion) and usually **sulfonylureas** (hypoglycemia when intake is poor). Insulin is the preferred agent in hospital because it is titratable. GLP-1 receptor agonists and DPP-4 inhibitors may be continued in selected patients. Check **HbA1c** if none within 3 months.

### Basal-bolus insulin

Sliding-scale (correction-only) insulin alone is discouraged: it chases hyperglycemia after the fact. Use three parts:

- **Basal** insulin (glargine, detemir or degludec) once daily.
- **Prandial (bolus)** rapid-acting insulin (lispro, aspart) with meals, skipped if the patient is not eating.
- **Correction** rapid-acting insulin added to the meal dose based on glucose.

A common starting **total daily dose (TDD)** for an insulin-naive patient is **0.4 units/kg/day** (about 0.3 for older patients, those with renal impairment or poor intake; up to 0.5 for severe hyperglycemia or obesity). Give half as basal and half as prandial, divided among three meals. For a patient already on insulin, start from the home dose, reduced if intake is poor. All doses in this lesson are typical adult study values; verify against current references and local protocols before any clinical use.

The **correction factor (CF)**, the glucose drop from 1 unit of rapid-acting insulin, is estimated by the **1800 rule**:

$$\text{CF} = \frac{1800}{\text{TDD}} \;\text{mg/dL per unit}, \qquad \text{correction dose} = \frac{\text{glucose} - \text{target}}{\text{CF}}.$$

Check glucose before meals and at bedtime (every 4-6 hours if NPO). Adjust daily: if fasting glucose is above target, raise basal by 10-20%; if any glucose is below 100 mg/dL, reduce the relevant dose by 10-20%; if below 70, reduce more and review the cause. **Glucocorticoid-induced hyperglycemia** peaks in the afternoon and evening; NPH insulin timed with the steroid dose is a common approach. Hypoglycemia is **level 1 below 70** and **level 2 below 54 mg/dL**: treat with 15-20 g of fast-acting oral carbohydrate if awake, repeat in 15 minutes; if NPO, give intravenous dextrose or intramuscular glucagon.

### Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)

DKA is insulin deficiency with ketone production: glucose of 200 mg/dL or more in the 2024 international consensus criteria (classic teaching used 250; glucose can be lower, especially with SGLT2 inhibitors, and a known history of diabetes counts even then), pH below 7.30, bicarbonate below 18 mEq/L, an elevated anion gap and ketonemia. Precipitants: infection, missed insulin, myocardial infarction, pancreatitis, new-onset type 1 diabetes. Patients have profound volume and total-body potassium depletion even if serum potassium is high.

Management: (1) **Fluids**: isotonic crystalloid, 1-1.5 L in the first hour, then adjust to hydration. (2) **Check potassium before insulin**: if below 3.3 mEq/L (3.5 in the 2024 consensus), hold insulin and replace potassium; from that level up to about 5.0 to 5.2, add 20-30 mEq potassium to each liter; if higher, start insulin without potassium and recheck every 2 hours. (3) **Regular insulin** infusion at 0.1 units/kg/h (with or without an initial bolus of 0.1 units/kg). (4) When glucose falls to about 200-250 mg/dL, add dextrose to fluids and continue insulin until the gap closes. (5) Transition to subcutaneous basal-bolus insulin with 1-2 hours of overlap before stopping the infusion. Bicarbonate is given only for severe acidosis (pH below 6.9 or 7.0). Treat the precipitant.

**HHS** (glucose above 600 mg/dL, effective osmolality above 320 mOsm/kg, or above 300 in the 2024 consensus, and no significant ketosis) needs more aggressive fluid replacement and a lower insulin need. Effective osmolality is $2 \times \text{Na} + \text{glucose}/18$.

@example
title: Basal and prandial doses
problem: An insulin-naive 90 kg patient with hyperglycemia is eating three meals daily. Calculate a starting TDD using 0.4 units/kg, then the basal and per-meal doses.
step: $\text{TDD} = 0.4 \times 90 = 36$ units/day.
step: Basal is half: $36/2 = 18$ units once daily.
step: Prandial total is $18$ units, split among three meals: $18/3 = 6$ units with each meal.
step: Correction doses are added to the meal dose based on pre-meal glucose.

@example
title: DKA insulin infusion rate
problem: A 62 kg patient with DKA has potassium 4.4 mEq/L. Calculate the insulin infusion rate at 0.1 units/kg/h and state the potassium decision.
step: Potassium 4.4 mEq/L is above the insulin-hold threshold (3.3 mEq/L, or 3.5 in the 2024 consensus) and below about 5.0, so insulin can start; add potassium to the fluids.
step: Rate $= 0.1 \times 62 = 6.2$ units/h.
step: Continue until the anion gap has closed, adding dextrose when glucose is about 200-250 mg/dL.

@mistakes
- Using only a sliding scale in a patient who needs basal insulin, or stopping basal insulin in a patient with type 1 diabetes (precipitates DKA).
- Starting insulin in DKA with potassium below 3.3 mEq/L (3.5 in the 2024 consensus), which can precipitate dangerous hypokalemia.
- Stopping the insulin infusion without overlapping subcutaneous insulin, or when glucose falls to normal before the anion gap closes.
- Continuing metformin and SGLT2 inhibitors in acutely ill patients.

@practice self
prompt: A 68-year-old man weighing 82 kg is admitted with pneumonia. He has type 2 diabetes, takes only metformin at home, has an HbA1c of 9.4% and an estimated GFR of 55 mL/min. He is eating three meals a day. Pre-meal glucose values are 280-320 mg/dL. Write an inpatient insulin plan with doses, monitoring and adjustment rules.
hint: Start from weight, find TDD, split basal and prandial, then add correction and rules.
solution: Hold metformin during acute illness. Target glucose 140-180 mg/dL. TDD at 0.4 units/kg is about 33 units (use 0.3 per kg, about 25 units, if intake is poor or kidney function worsens). Give half as basal, for example glargine 16 units once daily, and half as prandial rapid-acting insulin, about 5 units with each meal (study values), held if he eats less than half the meal. Add correction dose based on the 1800 rule (CF about 55 mg/dL per unit). Check glucose before meals and at bedtime. Each day raise basal by about 10-20% if fasting glucose is above 180; reduce the dose by 10-20% for any glucose below 100 and more for below 70. Order a hypoglycemia protocol. Plan for discharge: reassess HbA1c, kidney function, and decide on resuming metformin, adding or titrating other drugs, and diabetes education.
