@article
**Acute kidney injury (AKI)** by KDIGO criteria is any of: a rise in serum creatinine of 0.3 mg/dL or more within 48 hours; a rise to at least 1.5 times baseline within 7 days; or urine output below 0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 hours. Creatinine lags true filtration, so a normal value does not exclude early injury.

### Three categories

**Prerenal** (reduced perfusion): volume depletion, heart failure, cirrhosis, sepsis, and drugs that alter glomerular hemodynamics (NSAIDs constrict the afferent arteriole; ACE inhibitors and ARBs dilate the efferent arteriole). Kidney tissue is intact, so it reverses when perfusion is restored. **Intrinsic**: acute tubular necrosis (ATN; ischemia or toxins such as contrast, aminoglycosides, rhabdomyolysis), acute interstitial nephritis (drugs: penicillins, proton pump inhibitors, NSAIDs, with rash, fever, eosinophilia), glomerulonephritis, and vascular causes. **Postrenal**: obstruction (prostatic enlargement, stones, tumor, neurogenic bladder).

### Evaluation

History of fluid loss, new drugs and urinary symptoms; examine volume status and palpate or scan the bladder. Order BMP, urinalysis with microscopy, urine electrolytes and creatinine, and **renal ultrasonography** for hydronephrosis. Bladder scan is quick and may lead to a urinary catheter.

| Finding | Prerenal | ATN |
|---|---|---|
| BUN : creatinine | above 20 | about 10-15 |
| FeNa | below 1% | above 2% |
| Urine sediment | bland, hyaline casts | muddy brown granular casts |
| Urine sodium | below 20 mEq/L | above 40 mEq/L |

White blood cell casts suggest interstitial nephritis (or pyelonephritis); red blood cell casts suggest glomerulonephritis. Diuretics make FeNa unreliable; use fractional excretion of urea (below 35% suggests prerenal).

$$\text{FeNa} = \frac{U_{Na} \times P_{Cr}}{P_{Na} \times U_{Cr}} \times 100\%.$$

**Management:** treat the cause; restore volume with isotonic fluids if hypovolemic (but not in overload); stop nephrotoxins (NSAIDs, contrast, aminoglycosides, ACE inhibitors during the acute phase) and dose-adjust drugs; relieve obstruction; monitor intake, output and electrolytes. Indications for urgent dialysis: refractory hyperkalemia, refractory volume overload, severe metabolic acidosis, uremic complications (pericarditis, encephalopathy, bleeding) and certain toxic ingestions (mnemonic AEIOU).

### Hyperkalemia

Causes: AKI and chronic kidney disease, drugs (ACE inhibitors, ARBs, spironolactone, trimethoprim, potassium supplements), acidosis, tissue breakdown. ECG changes progress from peaked T waves to PR lengthening, QRS widening and sine wave. Treat in order of urgency:

1. **Intravenous calcium** (gluconate or chloride) to stabilize the myocardium when the ECG is abnormal or potassium is very high. It does not lower potassium.
2. **Shift** potassium into cells: regular insulin with dextrose, and inhaled albuterol; sodium bicarbonate if acidotic.
3. **Remove** potassium: loop diuretics, potassium binders (sodium zirconium cyclosilicate, patiromer), or dialysis.

### Hyponatremia

Classify by serum osmolality, volume status and urine studies. Hypotonic hyponatremia with euvolemia and urine osmolality above 100 mOsm/kg with urine sodium above 30 mEq/L suggests SIADH (treat the cause, fluid restriction, salt tablets, or tolvaptan in selected cases). Hypovolemic hyponatremia is treated with isotonic saline. Severe symptoms (seizures, coma) need 3% hypertonic saline in small boluses. The key safety rule: **do not correct too fast**. For most patients limit the rise to 10-12 mEq/L per 24 hours, and to 8 mEq/L per 24 hours when there is high risk of **osmotic demyelination** (alcoholism, malnutrition, advanced liver disease, hypokalemia, sodium at or below 105 mEq/L); many clinicians aim for 6-8 in everyone. Overcorrection occurs when the cause resolves (volume repletion, stopping thiazide), so check sodium every 2 to 4 hours. In hyperglycemia, corrected sodium rises by about 1.6 mEq/L per 100 mg/dL of glucose above 100 (some use 2.4).

### Anion gap

$$\text{AG} = \text{Na}^+ - (\text{Cl}^- + \text{HCO}_3^-),$$ normal about 8-12 mEq/L. A raised gap suggests lactic acidosis, ketoacidosis, uremia, or toxic alcohols or salicylates.

@example
title: FeNa in an oliguric patient
problem: A hypotensive patient with new oliguria has plasma creatinine 2.0 mg/dL, plasma sodium 138 mEq/L, urine sodium 10 mEq/L and urine creatinine 80 mg/dL. Calculate FeNa and categorize the AKI.
step: Use $\text{FeNa} = \frac{U_{Na} \times P_{Cr}}{P_{Na} \times U_{Cr}} \times 100$.
step: $\frac{10 \times 2.0}{138 \times 80} \times 100 = \frac{20}{11040} \times 100 \approx 0.18\%$.
step: FeNa below 1% indicates avid sodium retention: prerenal azotemia. Treat the cause and restore perfusion.

@example
title: Sodium corrected for hyperglycemia
problem: A patient's measured sodium is 128 mEq/L with glucose 600 mg/dL. What is the corrected sodium using 1.6 mEq/L per 100 mg/dL above 100?
step: Excess glucose is $600 - 100 = 500$ mg/dL.
step: Correction is $1.6 \times 5 = 8$ mEq/L.
step: Corrected sodium is $128 + 8 = 136$ mEq/L, so the sodium is actually near normal and the low value reflects water shifted by glucose.

@mistakes
- Using FeNa in a patient on loop diuretics rather than fractional excretion of urea.
- Giving calcium for hyperkalemia and stopping, forgetting that it does not lower potassium.
- Overcorrecting chronic hyponatremia, leading to osmotic demyelination (dysarthria, quadriparesis, locked-in state).
- Giving contrast or NSAIDs to a patient with AKI without weighing the harm.
