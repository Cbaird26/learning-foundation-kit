@article
An admission is a short, structured story that ends in a plan. The patient arrives with a symptom; you turn it into a **problem list**, each problem with its own assessment and orders. The note is not a copy of the chart. It is your reasoning, written so that the next clinician can follow and challenge it.

### The history

Start with the **chief complaint** in the patient's own words, then the **history of present illness (HPI)**: onset, location, duration, character, aggravating and relieving factors, radiation, timing and severity (OLDCARTS), plus pertinent positives and negatives that separate your leading diagnoses. Then past medical and surgical history, **medications**, allergies, family history, social history (tobacco, alcohol, drugs, occupation, living situation, who helps at home) and a review of systems.

**Medication reconciliation** is a core admission task. Use at least two sources (patient, pharmacy fill history, caregiver, prior discharge list) and for each home drug decide: continue, hold, adjust or stop. Hold drugs that are dangerous in the current illness, for example ACE inhibitors, diuretics and NSAIDs in volume depletion or kidney injury, and metformin with acute kidney injury or contrast exposure. Do not stop agents whose withdrawal is harmful, such as beta-blockers, antiepileptics, and chronic glucocorticoids (adrenal suppression). Clarify allergy labels: nausea after a drug is an intolerance, not an allergy; hives, angioedema or anaphylaxis are allergies.

### The examination and the one-liner

Record vital signs including oxygen saturation, and examine systematically with emphasis on the organ system of the complaint. Then summarize in one sentence: age, relevant history, the acute problem and its key findings. Example: "A 66-year-old man with hypertension and a 40 pack-year smoking history presents with 3 days of productive cough and fever with hypoxemia." The one-liner forces you to decide what matters.

### Assessment and plan

Write a prioritized, problem-based plan: the most dangerous or most acute problem first, each with a differential, the evidence for and against it, and what you will do next (tests, treatment, consults, monitoring).

### Admission orders

A reliable checklist is **ADC VAAN DIMLS**:

| Letter | Order |
|---|---|
| **A** | Admit to (service, unit, level of care: floor, telemetry, step-down, ICU) |
| **D** | Diagnosis |
| **C** | Condition (stable, guarded, critical) and **code status** |
| **V** | Vital signs and monitoring frequency, parameters for calling the physician |
| **A** | Allergies |
| **A** | Activity (bed rest, fall precautions, ambulate with assistance) |
| **N** | Nursing: I/O, daily weights, isolation, glucose checks |
| **D** | Diet |
| **I** | IV fluids |
| **M** | Medications (including **VTE prophylaxis**, held and continued home drugs, as-needed drugs) |
| **L** | Labs |
| **S** | Studies, consults, special instructions |

Always confirm **code status** with the patient early. Unless contraindicated, hospitalized medical patients need pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis (for example, subcutaneous heparin or enoxaparin); use mechanical prophylaxis if bleeding risk is high.

### Two bedside calculations

**Cockcroft-Gault creatinine clearance** estimates renal function for drug dosing:

$$\text{CrCl} = \frac{(140 - \text{age}) \times \text{weight (kg)}}{72 \times \text{serum creatinine (mg/dL)}} \quad (\times\,0.85 \text{ in women}).$$

**Maintenance fluids (4-2-1 rule)**, in mL/h: 4 mL/kg for the first 10 kg, 2 mL/kg for the next 10 kg, 1 mL/kg for each kilogram above 20. For a patient who can drink, oral intake is preferred over IV fluids.

@example
title: Renal dosing in an older woman
problem: An 80-year-old woman weighing 60 kg has a serum creatinine of 1.2 mg/dL. Estimate her creatinine clearance with the Cockcroft-Gault equation.
step: Write the equation for a woman: $\text{CrCl} = \frac{(140 - \text{age}) \times \text{weight}}{72 \times \text{Cr}} \times 0.85$.
step: Substitute: $\frac{(140 - 80) \times 60}{72 \times 1.2} \times 0.85 = \frac{3600}{86.4} \times 0.85$.
step: Compute: $41.7 \times 0.85 \approx 35$ mL/min.
step: Interpretation: despite a creatinine near the upper reference limit, her clearance is only about 35 mL/min, so renally cleared drugs need dose review.

@example
title: Maintenance fluid rate
problem: A 60 kg patient who is strictly nil per os needs maintenance IV fluids. Use the 4-2-1 rule to find the hourly rate.
step: First 10 kg: $4 \times 10 = 40$ mL/h.
step: Next 10 kg: $2 \times 10 = 20$ mL/h.
step: Remaining $60 - 20 = 40$ kg at 1 mL/kg: $40$ mL/h.
step: Total $40 + 20 + 40 = 100$ mL/h.

@mistakes
- Copying the home medication list into the orders without deciding to continue or hold each drug, so a nephrotoxic or hypotensive drug continues during acute illness.
- Forgetting VTE prophylaxis and code status in the admission orders.
- Using the serum creatinine alone to judge kidney function in a small or older patient instead of estimating clearance.
- Listing a long differential without prioritizing or stating what would separate the diagnoses.

Costovertebral angle tenderness is present. Urinalysis shows pyuria and bacteria. Write a one-line presentation and a prioritized problem list with one planned action for each problem.
hint: Start with the most dangerous problem and give each problem its own next step.
solution: One-liner: A 58-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes and hypertension presents with 2 days of fever, dysuria and flank pain with tachycardia, consistent with acute pyelonephritis. Problem 1: Acute pyelonephritis with early sepsis physiology (SIRS criteria met) - blood and urine cultures, lactate, start empiric intravenous antibiotics, assess for obstruction with imaging if not improving or if stones suspected. Problem 2: Type 2 diabetes - check glucose, hold metformin while acutely ill, use inpatient insulin if glucose is persistently high. Problem 3: Hypertension - hold ACE inhibitor or diuretic if blood pressure falls or creatinine rises; monitor. Problem 4: Prophylaxis and logistics - VTE prophylaxis, code status discussion, monitoring of vital signs.
rubric: The one-liner includes age, key history, acute problem and the leading diagnosis.
rubric: The most acute problem (infection with sepsis risk) is listed first.
rubric: Each problem has a concrete next step rather than only a diagnosis.
rubric: Diabetes medication and VTE prophylaxis are addressed.
