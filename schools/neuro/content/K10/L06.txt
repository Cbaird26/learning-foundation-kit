@article
Some jobs are big. A big job is easier when we **break it into small steps**.

Think about cleaning your room. That is a big job. But you can split it up:

1. Put the toys in the box.
2. Put the clothes in the drawer.
3. Make the bed.

Each small job is easy. Do them all, and the big job is done.

### Drawing a house

Say you want to draw a house. Break it up! A house is a square with a triangle on top. The square has 4 lines. The triangle has 3 lines. So the house has 4 plus 3, which is 7 lines.

### Giving steps a name

Sometimes a group of steps is used more than once. We can give the group a name. Then we use the name instead of writing the steps again.

```text
Name HOP means: ↑ →

Program:
HOP
HOP
```

The robot starts on A1. The first HOP takes it up to A2 and right to B2. The second HOP takes it up to B3 and right to C3. The program ends on C3.

Computer programmers do this all the time. They name small pieces and put the pieces together to do big jobs.

### Debugging is easier with small steps

When a program is split into small steps, a bug is easier to find. You test each small step by itself.

Grown-up helper: pick a big job at home, such as setting the table. Ask your child to say the small steps before starting.

@example
title: Breaking up a big job
problem: How can you break the job "make a card for a friend" into small steps?
step: Think about what must happen first. You need paper, so the first step is to fold a sheet of paper in half.
step: Next, draw a picture on the front, and write a message inside.
step: Last, write your friend's name on the card.
answer: Fold the paper, draw on the front, write a message inside, write your friend's name.

@mistakes
- Making the "small" steps still too big, like "clean the room". Keep splitting until each step is easy.
- Forgetting a step. Walk through your list, doing each step in your head.
- Using a name for steps without saying what the name means.

@practice text
prompt: In this program, HOP means → ↑. The robot starts on A1. Which square does it end on? Type the square name.

```text
Name HOP means: → ↑

Program:
HOP
HOP
→
```

```text
4 . . . .
3 . . . .
2 . . . .
1 R . . .
  A B C D
```
answer: D3
accept: d3
check: run("d={'R':(1,0),'L':(-1,0),'U':(0,1),'D':(0,-1)}\nx,y=0,1\nfor m in 'RU' + 'RU' + 'R':\n    x+=d[m][0]\n    y+=d[m][1]\nprint('ABCD'[x]+str(y))\n")
hint: Each HOP is one → and one ↑. Do the first HOP, then the second, then the last →.
solution: The first HOP takes the robot to B2. The second HOP takes it to C3. The last → takes it to D3.

@practice self
prompt: Here are three sets of instructions. Each has one bug. Find the bug in each set, and fix it.
Set 1, washing hands: dry your hands, turn on the water, rub soap on your hands, rinse.
Set 2, the robot starts on A1 and should end on C3: → → ↑ ↑ ↑.
Set 3, you want to clap 4 times: Repeat 3 times: clap.
hint: Take it one step at a time, and ask your grown-up to check each part with you.
solution: Set 1 is in the wrong order. A good order is: turn on the water, rub soap on your hands, rinse, dry your hands. Set 2 goes one square too far up and ends on C4. Take away one ↑. Set 3 claps only 3 times. Change it to Repeat 4 times: clap.
rubric: The child found the wrong order in set 1 and wrote a better order.
rubric: The child found that set 2 has one ↑ too many and fixed it.
rubric: The child found that the loop in set 3 has the wrong number and changed it to 4.
