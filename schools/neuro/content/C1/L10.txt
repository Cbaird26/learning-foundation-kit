@article
A **gene** has alternative versions called **alleles**. An individual's **genotype** is its allele combination; its **phenotype** is the observable trait. A **homozygote** has two identical alleles, a **heterozygote** two different ones; a **dominant** allele determines the phenotype in the heterozygote.

### Mendel's laws
1. **Law of segregation:** the two alleles of a gene separate during gamete formation (homologs separate in meiosis I), so each gamete carries one allele.
2. **Law of independent assortment:** alleles of genes on different chromosomes assort independently. A dihybrid cross $AaBb \times AaBb$ gives a $9:3:3:1$ phenotype ratio.

A **testcross** crosses an individual of dominant phenotype with a homozygous recessive to reveal its genotype.

### Probability rules
The **product rule**: the probability of independent events together is the product of their probabilities. The **sum rule**: the probability of either of two mutually exclusive outcomes is the sum. For a monohybrid cross $Aa \times Aa$: $P(aa) = 1/4$. For two carrier parents of an autosomal recessive disease, an unaffected child has probability $\tfrac{2/4}{3/4} = \tfrac{2}{3}$ of being a carrier (the $AA$ outcome is $1/4$, $Aa$ is $1/2$). The probability of exactly $k$ affected among $n$ children with risk $p$ is $\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$.

### Extensions
**Incomplete dominance** (heterozygote intermediate), **codominance** (both alleles expressed; ABO blood groups $I^A$, $I^B$ codominant, $i$ recessive), **multiple alleles**, **pleiotropy** (one gene, many effects, as in Marfan syndrome), **epistasis** (one gene masks another) and **polygenic** traits (many genes, continuous variation).

### Sex linkage
Males have one X, so recessive X-linked alleles show in males ("hemizygous"). **X-linked recessive** pedigrees (hemophilia A, Duchenne muscular dystrophy, red-green color blindness) show: more affected males; no male-to-male transmission; affected males have carrier daughters; carrier mothers pass the allele to half their sons (affected) and half their daughters (carriers).

### Linked genes
Genes close together on the same chromosome do not assort independently; they are **linked**. Crossing over in prophase I can separate them. In a testcross of a dihybrid with parental alleles $AB / ab$, offspring with parental combinations ($AB$, $ab$) are more common than **recombinants** ($Ab$, $aB$). The **recombination frequency** is
$$\text{RF} = \frac{\text{recombinant offspring}}{\text{total offspring}}.$$
One **map unit** (centimorgan) equals 1% recombination, so RF $\times\, 100$ gives distance in map units. RF approaches 50% for unlinked genes and never exceeds it.

### Chromosomal abnormalities in inheritance
Nondisjunction (week 9) changes chromosome number: a 47,XXY male (Klinefelter) arises if both X and Y passed together in meiosis I of the father, or two Xs passed in either meiotic division of the mother; if homologs are the problem it is meiosis I, and if identical sister chromatids it is meiosis II. Structural changes (deletions, duplications, inversions, translocations) also alter inheritance.

@example
title: Mapping two linked genes
problem: A testcross $AaBb \times aabb$ gives 380 $AB$, 360 $ab$, 140 $Ab$ and 120 $aB$ offspring. Are the genes linked, and how far apart are they?
step: Total offspring: $380 + 360 + 140 + 120 = 1000$. Under independent assortment each class would be about 250.
step: Parental classes ($AB$, $ab$) are much more common than recombinants, so the genes are linked.
step: Recombinants: $140 + 120 = 260$, so RF $= 260/1000 = 0.26$, or 26 map units.
@example
title: Carrier risk after an affected sibling
problem: Two unaffected parents have a child with cystic fibrosis. What is the probability that a phenotypically normal sibling is a carrier?
step: Both parents must be carriers ($Aa \times Aa$). The offspring probabilities are $AA = 1/4$, $Aa = 1/2$ and $aa = 1/4$.
step: The sibling is unaffected, so remove $aa$ from the sample space: $AA$ and $Aa$ make $3/4$.
step: $P(\text{carrier} \mid \text{unaffected}) = (1/2)/(3/4) = 2/3$.
@mistakes
- Reporting $1/4$ for the carrier risk of an unaffected sibling; the sample space excludes the affected class, so it is $2/3$.
- Treating recombinants as the most frequent class; for linked genes parental combinations are more common.
- Saying X-linked recessive disease passes from father to son; sons receive the father's Y.
- Multiplying probabilities for mutually exclusive events instead of adding.

@practice num
prompt: A woman who carries one hemophilia A allele (X-linked recessive) has children with an unaffected man. What is the probability that a given child is an affected son?
answer: 1/4
check: (1 / 2) * (1 / 2)
hint: Consider the sex of the child and the X inherited separately.
hint: Half of the children are sons, and half of the sons receive the mutant X.
solution: $P(\text{son}) = 1/2$ and $P(\text{mutant X}) = 1/2$, so $P(\text{affected son}) = 1/4$. Daughters are never affected because they receive the father's normal X.

answer: 17
check: (90 + 80) / (420 + 410 + 90 + 80) * 100
tol: 1%
unit: map units
hint: Recombinants are the classes that differ from both parental combinations.
hint: Divide the recombinants by all offspring and multiply by $100$.
solution: Recombinants: $90 + 80 = 170$ out of $1000$. RF $= 0.17$, so 17 map units.
@practice self
prompt: A boy has Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY). Explain two different nondisjunction events that could produce this karyotype and state in which parent and meiotic division each occurs.
hint: He receives two X chromosomes and a Y chromosome. Decide which parent could have supplied each combination.
solution: (1) Paternal meiosis I: the X and Y homologs fail to separate, so a sperm carries both X and Y; fertilization of a normal egg (X) gives XXY. (2) Maternal meiosis I or II: an egg carries two X chromosomes. If the two X homologs fail to separate in meiosis I, or the sister X chromatids fail to separate in meiosis II, the egg is XX; fertilization by a Y-bearing sperm gives XXY.
rubric: Names one paternal event and one maternal event.
rubric: Correctly states that the paternal case is meiosis I, since both X and Y homologs are in the sperm.
rubric: Distinguishes meiosis I (homologs fail to separate) from meiosis II (sister chromatids fail to separate).
@quiz num
prompt: A testcross of a dihybrid fly produces 460 parental type 1, 440 parental type 2, 55 recombinant type 1 and 45 recombinant type 2 offspring. What is the map distance between the genes in map units?
answer: 10
check: (55 + 45) / (460 + 440 + 55 + 45) * 100
tol: 1%
unit: map units
hint: Add the two recombinant classes and divide by the total.
hint: Total offspring is $1000$.
solution: Recombinants are $55 + 45 = 100$ out of $1000$, so RF $= 0.10$ and the genes are 10 map units apart.
@quiz num
prompt: Two carriers of an autosomal recessive disorder plan to have three children. What is the probability that exactly one child is affected? Give an exact fraction.
answer: 27/64
check: comb(3, 1) * (1 / 4) * (3 / 4) ** 2
hint: Use the binomial formula with $p = 1/4$ for each child.
hint: There are $3$ ways to choose which child is affected.
solution: $\binom{3}{1}(1/4)(3/4)^2 = 3 \times 9/64 = 27/64$.
