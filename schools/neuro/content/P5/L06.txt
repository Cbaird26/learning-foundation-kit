@article
A research group scans 40 participants and tests every voxel for a difference between two conditions. Of 60,000 voxels, a few hundred pass a threshold. The group then takes the average signal in those voxels and reports that the difference is large and significant, with a correlation to a behavioral score of $r = .8$. The result looks impressive. It is also nearly guaranteed, even if the brain contained nothing but noise. The reason is **circular analysis**, also called **double dipping**: using the same data to select what to test and to test it.

### The logic of circularity

Kriegeskorte and colleagues (2009) described how selection on the data followed by an analysis of the selected data that is not independent of the selection criterion biases the results. Vul and colleagues (2009) made a related point about unusually high correlations between brain measures and personality or behavior in fMRI studies: a correlation computed only in voxels chosen because they correlate strongly with the same behavior must be high, and its size does not estimate the true effect. The same logic applies far beyond fMRI. Selecting neurons because they respond to a stimulus and then showing that they respond to it, picking time windows where a difference peaks and testing there, or removing participants who do not show the effect, all produce false support.

A quantitative picture: suppose each voxel's test statistic is a standard normal $z$ under the null hypothesis. If you keep the top fraction $p$ of voxels, the mean of the selected $z$ values is not zero but $\varphi(c)/p$, where $c$ is the cutoff with $P(Z > c) = p$ and $\varphi$ is the standard normal density. Keeping the top 10% ($c = 1.2816$) gives a mean $z$ of $\varphi(1.2816)/0.10 = 1.75$ in pure noise.

### Safeguards

Make selection independent of the test. The standard options are:

- an **independent localizer**: a separate run or a separate data set defines the region, and the test uses different data;
- **split-half or cross-validation**: choose on one part of the data, test on the other, and rotate the roles; with participants, use leave-one-participant-out so that selection never sees the tested person;
- an **anatomically or theoretically defined region** chosen before the data are seen, ideally in the preregistration;
- a **permutation or simulation** that repeats the whole selection procedure on shuffled data, so that the null distribution includes the selection step.

For the whole brain, report the result of the whole-brain test as the evidence and do not recompute effects in the regions selected by it.

### Many comparisons

With $m$ tests at an uncorrected level $\alpha$, the expected number of false positives is $m\alpha$: for 60,000 voxels at $\alpha = 0.001$ that is 60. Corrections control either the family-wise error rate (Bonferroni, Holm, random-field theory or permutation maximum-statistic methods) or the **false discovery rate** (FDR). The **Benjamini-Hochberg** procedure (1995) sorts the $p$-values, $p_{(1)} \le \dots \le p_{(m)}$, finds the largest $k$ with $p_{(k)} \le (k/m)\,q$, and declares the $k$ smallest significant, controlling the expected proportion of false discoveries at $q$ under independence or certain positive dependence. Cluster-based inference needs care: Eklund, Nichols and Knutsson (2016) showed that with common settings, assumptions about spatial smoothness could fail and false-positive rates could exceed nominal levels, which led to changes in default methods and practice.

### Flexibility in the whole pipeline

Selection of voxels is only one of many choices: preprocessing, smoothing, exclusion rules, model, covariates, thresholds. Botvinik-Nezer and colleagues (2020) gave one fMRI data set to 70 teams and found that the teams' analyses and conclusions about the same hypotheses varied. A **multiverse analysis** (Steegen and colleagues, 2016) runs all defensible pipelines and reports how conclusions depend on the choices, and a specification curve is a related display. Preregistering the pipeline reduces the problem, and honest reporting of the exploration that happened addresses the rest.

@example
title: Benjamini-Hochberg on ten tests
problem: Ten tests give the sorted $p$-values $0.001, 0.008, 0.014, 0.019, 0.047, 0.20, 0.34, 0.61, 0.74, 0.88$. Control the false discovery rate at $q = 0.05$ and compare with the Bonferroni correction.
step: Compute the thresholds $(k/10)(0.05)$: $0.005, 0.010, 0.015, 0.020, 0.025, \dots$
step: Compare each $p_{(k)}$ with its threshold: $0.001 \le 0.005$, $0.008 \le 0.010$, $0.014 \le 0.015$, $0.019 \le 0.020$ all pass; $0.047 > 0.025$ fails.
step: The largest $k$ that passes is 4, so the four smallest $p$-values are declared discoveries.
step: Bonferroni uses $0.05/10 = 0.005$ and would accept only the first one.
answer: Four discoveries under Benjamini-Hochberg; one under Bonferroni, which controls a stricter error rate.

@example
title: Selection from noise
problem: A study selects the 10% of 1,000 noise voxels with the highest $z$ and averages those voxels' $z$ values in the same data. What mean do you expect, and what would you expect in a fresh data set?
step: The cutoff for the top 10% is $c = 1.2816$, with density $\varphi(c) = 0.1755$.
step: The expected mean of the selected values is $\varphi(c)/0.10 = 1.75$.
step: In an independent data set, the same voxels are still pure noise, so the expected mean is $0$.
answer: About $1.75$ in the selection data, and about $0$ in independent data; the apparent effect is created by the selection.

@mistakes
- Selecting a region with a contrast and then reporting the effect size of that same contrast in the region as if it were an independent estimate.
- Using the same trials to pick the neurons that respond best and to plot their average tuning.
- Treating a small number of uncorrected "hits" among thousands of tests as discoveries; with 60,000 tests, dozens of false positives are expected.
- Believing that cross-validation protects you when the selection step, such as choosing features, was done before the split.

answer: 40
check: 40000 * 0.001

@practice num
prompt: Six tests give the sorted $p$-values $0.004, 0.019, 0.023, 0.030, 0.045, 0.20$. Using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure with $q = 0.05$, how many tests are declared significant? (Find the largest $k$ with $p_{(k)} \le (k/6)(0.05)$.)
answer: 4
check: max([k for k, p in enumerate(sorted([0.004, 0.019, 0.023, 0.030, 0.045, 0.20]), 1) if p <= k * 0.05 / 6] + [0])
hint: Compute the thresholds $0.0083, 0.0167, 0.025, 0.0333, 0.0417, 0.05$ and find the largest $k$ that passes, not the first failure.
solution: The second $p$-value, $0.019$, exceeds its threshold $0.0167$, but the third ($0.023 \le 0.025$) and fourth ($0.030 \le 0.0333$) pass, and the fifth ($0.045 > 0.0417$) fails. The largest passing $k$ is 4, so the four smallest $p$-values are declared discoveries, including the second.

What is the problem with the reported effect size and $p$-value?
choice*: The region was selected using the same contrast and data that are then tested, so the effect is biased upward and the $p$-value is not valid.
choice: The effect is too large to be real, so the study must have been fraudulent.

A study will have 60 volunteers (adults who consent, with anonymous data) each do a color-word interference task online and rate their sleep quality the night before on a single question. @quiz num
prompt: Under pure noise, a study keeps the top 5% of voxels by $z$ (cutoff $c = 1.6449$) and averages their $z$ values in the same data. What is the expected mean $z$ of the selected voxels, $\varphi(c)/0.05$, to two decimal places?
answer: 2.06
tol: 0.01
check: exp(-1.6449 ** 2 / 2) / sqrt(2 * pi) / 0.05
hint: Compute the standard normal density at $1.6449$, which is $\exp(-c^2/2)/\sqrt{2\pi}$, then divide by $0.05$.
solution: $\varphi(1.6449) = 0.1031$, so the expected mean is $0.1031/0.05 \approx 2.06$, a sizable apparent effect from noise that will vanish in independent data.

@quiz self
prompt: A colleague says, "I picked the 20 electrodes with the strongest response to the target in the whole recording and then showed that those electrodes respond more to the target than to the distractor. It replicates what we know, so it is fine." In about 150 words, explain the problem and give two ways to redo the analysis that avoid it.
hint: Ask what part of the data chose the electrodes and what part is used to show the difference.
solution: The electrodes were chosen because they responded strongly to the target in these data, so their response to the target will, on average, be inflated by noise that happened to favor the target; comparing it with the distractor response in the same recording therefore overstates the difference even if the true difference is zero. That the result matches expectations does not make the analysis valid; it only means the circular analysis reached the answer people expected. To redo it, select electrodes using one subset of the data, for example odd-numbered trials, and test the target versus distractor difference on the held-out even-numbered trials, then swap the roles and average; or use an independent localizer block with different stimuli to define the electrodes, preregistered before the main data are examined. Either way, report how many electrodes were selected and what the effect size was in independent data.
rubric: Explains that selection and test use the same data, so noise is selected along with signal.
rubric: Gives at least two valid fixes (split trials, independent localizer, preregistered selection).
rubric: Notes that agreement with expectations does not validate a circular analysis.
