@article
In the early 2010s, several lines of work converged on an uncomfortable idea: a large fraction of published results in psychology, and probably in parts of neuroscience and biomedicine, would not hold up when repeated. Understanding why is the foundation for everything in the rest of this course.

### The evidence

The Open Science Collaboration (2015) attempted to replicate 100 studies from three psychology journals, using the original materials where possible and, in most cases, larger samples. About 97 of the original studies had reported significant results; about 36% of the replications were significant, and the average effect size of the replications was about half that of the originals. Other multi-site projects, such as the Many Labs studies (Klein and colleagues, 2014 onward), found a more varied picture: some effects replicated robustly across labs and countries, and others did not. In neuroscience, Button and colleagues (2013) argued that typical studies had low power. Eklund, Nichols and Knutsson (2016) showed that common cluster-based fMRI methods could give far more false positives than nominal when assumptions failed. Marek and colleagues (2022) showed that associations between brain measures and behavior in very large samples were small, so studies with tens of participants were badly underpowered for such questions and needed thousands.

### Causes

No single cause explains the pattern. The main ones interact.

**Low power and the winner's curse** (week 3): small studies find few true effects and exaggerate the ones they find.

**Publication bias:** significant results are more likely to be submitted and published than null ones, so the literature overrepresents false positives and overestimates effects (the "file drawer").

**Analysis flexibility:** Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn (2011) showed by simulation and a deliberately absurd experiment that ordinary researcher choices (which outcomes to report, when to stop, which covariates and exclusions to use) can push the false-positive rate far above 5%. Gelman and Loken (2013) called the many defensible analysis paths a "garden of forking paths": even without conscious fishing, if the analysis depends on the data, $p$-values lose their meaning. With $k$ independent tests at $\alpha = 0.05$, the chance of at least one false positive is $1 - 0.95^k$: $14\%$ for three tests, $41\%$ for ten.

**Questionable research practices:** John, Loewenstein and Prelec (2012) surveyed psychologists and found that many admitted to practices such as not reporting all outcomes, deciding whether to collect more data after looking at the results, or reporting an unexpected finding as if it had been predicted. The last is **HARKing**, hypothesizing after the results are known (Kerr, 1998). Most such practices arise from incentives and uncertainty about norms rather than intent to deceive; this is a system problem as well as an individual one.

**Weak theory and measurement:** vague hypotheses accommodate any result, and unreliable measures shrink true effects and inflate noise.

### How bad is it? Reading the numbers

Ioannidis (2005) argued that when power is low and prior plausibility is low, most significant findings may be false (the positive predictive value of week 2). Suppose 200 studies test hypotheses of which 10% are true, with power $0.4$: the 20 true effects yield 8 significant results; the 180 false hypotheses yield $180 \times 0.05 = 9$ false positives. About 53% of the significant results are false. Real fields vary, so this shows the mechanism, not a measured rate.

### Interpreting replications

A failed replication does not by itself show that the original was wrong. The replication has its own sampling error, it may differ in population or procedure, and the original effect may be smaller than first reported. Better practice compares effect sizes and their intervals, asks whether the replication was powered to detect a smaller effect, and uses meta-analysis. Researchers disagree about how serious the problem is, how much of it reflects hidden moderators versus bias, and whether "crisis" is the right word; they largely agree that power, transparency and the incentives for publication needed reform.

### What changed

Responses include larger samples and power analysis, preregistration and registered reports (week 5), data and code sharing (week 7), reporting guidelines, replications as publishable work, and multi-lab projects. How much each response helps is itself a research question.

@example
title: How many significant results are false?
problem: A field runs 200 studies. Ten percent of the tested hypotheses are true; power is $0.40$ and $\alpha = 0.05$. If every significant result is published, what fraction of published results are false positives?
step: Count true hypotheses: $200 \times 0.10 = 20$; of these, $20 \times 0.40 = 8$ reach significance.
step: Count false hypotheses: $180$; of these, $180 \times 0.05 = 9$ reach significance by chance.
step: Divide: false positives are $9$ of $8 + 9 = 17$ significant results, or $0.529$.
step: Interpret: a literature with no fraud and correct statistics can still be half false when power is low and tested hypotheses are mostly wrong.
answer: About 53% of the significant results are false positives.

@mistakes
- Reading the 36% replication figure as "64% of psychology is false"; replications have error, and the numbers depend on the criteria used.
- Assuming that unreliable results require fraud; most come from low power, flexible analysis and selective publication.
- Treating a failed replication as proof that the original effect is zero; compare intervals and consider power.
- Blaming individual researchers alone; incentives for novel, significant results shape what gets published.

@practice self
prompt: This is the deliverable for this block. A published study compared two groups of 18 participants each and reported a large effect ($d = 0.6$, $p = .03$ two-sided). Compute the power of the design at $\alpha = 0.05$ (two-sided, normal approximation, power $\approx \Phi(d\sqrt{n/2} - 1.96)$) at the reported $d = 0.6$ and at $d = 0.3$, which you consider more plausible. State the sample per group needed for 80% power at $d = 0.3$ (use $2(1.96 + 0.8416)^2/d^2$), and write two sentences interpreting the result.
hint: Compute $\delta = d\sqrt{9}$ for each effect size, subtract $1.96$ and use the normal cumulative distribution; for the sample size use the formula with $d = 0.3$.
solution: At $d = 0.6$ and $n = 18$ per group, $\delta = 0.6\sqrt{9} = 1.8$, so power $= \Phi(1.8 - 1.96) = \Phi(-0.16) \approx 0.44$. At $d = 0.3$, $\delta = 0.9$ and power $= \Phi(-1.06) \approx 0.14$. Reaching 80% power at $d = 0.3$ needs $n = 2(2.8016)^2/0.09 \approx 174.4$, so 175 per group. Interpretation: even if the true effect were as large as reported, this design would detect it less than half the time, and if the true effect is the more plausible $d = 0.3$, the design had a 14% chance, so a significant result is likely to come from an exaggerated sample estimate and should be treated as preliminary. The result calls for a replication with about ten times as many participants per group, rather than a conclusion that the effect is large.

@quiz num
prompt: A published design has 30 participants per group. Assuming a true effect of $d = 0.5$, what is the power at $\alpha = 0.05$ (two-sided), using $\mathrm{power} \approx \Phi(d\sqrt{n/2} - 1.96)$? Give your answer as a probability to two decimal places.
answer: 0.49
tol: 0.01
check: 0.5 * (1 + erf((0.5 * (30 / 2) ** 0.5 - 1.96) / 2 ** 0.5))
hint: Compute $\delta = 0.5\sqrt{15}$ first and subtract $1.96$.
solution: $\delta = 0.5 \times 3.873 = 1.936$, so power $= \Phi(-0.024) \approx 0.49$, which is about a coin flip, even for a medium effect.

@quiz self
prompt: In about 200 words, give a balanced explanation of why many published findings did not replicate. Include at least three causes, say how they interact, and state one point on which researchers disagree.
hint: Choose causes from power, publication bias, analysis flexibility and weak theory, and show how one amplifies another.
solution: Several causes work together. Low power means that studies find few true effects and, when they succeed, overestimate them (the winner's curse). Publication bias then filters the literature, so significant, exaggerated estimates are published and null results stay in the file drawer; the published record looks stronger than the evidence. Analysis flexibility lets chance results reach significance: when outcomes, exclusions or stopping points can depend on the data, the nominal 5% false-positive rate is not the actual one, and Gelman and Loken's garden of forking paths describes how this can happen even without intent. These causes amplify each other: with low prior plausibility and low power, even a modest rate of flexible analysis makes false positives a large share of significant results, and weak theory and noisy measurement make results fragile. Incentives that reward novel, significant findings sustain the system. Researchers disagree about how much of the problem is bias and how much heterogeneity in populations and procedures (hidden moderators) that makes direct replication differ from the original, and about whether "crisis" describes the situation or exaggerates it. There is broad agreement that larger samples, transparency and better incentives are worthwhile.

rubric: Explains how causes interact, for example low power plus publication bias or flexibility.
rubric: States a real disagreement (hidden moderators versus bias, or the label "crisis") without taking a side dishonestly.
