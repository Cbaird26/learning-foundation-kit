### Reproducible and replicable

The National Academies (2019) distinguished **reproducibility**, obtaining the same results from the same data and the same analysis steps, from **replicability**, obtaining consistent results in new data collected with similar methods. Reproducibility is the minimum: if your own analysis cannot be rerun, nobody can check or build on it. Neither word is used the same way in every field, so define yours.

### A shareable package

A useful package contains five parts.

1. **The data**, in an open, documented format, with raw data where permitted and processed data for each analysis step. Neuroimaging has the Brain Imaging Data Structure (BIDS; Gorgolewski and colleagues, 2016), a convention for naming and organizing files; Neurodata Without Borders is a common format for neurophysiology.
2. **A data dictionary**: every variable with its meaning, units, coding and missing values.
3. **The analysis code**, under version control, with a README that says how to run it, the order of the scripts and the software versions (pinned environments or containers help).
4. **The materials**: stimuli, task code, questionnaires, instructions (where copyright allows).
5. **A license**: without one, others do not know what they may do. Permissive options include CC0 or CC BY for data and an open-source license for code.

Practical habits: script every step from raw data to figures so no manual editing is needed, set random seeds, record the software versions, test the pipeline on a clean machine and have a colleague try to run it.

### FAIR data

The FAIR principles (Wilkinson and colleagues, 2016) say that data should be **findable** (persistent identifiers and rich metadata), **accessible** (retrievable by a standard protocol, with clear conditions where access is restricted), **interoperable** (shared formats and vocabularies) and **reusable** (clear license, provenance and documentation). FAIR is not the same as open: sensitive data can be FAIR with controlled access.

### Sharing human data responsibly

Sharing does not override participants' rights. Plan it from the start. The consent form should say what will be shared, with whom and under what conditions, since data cannot ethically be shared more widely than participants agreed to. Remove direct identifiers (names, contact details, dates of birth), consider indirect ones (rare diagnoses, small-town locations, exact dates) and remember that a structural MRI can reconstruct a recognizable face, so images are usually defaced. Where risk remains, use **controlled access**: users apply to a committee and sign a data use agreement. Ask your institution's ethics board and data office what applies. Neural and other physiological data are increasingly treated as sensitive in law and policy (week 8).

### Policies you may meet

Policies are changing quickly, so check the current ones for your funder, institution, country and journal. In the United States, the NIH Data Management and Sharing Policy took effect on 25 January 2023: applications for NIH funding include a plan for managing and sharing the scientific data, and NIH-funded work is expected to follow it. NIH's 2024 Public Access Policy took effect on 1 July 2025 and requires papers from NIH-funded research to be publicly available on the date of publication, with no embargo. Other funders, countries and journals set their own rules on data sharing and open access, and some journals require data and code availability statements. "Data available on request" is a weak form of sharing: studies have found that such requests often fail.

### The audit

The week 7-8 deliverable is an audit of a paper with a reproducibility checklist: for each item (is the sample size justified, were exclusions fixed in advance, are data, code and materials available, is the analysis pipeline fully described), mark yes, no or unclear, citing the text. Two auditors seldom agree perfectly, so a chance-corrected agreement statistic, Cohen's kappa, measures how reliable the checklist is in practice. Kappa is $\kappa = (p_o - p_e)/(1 - p_e)$, where $p_o$ is the observed proportion of agreement and $p_e$ is the agreement expected by chance from each rater's yes and no rates.

@example
title: Agreement between two auditors
problem: Two auditors independently rated 100 checklist items as met (yes) or not met (no). Both said yes for 30 items; the first said yes and the second no for 10; the first said no and the second yes for 10; both said no for 50. Compute Cohen's $\kappa$.
step: Observed agreement: $p_o = (30 + 50)/100 = 0.80$.
step: Marginals: the first auditor said yes to $40$ items and the second to $40$, so each said yes with probability $0.4$ and no with probability $0.6$.
step: Chance agreement: $p_e = 0.4 \times 0.4 + 0.6 \times 0.6 = 0.16 + 0.36 = 0.52$.
step: Kappa: $\kappa = (0.80 - 0.52)/(1 - 0.52) = 0.28/0.48 = 0.583$.
answer: $\kappa \approx 0.58$: agreement well above chance but far from perfect, so the checklist items need clearer wording.

@mistakes
- Posting data without a data dictionary or code; files nobody can interpret or rerun give the appearance of sharing without the substance.
- Promising "data available on request" as the only sharing plan.
- Sharing human data that goes beyond what the consent form allows, or sharing images without considering whether a face can be reconstructed.
- Treating "open" and "FAIR" as the same; restricted data can be FAIR, and open data can be unusable.

@practice num

tol: 0.01

hint: The first auditor said yes to $50$ items and the second to $45$; use these marginals for $p_e$.

@practice self
prompt: You will run a low-risk reaction-time study with 80 adult volunteers who consent, with anonymous data recorded as trial-by-trial reaction times, accuracy and an age band. Write a data and code sharing plan of about 180 words that covers what will be shared and where, the formats and documentation, the license, what the consent form will say, and what will not be shared.
hint: Think of the five parts of a package and make sure that the consent and the sharing plan agree.
solution: I will share the raw trial-level data (one row per trial: anonymous participant code, condition, reaction time in ms, accuracy as 0 or 1, age band), the processed participant-level summaries, the task script and stimuli, and the analysis scripts, in a public repository that issues a persistent identifier (DOI). Data will be in open CSV files with a data dictionary defining each column, its units and missing-value codes, and a README giving the run order and the software and package versions (a pinned environment file), with a fixed random seed for any resampling. Data will carry a CC0 or CC BY license and code an open-source license. The consent form will state that anonymous data will be deposited in a public repository and used by other researchers, that no name or contact detail will be stored with them, and that participants may withdraw before data are anonymized. Not shared: names, e-mail addresses, the consent forms and any code linking participant codes to people, which stay encrypted at the institution; age is released only as a band to limit re-identification. Before release a colleague will run the scripts on a clean machine to check that the figures and tables reproduce.

rubric: Says the consent form covers public sharing and states what will be withheld to protect identity.
rubric: Includes documentation and a reproducibility check (versions, seeds, a clean-machine run).

@quiz num
prompt: Two auditors rated 50 checklist items. Both said yes for 18; the first said yes and the second no for 2; the first said no and the second yes for 4; both said no for 26. Compute Cohen's $\kappa$ to two decimal places.
answer: 0.75
tol: 0.01
check: (44 / 50 - (20 / 50 * 22 / 50 + 30 / 50 * 28 / 50)) / (1 - (20 / 50 * 22 / 50 + 30 / 50 * 28 / 50))
hint: The first auditor said yes to $20$ items and the second to $22$; compute $p_o$ and $p_e$ from these.
solution: $p_o = 44/50 = 0.88$. $p_e = 0.40 \times 0.44 + 0.60 \times 0.56 = 0.176 + 0.336 = 0.512$. Then $\kappa = (0.88 - 0.512)/(0.488) \approx 0.75$.

@quiz self
prompt: A paper's data statement says "Data are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request." In about 150 words, explain why this is weaker than deposit in a repository, and suggest a better statement for a study with human volunteers whose consent allows controlled sharing.
hint: Consider who must act, for how long and under what conditions, and what a repository adds.
solution: Availability on request depends on one person's willingness, address and memory: authors move, lose files or fail to reply, so the data are not findable or reliably accessible, the conditions are not stated, and nobody can check that the data support the paper. A repository gives a persistent identifier, documentation, a license and long-term storage, which are the FAIR principles in practice. For human data whose consent allows controlled sharing, a better statement would say that de-identified data and the code are deposited in a named repository under a DOI, that the data are available to qualified researchers who sign a data use agreement and apply through the repository's access committee, state what the data dictionary contains and under which license the code is shared, and give a contact for questions. Where full sharing is impossible, it should explain why and share the code, the data dictionary and any data that can be released.
rubric: Explains concrete weaknesses of "on request" (reliability, no persistent identifier, unchecked conditions).
rubric: Proposes a specific replacement naming a repository, an access route and a license.
rubric: Respects consent and privacy for the controlled-access case.
