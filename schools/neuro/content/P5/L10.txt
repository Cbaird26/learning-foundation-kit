@article
A manuscript arrives with a request: "Please review this paper within three weeks." You are a second-year graduate student, and your advisor is out of time and asks you to draft a report. What does the editor want, what makes a report useful and what are your obligations? Peer review is a core practice of science and one you will do for decades, so it is worth learning as a craft.

### What peer review is

Researchers who are not the authors evaluate a manuscript, usually for a journal, a conference or a funder, and advise an editor, who decides. Journals differ in the model. In **single-blind** review, reviewers know the authors, but the authors do not know the reviewers; in **double-blind** review, neither knows the other; in **open** review, identities or reports are published. Some journals, including those publishing registered reports (week 5), review in two stages. Reviewers are generally unpaid volunteers. Peer review improves papers, but it does not guarantee that a published paper is correct: reviewers see only what the authors provide, rarely rerun analyses, and differ in judgments. Researchers argue about how well it works and about alternatives such as post-publication review and preprint review.

### Duties of a reviewer

Treat the manuscript as **confidential**: do not share or use it, and do not discuss it with others unless the journal allows, and check the journal's policy before involving a trainee or software. **Declare conflicts of interest** (a recent collaboration, a competing project, a close personal relationship) and decline if you cannot be impartial. Be **timely**, since delays hurt authors. Be **fair and courteous** about the work, not the authors. Be **honest about your limits**: say which parts you are not expert in.

### Anatomy of a useful report

1. **Summary** (a short paragraph): what the paper claims and does, in your words. This shows the editor and authors that you understood it, and it exposes mismatches between the claim and the evidence.
2. **Assessment of significance and strengths:** what is valuable and what is done well.
3. **Major concerns:** the issues that affect the conclusions, ranked by importance. For each, give the problem, why it matters and what would address it (an analysis, a control, a revised claim or a new experiment). Specific requests such as "report the effect size and interval," "show the data for individual participants" and "test the group difference directly rather than comparing significance" are more useful than "improve the statistics."
4. **Minor points:** clarity, missing details, typographical errors, references.
5. **Recommendation** (accept, minor revision, major revision, reject) with a brief justification, where the journal asks for it; some journals ask for comments to the editor separately.

Review the paper that was written, not the one you would have written. Distinguish "this conclusion does not follow" from "I would have chosen a different question." Check the reporting items from earlier weeks: sample size justification, exclusions, preregistration and deviations, multiplicity, circularity, data and code availability, ethics approval and consent, and whether the claims fit the design. Remember that several reviewers are more likely to find a given flaw than one, so aim to add something the others may miss and not to repeat everything.

### Responding to reviewers

As an author, reply to every comment, in order, with a **point-by-point response**: quote or number each comment, say what you changed and where (page and line), or explain respectfully and with evidence why you did not. Thank reviewers for useful points without flattery. Disagreement is allowed, but support it with data, citations or reasoning; do not ignore a concern or hide a change. A rejection is common and is not a judgment of you: revise using what is useful and submit elsewhere. Journals' conventions differ, so read the decision letter and the instructions.

### Deliverable: a referee report

The deliverable for weeks 9 and 10 is a referee report on a supplied short paper. The practice item below gives an invented short paper for you to review as a full report.

@example
title: How many reviewers does it take to find a flaw?
problem: A serious flaw in a manuscript has a probability of $0.4$ of being noticed by any single reviewer, independently. What is the probability that at least one of three reviewers notices it?
step: Compute the probability that one reviewer misses it: $1 - 0.4 = 0.6$.
step: Compute the probability that all three miss it: $0.6^3 = 0.216$.
step: The probability that at least one notices it is $1 - 0.216 = 0.784$.
answer: About 78%: three independent reviewers are far better than one, but one in five serious flaws still passes.

@mistakes
- Writing "this paper is poor" without saying what is wrong, why it matters and what would fix it.
- Reviewing the paper you would have written instead of the paper in front of you.
- Sharing a manuscript with a colleague or student without the journal's permission, or failing to declare a conflict of interest.
- Answering reviewers with defensiveness or silence on a point, instead of a reasoned, point-by-point reply.

@practice self
prompt: This is the deliverable for this block. Write a referee report of about 300 words on this invented short paper. "Forty volunteers were randomly assigned to walk for ten minutes or to sit for ten minutes and then completed a vigilance task on which reaction times were measured. Three outcomes were collected (mean reaction time, lapses and accuracy). Participants who 'appeared inattentive' (n = 4) were excluded. The walking group's mean reaction time was 12 ms faster ($p = .04$); lapses and accuracy did not differ. We conclude that walking improves attention in everyday life. Data are available on request." Include a summary, strengths, at least three major concerns with specific requests, minor points and a recommendation.
hint: Check the unit of the claim against the design, the number of outcomes and the exclusion, and ask what a reader would need.
solution: Summary: the authors randomized 40 volunteers to ten minutes of walking or sitting and report a 12-ms faster mean reaction time on a vigilance task for walkers ($p = .04$), with no differences in lapses or accuracy, and conclude that walking improves attention in everyday life. Strengths: random assignment is an appropriate design for a causal question, and the manipulation and task are simple and low-risk. Major concerns: first, the conclusion about "everyday life" does not follow from a single ten-minute manipulation followed by one laboratory task in 36 or 40 volunteers; the claim should be limited to this task and timescale. Second, three outcomes were measured, and only one was significant at $p = .04$; the authors should say which outcome was primary before data collection, whether the study was preregistered, and apply a correction or label the result exploratory. Third, four participants who "appeared inattentive" were excluded, with no criterion and no statement of the group each came from or whether the decision was made blind to condition; the analysis should be shown with and without them, with the rule defined. Fourth, there is no sample size justification, and the interval around 12 ms is not reported; the authors should report effect size with a 95% interval and discuss whether the lower limit matters. Fifth, expectancy and arousal are not controlled: walkers may expect improvement, and the effect may reflect arousal that fades, so a timeline of test delay is needed. Minor points: report the unit of analysis, the exact test, whether participants were blind to the hypothesis, ethics approval and consent, and replace "available on request" with a repository deposit and code. Recommendation: major revision. The question is reasonable and the design is suitable, but the current evidence is weak, the claim is overstated, and exclusions and outcome handling must be clarified before the conclusions can be assessed.
rubric: The summary is accurate and neutral, and strengths are named fairly.
rubric: At least three major concerns are specific to the described paper (overclaim, multiple outcomes, undefined exclusions, no justification or interval).
rubric: Each major concern comes with a specific request that would address it.
rubric: Minor points and a recommendation with a justification are included, and the tone is constructive.

@quiz num
prompt: A journal receives 400 submissions and rejects 55% without external review. Each remaining paper needs 3 reviewers, and on average one invitation in three is accepted. How many reviewer invitations are needed in total?
answer: 1620
check: 400 * 0.45 * 3 * 3
hint: Find the number of papers sent out for review, multiply by 3 reviews each, then by 3 invitations per accepted review.
solution: $400 \times 0.45 = 180$ papers go to review. They need $180 \times 3 = 540$ reviews, and at one acceptance per three invitations, $540 \times 3 = 1620$ invitations, which shows the load that review places on the community.

@quiz self
prompt: A reviewer wrote: "The sample is too small and the analysis is questionable. The authors should consult a statistician." Write, in about 150 words, how you would revise this comment so that it is specific and constructive, and then write a short response an author could give if the sample size cannot be increased.
hint: Say what is too small relative to what, what exactly is questionable and what would help; the author's reply should show evidence and limits.
solution: Revised comment: "The study has 12 participants per group. With this sample, the design has about 80% power only for effects near $d = 1.2$, so smaller effects would be missed and a significant result would likely exaggerate the true effect. Please justify the sample size with a stated smallest effect of interest, report the effect size with a 95% interval, and discuss what the interval excludes. The analysis treats the 40 trials per participant as independent observations; please use a model that accounts for the nesting of trials within participants (for example a mixed model, or the participant means) and show that the conclusion does not depend on this choice." Author reply: "We agree that the sample is small and have added a sensitivity analysis showing that the design detects effects of about $d = 1.2$ (new Methods paragraph, p. 6). Recruitment of more participants is not possible, so we now report the effect and its interval (Table 2), reanalyzed using participant means and a mixed model (Supplement), and have softened the conclusions to say that the data are compatible with a wide range of effects (Discussion, p. 11)."
rubric: The revised comment names what is wrong and why it matters, with a concrete request.
rubric: The author reply addresses each point, says where changes were made and acknowledges a limitation honestly.
rubric: Tone is courteous, and the reply shows evidence instead of defensiveness.
