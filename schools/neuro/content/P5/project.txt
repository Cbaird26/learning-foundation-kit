@project
title: A Stage 1 registered report and a referee critique of your own proposal
overview: You will write a Stage 1 registered report for a low-risk behavioral study that you could run with modest resources: introduction, hypotheses, design with a power analysis, a preregistered analysis plan, the ethics approval you would need and a data and code sharing plan. Then you will review your own proposal as a referee would and revise it. The aim is to practice every skill in the course on one coherent document, and you do not need to run the study.
goal: Turn a topic into a testable question, hypotheses with directions and a justified smallest effect size of interest.
goal: Compute a sample size and the power of your design from first principles, and write an analysis plan specific enough that two analysts would run the same analysis.
goal: Describe the ethics review, consent, privacy and sharing arrangements a real version of the study would need, and criticize your own proposal honestly.
step: Choose a question about a low-risk task with adult volunteers (a reaction-time, perception or short memory task, or a brief questionnaire without sensitive questions). Write the ladder from topic to question to hypothesis to operationalization to statistical prediction, in one page.
step: Write the introduction (about 400 words: the problem, what is known, the gap) and state one primary hypothesis with a direction, plus any secondary or exploratory questions, labeled as such. Justify a smallest effect size of interest in the units of your measure.
step: Describe the design: participants and recruitment, the task, the conditions, the measures, the order, and any randomization or counterbalancing. Use only tasks that are low risk, with volunteers who agree, no deception, no stimulation or devices and no animals.
step: Write the power analysis. Using the supplied pilot data for the spread of the measure, compute the sample size for 80% or 90% power at your smallest effect of interest, with a normal-approximation formula that you implement without statistics libraries (you may use a library afterward to check it), and add a sensitivity analysis giving the smallest effect your final sample can detect. Add an allowance for exclusions.
step: Write the analysis plan in the style of a preregistration: exact outcome, exclusion rules with numeric cutoffs, the test and its tail, the alpha level and multiplicity correction, the stopping rule and how deviations will be reported. Mark which analyses are confirmatory and which are exploratory.
step: Write the ethics and sharing sections: which review you would need and who decides at your institution, consent and withdrawal, anonymity and data protection, what data, code and materials you will share, where and under what license, and what you will not share.
step: Write a referee report of about 400 words on your own proposal: a summary, strengths, at least three major concerns with specific requests, minor points and a recommendation. Then revise the proposal in response and write a short point-by-point reply.
deliverable: The Stage 1 registered report (introduction, hypotheses, design, power analysis with your calculations, analysis plan, ethics section and sharing plan).
deliverable: The referee-style critique of your proposal and a point-by-point response describing the revisions.
deliverable: The code or worked calculations for the power analysis, with the parts written without libraries clearly marked.
rubric: The question, hypothesis and smallest effect size of interest follow from one another, and a result that would count against the hypothesis is named.
rubric: The power analysis is correct, justified by a smallest effect of interest and not by the pilot estimate alone, and includes a sensitivity analysis and an allowance for exclusions.
rubric: The analysis plan is specific (numeric cutoffs, exact test, correction, stopping rule), and confirmatory and exploratory analyses are separated.
rubric: The ethics and sharing sections are realistic, protect participants and agree with each other (consent covers the sharing).
rubric: The self-critique finds real weaknesses and the revision responds to them honestly.
safety: Use only low-risk tasks with adult volunteers who agree, no deception, no stimulation or devices, no sensitive questions and anonymous data, and never animals. The project is a written proposal; if you or your program decide to run the study, obtain approval through your institution's ethics review process first, and follow the rules of your institution and country, which differ.

material: **Formulas for the power analysis (to implement without libraries).** Between two groups with $n$ per group and standardized effect $d$: power $\approx \Phi(d\sqrt{n/2} - z_{1-\alpha/2})$ and $n \approx 2(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})^2/d^2$. For a within-participant difference with standard deviation $s_D$ of the difference scores and a raw smallest effect $\Delta$ (in ms), $d_z = \Delta/s_D$ and $n \approx (z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})^2/d_z^2$. Use $z_{0.975} = 1.96$, $z_{0.80} = 0.8416$ and $z_{0.90} = 1.2816$. Here $\Phi(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}\big(1 + \mathrm{erf}(x/\sqrt{2})\big)$, available as `math.erf`. Write the functions yourself, then verify them against a library such as G*Power, SciPy or statsmodels, and say how closely they agree (the exact $t$-based answer is slightly larger).

@review
This guide follows the course week by week. Use it to find the ideas you need to revisit, not as a substitute for the lessons.

**Week 1, reading a paper.** Read in three passes (question and claim, design and figures, statistics and limits). Recover the effect size: $d = t\sqrt{1/n_1 + 1/n_2}$, with an approximate interval $d \pm 1.96\,\mathrm{SE}(d)$. Watch for the difference between significant and not significant, reverse inference and causal language without a causal design.

**Week 2, the question.** Climb the ladder from topic to question, hypothesis, operationalization and statistical prediction. Know that $\mathrm{PPV} = (1-\beta)\pi/\big((1-\beta)\pi + \alpha(1-\pi)\big)$, and the difference between confirmatory and exploratory work.

**Week 3, design and power.** $n \approx 2(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})^2/d^2$ per group; power $\approx \Phi(d\sqrt{n/2} - 1.96)$; paired designs use $d_z = d/\sqrt{2(1-r)}$. Justify the effect size by a smallest effect of interest and run a sensitivity analysis. Observed power is uninformative, and low power inflates significant estimates (the winner's curse).

**Week 4, the replication crisis.** Know the evidence (Open Science Collaboration, 2015; Button and colleagues, 2013; the fMRI cluster results) and the causes: low power, publication bias, analytic flexibility, questionable practices and weak theory. Family-wise error: $1 - (1-\alpha)^k$. Interpret replications by effect sizes and intervals.

**Week 5, preregistration and registered reports.** A time-stamped plan separates confirmatory from exploratory analysis; Stage 1 review before data, in-principle acceptance, Stage 2 after. Bonferroni threshold $\alpha/m$. Disclose deviations.

**Week 6, flexibility and double dipping.** Never select and test on the same data: use independent localizers, cross-validation or preregistered regions. Benjamini-Hochberg: the largest $k$ with $p_{(k)} \le (k/m)q$. The expected false positives are $m\alpha$.

**Week 7, open data, code and materials.** Reproducibility versus replicability, a shareable package, FAIR, consent and de-identification, and the current NIH policies (25 January 2023 and 1 July 2025). Cohen's kappa for audits.

**Week 8, ethics.** Belmont principles, the Declaration of Helsinki (revised 19 October 2024), the Common Rule and IRBs, IACUCs and the 3Rs, and fabrication, falsification and plagiarism.

**Weeks 9 to 12.** Calibrate claims to the design; show data in figures; write useful referee reports and point-by-point responses; build talks around one message with a time budget; know that careers vary by field and country.

**Self-check.** Can you compute a sample size and a power by hand? Can you explain why the same data cannot select and test? Can you write an analysis plan that two analysts would follow identically? Can you name three ways an apparent effect can arise from flexible analysis?

**Common errors.** Using a pilot's point estimate as the true effect; computing observed power; comparing significance across groups instead of testing the difference; calling a null result evidence of no effect; reading "preregistered" as "high quality"; sharing data beyond what consent allows.

@exam num
prompt: A preregistered study will test three primary outcomes, controlling the family-wise error rate at $0.05$ with the Bonferroni correction, so each outcome is tested at the two-sided level $0.05/3 = 0.0167$, for which $z_{1-\alpha/2} = 2.394$. With power $0.80$ ($z = 0.8416$) for a standardized effect of $d = 0.45$ in a two-group design, how many participants per group are needed? Use $n = 2(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})^2/d^2$ and round up.
answer: 104
check: ceil(2 * (2.394 + 0.8416) ** 2 / 0.45 ** 2)
hint: Add the two $z$ values, square the sum, multiply by 2 and divide by $0.45^2$.
solution: $n = 2(3.2356)^2/0.2025 = 2 \times 10.469/0.2025 \approx 103.4$, so 104 per group. Without the correction, $z = 1.96$ would give about 78 per group, so protecting against multiple tests costs participants.

@exam mc
prompt: A team selects the 50 voxels with the largest difference between two conditions in all 30 participants' data, and then reports a large and significant difference between those conditions in the average of those voxels. They say a power analysis showed 90% power. What is the main problem with the reported result?
choice*: The voxels were selected with the same data and contrast that are then tested, so the effect and $p$-value are biased, whatever the power of the design.
choice: A power analysis is never appropriate for neuroimaging studies.
choice: The number of voxels is too small for any test to be valid.
choice: The result must be true because the power was high.
hint: Ask whether the selection and the test used independent data.
solution: Power concerns the probability of detecting a real effect in an independent test; it does not repair circular selection, which produces an apparent effect from noise. An independent localizer, cross-validation or a preregistered region would give a valid estimate.

@exam self
prompt: Timed answer (about 25 minutes). A lab proposes a study of whether a ten-minute breathing exercise improves attention. Forty adult volunteers will complete the exercise or listen to music for ten minutes, then do a sustained-attention task; the lab will measure reaction time, lapses and errors, and "see which shows an effect." Write a Stage 1-style critique (about 350 words) of the proposal. Name at least four problems drawn from different weeks of the course, give a specific fix for each, and say which ethics and data-sharing arrangements the study would need.
hint: Consider the hypothesis and outcomes, the sample size, the analysis plan and multiplicity, causal design, and the ethics and sharing.
solution: The proposal's central weakness is that it does not commit to what would count as support: three outcomes will be examined and the one that "shows an effect" will be reported, so the false-positive rate is inflated and a significant result would be hard to interpret. The fix is to preregister one primary outcome (for example, mean reaction time on correct trials) with a direction, and treat lapses and errors as secondary with a multiplicity correction or as exploratory. Second, forty participants have 80% power only for effects of about $d = 0.9$, which is larger than a ten-minute exercise is likely to produce; the authors should state a smallest effect size of interest and compute the sample size, or present a sensitivity analysis and interpret a null result with an interval or equivalence test. Third, the design needs to support the causal claim: random assignment, matched time and contact in the music condition, and a check on expectations and arousal, because a breathing exercise may work through expectation. Fourth, the analysis plan is missing exclusion rules and a stopping rule; these should be numeric and fixed before data collection, which a Stage 1 registered report would also lock in through in-principle acceptance. Fifth, ethics: this is low-risk research with adult volunteers, but it still needs the institution's ethics review procedure, informed consent with withdrawal and no deception, and the data should be anonymous. Finally, a sharing plan should deposit the anonymous trial-level data, the task code, a data dictionary and the analysis scripts under an open license, with consent wording that allows it. With these changes the study would test its hypothesis in a way that others could check.
rubric: Identifies at least four distinct problems (outcome flexibility, power, design controls, undefined exclusions or stopping, ethics, sharing), each tied to the proposal.
rubric: Gives a specific, correct fix for each problem (primary outcome and correction, smallest effect and sample size, control condition and expectation check, numeric rules).
rubric: Describes proportionate ethics review, consent and anonymity, and a data and code sharing arrangement consistent with consent.
rubric: Tone is that of a constructive referee and the conclusions follow from the evidence.
