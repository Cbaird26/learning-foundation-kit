@article
A lab compares two groups of 15 participants on a reaction-time task, finds $p = .08$ and reports "no effect." Another lab runs the same comparison with 15 per group, finds $p = .03$ and reports a large effect. Both studies might be of the same true effect, and a design with such small groups cannot tell. **Power** is the tool for thinking about this before collecting data.

### Errors, power and effect size

A test with significance level $\alpha$ (usually $0.05$) falsely rejects a true null hypothesis with probability $\alpha$: a **Type I error**. When a real effect of a given size exists, the test fails to detect it with probability $\beta$: a **Type II error**. **Power** is $1 - \beta$, the long-run probability of a significant result when the effect is real. Conventional targets of 80% to 95% power are conventions, not laws; a justification should say what error rates the question needs.

Power depends on four linked quantities: $\alpha$, the effect size, the sample size and the variability of the measure. Fix three and the fourth follows. For two independent groups of $n$ per group and a standardized effect $d$ (Cohen's $d$), the test statistic has a standardized mean of about $\delta = d\sqrt{n/2}$. With the normal approximation, the power of a two-sided test at $\alpha = 0.05$ is about $\Phi(\delta - 1.96)$, where $\Phi$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function (the tiny contribution of the opposite tail is ignored). Solving for $n$ at power $1-\beta$ gives

$$n \text{ per group} \approx \frac{2\,(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})^2}{d^2}.$$

For $\alpha = 0.05$ two-sided, $z = 1.96$; for 80% power, $z = 0.8416$; for 90%, $z = 1.2816$. For $d = 0.5$ and 80% power, $n \approx 2(2.8016)^2/0.25 \approx 62.8$, so 63 per group; the exact calculation with the $t$ distribution gives about 64. Software such as G*Power or the R package pwr does the exact calculation, and for complex designs researchers simulate.

### Which effect size?

The hardest input is the effect size. Three defensible sources: the **smallest effect size of interest** (the smallest effect that would matter theoretically or practically, which is a scientific judgment you must argue for); a meta-analytic estimate (adjusted for bias, since published effects are inflated); or the lower end of an interval from a prior study rather than its point estimate. A single published effect from a small, significant study is a poor guide, for the reason below. A **sensitivity analysis** turns the question around: with the sample you can afford, what is the smallest effect you could detect with 80% power? For $n = 50$ per group it is $d = 2.8016\sqrt{2/50} \approx 0.56$.

### Why low power matters beyond missed effects

Button and colleagues (2013) argued that typical studies in neuroscience had low power, with consequences beyond missed effects. Low power lowers the positive predictive value of significant results (week 2). It also causes the **winner's curse**: when power is low, only unusually large sample effects cross the threshold, so significant estimates exaggerate the true effect, and replications of them tend to find less. Small samples also make results fragile, because a few participants can move a statistic.

### Design choices that raise power

A larger sample is the obvious route, but others cost less: reduce measurement noise (reliable measures, more trials per participant), use a **within-participant design** (each person serves as their own control), use a stronger or more targeted manipulation, and include covariates that explain variance. For a paired design, the relevant effect is $d_z = d/\sqrt{2(1-r)}$, where $r$ is the correlation between a participant's two measurements: with $r = 0.6$, a within-participant design needs far fewer participants than a between-participant design for the same raw difference.

### What not to do

**Observed (post hoc) power** computed from the effect in your own data is a one-to-one function of the $p$-value and tells you nothing new: a non-significant result always has low observed power. Do not stop collecting data when $p < .05$ unless you planned a **sequential design** with corrected thresholds, because that raises the false-positive rate. Report your justification: the effect size, $\alpha$, power, the software and any assumptions.

Check what your funder, committee or journal requires for sample size justification; practices and requirements vary.

@example
title: Sample size for a within-participant design
problem: Participants complete two conditions, and you expect a difference of $d = 0.5$ in units of the between-condition standard deviation, with a correlation of $r = 0.6$ between a participant's two scores. How many participants do you need for 80% power at $\alpha = 0.05$ (two-sided, normal approximation, $z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta} = 2.8016$)?
step: Convert to the effect size for paired data: $d_z = 0.5/\sqrt{2(1 - 0.6)} = 0.5/0.894 = 0.559$.
step: Apply the one-sample form of the formula: $n = (z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})^2/d_z^2 = 2.8016^2/0.559^2 = 7.849/0.3125 = 25.1$.
step: Round up to the next whole participant.
step: Compare with a between-group design for the same raw effect: 63 per group, 126 in total.
answer: 26 participants, against 126 for the between-group version.

@example
title: A sensitivity analysis
problem: A student can recruit only 50 participants per group. What is the smallest standardized effect the design detects with 80% power at $\alpha = 0.05$?
step: Rearrange the formula: $d = 2.8016\sqrt{2/n}$.
step: Substitute $n = 50$: $d = 2.8016 \times 0.2 = 0.560$.
step: State the consequence: effects smaller than about $d = 0.56$ will mostly be missed, so a non-significant result cannot rule out a small effect.
answer: About $d = 0.56$; the study can be informative only about larger effects.

@mistakes
- Using the effect size from one small significant study as the true effect; because of the winner's curse it is probably too large.
- Calculating "observed power" after a non-significant result and concluding that the null is true.
- Confusing the effect for a paired design ($d_z$) with $d$ for two groups; they differ unless the correlation is $0.5$.
- Treating 80% power as a rule of nature rather than an error rate that should match the stakes of the decision.

choice*: Observed power is a direct function of the $p$-value and adds no information; the interval around the effect shows what sizes of effect the data rule out.
choice: Power can only be computed after data collection, so the authors were right to compute it.
choice: A low observed power proves that the true effect is zero.
choice: The authors should recompute observed power with a one-tailed test and report the larger value.
hint: What changes in the data would change the observed power? Compare with what changes $p$.
solution: The observed effect determines both the $p$-value and the observed power, so for a given test and sample size they carry the same information. @practice num
prompt: A design has 25 participants per group. Assuming a true effect of $d = 0.6$, compute the power at $\alpha = 0.05$ (two-sided) using $\mathrm{power} \approx \Phi(\delta - 1.96)$ with $\delta = d\sqrt{n/2}$. Give your answer as a probability to two decimal places.
answer: 0.56
tol: 0.01
check: 0.5 * (1 + erf((0.6 * (25 / 2) ** 0.5 - 1.96) / 2 ** 0.5))
hint: First compute $\delta = 0.6\sqrt{12.5}$, subtract $1.96$, and look up the normal cumulative probability.
solution: $\delta = 0.6 \times 3.536 = 2.121$, so power is $\Phi(0.161) \approx 0.56$. A study of this size misses a medium-to-large true effect almost half the time.

@practice ms
prompt: A lab wants to increase the power of a planned reaction-time study without simply recruiting more participants. Which of the following are legitimate ways to do it? Select all that apply.
choice*: Collect more trials per participant, which reduces the measurement noise in each person's mean.
choice*: Use a within-participant design when conditions can be run in the same participants and carry-over can be managed.
choice: Check the $p$-value after each participant and stop as soon as it falls below .05.

solution: More trials reduce noise and a within-participant design removes between-person variance, and both are chosen in advance. Stopping when $p < .05$ without a planned correction and dropping participants because of their effects inflate false positives and bias the estimate.

@practice self
prompt: A student plans a two-group reaction-time study. A published pilot of 14 per group reported $d = 0.9$ ($p = .03$). Write a sample-size justification of about 150 words for a two-group study at $\alpha = 0.05$ (two-sided) and 90% power ($z = 1.2816$), explaining which effect size you choose and why, and what you would conclude if the final study were non-significant.
hint: Ask whether the pilot's $d$ is a trustworthy estimate, and use the smallest effect of interest to fix the effect size.
solution: I would not use $d = 0.9$. It comes from 14 participants per group and was selected by being significant, so by the winner's curse it probably overstates the true effect, and its confidence interval runs from near zero to above 1.6. Instead I would use a smallest effect size of interest: for this task, a difference of $d = 0.5$ is roughly the smallest that would matter for the applied question (a 20-ms difference given a standard deviation of 40 ms), and smaller effects would not change the conclusions. With $\alpha = 0.05$ two-sided and 90% power, $n = 2(1.96 + 1.2816)^2/0.5^2 \approx 84.1$, so 85 per group, which I would round up to 90 per group to allow for exclusions. If the final result is non-significant, I would examine the confidence interval and run an equivalence test against $d = \pm 0.5$: if the interval excludes effects that large I can say the effect is smaller than the effect that matters; if not, I can only say the study was inconclusive about the effect, not that it is absent.
rubric: Declines to rely on the pilot's point estimate and gives the winner's curse or imprecision as the reason.
rubric: Chooses an effect size justified as the smallest effect of interest and computes $n$ correctly with the stated $z$ values.
rubric: States the error rates, an allowance for exclusions and the software or formula.
rubric: Explains how a non-significant result will be interpreted (interval or equivalence test), not as proof of no effect.

@quiz self
prompt: A lab's proposal says, "We will test 12 participants per group, as in previous studies in this area." Write a referee-style comment of about 120 words on this justification and suggest what the lab should do instead.
hint: Name what the sample-size justification should contain and what a sample of 12 can and cannot detect.
solution: This justification is conventional rather than analytic: precedent shows what others did, not what the question requires. With 12 per group, 80% power at $\alpha = 0.05$ is reached only for effects near $d = 1.2$, which are rare in this field, so the study will miss plausible smaller effects and, if it finds a significant result, will probably exaggerate it. The authors should state the smallest effect size of interest and why it matters, compute the sample needed for a stated power, report the formula or software, and, if constraints cap the sample, present a sensitivity analysis showing what effects are detectable and interpret a null result accordingly. They might also reduce noise through more trials or a within-participant design, and consider a sequential design with pre-specified stopping rules.
