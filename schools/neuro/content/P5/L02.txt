@article
"How does sleep affect memory?" is a topic, not a research question. You cannot run a study on it, because it does not say which memory, which aspect of sleep, in whom, or what result would count against you. Sharpening a question is the most important and least taught skill in research. Weak questions lead to studies that cannot fail, and studies that cannot fail teach little.

### From topic to prediction

Move down a ladder, writing one line at each rung.

1. **Topic:** sleep and memory.
2. **Question:** does a night of sleep, compared with the same interval awake, improve retention of word pairs learned beforehand in healthy young adults?
3. **Hypothesis:** sleep after learning stabilizes new memories, so retention is better after sleep than after wake.
4. **Operationalization:** the construct "retention" becomes the proportion of 40 word pairs recalled correctly after 12 hours; "sleep" is a night in the lab or a daytime period awake, with random assignment.
5. **Statistical prediction:** the mean recall proportion is higher in the sleep group, and the difference is at least a stated smallest effect size of interest, for example $d = 0.4$.

Each step can fail. Operationalizations can have poor **construct validity** (the measure may not capture the construct), and a statistical prediction can fit the theory loosely (a significant $p$-value does not test a theory by itself). Writing the chain down makes these gaps visible. The philosopher Karl Popper argued that good hypotheses forbid something, and Platt (1964) urged "strong inference": design studies in which the possible outcomes discriminate between rival explanations, rather than ones that any outcome would accommodate.

### Good questions: common criteria

A widely used checklist asks whether a question is **feasible** (can you recruit enough participants, afford the equipment, finish in time?), **interesting** (would the answer change what someone believes or does?), **novel** (or a deliberate replication of something important), **ethical** (week 8) and **relevant**. Programs differ in what they weigh most, so ask your advisor, but notice that "novel" is the criterion most often over-weighted. A well-powered replication or a study that discriminates between two accounts can be worth more than a new but untestable idea.

### Prior plausibility and what a significant result is worth

Why does question choice matter statistically? Suppose a fraction $\pi$ of the hypotheses a field tests are true, studies have power $1 - \beta$, and the significance level is $\alpha$. Among all studies, the share of true effects detected is $\pi(1-\beta)$ and the share of false positives is $(1-\pi)\alpha$. The **positive predictive value** (PPV), the probability that a significant result reflects a true effect, is

$$\mathrm{PPV} = \frac{(1-\beta)\,\pi}{(1-\beta)\,\pi + \alpha\,(1-\pi)} .$$

This is the logic of Ioannidis (2005). With $\pi = 0.2$, power $0.8$ and $\alpha = 0.05$, the PPV is $0.16/(0.16 + 0.04) = 0.80$. Cut the power to $0.3$ and it falls to $0.06/(0.06 + 0.04) = 0.60$. Bold, surprising hypotheses have low $\pi$, which is why they need larger samples and independent replication before you rely on them. This does not make bold questions bad; it means that the evidence for them should be stronger.

### Confirmatory and exploratory work

**Confirmatory** research tests a hypothesis stated in advance with an analysis plan fixed before the data are seen, and its $p$-values mean what they say. **Exploratory** research looks for patterns and generates hypotheses; it is valuable but its $p$-values are descriptive, and its findings need a new data set to be tested. Problems arise when exploratory results are presented as confirmatory (weeks 4 to 6). Label each analysis honestly.

### Finding the literature

Search by following citations forward and backward from a key paper, look for recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses, check preprint servers and trial or study registries for work in progress, and ask people in the field what has been tried and failed (failed attempts are rarely published). Record your search so a colleague could repeat it. Databases and norms differ by field, and your library can help.

### Writing the summary and critique

The week 1 and 2 deliverable is a one-page summary and critique of a paper. Spend about a third of the page on the question, design and findings in your own words, a third on what the evidence supports (with effect size and uncertainty), and a third on specific, constructive concerns and what a follow-up should do.

@example
title: What a significant result is worth
problem: A lab tests hypotheses of which 10% are true, using studies with 50% power and $\alpha = 0.05$. Of the significant results, what fraction are real effects?
step: Compute the true detections: $\pi(1-\beta) = 0.10 \times 0.50 = 0.05$.
step: Compute the false positives: $(1-\pi)\alpha = 0.90 \times 0.05 = 0.045$.
step: Divide: $\mathrm{PPV} = 0.05/(0.05 + 0.045) = 0.526$.
step: Interpret: about half of this lab's significant results would be false positives, even though each study was analyzed correctly.
answer: About 53% of the significant results reflect true effects; the other 47% are false positives.

@mistakes
- Treating a topic ("attention and aging") as a question; if no result could count against your idea, the study is not yet designed.
- Choosing a question because it is novel while ignoring feasibility; an underpowered study of an exciting idea tells you little.
- Presenting an exploratory finding as if it had been predicted; this is hypothesizing after the results are known.
- Equating a significant $p$-value with support for a theory; the link from theory to statistical prediction needs its own argument.

@practice num
prompt: A field tests hypotheses of which 5% are true. Its studies have power $0.80$ and use $\alpha = 0.05$. What is the probability that a significant result reflects a true effect, using $\mathrm{PPV} = (1-\beta)\pi/\big((1-\beta)\pi + \alpha(1-\pi)\big)$? Give your answer to two decimal places.
answer: 0.46
tol: 0.01
check: 0.8 * 0.05 / (0.8 * 0.05 + 0.05 * 0.95)
hint: True detections are $0.8 \times 0.05$; false positives are $0.05 \times 0.95$.
solution: $\mathrm{PPV} = 0.04/(0.04 + 0.0475) \approx 0.46$. Even with 80% power, when only 5% of tested hypotheses are true, more than half of the significant findings are false positives.

@practice mc
prompt: A researcher's hypothesis is "the intervention affects performance." Whether performance goes up, goes down, or shows no change in any subgroup, she plans to describe the result as consistent with the hypothesis. What is the main problem?
choice*: The hypothesis forbids no outcome, so no data could count against it and the study cannot test it.
choice: The hypothesis is too narrow to be of interest to other researchers.
choice: The study will be underpowered because the hypothesis does not specify a sample size.
choice: Hypotheses must always be about group means rather than individuals.
hint: Ask which result would have made her give up the hypothesis.

@practice self
prompt: Below is an invented abstract. "Forty adults learned a list of words, then either slept for eight hours or stayed awake. After a delay, participants who slept recalled 6.2 more words out of 60 than those who stayed awake ($t(38) = 2.4$, $p = .02$). We conclude that sleep consolidates declarative memory." Write the deliverable for this block: a summary of about 80 words and a critique of about 150 words that states what the evidence supports and names at least three specific concerns and a follow-up study that addresses them.
hint: Recover the effect size ($d = t\sqrt{2/n}$ with $n = 20$ per group) and ask about assignment, timing and the measure.
solution: Summary: forty adults learned 60 words, then slept or stayed awake for a period; those who slept recalled about 6 more words on a later test, a difference reported as significant ($t(38) = 2.4$, $p = .02$), and the authors conclude that sleep consolidates declarative memory. Critique: the recovered effect size is $d = 2.4\sqrt{2/20} \approx 0.76$ with an approximate 95% interval of roughly $0.1$ to $1.4$, so the data are compatible with a small or a very large benefit; a single study of 20 per group is a weak basis for the conclusion. First, the abstract does not say whether participants were randomly assigned, so differences in motivation or circadian time at learning and testing could explain the result. Second, the sleep group may have been tested at a different clock time than the wake group, and recall varies with time of day, so time and sleep are confounded. Third, "consolidation" is a mechanism, but the measure is only recall after a delay: interference from waking experience would give the same result without any active consolidation. Fourth, there is no test of whether the benefit lasts or generalizes beyond word lists. What the evidence supports is that, in this sample, recall after sleep exceeded recall after wake. A follow-up should randomize assignment, equate test times (for example by comparing a night with a day condition at both orders), use a larger preregistered sample with a justified smallest effect of interest, and include an immediate-recall baseline to measure forgetting rather than final recall alone.
rubric: Summary is accurate and in the learner's own words, with design, result and claim.
rubric: Reports the effect size or interval and says what it implies about precision.
rubric: Names at least three concerns that are specific to the described study (assignment, timing confound, mechanism, baseline, generalization).
rubric: Proposes a follow-up that addresses the concerns and says what the current evidence does support.

@quiz num
prompt: A field tests hypotheses of which half are true, using studies with power $0.80$ and $\alpha = 0.05$. What is the probability that a significant result reflects a true effect? Give your answer to two decimal places.
answer: 0.94
tol: 0.01
check: 0.8 * 0.5 / (0.8 * 0.5 + 0.05 * 0.5)
hint: With $\pi = 0.5$ the true detections are $0.8 \times 0.5$ and the false positives are $0.05 \times 0.5$.
solution: $\mathrm{PPV} = 0.40/(0.40 + 0.025) \approx 0.94$. When hypotheses are likely to be true and studies are well powered, significant results are mostly real.

@quiz mc
prompt: Which pair correctly distinguishes confirmatory from exploratory analysis?
choice*: A confirmatory analysis is specified before seeing the data and tests a stated hypothesis; an exploratory analysis searches for patterns, and its results need a fresh data set to be tested.
choice: A confirmatory analysis uses a larger sample; an exploratory analysis always uses a smaller one.
choice: A confirmatory analysis uses $p$-values; an exploratory analysis never reports them.
choice: A confirmatory analysis is published in better journals; an exploratory analysis is not publishable.
hint: The difference is about when the analysis was fixed relative to the data, not about size or prestige.
solution: What distinguishes the two is whether the hypothesis and analysis were fixed before seeing the data. Exploratory work is valuable and publishable when it is labeled honestly, and its $p$-values are descriptive rather than error-controlled.

@quiz self
prompt: Take the topic "stress and decision making" and write the ladder from topic to statistical prediction: a question, a hypothesis, an operationalization of both constructs using a low-risk laboratory task, and a statistical prediction with a smallest effect size of interest. Then name one way the chain could fail.
hint: Choose a specific manipulation and outcome, and say which result would count against you.
solution: Question: does a brief, mild laboratory stressor (a timed mental-arithmetic task with a public-speaking cue, approved by an ethics committee) change how often adults choose a risky option in a gambling task, compared with a control task? Hypothesis: acute stress increases risk taking. Operationalization: stress is the stressor condition, checked by a self-rated stress item and not by assumption; risk taking is the proportion of risky choices out of 60 trials in which a risky gamble has the same expected value as a sure amount. Participants are randomly assigned to the stressor or control task. Statistical prediction: the proportion of risky choices is higher in the stress group, with a difference of at least $d = 0.4$ as the smallest effect of interest; the hypothesis is disconfirmed if the 95% interval excludes effects of that size. Failure points: the manipulation may not induce stress (hence the check), or may induce fatigue instead; risk taking in this task may not reflect real-world decisions; and a null result could reflect low power, so an equivalence test against the smallest effect of interest is planned.
rubric: Gives a question, hypothesis, operationalizations and a statistical prediction that follow from one another.
rubric: States a smallest effect size of interest and a result that would count against the hypothesis.
rubric: Uses a low-risk, ethical manipulation with a manipulation check.
rubric: Names a specific way the chain could fail (manipulation validity, construct validity or power).
