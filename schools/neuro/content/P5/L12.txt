@article
A second-year student asks, "Do I need a postdoc to be a researcher?" The honest answer is that it depends on the field, the country, the kind of work and the person, and anyone who gives you one answer for all of them is simplifying. This lesson describes the landscape in general terms and says what the evidence for any particular path is: ask the people who do those jobs, and check the rules of your own program, country and funders.

### Where research training leads

People trained in neuroscience and cognitive science work in several kinds of settings, and many move between them.

- **Academic research and teaching:** universities and research institutes. Typical stages in many systems are doctoral study, one or more postdoctoral positions, and an independent position leading a research group or teaching, but the stages, their names and their lengths differ between fields and countries, and some systems have different structures altogether.
- **Industry research:** companies developing drugs, medical devices, software or analytics roles that use research skills.
- **Clinical and applied roles:** roles combining research with patient care or services, such as clinical psychology, neuropsychology, or medicine; these usually require a license or qualification, and the requirements depend on the country or state.
- **Government, non-profit and regulatory work:** research agencies, health organizations, standards bodies and foundations.
- **Data science and quantitative roles,** in many sectors.
- **Science communication, journalism, publishing, policy, education and research administration,** including managing laboratories, core facilities and grants.

Nothing here predicts how many positions exist now or in the future, which varies by field, country and time. Do not rely on one person's story, and check data from your own program and professional societies about where their graduates go.

### What every path uses

Most paths reward the same skills: framing questions, designing studies, analyzing data and reasoning about uncertainty (the subject of this course), programming and data management, writing, presenting, collaboration, project management and the ability to learn new methods. Transferable evidence of skill includes papers and preprints, preregistrations, shared code and data, talks, teaching and the ability to explain your work to specialists and to the public.

### Habits that help

**Choose mentors and environments carefully:** ask current and former members about how the lab works, how authorship is decided, how students are supported and how much independence they have. **Write down expectations:** many programs encourage a mentoring agreement or an individual development plan covering milestones, meeting frequency, authorship expectations, data and code ownership, time off, and how conflicts are handled; revisit it each year. **Authorship:** conventions vary (for example the order of first, last and corresponding authors differs by field), so discuss it early and in writing, and use a contributor taxonomy such as CRediT. **Build a network** by attending talks and conferences, reviewing, collaborating and keeping in touch with colleagues. **Learn about funding:** fellowships and grants exist in most systems, with rules that differ by funder and country. **Look after yourself:** research is uncertain and long, and care for health, rest and relationships supports good work; seek help from your institution's resources when you need it.

### Integrity across a career

Everything in the course about honest reporting, transparency, ethics and fair credit applies throughout a career. Reputations are built by careful work that others can check, by fair credit to colleagues and by handling mistakes openly: correcting your own errors in the published record is a sign of integrity, not failure.

### Deliverable: a recorded ten-minute talk

The week 11-12 deliverable is a recorded ten-minute talk on a paper. Choose a paper whose claim you can state in one sentence, build the talk around that message, budget time and words (week 11), rehearse, record and listen back. The practice items give a time budget and a script outline to model.

@example
title: Timing a recorded talk
problem: A ten-minute recorded talk has an opening slide of 45 seconds, a closing slide of 60 seconds and six content slides sharing the rest of the time equally. How long does each content slide get?
step: Convert the total: 10 minutes is 600 seconds.
step: Subtract the opening and closing: $600 - 45 - 60 = 495$ seconds for the content slides.
step: Divide by six: $495/6 = 82.5$ seconds per slide.
step: Check the word budget: at 130 words per minute, 82.5 seconds is about 179 words per slide, which is more than most slides need; a dense slide may be split or trimmed.
answer: 82.5 seconds per content slide.

@mistakes
- Assuming that a single path (a postdoc, then a faculty position) is the default for everyone trained in the field; paths vary by field and country.
- Relying on one anecdote or on general claims about jobs rather than information from your own program, society and field.
- Leaving expectations about authorship, data ownership and meeting frequency unspoken until a conflict arises.
- Taking a recorded talk in one take with no rehearsal, trial recording or timing check.
