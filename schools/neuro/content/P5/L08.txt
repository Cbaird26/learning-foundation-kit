@article
A graduate student wants to study attention by asking undergraduates to complete a brief computer task and to answer questions about stress. She assumes that because the task is harmless, no ethics review is needed. Whether she is right is not hers alone to decide. Research with people and animals has an organized system of review, and a researcher who understands its reasons will move through it faster and run better studies.

### Principles and documents

Modern research ethics grew from abuses, and its documents name the lessons. The Nuremberg Code (1947) put voluntary consent first. The Belmont Report (1979) in the United States set out three principles: **respect for persons** (autonomy and protection of those with diminished autonomy), **beneficence** (minimizing harms and maximizing benefits) and **justice** (fair selection of participants and distribution of burdens and benefits). The World Medical Association's **Declaration of Helsinki** is the best known international statement for medical research; it was last revised on 19 October 2024, the first revision since 2013, and it now refers to research participants rather than subjects, with stronger emphasis on engagement with communities and on vulnerability. These are not laws. They shape laws, journal policies and institutional rules.

### How oversight works

In the United States, research with people funded by federal agencies, and most research at institutions that accept such funds, is governed by the **Common Rule** (45 CFR 46), whose revised version had a general compliance date of 21 January 2019. Studies are reviewed by an **institutional review board** (IRB; called an ethics committee or research ethics board elsewhere). Review is proportional to risk: some low-risk research is **exempt** or reviewed by an expedited process, and at many institutions the decision about exemption is made through the institution's procedures, not by the investigator alone. Check your own institution's rules, since procedures, categories and local policies differ between institutions and countries.

Elements that review typically examines: a scientific rationale (a pointless study wastes participants' time), risks and benefits, selection of participants, **informed consent** (information, understanding and voluntariness, treated as a process and not only a signature), privacy and data protection, protections for vulnerable groups (children, patients, people in dependent relationships with the researchers) and plans for reporting problems. Low-risk work such as reaction-time tasks or questionnaires without sensitive questions still needs consent and privacy care, and some uses of human data raise new questions: neural data from consumer devices are sensitive. In November 2025, UNESCO's member states adopted a non-binding Recommendation on the Ethics of Neurotechnology; some countries and US states have laws on neural data, and the details differ and are changing, so check the current rules.

### Animals

In the United States, research with animals is reviewed by an **institutional animal care and use committee** (IACUC) under the Animal Welfare Act and the Public Health Service policy; the European Union regulates it under Directive 2010/63/EU, and other countries have their own systems. The guiding framework is the **3Rs** of Russell and Burch (1959): **replacement** (use non-animal methods where possible), **reduction** (use the smallest number of animals that gives reliable answers, which is why power analysis is an ethical issue) and **refinement** (minimize pain and distress, through housing, anesthesia and analgesia, and humane endpoints). The ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines (2020) say what to report about animal studies, such as the randomization, blinding and sample size. Thoughtful people disagree about whether and when animal research is justified: one position holds that some research (understanding disease, developing treatments) has benefits that justify regulated use of animals when alternatives are not adequate; another that animals have interests that make such use wrong or that cannot be outweighed; and many hold intermediate views, accepting some uses and not others. Both sides can agree that poorly designed studies are unethical on any view, since they cost lives without gain.

### Integrity

Research misconduct under US federal policy means **fabrication** (making up data), **falsification** (changing data or results) and **plagiarism** (using others' work without credit). Honest error and differences of opinion are not misconduct. Other duties matter as well: fair **authorship** (the CRediT taxonomy lists contributor roles, and journals often follow criteria for what earns authorship, which vary), disclosure of conflicts of interest, honest reporting of exclusions and deviations, and care with data. Reporting of concerns goes to the institution's research integrity office, and policies protect good-faith reporters in many places.

### The audit

The week 7-8 deliverable is an audit of a paper with a reproducibility checklist. For each item, mark yes, no or unclear and cite the text: ethics approval and consent reported, sample size justified, exclusions and stopping rule stated, randomization and blinding where relevant, data, code and materials available, analysis plan registered, and all outcomes reported.

@example
title: Planning enrollment with dropout
problem: A study needs 66 complete data sets, and past studies with the same task lost about 12% of participants to exclusions and dropout. How many volunteers should the ethics application request?
step: With 12% lost, 88% remain: $0.88 \times N \ge 66$.
step: Solve: $N \ge 66/0.88 = 75$.
step: State the number in the application, with the reason: asking for more than needed exposes extra participants without scientific gain, and asking for fewer risks an underpowered study.
answer: 75 volunteers.

@mistakes
- Assuming that a harmless-looking task needs no review; the decision belongs to the institution's procedure, not to the researcher alone.
- Treating consent as a signature rather than a process of information, understanding and voluntariness.
- Treating the Declaration of Helsinki or the Belmont Report as laws; they are guidance that shapes laws and policies.
- Regarding sample size as only a statistical matter; too large exposes participants or animals needlessly, too small wastes them.

prompt: A study needs 48 animals to complete its design and expects that 10% will be lost to illness or humane endpoints before data collection finishes. How many animals should be requested in the protocol, rounded up to a whole animal?
answer: 54
check: ceil(48 / 0.9)
hint: With 10% lost, 90% remain, so divide the number needed by $0.9$.
solution: $48/0.9 = 53.3$, which rounds up to 54. Requesting this number, no more, follows the principle of reduction.

@practice mc
prompt: Which of the following is an example of refinement in the 3Rs framework?
choice*: Providing analgesia and enriched housing and setting humane endpoints so that animals experience less pain and distress.
choice: Replacing live animals with a computational model for a question the model can answer.
choice: Using a power analysis to choose the smallest sample that gives reliable results.
choice: Publishing the results of the experiment in an open-access journal.
hint: The three Rs are replace, reduce and refine; this one concerns how the animals are treated.
solution: Refinement means minimizing suffering and improving welfare. Replacing animals with a model is replacement, and a power analysis to limit numbers is reduction.

@practice self
prompt: This is the deliverable for this block. Audit the following invented methods paragraph against a reproducibility and ethics checklist. "Thirty students were recruited from a psychology class and completed a memory task for course credit. Participants who performed poorly were removed. Reaction times were analyzed with an ANOVA, and the effect of training was significant ($p = .04$). Data are available on request." For each of the items ethics approval and consent, sample size justification, exclusions, analysis detail, data and code availability and voluntariness, mark yes, no or unclear, cite the text, and add one sentence on what you would ask the authors.
hint: Mark "unclear" where the text is silent, and notice two features that bear on voluntariness and exclusions.
solution: Ethics approval and consent: unclear. The paragraph does not mention an IRB or consent, so I would ask for the approval number and consent procedure. Sample size justification: no. "Thirty students" has no stated power analysis or smallest effect size of interest; I would ask how 30 was chosen. Exclusions: no. "Participants who performed poorly were removed" gives no criterion or number removed and does not say whether the rule was fixed before the data were examined; I would ask for the rule and the count and whether the result holds with all participants. Analysis detail: unclear. "An ANOVA" does not specify the design, factors, corrections or effect size; I would ask for the model, degrees of freedom and effect sizes with intervals. Data and code availability: no. "Available on request" is not deposit and no code is mentioned; I would ask for a repository deposit. Voluntariness: unclear, with concern. Recruitment from a class for course credit can pressure participation unless an equivalent alternative of similar effort is offered, so I would ask whether one was provided and how it was explained. Overall, the evidence for the training effect ($p = .04$) is weak and cannot be assessed until the exclusion rule and analysis are specified.

@quiz self
prompt: In about 200 words, give a balanced account of the debate over the use of animals in neuroscience research. Present the strongest version of two positions, say what the 3Rs contribute, and avoid stating which position is correct.
hint: Present each position as its holders would, and show what the 3Rs add for people on both sides.
solution: One position holds that carefully regulated research with animals is justified when it yields knowledge or treatments that cannot be obtained otherwise, such as the study of circuits, development or disease in whole living brains, and when the harms are minimized and weighed against expected benefits through independent review. Its strongest form notes that the knowledge gained has helped both people and animals, and that alternatives do not yet reproduce the complexity of an intact nervous system. A second position holds that animals have interests, especially in avoiding suffering, which cannot simply be traded against human benefits, and that where animals are sentient the use of them as research tools is wrong or should be sharply limited; its strongest form points to the limited translation of some animal findings to people and to the moral weight of suffering in a being that cannot consent. Many people hold views in between, accepting some uses and rejecting others depending on the species, the severity of procedures and the importance of the question. The 3Rs matter to all of these views: replacement and refinement lower the harm, reduction demands that studies be designed and powered well enough to be informative, and the ARRIVE guidelines make reporting transparent so that others can judge whether the suffering was justified.
rubric: States two positions in strong forms that their holders would accept.
rubric: Does not declare one position correct or dismiss the other.
rubric: Explains the contribution of the 3Rs (replacement, reduction, refinement), and ties reduction to design and power.
