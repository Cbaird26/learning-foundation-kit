@article
An abstract reports that a brain region was more active in patients than in controls ($p = .03$) but not in a comparison group ($p = .20$), and concludes that the region is "specifically impaired" in patients. Should you believe it? Experienced readers do not start with the conclusion. They ask what was measured, in whom, compared with what, and how large and how uncertain the difference is. This week gives you a routine for answering those questions quickly and fairly.

### Three passes

**Pass one (five minutes): the question and the claim.** Read the title, abstract, first paragraph of the introduction, the last paragraph of the introduction (usually the hypotheses) and the first paragraph of the discussion. Write one sentence for the question and one for the claim. If you cannot, the paper is unclear, not you.

**Pass two (twenty minutes): the design and the figures.** Read the methods before the discussion, and study the figures with their legends. Note the sample (who, how many, how recruited), the manipulation or comparison, the measures, the exclusions and when they were decided, and the unit of analysis: participants, trials, neurons, sessions or animals. Many problems come from analyzing trials or neurons as if they were independent participants.

**Pass three (as long as needed): statistics and limits.** Ask whether the statistical test answers the question the paper claims to answer, whether effect sizes and intervals are reported or recoverable, and what the authors say about limits.

### Recovering effect size and uncertainty

A $p$-value says little about size. When a paper reports an independent-samples $t$ test with $n_1$ and $n_2$ participants per group, you can recover Cohen's $d$ (the standardized mean difference, the difference in means divided by the pooled standard deviation):

$$d = t\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}} .$$

An approximate standard error is $\mathrm{SE}(d) \approx \sqrt{\frac{n_1 + n_2}{n_1 n_2} + \frac{d^2}{2(n_1 + n_2)}}$, so an approximate 95% confidence interval is $d \pm 1.96\,\mathrm{SE}(d)$. A confidence interval is a procedure: across many repetitions, 95% of intervals built this way would contain the true value, assuming the model holds. Small studies give wide intervals, so a "significant" $d$ from a small sample is often compatible with an effect that is tiny or enormous.

### Common claim-evidence mismatches

| Claim in the paper | Why the evidence may not support it |
|---|---|
| "Significant in group A, not in group B, so the groups differ" | The difference between significant and not significant is not itself significant (Gelman and Stern, 2006). Test the difference or the interaction directly. |
| "Region X was active, so the task engaged process Y" | This is reverse inference: X may be active in many processes, so activity supports Y only to the degree that X is selective for Y. |
| "Training improved memory" in a design without random assignment | Correlation or group differences do not establish a causal effect; selection and expectations may explain them. |
| "No effect" from a non-significant result | Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence unless the interval excludes effects that matter or an equivalence test was done. |
| "Brain-based explanation of a behavior" from a correlation in a small sample | Brain-behavior correlations in small samples are noisy and tend to be inflated (later weeks explain why). |

### What to write down

Keep a reading log with a fixed template: question, claim, design, sample, key result with effect size and interval, the one analysis choice you would check, and one question for the authors. A summary in your own words shows whether you understood, and it becomes the first half of the deliverable for weeks 1 and 2: a one-page summary and critique. A critique is fair when it names what the paper does well, states what the evidence supports, and identifies what would be needed to support more.

Journals, fields and labs differ in their conventions (some put methods at the end, some report effect sizes routinely and some do not), so adjust the routine, but keep the order: claim, design, then statistics.

@example
title: From a reported t to an effect size
problem: A paper reports that 20 participants per group differed in a reaction-time task, $t(38) = 2.1$, $p = .04$, and calls the effect "robust." Recover Cohen's $d$ and an approximate 95% confidence interval, and say what the interval implies.
step: Convert: $d = t\sqrt{1/20 + 1/20} = 2.1 \times 0.3162 \approx 0.66$.
step: Compute the standard error: $\sqrt{(40/400) + 0.66^2/(2 \times 40)} = \sqrt{0.100 + 0.0055} \approx 0.325$.
step: Form the interval: $0.66 \pm 1.96 \times 0.325$ gives about $[0.03, 1.30]$.
step: Interpret: the data are compatible with an effect near zero and with a very large one. "Robust" is not supported by a single small study whose interval nearly touches zero.
answer: $d \approx 0.66$ with an approximate 95% interval of $[0.03, 1.30]$: significant, but imprecise.

@example
title: Is the difference between two effects significant?
problem: Group A shows a mean change of $5.0$ units with standard error $2.0$; group B shows $3.0$ with standard error $2.0$. The groups are independent. A is significant at the .05 level and B is not. Do the groups differ?
step: Compute each $z$: A has $5.0/2.0 = 2.5$ (significant); B has $3.0/2.0 = 1.5$ (not significant).
step: Test the difference directly: $5.0 - 3.0 = 2.0$, with standard error $\sqrt{2.0^2 + 2.0^2} \approx 2.83$.
step: Divide: $z = 2.0/2.83 \approx 0.71$, far below $1.96$.
answer: No. The difference is not significant ($z \approx 0.71$): one effect crossing the threshold and the other not tells you nothing about whether they differ.

@mistakes
- Reading the discussion first and then reading the methods to confirm it; you inherit the authors' interpretation before you see the evidence.
- Treating "p < .05" as "large" or "important"; the size and the interval carry that information.
- Calling a paper wrong because of one weakness; a fair critique says which conclusions the evidence still supports.
- Counting trials, neurons or voxels as independent samples when the participants or animals are the units that vary.

@practice num
prompt: A paper reports an independent-samples $t$ test comparing two groups of 30 participants each, $t(58) = 2.8$. Compute Cohen's $d$ using $d = t\sqrt{1/n_1 + 1/n_2}$. Give your answer to two decimal places.
answer: 0.72
tol: 0.01
check: 2.8 * (1 / 30 + 1 / 30) ** 0.5
hint: Put $n_1 = n_2 = 30$ into the formula and take the square root of $1/30 + 1/30$.
solution: $d = 2.8\sqrt{2/30} = 2.8 \times 0.2582 \approx 0.72$, a medium-to-large standardized difference, though its interval with 30 per group is still wide.

@practice mc
prompt: A study reports that a drug-treated group improved significantly on a memory score ($p = .02$) while a placebo group did not ($p = .11$), and concludes that the drug works better than placebo. What is the appropriate analysis?
choice*: Compare the groups directly, for example the change scores or the group by time interaction, and report the difference with an interval.
choice: Run a second test within the placebo group with a larger sample until it also reaches significance.
choice: Accept the conclusion, because a significant result in the treated group is sufficient evidence of a drug effect.
choice: Average the two $p$-values to get one overall significance level.
hint: The claim concerns a difference between groups, so the test must be about that difference.
solution: The claim is comparative, so the comparison itself must be tested. A significant change in one group and a non-significant change in the other can occur when the groups differ little; adding participants until the placebo group is significant is a form of flexible stopping and answers a different question.

@practice ms

@practice num
prompt: Group A shows a mean change of $9.0$ units with standard error $3.0$, and group B shows a mean change of $2.0$ units with standard error $2.0$. The groups are independent. Compute the $z$ statistic for the difference between the two changes, $z = (\Delta_A - \Delta_B)/\sqrt{\mathrm{SE}_A^2 + \mathrm{SE}_B^2}$, to two decimal places. (For reference, $1.96$ is the two-sided .05 cutoff.)
answer: 1.94
tol: 0.01
check: (9 - 2) / (3 ** 2 + 2 ** 2) ** 0.5
hint: The standard error of the difference is the square root of the sum of the squared standard errors.
solution: The difference is $7.0$ and its standard error is $\sqrt{9 + 4} = 3.606$, so $z = 7.0/3.606 \approx 1.94$, just under $1.96$. Here A alone is significant ($z = 3.0$) and B is not ($z = 1.0$), yet the difference between them is itself not quite significant.

@practice self
prompt: Here is an invented abstract. "Twenty-four undergraduates viewed faces while undergoing fMRI. Activity in the amygdala correlated with self-reported social anxiety ($r = .58$, $p = .003$). Because the amygdala processes fear, this shows that social anxiety is caused by an overactive amygdala and suggests that amygdala-targeted treatments will help." Write a structured summary in three sentences (question, evidence, claim) and a critique paragraph naming at least three problems with the claim.
hint: Separate what was measured (a correlation in 24 people) from what is claimed (a cause, a mechanism and a treatment).
solution: Summary: the study asks whether amygdala activity during face viewing relates to social anxiety; in 24 students the activity correlated positively with self-reported anxiety ($r = .58$); the authors conclude that an overactive amygdala causes social anxiety and that amygdala-targeted treatment will help. Critique: first, the design is correlational, so the data cannot show cause; anxiety could change attention to faces and thereby amygdala response, or a third factor could drive both. Second, "the amygdala processes fear" is a reverse inference: the amygdala responds to many things (novelty, salience, ambiguity), so activity does not establish a fear process. Third, with 24 participants the correlation has a wide interval (roughly $.2$ to $.8$) and, if it was one of many correlations examined across regions or measures, it is likely inflated; the abstract does not say whether the amygdala region was chosen before the data were seen. Fourth, undergraduate volunteers and self-report limit generalization to patients, and nothing in the study tests any treatment. A fair reading is that the study offers a preliminary association worth testing in a larger, preregistered sample with a design that can address direction.
rubric: Summary separates the question, the evidence (correlation, $n = 24$) and the claim.
rubric: Identifies that a correlation cannot support a causal claim and names an alternative explanation.
rubric: Identifies reverse inference from amygdala activity to fear, or the small sample and possibly flexible region selection.
rubric: Ends with what the evidence does support and what would be needed to support more.

@quiz num
prompt: In a study with 25 participants per group, both groups have a standard deviation of 15 ms on a reaction-time measure. What is the standard error of the difference between the two group means, $\mathrm{SE} = s\sqrt{1/n_1 + 1/n_2}$, in milliseconds, to two decimal places?
answer: 4.24
tol: 0.01
unit: ms
check: 15 * (1 / 25 + 1 / 25) ** 0.5
hint: With equal standard deviations, the standard error of the difference is $s\sqrt{2/n}$.
solution: $\mathrm{SE} = 15\sqrt{2/25} = 15 \times 0.2828 \approx 4.24$ ms. A difference of about 8.3 ms would be needed for the 95% interval to exclude zero.

@quiz mc
prompt: A paper finds that a prefrontal region is more active during a hard task than an easy task and concludes that this region implements "cognitive control." Which statement best describes the logical weakness?
choice*: It is a reverse inference: the region may be active in many different processes, so its activity is weak evidence for any one of them unless it is selective.
choice: It confuses a one-tailed with a two-tailed test, which invalidates the activation result.
choice: It shows that the BOLD signal cannot measure neural activity, so no inference is possible.
choice: It proves the region is not involved in cognitive control, since activity appeared in both tasks.
hint: Ask how strongly activity in this region implies the presence of that specific process.
solution: Observing activation in a region during a task supports a process only to the degree that the region is selective for that process; otherwise other processes that differ between hard and easy tasks (effort, time on task, arousal) could explain it. The other choices claim more than the logic allows.

@quiz self
prompt: In about 150 words, explain to a new graduate student why the reading order "claim, design, then statistics" is better than reading a paper from start to finish, and give two specific questions to ask at the design stage.
hint: Think about what an early view of the claim lets you check later.
solution: Starting with the claim gives you a yardstick: once you know what the authors say they have shown, you can read the methods and figures asking whether they could show it. Reading front to back lets the introduction and discussion frame your interpretation before you see the evidence, and a persuasive narrative can make weak data feel strong. Reading the design before the statistics lets you judge what comparisons are possible at all, since no analysis can repair a design that cannot answer the question. At the design stage ask: who or what is the unit of analysis and how many of them are there (participants versus trials or neurons)? Was the comparison made by random assignment, or could the groups differ in other ways? Were exclusions and the analysis pipeline decided before the data were seen? Then read the statistics knowing which of the authors' claims they must carry.
rubric: Explains that the claim gives a yardstick against which to judge the evidence.
rubric: Notes the risk of absorbing the authors' framing before seeing the evidence, or that design limits what analyses can show.
rubric: Gives two specific, well-formed design questions.
