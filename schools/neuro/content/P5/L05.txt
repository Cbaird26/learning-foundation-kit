@article
A student plans to compare two groups on "memory performance" and will "analyze the data appropriately." When the data arrive, the groups differ on one of four memory measures, after excluding three participants who "seemed distracted." Everything the student did was defensible, and the result is nearly uninterpretable, because neither the student nor a reader can tell how many other analyses the data could have produced. **Preregistration** and **registered reports** are two ways of making that history visible.

### Preregistration

A preregistration is a time-stamped, read-only record of a study's plan, deposited before the data are examined (or, for existing data, before they are analyzed, with a statement of what was already known). Common registries and platforms include the Open Science Framework and AsPredicted, and ClinicalTrials.gov and similar registries for clinical trials (which many journals and funders require), and PROSPERO for systematic reviews. Funders, journals and institutions differ in what they require, so check the rules that apply to you.

A useful preregistration states the hypotheses (with directions), the design and manipulation, the sample size and its justification, the stopping rule, the measures, the exclusion criteria, the transformations, the exact statistical tests for each hypothesis, the correction for multiple tests and what will be called confirmatory. The test of specificity is whether two analysts reading it would run the same analysis. "We will exclude outliers" is not specific; "we will exclude trials with reaction times below 200 ms or above 3000 ms, and participants with accuracy below 60% in the control condition" is.

### What preregistration does

It separates **confirmatory** from **exploratory** analyses, so readers know which $p$-values are error-controlled; it makes outcome switching, optional stopping and hypothesizing after the results are known visible; and it encourages careful thought before data collection. It does not make a study good: it cannot fix a weak theory, an unreliable measure, a small sample or a vague plan. Some researchers also argue that rigid plans can stifle valuable exploration or give a false sense of rigor; the usual response is that exploratory analyses remain welcome if labeled.

### Deviations

Plans fail: a participant is tested on the wrong day, a measure is unreliable, an assumption is violated. Deviations are acceptable if they are disclosed, justified and, ideally, accompanied by the planned analysis as well as the revised one. Journals and communities have templates for reporting deviations. Hiding them defeats the purpose.

### Registered reports

A **registered report** moves the peer-review decision before the results. The format was introduced at the journal Cortex in 2013 (Chambers) and is now offered by many journals. At **Stage 1**, authors submit the introduction, hypotheses, design, power analysis and analysis plan; reviewers judge the importance of the question and the quality of the plan, and the journal can grant **in-principle acceptance**. After data collection, at **Stage 2**, authors add results and discussion, and the journal publishes the paper regardless of the outcome if the plan was followed (with deviations explained) and the conclusions follow from the evidence. This removes the incentive to produce a significant result in order to publish. A comparison of the published literature in psychology found far fewer positive results in registered reports than in standard articles (Scheel, Schijen and Lakens, 2021), consistent with publication bias in the standard literature. The format is not a fit for every project, and procedures differ by journal.

### Controlling error across several tests

If a study tests $m$ hypotheses and each is tested at $\alpha$, the probability of at least one false positive (the **family-wise error rate**) rises to $1 - (1-\alpha)^m$ for independent tests. The **Bonferroni** correction tests each at $\alpha/m$, which is simple, valid under any dependence and conservative. Holm's step-down procedure is uniformly more powerful and equally valid. Alternatively, a preregistration can designate one primary hypothesis for confirmatory testing and treat others as secondary or exploratory.

@example
title: Correcting for four primary tests
problem: A preregistered study has four primary outcomes, each tested at the family-wise level $\alpha = 0.05$. What is the Bonferroni threshold per test, and what would the family-wise error rate be without correction, if the tests were independent?
step: The Bonferroni threshold is $\alpha/m = 0.05/4 = 0.0125$.
step: Without correction, the family-wise error rate is $1 - 0.95^4 = 1 - 0.8145 = 0.185$.
step: A result with $p = 0.02$ would be significant uncorrected but not significant under Bonferroni.
answer: A threshold of $0.0125$ per test; uncorrected, the family-wise error rate would be about $18.5\%$.

@mistakes
- Writing a preregistration so vague ("appropriate analysis," "outliers excluded as needed") that every outcome is consistent with it.
- Treating preregistration as proof that a study is high quality; it documents the plan and does not repair weak design.
- Hiding deviations or silently dropping a planned analysis; disclosed deviations are acceptable and hidden ones are not.
- Believing that exploratory analyses are forbidden after preregistration; they are welcome if labeled as exploratory.

@practice self
prompt: A preregistration says: "We will test whether bilingual adults are better at attention than monolingual adults using a standard task. We will recruit about 60 people, exclude outliers and analyze the data with the appropriate tests." Write a critique of about 120 words naming at least four ways the plan is too vague to constrain choices, and rewrite the plan in two or three sentences so that two analysts would run the same analysis.
hint: For each vague phrase ask what choices it leaves open (which measure, how many participants and when to stop, which outliers, which test).
solution: Critique: "better at attention" names no measure or direction, so any attention score could be reported; "a standard task" does not identify which task or which outcome (accuracy, reaction time, or an interference cost); "about 60" leaves the sample size and stopping rule open and gives no power justification; "exclude outliers" does not define outliers or say whether exclusion happens before or after seeing the group difference; and "appropriate tests" leaves the test, covariates and correction unspecified. rubric: Identifies at least four distinct vague elements and the choices each leaves open.
rubric: The rewrite specifies the measure, direction, sample size with justification, exclusions and the exact test.
rubric: Distinguishes the one primary confirmatory hypothesis from exploratory analyses.

@quiz self
prompt: In about 200 words, give a balanced assessment of the claim that "preregistration solves the replication problem." Give the strongest case for the claim, the strongest objection, and your conclusion about what it can and cannot fix.
hint: Separate the problem of analytic flexibility, which preregistration addresses, from problems of theory, measurement and power.
solution: The case for the claim is that much of the false-positive inflation comes from analytic flexibility and selective reporting; a time-stamped plan removes that flexibility for confirmatory tests and exposes outcome switching, and registered reports additionally remove publication bias for the studies they cover. The strongest objection is that many failures of replication have other roots: weak theories that do not make specific predictions, unreliable or invalid measures, small samples and heterogeneous populations. Preregistration documents the plan for such a study but does not make it informative, and a preregistered but underpowered study of a vague hypothesis can still mislead. There is also a concern that plans can be written loosely, deviations go unreported, or the practice becomes a box-ticking exercise. My conclusion is that preregistration is a useful tool against one significant cause of unreliable findings, flexible analysis and selective publication, but it works best alongside power analysis, good measurement, theory that makes risky predictions, and data and code sharing; it is not a solution on its own.
rubric: Gives the strongest case (flexibility, outcome switching, publication bias for registered reports) accurately.
rubric: Gives a real objection (theory, measurement, power, vague plans) and takes it seriously.
rubric: Concludes what preregistration can and cannot fix without overstating either.
