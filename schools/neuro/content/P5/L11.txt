@article
A graduate student gives a twelve-minute talk at a lab meeting: eighteen slides, two of them with dense tables, and a conclusion slide shown while the chair holds up a sign reading "stop." The data were good and nobody remembers them. Giving talks is a skill that careers depend on, and most of its rules are about the audience's limited time and attention.

### One message

Ask what you want listeners to remember a day later. Write it as one sentence, such as "Practice speeds responses to trained faces, but the effect is gone when the test location changes." Everything in the talk should serve it. A talk is not a paper read aloud: audiences cannot reread, so repeat the message at the start and the end and omit what does not support it. Because the site has no pictures, imagine each slide as a headline plus one figure: if a slide needs a paragraph to explain it, it is two slides.

### A ten-minute structure

A common allocation for ten minutes is about 1.5 minutes for the question and why it matters, 1.5 to 2 minutes for the design (only what listeners need to read the results), about 4 minutes for two or three results, 1.5 minutes for what it means, its limits and next steps, and a minute of slack. Many speakers need about one minute per content slide. Fields and conferences differ, so check your session's format and any time limits, which chairs enforce.

People speak at roughly 120 to 150 words per minute in a prepared talk; at 130 words per minute, nine minutes of speaking is about $9 \times 130 = 1170$ words, much less than a typical introduction section. Write a script if it helps you, but practice from an outline so that you sound like a person talking.

### Slides

Use a clear headline that states the finding ("Practice speeds responses by 14 ms"), not a topic ("Results"). One idea per slide. Make text large enough to read from the back of the room, with few words, and prefer a labeled figure to a table. In figures, plot the data, label axes with units, make the key comparison obvious (an annotation, a color, a bracket), and remove clutter. About one in twelve men and a smaller share of women have some form of red-green color-vision deficiency, so do not rely on red versus green alone: vary lightness or use patterns or labels, and use palettes designed for accessibility. Show effect sizes with intervals, not only $p$-values, as in a paper. Give credit to collaborators and to sources of figures and data.

### Posters

A poster is read by people walking by, with the author nearby for conversation. Give it a title and a one-sentence takeaway that can be read from a few meters away, a flow from left to right or top to bottom, few words, large figures with legends that stand alone, and a QR code or short link for the full paper or data, if the venue allows. Prepare a thirty-second and a three-minute spoken version.

### Recorded talks

The week 11-12 deliverable is a recorded ten-minute talk on a paper. Recording adds technical rules: use a quiet room, put the microphone close, use the camera at eye level if you are on camera, check that your slides are readable in the recording, speak a little slower than normal and leave a short pause at slide changes. Consider adding captions, and check what your venue requires for accessibility. Record a trial, listen to it and time it.

### Questions

Prepare for the questions you expect, including the main weakness of your study. Listen to the whole question, repeat or rephrase it for the room, answer what was asked, and say "I do not know, but here is how I would find out" when that is true. A hostile or confused question is usually best answered briefly and politely: thank the person, answer the factual part and offer to discuss it afterward. Do not defend yourself against a point that is correct; say what it changes.

### Rehearse

Rehearse aloud with the slides and a timer, ideally in front of colleagues who can tell you which slide lost them. Cut a slide or a section if you run over; do not speak faster. Most talks improve a great deal over the first few rehearsals.

@example
title: Budgeting a ten-minute talk
problem: You have 10 minutes. Allocate the time to the sections, decide how many slides you can show at roughly one minute per slide, and find the average time per slide.
step: Allocate time: question 1.5 min, design 2 min, results 4 min, meaning and limits 1.5 min, slack 1 min, for a total of 10 min.
step: Assign slides at about a minute each: 1 for the question, 2 for the design, 4 for the results and 1 for the meaning and limits, so 8 slides in all.
step: Compute the speaking time: the 9 minutes without the slack, so the average is $9/8 \approx 1.1$ minutes per slide, close to the one-minute guideline.
step: If you find yourself with 12 slides, the average falls below 45 seconds, and the cure is to cut slides, not to speed up.
answer: Eight slides in 9 minutes of speaking (about 1.1 minutes each), with 1 minute of slack.

@mistakes
- Putting the whole paper into a ten-minute talk instead of one message and the evidence for it.
- Using headlines such as "Results" or "Methods" instead of stating the finding; the audience reads the headline first.
- Relying on red versus green, or on tiny fonts, to carry the key comparison.
- Speaking faster to fit more material in; cutting material is almost always better.

@practice self
prompt: Plan a ten-minute talk on this invented study: "Forty-eight volunteers learned to search for a target among distractors, with the target's color either predictive or not predictive of its location. Search was 85 ms faster when color predicted location (95% CI [40, 130] ms), but the benefit disappeared after the colors were swapped." Write the one-sentence message, a slide-by-slide outline (headline and what the figure shows) with times that total ten minutes, and the two questions you most expect and how you would answer them.
hint: Start from the message, then choose the fewest slides that support it, and give each a finding-style headline and a time.
solution: Message: people quickly learn to use a predictive color to guide search, but the learning is tied to the specific colors and does not transfer when they are swapped. Outline: (1) Opening, 1.0 min: headline "Can we learn which features predict where a target is?" with a picture of a search display. (2) Design, 1.5 min: "Color predicted the target's location for half of the 48 volunteers" with a diagram of the two conditions. (3) Result 1, 1.5 min: "Search was 85 ms faster when color predicted location" with individual-participant points, means and the 95% interval [40, 130] ms. (4) Result 2, 2.0 min: "The benefit vanished when the colors were swapped" with the same plot before and after the swap. (5) Result 3, 1.0 min: "The effect was present in most participants" with a paired-line plot. (6) Meaning and limits, 1.5 min: "Learning is feature-specific; one task and student volunteers" with next steps. (7) Closing and thanks, 0.5 min, repeating the message. (8) Slack, 1.0 min. Total: 10.0 min. Expected questions: "Could the swap effect be due to fatigue?" I would answer that the order was fixed, so fatigue cannot be excluded, but accuracy did not change, and a counterbalanced follow-up would address it. "Why is the interval so wide?" I would answer that with 48 participants the effect is estimated imprecisely, that even the lower limit of 40 ms is meaningful in search, and that a larger sample is planned.
rubric: States one clear message that the slides all serve.
rubric: Gives headlines that state findings, figures described concretely, and times that sum to ten minutes.
rubric: Anticipates two plausible questions, including a real limitation, and answers honestly.

@quiz self
prompt: A colleague's slide shows a table with 12 rows and 6 columns of means and $p$-values, headed "Results." In about 150 words, explain how you would redesign it for a ten-minute talk and why.
hint: Decide what the audience should take from the slide, then choose a figure and a headline that deliver it.
solution: I would first choose the single comparison that serves the talk's message, for example the difference between two conditions, and make that the slide. Instead of a table, I would show a figure with the individual participants' values as points, the group means and 95% confidence intervals, with axes labeled in units, and annotate the key difference (for example, a bracket labeled "14 ms, 95% CI [5, 23]"). The headline would state the finding, such as "Practice speeds responses by 14 ms," instead of "Results." The remaining rows could go in a backup slide or the paper, because listeners cannot read 72 numbers in under a minute and will stop listening while they try. If there are two or three comparisons that matter, each gets its own slide with the same layout so that the audience learns to read the plot once. A table is justified only when exact values matter, and then it should have three or four rows and a highlighted row.
rubric: Chooses the comparison that serves the message, and moves the other numbers to backup.
rubric: Proposes a figure that shows data, uncertainty and units and a finding-style headline.
rubric: Explains the audience's limits (cannot reread, cannot read many numbers quickly).
