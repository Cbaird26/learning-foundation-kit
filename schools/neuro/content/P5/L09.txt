@article
Two papers report the same experiment. One says, "Training changes how the brain represents faces." The other says, "In 36 adults, 5 days of face-matching practice was followed by a 14-ms faster response and a small increase in a face-selective region's response to trained faces (difference 0.12 percent signal change, 95% CI [0.02, 0.22]); with no untrained control group, we cannot separate training from repeated testing." The second paper is less dramatic and much more useful. Writing well in science means telling the reader exactly what you found and how sure you are.

### Structure around one claim

A paper is an argument for a claim. Decide the claim before you write, in one sentence that your title or abstract could carry, and ask of every section whether it serves that claim.

- **Introduction:** the broad problem, what is known, the specific gap or conflict, and the question (with hypotheses) your study addresses. Funnel from general to specific, and cite what a reader needs, not all you have read.
- **Methods:** enough that a competent reader could repeat the study: participants and recruitment, ethics approval and consent, design, materials, procedure, exclusions, the sample size justification, the preregistered or exploratory status of each analysis, and the analysis in full detail. Follow the reporting guideline for your design, because they list what readers need: CONSORT (updated as CONSORT 2025) for randomized trials, ARRIVE 2.0 for animal research, and others for other designs.
- **Results:** state what you found in the order of the questions, with effect sizes and intervals, and keep interpretation for the discussion. Separate confirmatory from exploratory findings and report all planned outcomes, including those that did not support your hypothesis.
- **Discussion:** summarize the main result, relate it to prior work, give alternative explanations, state limitations honestly (including those that would change the conclusion) and say what should be done next.
- **Abstract:** the question, the design and sample, the main result with a number, and the conclusion calibrated to the evidence. Many readers see only the abstract.

Journals and fields differ in order, length and style, so read the author instructions and recent papers in your target journal.

### Calibrating claims

The verbs in your sentences carry causal and certainty claims. "Training improved performance" is causal and needs a design that supports it (random assignment, a control group). "Was associated with" describes a correlation. "Suggests" signals that the evidence is partial. Use "show" and "demonstrate" sparingly. Avoid generalizing from a sample to "humans" or "the brain" when the sample is 30 undergraduates, and say what population and task you studied. A nonsignificant result is not "no effect": report the interval and what effects it excludes. Do not present exploratory findings as predictions, and do not write the introduction after the results so that it appears to predict them.

### Figures that show the data

Show the data, not only summaries. Bar charts of means with error bars hide distributions, and a similar bar can arise from very different data (Weissgerber and colleagues, 2015, urged showing individual points, especially in small samples). Good practice: plot individual participants or trials (dots, jittered, or lines connecting paired measurements), add a summary and an interval, label axes with units, state $n$ and what the error bars show (standard deviation, standard error or 95% confidence interval) in the legend, use a baseline of zero for bars, and make each figure understandable with its legend alone. Color choices should survive grayscale printing and color-vision deficiency. Because the site shows no images here, imagine each figure as a table of values: if you cannot say in one sentence what the figure shows, redesign it.

### Reporting statistics

Report the test, the degrees of freedom, the effect size with an interval, and the exact $p$-value (for instance $p = .032$, not "$p < .05$"), and say whether the test was one- or two-sided. For a difference in means, the 95% interval is approximately $\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2 \pm 1.96\,\mathrm{SE}$ with the standard error of the difference. Provide the descriptive statistics so that others can use them in meta-analyses.

### Authorship and credit

Agree on authorship early. The CRediT taxonomy lists 14 contributor roles, and many journals ask for them. Journals' criteria for authorship differ, so check the ones you target. Share the preprint where the field allows it, and cite data and code you reuse.

@example
title: Reporting a difference with its interval
problem: In a study, the mean reaction time was 8.0 ms faster after training, with a standard error of the difference of $2.5$ ms. Write the result as it should appear, and say what the interval shows.
step: Compute the interval: $8.0 \pm 1.96 \times 2.5 = 8.0 \pm 4.9$, so $[3.1, 12.9]$ ms.
step: Write the sentence: "Responses were 8.0 ms faster after training (95% CI [3.1, 12.9] ms)."
step: Interpret: the data are compatible with a benefit from about 3 ms to about 13 ms, so a claim of a "large" improvement is not supported without a judgment about what size matters.
answer: 8.0 ms faster, 95% CI [3.1, 12.9] ms; the lower limit is small and the upper limit is four times larger.

@mistakes
- Using causal verbs ("improves," "causes," "drives") for correlational or uncontrolled designs.
- Showing only bar charts of means with error bars, without individual data or a statement of what the bars represent.
- Reporting only "$p < .05$" and a star, with no effect size or interval.
- Rewriting the introduction after seeing the results so the findings appear predicted.

tol: 0.01
unit: ms
check: 12.0 + 1.96 * 3.5

@practice num
prompt: Group 1 has a mean reaction time of 520 ms and group 2 has 498 ms, with a pooled standard deviation of 40 ms. What is Cohen's $d$, $(M_1 - M_2)/s_{\text{pooled}}$, to two decimal places?
answer: 0.55
tol: 0.01
check: (520 - 498) / 40
hint: Subtract the means and divide by the pooled standard deviation.
solution: The mean difference is $22$ ms, and $22/40 = 0.55$, a medium effect by conventional benchmarks that readers should judge against effects that matter in the field.

@practice self
prompt: Rewrite this invented abstract in about 100 words so that its claims are calibrated: "We show that meditation rewires the brain. In 20 meditators and 20 non-meditators, meditators had greater cortical thickness in a prefrontal region ($p = .03$). This proves that meditation increases brain size and will protect against aging." Then list in two or three sentences what the design can and cannot support.
hint: Note the design (cross-sectional, groups not randomized), the sample and the strength of the verbs.
solution: Rewrite: "In a cross-sectional comparison of 20 adults who meditated regularly and 20 who did not, meditators had greater cortical thickness in a prefrontal region (difference and 95% interval reported in the paper; $p = .03$ for one of several regions examined). The groups were not randomly assigned and were not compared before the meditators began practicing, so the difference may reflect training, preexisting differences or other differences between the groups such as age, education or lifestyle. The sample is small and the result needs replication, ideally with a preregistered region." What the design can support: an association between regular meditation and thickness in this sample at one time. What it cannot support: that meditation changed the brain, increased its size, or protects against aging, since it has no longitudinal data, no randomization and no measure of aging outcomes. The words "rewires," "proves" and "will protect" go beyond any such design.
rubric: Removes causal and certainty language ("rewires," "proves," "will protect") and replaces it with associative claims.
rubric: Names the sample, design and one concrete alternative explanation (selection, confounds, age).
rubric: States clearly what the design can and cannot support.

@quiz num
prompt: A paper reports a 95% confidence interval for a mean difference of $[2.0, 10.0]$ ms. Assuming the interval is $\text{difference} \pm 1.96\,\mathrm{SE}$, what is the standard error of the difference, in milliseconds, to two decimal places?
answer: 2.04
tol: 0.01
unit: ms
check: (10.0 - 2.0) / (2 * 1.96)
hint: The width of the interval is $2 \times 1.96 \times \mathrm{SE}$.
solution: The width is $8.0$ ms, so $\mathrm{SE} = 8.0/(2 \times 1.96) \approx 2.04$ ms; the mean difference is the midpoint, $6.0$ ms.

@quiz mc
prompt: A researcher notices after analyzing the data that the strongest result is for an outcome she had not planned to emphasize. In the paper she writes the introduction so that this outcome appears to be the main hypothesis. What is the problem?
choice*: This is hypothesizing after the results are known (HARKing): it presents an exploratory finding as a confirmatory prediction and misleads readers about the error rate.
choice: Nothing; introductions should always be written after the results.
choice: The problem is only stylistic, because the results are unchanged.
choice: The problem is that the strongest result has a small $p$-value.
hint: What does the reader believe about when the hypothesis was formed?
solution: Presenting a post hoc finding as a prediction hides the analytic flexibility behind it and makes the $p$-value look error-controlled. The honest approach is to label the result exploratory.

@quiz self
prompt: A figure shows a bar chart of the mean performance of two groups (n = 12 each), with error bars. The legend says only "Performance in the two groups." In about 150 words, critique the figure and describe a redesigned figure and legend.
hint: Consider what is hidden by a bar chart, what the legend omits and what a reader needs to judge the difference.
solution: The bar chart hides the distribution: with 12 per group, a reader cannot tell whether the means reflect a shift of the whole group, a few outliers or a bimodal pattern. The legend does not say what "performance" is (units, scale), what the error bars show, or how many participants each bar has, so the reader cannot judge the uncertainty. I would plot each participant as a point, jittered horizontally and drawn beside the group mean with a 95% confidence interval; if the measures are paired, I would connect each participant's two values with a line. The y-axis would be labeled with the measure and its units, and the legend would say: "Accuracy (percent correct) for each of 12 participants per group. Points are individual participants; horizontal lines show group means; vertical bars show 95% confidence intervals. The difference between means was X (95% CI [a, b])." Colors would differ in lightness as well as hue so that they are readable in grayscale.
rubric: Identifies that the bar chart hides the distribution and that the legend omits units, n and the meaning of the error bars.
rubric: Proposes showing individual data with a summary and an interval.
rubric: Gives a complete legend that includes the measure, n, what the intervals show and the effect with its interval.
