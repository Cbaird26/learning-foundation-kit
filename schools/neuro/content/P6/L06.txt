@article
A short paper is an argument with evidence. In a reanalysis, the argument is small and clear: "We tried to reproduce result X from open data using plan P. This is what we found, with this uncertainty, and these are the limits." Everything in the paper should serve that sentence. Four to six pages is enough; the discipline of length forces you to decide what matters.

### The skeleton

Journals differ in their formats, but a short empirical paper usually has these parts.

- **Title.** Says what was done: a reproduction, an extension, the data.
- **Abstract.** About 150 to 250 words: the question, the data, the method, the main numerical result with its interval, and the conclusion with its limit. Write it last.
- **Introduction.** One to three paragraphs: what the original paper claimed, why it is worth reproducing, and what you did. Cite the original precisely.
- **Methods.** Enough detail that a competent stranger could repeat your work: data set, version, license and participants; the exclusion flow; preprocessing and statistics, with software versions; the plan and the deviations; the extension and its decision rule.
- **Results.** The planned result first, then the comparison with the original, then the extension. Report numbers in text, with a table or figure for the main result.
- **Discussion.** What the results mean, what they do not show, limitations, and what a next step would be. Do not repeat the results at length.
- **Data and code availability.** Where the code lives and how to run it.
- **References and acknowledgments.** Cite the original paper, the data set and its creators, and the software you used.

### Writing the methods

The test for a methods section is whether someone could repeat the analysis from it and the data alone. State numbers, not adjectives: "participants with fewer than 40 valid trials (of 120) were excluded" rather than "poor data were removed". Report what was decided before the analysis and what after, with a short list of deviations. Mention software and versions. If you must choose between brevity and a reproducible description, put the detail in a supplement or in the code's README, and point to it.

### Writing the results

Report the estimate, the sample size and the uncertainty, in that order of importance. For each main result give an effect size in interpretable units (milliseconds, percent signal change, a correlation) and an interval, not just a p-value or the word significant. A common form is: "the difference was 14 ms (95% interval 3 to 25 ms; $d_z = 0.41$, $n = 38$)". For two independent groups with means $\bar{x}_1, \bar{x}_2$, standard deviations $s_1, s_2$ and sample sizes $n_1, n_2$, the standardized effect (Cohen's $d$) divides the difference by the pooled standard deviation:

$$d = \frac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{s_p}, \qquad s_p = \sqrt{\frac{(n_1 - 1)s_1^2 + (n_2 - 1)s_2^2}{n_1 + n_2 - 2}} .$$

Figures should show the data, not only summaries: individual participants' values, or distributions, with intervals marked, and axes labeled with units. Say what each error bar represents. Put exploratory results under their own heading with a plain label, and give the same detail to null and mixed results as to those that agree.

### Make the paper reproducible

A reproduction paper that cannot itself be reproduced would be ironic. Put the code in a public repository with a README that says how to obtain the data, which software and versions are needed, and the commands to run in order, with an estimate of run time. Archiving a tagged release of the code in a service that issues a persistent identifier makes it citable. The FAIR principles (Wilkinson and colleagues, 2016) ask that data and code be findable, accessible, interoperable and reusable. Check the license of the data and of the original code, and give credit: cite the data set and its creators as its page asks, and cite the original paper. Rules about sharing and public access differ by funder, journal and institution, so check those that apply to you.

### Tone and claims

Match the strength of your claims to the strength of the evidence. "Our estimate was smaller than the original, and the data are compatible with effects from near zero to as large as the original" is accurate; "the original finding does not replicate" claims more than a single reanalysis shows. Be generous and exact about the original authors' work: say what they did, and what you could not determine from their description. Describe differences factually. A reanalysis is part of a conversation, not a verdict. Authorship, the use of a preprint server and the handling of comments differ between labs and programs, so agree these with your advisor.

@example
title: A results sentence with uncertainty
problem: Twelve participants contributed paired differences with mean 14 ms and standard error 5 ms. The original reported 24 ms. Write the key results sentence using a 95% interval based on 1.96 standard errors, and decide how to describe the comparison.
step: Compute the interval: $14 \pm 1.96 \times 5 = 14 \pm 9.8$, so 4.2 to 23.8 ms.
step: Compare with the original: 24 ms lies just above the upper limit, so the data are in mild tension with the original estimate but not far from it.
step: Write: "The mean difference was 14 ms (95% interval 4 to 24 ms; $n = 12$), smaller than the 24 ms originally reported; the original value lies at the edge of this interval."
step: Avoid claiming failure or success: with 12 participants the interval is wide, and the sentence should say that more data would be needed to tell the estimates apart.
answer: "The mean difference was 14 ms (95% interval 4 to 24 ms; $n = 12$), smaller than the original 24 ms, which lies at the edge of the interval."

@mistakes
- Reporting only p-values or the word "significant", without the estimate, its interval and the sample size.
- A methods section with adjectives instead of numbers, so the analysis cannot be repeated.
- Mixing planned and exploratory results in one narrative, so that the reader cannot tell which were chosen in advance.
- Claiming that the original "does not replicate" or "is confirmed" on the strength of a single, small reanalysis.

@practice self
prompt: Draft a methods paragraph and a results paragraph (about 200 words together) for this invented reanalysis: open reaction-time data from 40 participants; plan: paired comparison of two conditions after discarding trials below 200 ms and participants with accuracy under 60% (two excluded); the planned estimate was 31 ms (standard error 8 ms, $n = 38$); the original paper reported 38 ms; one deviation, trimming trials above 2000 ms, which the paper did not specify; the sensitivity analysis without that trimming gave 33 ms.
hint: Use numbers, give the interval from 1.96 standard errors, state the deviation and keep the claim modest.
solution: Methods. We analyzed trial-level reaction times from 40 participants in the archived data set (version and license stated in the data section). Following the plan written before analysis, trials below 200 ms were discarded, and participants with accuracy below 60% were excluded (two participants), leaving 38. We computed each participant's mean reaction time in the two conditions and the paired difference, and report its mean, standard error and 95% interval (1.96 standard errors). One choice was not specified by the original paper and was added during analysis: trials above 2000 ms were removed; we report the analysis without this trimming as a sensitivity check. Results. The mean difference was 31 ms (standard error 8 ms; 95% interval 15 to 47 ms; $n = 38$), compared with 38 ms in the original report, which lies within our interval. Without the 2000 ms trimming the difference was 33 ms. We therefore regard the planned result as reproduced to within the uncertainty of this sample, while noting that the interval is wide.
rubric: Methods give the numbers needed to repeat the analysis (exclusion thresholds, counts, the statistic, software or data pointers).
rubric: Results give the estimate, standard error or interval (15 to 47 ms) and sample size, and compare with the original.
rubric: The deviation and sensitivity analysis are stated as such.
rubric: The claim is modest and matches the uncertainty.
