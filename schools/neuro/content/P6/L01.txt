@article
You are about to spend several weeks of your first research year on one published result. The most expensive mistake you can make is the first one: choosing a result that cannot be reproduced for reasons that have nothing to do with the science, such as data that are not really available, an analysis that is described in one sentence, or a computation that needs a cluster. A good choice takes an afternoon of careful screening and saves weeks.

### Three words that are used loosely

Usage differs between fields, so state your own definitions. A common set, used in a 2019 report of the US National Academies, is this. **Reproducibility** means obtaining consistent results with the same data and the same computational steps. **Replicability** means obtaining consistent results in a new study that collects its own data to answer the same question. **Robustness** (a term that varies more) means that a conclusion survives reasonable changes to the analysis. A reanalysis of an open data set tests reproducibility first. When you then change a choice, you test robustness. If you apply the same analysis to a different open data set of the same kind, you are doing something closer to a replication, although you did not collect the data. Say which of these you are doing in the first sentence of your plan.

### What makes a candidate feasible

Screen each candidate on five questions.

1. **Are the data really open?** The data set should be downloadable without a special agreement, with a documented structure (for neuroimaging, often BIDS), a participant table and a license. Check that the files the paper used are among the files you can get: some archives hold only the raw data, some only derivatives.
2. **Does the paper state a target?** You need a number: a mean difference, a correlation, a regression coefficient, a classification accuracy, with a test statistic, a sample size and ideally an interval. A claim such as "region X is involved in Y" is not a target; a statement such as "the difference in reaction time was 24 ms, $t(27) = 3.5$" is.
3. **Is the analysis described well enough?** Preprocessing steps, exclusions, the model and the software should be stated. Shared code is a large advantage. Vague methods are a reason to pick another paper, not a reason to guess.
4. **Is the computing modest?** Estimate storage and run time before you download anything. Raw functional MRI is large; behavioral data, event-related potentials from a few dozen participants, or already-preprocessed derivatives are often practical on a laptop.
5. **Is the result worth the effort?** Choose a result that matters to the field or to your future thesis, but not one whose reproduction would need a skill you cannot learn in this time.

Practice varies on one more point: whether to tell the original authors. A courteous message asking a specific question about a method is normal and often welcome, but you should not wait for permission to analyze open data. Check your program's and lab's norms.

### From a reported statistic to a target

Papers report different things, so convert them to a common scale. For a paired or one-sample design with $n$ participants, the standardized effect size is $d_z = t/\sqrt{n}$, and the standard error of the mean difference is the mean difference divided by $t$. A 95% interval for the mean difference is the estimate $\pm$ the critical value of $t$ (with $n-1$ degrees of freedom) times the standard error. Writing down the original's estimate and interval before you touch the data fixes the target and prevents you from deciding afterward what counts as agreement.

### A screening table

Score each candidate 0 (fails), 1 (partly) or 2 (clearly) on each criterion and add them up. The sum is not a measurement, only a way to make your reasoning visible and comparable. A zero on data availability or on the numerical target rules a candidate out whatever the total.

| criterion | candidate A | candidate B |
|---|---|---|
| open data with documented structure | 2 | 2 |
| numerical target stated | 2 | 1 |
| analysis described, code shared | 1 | 0 |
| modest computing | 2 | 1 |
| value to you | 1 | 2 |
| **total** | **8** | **6** |

Candidate A is the better first reanalysis even though candidate B may be more exciting; you can attempt B later with the skills you gain.

@example
title: Turning a reported result into a target
problem: A paper reports that in 28 participants, reaction times were 18 ms slower in one condition than another, $t(27) = 3.5$ (paired test). Compute the standardized effect size, the standard error of the mean difference, and the 95% interval you will use as the target (the critical value of $t$ with 27 degrees of freedom is 2.052).
step: Compute the standardized effect size: $d_z = t/\sqrt{n} = 3.5/\sqrt{28} = 3.5/5.292 \approx 0.66$.
step: Recover the standard error from the estimate and the test statistic: $\text{SE} = 18/3.5 \approx 5.14$ ms.
step: Build the interval: $18 \pm 2.052 \times 5.14 = 18 \pm 10.55$, so $[7.4, 28.6]$ ms.
step: Record the target before analyzing: "estimate near 18 ms, interval about 7 to 29 ms, $d_z \approx 0.66$". A reanalysis estimate of 17 ms agrees; one of 5 ms does not.
answer: $d_z \approx 0.66$, SE $\approx 5.14$ ms, target interval about $[7.4, 28.6]$ ms.

@mistakes
- Choosing a paper because its claim is interesting without checking that the numbers you need (estimate, sample size, interval) are reported.
- Assuming that open data means the same data the paper analyzed: archives may hold raw files while the paper used a filtered subset, so compare participant counts first.
- Starting the download before estimating storage and run time, then discovering that the computation does not fit on your machine.
- Deciding what counts as agreement after seeing your own estimate.

@practice num
prompt: A candidate data set has 36 participants with 2.4 GB of raw data each. Preprocessing creates derived files that take 1.5 times the size of the raw data. How much disk space, in gigabytes, do the raw and derived data need together?
answer: 216
unit: GB
tol: 0.01
check: 36 * 2.4 * (1 + 1.5)
hint: The derived files are 1.5 times the raw size, so each participant needs 1 + 1.5 times 2.4 GB.
solution: Each participant needs $2.4 \times (1 + 1.5) = 6.0$ GB, and $36 \times 6.0 = 216$ GB. Compare this with the free space on your machine before downloading.

@practice num
prompt: A paper reports a paired test with $t(19) = 2.8$ in 20 participants. What is the standardized effect size $d_z = t/\sqrt{n}$? Give your answer to two decimal places.
answer: 0.63
tol: 0.01
check: 2.8 / sqrt(20)
hint: Degrees of freedom are $n - 1$, so $n = 20$.
solution: $d_z = 2.8/\sqrt{20} = 2.8/4.472 \approx 0.63$, a medium-sized effect that you can compare with your own estimate.

@practice self
prompt: Describe, in 150 to 250 words, how you would screen two candidate papers (invent plausible details: one with shared code and an electroencephalography data set of 30 participants, one with a large functional MRI data set and a method described in two sentences). Name your criteria, give a score or a verdict for each paper, and say which you would choose and why.
hint: Use the five screening questions and decide in advance which failures rule a candidate out.
solution: I would screen on five criteria: open and documented data, a numerical target, an analysis described well enough to repeat, modest computing, and value to my own work. The electroencephalography paper scores well on the first four: the data are open, the shared code removes guesswork about preprocessing, and 30 participants of electroencephalography fit on a laptop. Its target is a specific amplitude difference with a test statistic, which I can write down as an interval before I begin. The functional MRI paper may be more interesting, but a two-sentence method means I would be guessing preprocessing choices, and I would have to estimate a long run time and large storage before I knew whether it fit. Because a described analysis and feasible computing are conditions for a clean reproduction, I would choose the electroencephalography paper first, record its estimate and interval as my target, and keep the functional MRI paper for later, after I have more experience, perhaps after asking the authors a specific question about their pipeline. I would also check that the participants in the open files match the paper's sample.

rubric: Rules a candidate out or in for a stated reason, such as an undescribed method or infeasible computing, rather than by preference alone.
rubric: Plans to record the target estimate and check the participant count before analysis.

@quiz num
prompt: A reanalysis of 24 participants needs preprocessing that takes 35 minutes per participant on your computer. How many hours of preprocessing will one complete run need?
answer: 14
unit: hours
check: 24 * 35 / 60
hint: Multiply the participants by the minutes, then convert minutes to hours.
solution: $24 \times 35 = 840$ minutes, which is $840/60 = 14$ hours. Plan for several runs, because you will probably need to rerun after fixing mistakes.

@quiz mc
prompt: Using the common definitions in a 2019 National Academies report, a reanalysis of a published study's own open data with the same computational steps tests which of these?
choice*: Reproducibility: whether the same data and computational steps give consistent results.
choice: Replicability: whether a new study with new data gives consistent results.
choice: Construct validity: whether the task measures the intended concept.
choice: External validity: whether the finding generalizes to other populations.
hint: Ask whether any new data are collected.
solution: With the same data and the same steps, the question is whether the computation gives consistent results, which is reproducibility. Replicability requires new data, and the validity questions concern what the measurement means and where it applies.

@quiz self
prompt: A student plans to reproduce a result and says, "I'll decide how close is close enough once I see my numbers." Explain what is wrong with this plan and how to fix it, using a reported estimate of 0.45 with interval $[0.20, 0.70]$ as an example.
hint: Compare deciding before and after seeing the result.
solution: If the criterion is chosen after the result is seen, almost any outcome can be described as agreement or as failure, so the reanalysis cannot be wrong and teaches nothing. The fix is to write the target and the agreement rule before analysis: for example, "the new estimate must have the same sign and lie within the original interval $[0.20, 0.70]$, and I will also report the difference between the two estimates." With this rule an estimate of 0.10 clearly fails and 0.40 clearly passes, whatever I hoped for. I would also record the rule with a date in the plan or a version-controlled file, so the order of events is visible to readers.
rubric: Explains that a criterion chosen after the result makes any outcome compatible and so removes the test.
rubric: Gives a concrete agreement rule stated in terms of the example interval.
rubric: Says the rule must be recorded before analysis.
