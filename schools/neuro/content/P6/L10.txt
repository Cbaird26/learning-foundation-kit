@article
An oral examination is a conversation under pressure, and it is graded as one. Formats differ greatly: a committee of three to five faculty, a prepared presentation followed by questions, questions on a reading list, a defense of a written proposal, or no oral component at all. Some programs give a pass, a conditional pass or a fail, with a chance to retake; the rules, and what happens after a poor performance, differ by program and country. Ask your advisor and the graduate office for the format, the rules and past experience, and ask former students what they were asked. This week gives you tools that work in most formats.

### What examiners are looking for

Faculty are usually checking four things: that you know the central facts and methods of the field accurately; that you can reason from them to new questions; that you can see the limits of evidence, including your own knowledge; and that you could be trusted to work independently, which means you can say "I would check that" as well as "I know". A candidate who recites more than the question asked often does worse than one who answers the question and then stops.

### Turn the list into a map

A reading list of 30 to 60 papers is too long to remember as text. For each paper write a five-line card: the **question**, the **method** (design, measure, sample), the **main result** with a number if you can, the **limits or competing interpretations**, and **what it changed** in the field. Then group cards by theme and draw the links: which paper's limit motivated which later study? Examiners often ask about connections ("how does this result bear on that debate?"), and a map gives you the answer. Know the effect, the control that makes it interpretable, and the figure's axes: committee members will often ask what a plot shows.

### The shape of a spoken answer

A reliable structure has five beats, and each takes from ten seconds to a minute:

1. **Clarify** the question if needed ("Do you mean the cellular mechanism or the behavioral evidence?").
2. **Claim:** give the answer in one sentence.
3. **Mechanism or argument:** the steps, in order, at the level asked.
4. **Evidence:** one or two key experiments, with what they showed and the control.
5. **Limits and next step:** what is unsettled and how you would test it.

Draw or write on the board when a picture is faster than words: a circuit, a time course, an axis labeled with units. Think aloud in a way that lets the examiner follow, and pause to think; silence for ten seconds feels long to you and is normal to them.

### Follow-ups, pushback and not knowing

Follow-up questions are the examination, not a sign of failure: examiners keep going until they find the edge of your knowledge, and the edge is expected. When you do not know, say so, then reason: "I am not sure of the number, but the time constant should be in the tens of milliseconds, because the membrane resistance and capacitance are of this order; I would check it." Never bluff; examiners can tell, and a wrong confident answer costs more than an honest limit. When an examiner pushes back, ask whether it is a challenge to test your reasoning or a correction. If it is a challenge, defend your position with evidence or concede a specific point gracefully; if it is a correction, accept it and adjust. Do not argue about opinions where informed researchers disagree: state the strongest versions of each view and say what evidence would help.

### Practice that works

Reading cards is recognition; examinations require recall and speech. Practice retrieving from memory, speaking aloud, with a timer. **Spaced retrieval** (reviewing at increasing gaps, for example after a day, then after two, four and eight more days) beats rereading in the long run. Hold mock orals with fellow students or a faculty volunteer, ask them to interrupt, and record yourself to hear filler words and long answers. Start early enough that the last week is for review and rest. A completion test for this course is to explain one major idea aloud, without notes, to a listener who stops you with questions.

### A note on well-being

Orals are stressful and your preparation should include sleep, breaks and people you can talk to. If stress becomes overwhelming, your university's counseling services exist for this purpose.

title: Budgeting an oral examination
problem: A 90-minute oral examination has a 10-minute opening statement, a 5-minute closing discussion and six questions of equal time. How long can each question take?
step: Subtract the fixed parts: $90 - 10 - 5 = 75$ minutes.
step: Divide equally among the six questions: $75/6 = 12.5$ minutes each.
step: Plan a five-beat answer inside this window: about 1 minute to clarify and claim, 5 for mechanism and evidence, 2 for limits, leaving about 4.5 minutes for follow-ups.
answer: About 12.5 minutes per question, including follow-ups.

@example
title: A spaced retrieval schedule
problem: You first study a topic on day 0 and plan reviews after gaps of 1, 2, 4 and 8 days, each gap counted from the previous review. On which days do the reviews occur?
step: First review: $0 + 1 = $ day 1.
step: Second review: $1 + 2 = $ day 3.
step: Third review: $3 + 4 = $ day 7.
step: Fourth review: $7 + 8 = $ day 15. Record each in a calendar.
answer: Days 1, 3, 7 and 15.

@mistakes
- Preparing only by rereading papers and notes, which builds recognition and not the ability to speak from memory.
- Answering with everything you know on a topic, instead of the answer to the question asked, and then losing control of the time.
- Bluffing a number or a result you do not know, instead of reasoning aloud and flagging what you would check.
- Treating a challenging follow-up as a sign of failure, or arguing about a contested question as if it were settled.

@practice self
prompt: Mock oral (about 250 words, written as you would say it): an examiner asks, "How would you test whether hippocampal sharp-wave ripples are causally important for memory consolidation?" Answer using the five beats: clarify, claim, mechanism or argument, evidence, limits and next step.
hint: Think of recording ripples to show correlation, of disrupting them online to test necessity, and of controls for the disruption itself.
solution: Clarify: I will take it that the question concerns spatial memory in rodents and the ripples seen in the hippocampus during rest and sleep. Claim: correlation is not enough; I would detect ripples as they happen and disrupt them, and ask whether memory suffers. Mechanism: ripples, brief high-frequency events in the hippocampal local field potential, are accompanied by replay of activity sequences from earlier experience, which is why they are proposed as a consolidation mechanism. Design: train animals on a spatial task; during rest after training, record the field potential, detect ripples in real time and deliver a brief electrical or optical perturbation that disrupts them. Compare with a control group that gets the same number of perturbations at delayed times, outside ripples, so that any effect of the stimulation itself, such as arousal or tissue effects, is matched. Test memory the next day. Evidence: studies using closed-loop ripple disruption have reported impaired learning of spatial tasks relative to delayed-stimulation controls, which supports necessity. Limits: disruption may remove more than ripples; the effect may reflect interference with other activity; and necessity for one task does not show what ripples carry. A next step would be to disrupt only ripples that follow particular cell sequences, or to read out replay content before and after the disruption.
rubric: Clarifies scope and states a claim (necessity needs a causal manipulation, not only correlation).
rubric: Describes a closed-loop design with a matched control (delayed or out-of-ripple stimulation) and a behavioral test.
rubric: Names a limit of the manipulation or the inference and proposes a next step, without overstating the published evidence.

@quiz self
prompt: Write the one-minute spoken explanation (about 130 words) of the idea of a prediction error, as you would give it to an examiner from a different subfield, without notes. Use one concrete example and say one limit.
hint: Start with the example, then say what the error is, what it does and where it applies.
solution: Imagine a rat that hears a tone and then gets food. At first the food is a surprise, and the brain's learning machinery responds to the surprise. After repeated pairings, the tone predicts the food, so the food no longer surprises and the response to it fades, while a response appears to the tone itself. That difference between what happened and what was predicted is the prediction error, and learning proceeds in proportion to it: no surprise, no learning. Models such as Rescorla–Wagner and temporal-difference learning formalize it, and recordings from dopamine neurons behave much like such an error signal. If the food is omitted, the error is negative and the response dips. A limit is that dopamine activity has been linked to other things as well, so the prediction error is a strong account of an important part of its activity, not of everything it does.
rubric: Uses a concrete example and states the prediction error as the difference between outcome and expectation.
rubric: Explains that learning scales with the error and mentions the shift to the predictive cue or the dip on omission.
rubric: States one limit accurately, without overclaiming about dopamine.
