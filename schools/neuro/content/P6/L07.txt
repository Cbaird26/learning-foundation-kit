@article
Qualifying examinations differ widely: some programs set written papers, some an oral examination on a reading list, some a proposal defense, some none at all, and the timing differs by program and country. Check your program's rules and past examples. Whatever the format, examiners tend to probe the same things: can you recall the core quantities and derive them, can you explain a mechanism from first principles, and do you know what the classic experiments showed and what they did not. This week and the next two review the core, with problems whose answers are exact.

### Quantities you should be able to compute

For an ion $X$ of charge $z$, the **Nernst potential** is $E_X = \frac{RT}{zF}\ln\frac{[X]_{\text{out}}}{[X]_{\text{in}}}$; at body temperature (37 °C), $RT/F \approx 26.7$ mV (about 25 mV near room temperature, so always state the temperature). With several permeant ions, the **Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz** voltage equation (Goldman 1943; Hodgkin and Katz 1949) weights each concentration by its permeability. For K$^+$ and Na$^+$,
$$V_m = \frac{RT}{F}\ln\frac{P_K[K]_{\text{out}} + P_{Na}[Na]_{\text{out}}}{P_K[K]_{\text{in}} + P_{Na}[Na]_{\text{in}}}.$$
A passive patch of membrane is an RC circuit: the **time constant** is $\tau_m = R_m C_m$, and a current step $I$ moves the voltage as $V(t) = V_\infty(1 - e^{-t/\tau_m})$ with $V_\infty = I R_m$. Along a passive cable, a steady voltage decays as $V(x) = V_0 e^{-x/\lambda}$ with the **length constant** $\lambda = \sqrt{r_m/r_i}$, larger for fatter fibers and for membranes with higher resistance. In quantal analysis (Fatt and Katz 1952; del Castillo and Katz 1954), the mean synaptic response is $n p q$: $n$ release sites, release probability $p$ and quantal size $q$.

### Mechanisms you should be able to narrate

**The action potential** (Hodgkin and Huxley, 1952). Depolarization to threshold opens voltage-gated Na$^+$ channels, which activate quickly; the inward current depolarizes the membrane further (positive feedback) toward $E_{Na}$. Na$^+$ channels then inactivate and delayed-rectifier K$^+$ channels open, repolarizing the membrane, which briefly undershoots toward $E_K$. Inactivation makes the absolute refractory period and keeps the spike traveling in one direction. Propagation: current entering at one node of membrane spreads along the axon and depolarizes the neighboring patch to threshold. **Myelin** raises the effective membrane resistance and lowers its capacitance, so local currents spread farther and faster, and the spike is regenerated only at the nodes of Ranvier (saltatory conduction).

**Synaptic transmission.** Presynaptic depolarization opens voltage-gated Ca$^{2+}$ channels; Ca$^{2+}$ entry triggers vesicle fusion; transmitter diffuses across the cleft and opens postsynaptic receptor channels, producing an excitatory or inhibitory postsynaptic potential whose sign depends on the reversal potential of the channel relative to the threshold, not on the transmitter alone. Postsynaptic potentials sum in space and time, and the cell body integrates them.

**Long-term potentiation** (Bliss and Lømo, 1973). At Schaffer collateral–CA1 synapses, a strong, brief, high-frequency input produces a lasting increase in the synaptic response. In the classic NMDA-receptor-dependent form, glutamate must bind while the postsynaptic cell is depolarized, which relieves the magnesium block and lets Ca$^{2+}$ enter. This gives **input specificity**, **cooperativity** and **associativity**, the properties Hebb's postulate (1949) suggests. LTP is established as a cellular mechanism; its role in behavioral memory is supported by many manipulations but remains debated in detail.

### Systems results you should be able to state

- **Receptive fields.** Neurons in cat primary visual cortex respond best to bars of a particular orientation and position (Hubel and Wiesel, 1959 and 1962). They described simple cells, with separate on and off subregions, and complex cells, with position tolerance, and proposed a feedforward model. How much orientation tuning comes from feedforward versus recurrent cortical circuits is still discussed.
- **Maps.** The primary somatosensory and motor cortices contain body maps, with representation proportional to the density of receptors or to the precision of control, not to body size (Penfield and Boldrey, 1937). Visual cortex is retinotopic, with the fovea over-represented.
- **Hippocampus and memory.** Bilateral medial temporal lobe resection in patient H.M. impaired new declarative memories while sparing motor skill learning and short-term retention (Scoville and Milner, 1957). Place cells fire in specific locations (O'Keefe and Dostrovsky, 1971), and grid cells in entorhinal cortex fire at the vertices of a hexagonal lattice (Hafting and colleagues, 2005).
- **Reward.** Dopamine neurons signal a reward prediction error, resembling the temporal-difference error of reinforcement learning (Schultz, Dayan and Montague, 1997).

### How to answer

A strong answer states the principle, gives the mechanism in order, includes a number or a limit when asked, and names a standard experiment. Say what is established, what is debated and what you do not know.

@example
title: The sodium equilibrium potential
problem: At 37 °C ($RT/F = 26.7$ mV), a neuron has $[\text{Na}^+]_{\text{out}} = 145$ mM and $[\text{Na}^+]_{\text{in}} = 12$ mM. What is $E_{Na}$?
step: Na$^+$ has $z = +1$, so $E_{Na} = 26.7 \ln(145/12)$ mV.
step: The ratio is $145/12 \approx 12.08$ and $\ln 12.08 \approx 2.49$.
step: Multiply: $26.7 \times 2.49 \approx 66.5$ mV.
step: Check the sign: sodium is more concentrated outside, so positive ions flow in until the inside is positive, and the answer is positive.
answer: $E_{Na} \approx +66.5$ mV.

@example
title: A passive voltage step
problem: A patch of membrane has $R_m = 50$ MΩ and $C_m = 200$ pF. A current of 0.4 nA is switched on. What is the voltage change 15 ms later?
step: Compute the time constant: $\tau_m = 50\ \text{MΩ} \times 200\ \text{pF} = 10$ ms.
step: Compute the steady-state change: $V_\infty = I R_m = 0.4\ \text{nA} \times 50\ \text{MΩ} = 20$ mV.
step: Apply the step response: $V(15) = 20(1 - e^{-1.5}) = 20 \times 0.777 \approx 15.5$ mV.
answer: About 15.5 mV, which is 78% of the steady-state change.

@mistakes
- Using the equilibrium potential and the resting potential as if they were the same: the resting potential is a weighted average set by permeabilities, and lies near $E_K$ because K$^+$ permeability dominates at rest.
- Writing $RT/F$ without stating the temperature, or forgetting the sign of the charge $z$ for anions.
- Saying that an inhibitory transmitter always hyperpolarizes; the effect depends on the reversal potential of the channel it opens.
- Saying that place cells or H.M. show that the hippocampus "stores" memories, without the conditions: the evidence concerns specific kinds of memory and how they are formed.

tol: 0.01

answer: 4

@practice self
prompt: In about 250 words, as you would answer in a timed written examination: describe how an action potential is generated and propagates along a myelinated axon, and explain why myelin increases conduction velocity.
hint: Order the events (threshold, Na$^+$ activation, inactivation, K$^+$ repolarization), then explain local current spread and the nodes.
solution: At rest the membrane is near $E_K$. A depolarization that reaches threshold opens voltage-gated Na$^+$ channels; the inward Na$^+$ current depolarizes the membrane further, which opens more channels, a regenerative positive feedback that drives the voltage toward $E_{Na}$ (Hodgkin and Huxley, 1952). Within about a millisecond the Na$^+$ channels inactivate and delayed-rectifier K$^+$ channels open, so outward K$^+$ current repolarizes the membrane, with a brief undershoot toward $E_K$. Inactivation recovers only after repolarization, which creates the refractory period and ensures that the impulse moves away from the region it has just crossed. Propagation: current entering at one site spreads along the axon through the cytoplasm and depolarizes the adjacent membrane to threshold, so the spike is regenerated in successive patches. In a myelinated axon, glial wrapping increases the effective membrane resistance and decreases its capacitance, so less current leaks out and less charge is needed to depolarize each segment; the length constant is larger, and local current spreads farther before it must be regenerated. Voltage-gated Na$^+$ channels cluster at the nodes of Ranvier, so the spike is regenerated only there and appears to jump between nodes (saltatory conduction). The result is a much higher conduction velocity than in an unmyelinated axon of the same diameter, and less metabolic cost, since ions cross the membrane only at the nodes. Velocity also rises with axon diameter, which lowers axial resistance.
rubric: Orders the events correctly: threshold, Na$^+$ activation with positive feedback, inactivation, K$^+$ repolarization, undershoot and refractoriness.
rubric: Explains propagation by current spread to neighboring membrane, with regeneration at each patch or node.
rubric: Explains myelin's effect through higher resistance and lower capacitance (a larger length constant) and saltatory conduction at the nodes.
rubric: Mentions that diameter also matters or that conduction is economical, and keeps the answer organized for a timed setting.

@quiz num
prompt: Using the Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz equation at 37 °C ($RT/F = 26.7$ mV), find the membrane potential for $P_{Na}/P_K = 0.05$ with $[\text{K}^+]_{\text{out}} = 5$ mM, $[\text{K}^+]_{\text{in}} = 140$ mM, $[\text{Na}^+]_{\text{out}} = 145$ mM and $[\text{Na}^+]_{\text{in}} = 12$ mM. Give your answer in millivolts to one decimal place.
answer: -65.2
tol: 0.1
unit: mV
check: 26.7 * log((5 + 0.05 * 145) / (140 + 0.05 * 12))
hint: Express the numerator and the denominator in units of $P_K$, so that $P_K = 1$ and $P_{Na} = 0.05$.
solution: The numerator is $5 + 0.05 \times 145 = 12.25$ and the denominator is $140 + 0.05 \times 12 = 140.6$, so $V_m = 26.7 \ln(0.0871) = 26.7 \times (-2.440) \approx -65.2$ mV. This is less negative than $E_K \approx -89$ mV because of the small Na$^+$ permeability.

@quiz self
prompt: In about 150 words, explain the feedforward model that Hubel and Wiesel proposed for orientation selectivity in primary visual cortex, and say what is established and what is still debated about its origin.
hint: Describe how inputs aligned in space could build an elongated receptive field, then mention the role of recurrent connections and inhibition.
solution: Hubel and Wiesel found that cortical simple cells respond to bars of a given orientation at a given position, unlike the circular center-surround receptive fields of cells earlier in the pathway. They proposed that a simple cell receives input from several thalamic cells whose circular receptive fields are lined up along a line in visual space, so that their summed input is elongated and orientation selective, and that complex cells combine simple cells with the same orientation but different positions to gain position tolerance. It is well established that orientation selectivity first appears in cortex and that thalamic input is arranged in ways compatible with this model. What is still debated is how much of the tuning comes from the feedforward arrangement and how much from recurrent excitation, inhibition and other cortical circuit properties, and different experimental approaches have weighted these differently.
rubric: Describes the convergence of aligned center-surround inputs onto a simple cell and the complex cell's pooling across positions.
rubric: States that selectivity first arises in cortex and that the feedforward arrangement is compatible with evidence.
rubric: Names recurrent excitation and inhibition as the part still debated, without claiming the question is settled.
