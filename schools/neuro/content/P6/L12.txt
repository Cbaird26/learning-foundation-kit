@article
By now you have done something many applicants have not: you took a published result, reproduced or tried to reproduce it, extended it, wrote it up and defended your knowledge in a mock examination. This week turns to what comes next. The honest first message is that there is no single path. Structures, timelines, funding and requirements differ by country, institution and field, and they change, so treat the following as a map to be checked against current information from the programs and employers themselves. This course makes no claims about salaries or about the present job market; ask the programs for their own placement information and read it critically.

### Common routes

- **PhD programs.** Models differ. In many US programs, students enter a broad neuroscience program, take courses, rotate through labs, pass a qualifying exam, form a thesis committee and write a dissertation. In many European and some other systems, students are admitted to a defined project or lab, with fewer courses, and may need a master's degree first. Length varies, and so do funding arrangements (a stipend, a salary or fellowship support), so ask what is guaranteed and for how long.
- **Master's degrees.** A research master's can strengthen preparation, give lab experience and allow a change of field. Some are funded and some are not.
- **Combined degrees.** Physicians and clinicians who want research careers may enter combined programs, which differ widely by country.
- **Research assistant, technician and post-baccalaureate roles.** These positions place you in a lab, often for one to three years, and are a common way to build experience, publish and decide on further study. They differ in scope and independence.
- **Postdoctoral positions.** After a PhD, researchers often spend time in one or more labs to deepen their skills and build an independent research program. Expectations vary by field and country.
- **Research-related careers outside the laboratory bench.** These include data analysis and research software engineering, work in industry research and development (including in the development of neurotechnology and in artificial intelligence), clinical research coordination, regulatory and ethics roles, science policy, medical and science writing, teaching, museum and public engagement work, and core facility management. Many use the skills you have just practiced: careful analysis, writing, reproducible code and ethical judgment.
- **Clinical training.** Becoming a clinical neuropsychologist, a psychiatrist or a neurologist involves separate professional training and licensing, with different requirements by country.

### Choosing and planning

Decide what you want to be doing in a typical week at age 35, and work backward: do you want to design experiments, analyze data, build tools, teach, advise on policy or care for patients? Each answer suggests different next steps. Then gather information from many people: faculty, postdocs, students, people who left academia and people in the roles you are curious about. Ask about daily work, what they wish they had known and how they found their position. Treat advice as data from one person's experience, which may not match yours.

For applications, plan backward from the deadlines. Typical materials are a research statement or personal statement, a curriculum vitae, transcripts, letters of recommendation and sometimes a writing sample or interviews. Ask recommenders weeks in advance, give them your statement, CV and a list of programs with deadlines, and thank them. Contact potential advisors where your program allows it, with a short, specific message that shows you have read their work. Tailor each application to the program, because it is read by people who want to know why you want to work with them.

### The one-page research statement

A research statement is not a list of accomplishments; it is a short argument that you are ready and have a direction. A good one-page statement has these parts, in roughly this order:

1. **A question.** One paragraph on a specific question that interests you and why it matters, written so that a non-specialist in your subfield can follow it.
2. **Evidence of what you have done.** Your reanalysis project: what you reproduced, what you found, what you learned about uncertainty and reporting, with one concrete number or result.
3. **Skills.** The methods and habits you have: analysis, writing, code sharing, reading critically.
4. **What you want to do next and why here.** The kind of project you would like to work on and the specific labs, resources or faculty that fit it.
5. **Honesty.** Say what you do not yet know and how you plan to learn it.

Keep it concrete, avoid superlatives and clichés, and let a faculty member from outside your lab read a draft. Do not claim results you do not have or interests you cannot back up; committees notice.

@example
title: Planning backward from a deadline
problem: Applications are due on 1 December. You want your statement finished 3 weeks before the deadline, letters requested 6 weeks before the deadline, and a first draft of the statement shown to a reader 5 weeks before the deadline. How many weeks pass between showing the draft to a reader and finishing the statement, and between requesting letters and showing the draft?
step: Write each milestone as weeks before the deadline: letters 6, first draft shown 5, statement finished 3.
step: Weeks between showing the draft and finishing: $5 - 3 = 2$ weeks to revise.
step: Weeks between requesting letters and showing the draft: $6 - 5 = 1$ week.
step: Put each date in a calendar and add a buffer, since readers and recommenders may be slow.
answer: 2 weeks to revise after the draft is shown, and 1 week between the letter request and the draft.

@mistakes
- Assuming one route (or one country's system) applies everywhere, instead of checking each program's rules, funding and timeline.
- Using one person's career advice as a general rule, or relying on claims about the job market without asking for source data.
- Writing a statement that lists accomplishments without a question or a plan.
- Asking for letters of recommendation at the last minute and without materials.

@practice self
prompt: Write a one-page research statement (about 300 words) for an invented applicant who has done the reanalysis project of this course: a reproduction of a reaction-time effect from an open data set (38 participants, estimate 31 ms with a 95% interval of 15 to 47 ms, against 38 ms originally), with one extension controlling for overall speed. Include a question, the evidence of what was done, skills, a plan and what the applicant does not yet know.
hint: Follow the five parts, give one concrete number, and keep every claim modest.
solution: I am interested in how the brain resolves conflict between competing responses: why do some people slow down after an error while others do not, and what does the slowing do for later accuracy? Understanding this could help explain both everyday control and situations in which control is disrupted. To learn how claims in this area are built, I reproduced a published reaction-time effect from an open data set. I pre-specified the analysis, found an interference effect of 31 ms (95% interval 15 to 47 ms, 38 participants) against the 38 ms originally reported, and classified the result as reproduced within the uncertainty of the sample. I then asked whether the effect survived controlling for each participant's overall speed, and it shrank only slightly. Writing the paper taught me to report intervals rather than verdicts, to separate planned from exploratory analyses and to share code with instructions that a stranger could follow. I can program analyses from scratch, read and critique methods sections, and write clearly. Next I would like to study the question with trial-level modeling, such as drift-diffusion models, combined with an experiment, and your group's work on adjustments after errors fits this exactly. I would like to learn the methods of electrophysiological recording and more advanced statistical modeling, and I do not yet know how to design an experiment from scratch to the standard of a registered report, which I hope to learn in the lab. I would be glad to discuss which projects would suit the group.
rubric: Begins with a specific question stated in plain language, with a reason it matters.
rubric: Gives concrete evidence from the project (the estimate, interval and comparison) and what was learned about method and reporting.
rubric: Names skills, a plan and a specific fit with the program or lab, and says what the applicant does not yet know.
rubric: Keeps claims modest and avoids clichés or unsupported claims.
