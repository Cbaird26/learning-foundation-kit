@article
A plan is a promise to your future self. Once your own numbers appear, you will be tempted to adjust the analysis, to find a reason to drop a participant, or to reinterpret what "agreement" means. A written plan, dated before the analysis, is the cheapest protection. It is also what a reader needs to judge your paper. The same logic lies behind registered reports, a journal format introduced at the journal Cortex in 2013, in which the plan is peer reviewed before the results exist. Your plan is private and informal by comparison, but it should follow the same discipline.

### The parts of a plan

1. **Target.** Cite the paper, name the figure or table, and copy the estimate, the sample size and the interval or test statistic.
2. **Question type.** Say whether this is a reproduction (same data, same steps) or a test on independent data (a different data set of the same task).
3. **Data.** Give the data set's name, its version or snapshot identifier, the license, the participants you will use and the files. Fixing the version matters because archives are corrected and extended.
4. **Analysis steps.** Preprocessing, exclusions, the statistical model, and the software and its versions. Where the paper is silent, write down your choice and mark it as a choice, not as the paper's method.
5. **Uncertainty.** How you will compute intervals: a standard error from the model, a bootstrap, or a permutation test.
6. **Success criteria.** What result will count as reproduced, partly reproduced and not reproduced.
7. **Deviations.** A rule that every departure from the plan will be listed with its date and reason, and kept in the final report.
8. **Resources.** Time, disk space and software, with a fallback if the full data set is too large.

### Success criteria depend on the design

If you reanalyze the same data the authors used, any difference between their estimate and yours comes from analysis choices or from errors, not from sampling. So a statistical test of the difference between two estimates is the wrong tool. Instead state a tolerance: for example, "the estimate must agree with the published one to the number of digits reported, or within 5%, and the participant count must match exactly". A deliberate difference in the analysis (a different software package, say) can justify a wider tolerance, which you state in advance.

If you apply the analysis to an independent sample, your estimate differs from the original partly because of sampling error. Then compare estimates with their uncertainty. For two independent estimates $\hat\theta_1$ and $\hat\theta_2$ with standard errors $s_1$ and $s_2$, the difference has standard error $\sqrt{s_1^2 + s_2^2}$, and $z = (\hat\theta_1 - \hat\theta_2)/\sqrt{s_1^2 + s_2^2}$ measures how surprising the difference is if the true effects are equal. A small $z$ means the data are compatible with the original estimate, but it does not show that the effects are equal, especially when your sample is small and the standard error is large. Do not rely on "the original was significant and mine was not": a significant result and a non-significant one can differ by less than their sampling variability.

### What can your sample detect?

When the open data set has a fixed sample size, ask first what it can detect. For a paired or one-sample test at a two-sided significance level of 0.05, a normal approximation to the power is $\Phi(d\sqrt{n} - 1.96)$, where $d$ is the standardized effect and $\Phi$ is the standard normal distribution function, ignoring the tiny chance of a significant result in the opposite direction. Setting power to 0.80 gives the smallest effect detected with 80% power:

$$d_{\min} \approx \frac{1.96 + 0.84}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{2.8}{\sqrt{n}} .$$

If the original effect is smaller than $d_{\min}$, a non-significant result in your data will be uninformative, and you should plan to report an interval rather than a verdict. Also remember that published effects are often overestimated, so the true effect may be smaller than the original estimate.

### Keep the plan short and checkable

A plan of one to two pages is enough, provided every line is concrete. "Participants with poor data will be excluded" is not a plan; "participants whose accuracy is below 60% or who have fewer than 40 valid trials will be excluded, and the number excluded will be reported" is. Save the plan in a version-controlled file, and give it a date. Nothing in it stops you from doing extra analyses later, as long as they are labeled as exploratory.

@example
title: Comparing two independent estimates
problem: An original study of one task reports a mean difference of 24 ms with a standard error of 6 ms. Your analysis of a different, independent open data set of the same task gives 19 ms with a standard error of 5 ms. Compute $z$ for the difference and say what it means.
step: Compute the difference of estimates: $24 - 19 = 5$ ms.
step: Combine the standard errors: $\sqrt{6^2 + 5^2} = \sqrt{61} \approx 7.81$ ms.
step: Divide: $z = 5/7.81 \approx 0.64$.
step: Interpret: a difference this size is well within sampling variability if the true effects were equal, so the data are compatible with the original estimate. This does not prove the effects are equal: an interval for the difference, $5 \pm 1.96 \times 7.81$, runs from about $-10.3$ to $20.3$ ms.
answer: $z \approx 0.64$; the estimates are compatible, but the interval for their difference is wide.

@mistakes
- Testing the difference between two estimates computed from the same data as if they were independent samples: the difference there reflects analysis choices, not sampling error.
- Treating "significant in the original, not significant in mine" as a failure to reproduce, when the difference between the two may be small compared with its uncertainty.
- Writing criteria such as "the results should be similar" that cannot be checked.
- Leaving the data version out, so that readers cannot tell which files you analyzed.

answer: 1.09
tol: 0.01
check: (0.62 - 0.45) / sqrt(0.10**2 + 0.12**2)
hint: Add the squared standard errors before taking the square root.
solution: The difference is 0.17 and its standard error is $\sqrt{0.0100 + 0.0144} = 0.1562$, so $z = 0.17/0.1562 \approx 1.09$: the estimates are compatible within sampling error, although the interval for their difference is wide.

@practice num
prompt: An open data set has 45 participants for a paired design. Using the approximation $d_{\min} = 2.8/\sqrt{n}$, what is the smallest standardized effect that a two-sided test at the 0.05 level detects with 80% power? Give your answer to two decimal places.
answer: 0.42
tol: 0.01
check: 2.8 / sqrt(45)
hint: Take the square root of 45 first.
solution: $\sqrt{45} \approx 6.708$ and $2.8/6.708 \approx 0.42$. If the original effect was about 0.3, this data set is underpowered for it, so you should plan to report an interval.

@practice self
prompt: Write the reanalysis plan (about 250 words, in numbered parts) for this invented case: a published study of 40 adults reports that reaction times in a color-word interference task are 55 ms slower for incongruent than for congruent trials, $t(39) = 8.1$; the trial-level data are in an open archive with a participant table. Include the target, question type, data, analysis steps, exclusions, uncertainty, success criteria and deviation rule.
hint: Compute the standard error from the difference and $t$, then write each part as a decision a stranger could follow.
solution: 1. Target: the 55 ms difference (incongruent minus congruent), $t(39) = 8.1$, $n = 40$; the standard error is $55/8.1 \approx 6.8$ ms, so the 95% interval is about $55 \pm 2.02 \times 6.8$, that is, 41 to 69 ms. 2. Question type: reproduction, since the open data are those of the original study. 3. Data: the archive's data set at a named version, its license, the 40 participants listed in the participant table; confirm the count before analysis. 4. Steps: use correct trials only, discard reaction times below 200 ms, compute each participant's mean for each condition, take the difference, and run a paired $t$-test in my own scripts, recording software versions. Where the paper is silent about trimming slow trials, I will use no upper cutoff and mark this as my choice. 5. Exclusions: none beyond those the paper states; any other exclusion will be reported. 6. Uncertainty: the standard error of the mean difference and a 95% bootstrap interval with a fixed seed. 7. Success: reproduced if the participant count matches and the difference agrees with 55 ms within 5% (52 to 58 ms); partly reproduced if it agrees within 15%; otherwise not reproduced. 8. Deviations: every change from this plan will be listed with its date and reason in the report.

@quiz num
prompt: In an independent open data set of 36 participants (paired design), what is the approximate power, using $\Phi(d\sqrt{n} - 1.96)$ with the standard normal distribution function $\Phi$, to detect a standardized effect of $d = 0.4$? Give your answer as a decimal to two places.
answer: 0.67
tol: 0.01
check: 0.5 * (1 + erf((0.4 * sqrt(36) - 1.96) / sqrt(2)))
hint: Compute $0.4 \times 6$ first, subtract 1.96, and look up the normal distribution function.
solution: $0.4 \times 6 - 1.96 = 0.44$, and $\Phi(0.44) \approx 0.67$. A true effect of 0.4 would be found about two times in three, so a null result in this sample would not be strong evidence against it.

@quiz mc
prompt: Midway through the analysis you notice that a different exclusion rule would bring your estimate closer to the published one. What is the best action?
choice*: Keep the planned rule for the main result, report the alternative as a labeled exploratory analysis, and list the change in the deviation log if you adopt it.
choice: Switch to the alternative rule quietly because it gives agreement.

hint: The plan exists to keep the main result independent of the outcome.
solution: Keeping the planned rule for the main result preserves the test, and reporting the alternative as exploratory, with the reason for any change logged, lets readers see what depends on the choice. Switching quietly or reporting only the matching rule hides the flexibility.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain why a reanalysis of the same data should not use a test of the difference between your estimate and the published one, and say what to use instead.
hint: Ask where the difference between two estimates computed from the same data comes from.
solution: A test of the difference between two estimates models sampling error: it asks how different two estimates would be by chance when each comes from its own random sample. When both come from the same participants and trials, there is no new sampling, so the difference comes only from analysis choices or errors, and the test's standard error would be meaningless. A better approach is to compare the computation step by step: check the participant and trial counts, compare descriptive statistics with any published table, and require the final estimate to fall within a tolerance stated in advance (for example, agreement to the reported precision). Where a test of difference is relevant is an independent data set, where $z = (\hat\theta_1 - \hat\theta_2)/\sqrt{s_1^2 + s_2^2}$ is meaningful.
rubric: States that the difference between estimates from the same data reflects analysis choices or errors, not sampling error.
rubric: Proposes counts, descriptive statistics and a pre-stated tolerance as the comparison.
rubric: Says that a test of the difference applies to independent samples.
