@article
Methods, statistics and ethics questions are where qualifying exams most often catch candidates, because each needs precision about conditions: what a method measures, what a test assumes, which rule applies. This week's review is therefore organized as conditions to check.

### What a method measures

- **BOLD fMRI** measures a blood-oxygenation-dependent signal (BOLD contrast, Ogawa and colleagues, 1990), an indirect and slow consequence of neural activity, not spiking itself. Its relation to spiking is better in some conditions than others, and its time scale is seconds.
- **EEG and MEG** record electric or magnetic fields from synchronized synaptic currents, with millisecond timing and limited spatial resolution; source localization from scalp data is an ill-posed inverse problem.
- **Two-photon calcium imaging** reads out a slow fluorescent indicator of calcium, such as GCaMP6 (Chen and colleagues, 2013), which blurs spike timing; it samples many identified neurons (hundreds or more) near the surface.
- **Optogenetics** (Boyden and colleagues, 2005, in neurons) uses light to control cells expressing a light-sensitive channel; **chemogenetics** uses engineered receptors (DREADDs, Armbruster and colleagues, 2007). Both need controls: light or drug in animals without the construct, because heating, light and, for the ligand clozapine-N-oxide, conversion to clozapine (Gomez and colleagues, 2017) have effects of their own. Acute circuit manipulations can also cause off-target changes in other regions (Otchy and colleagues, 2015), so a behavioral effect needs more than the manipulation.
- **Lesions, stimulation and recordings** answer different questions: necessity, sufficiency and correlation. A claim should be matched to the evidence type.

### Statistics that govern what data can show

**Power and sample size.** For a two-group comparison of means with equal groups, a normal approximation gives the sample size per group $n \approx 2(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})^2/d^2$, where $d$ is the standardized effect; with $z = 1.96$ for $\alpha = 0.05$ (two-sided) and $z = 0.84$ for power 0.80, $n \approx 2 \times 2.8^2/d^2 = 15.7/d^2$. Exact $t$-based calculations give slightly larger values. Low power both wastes resources and, because significant results from small samples exaggerate the true effect, inflates the published effect sizes; this is a central argument of Button and colleagues (2013).

**Positive predictive value.** If a fraction $\pi$ of tested hypotheses are true, the power is $1 - \beta$ and the significance level is $\alpha$, the probability that a significant result reflects a true effect is
$$\text{PPV} = \frac{(1-\beta)\,\pi}{(1-\beta)\,\pi + \alpha\,(1-\pi)}.$$
It falls when power is low or when the field tests many unlikely hypotheses.

**Multiple comparisons.** A whole-brain analysis tests tens of thousands of voxels, so uncorrected thresholds are not acceptable. Options include the Bonferroni correction, control of the false discovery rate (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995) and cluster-based inference. Eklund, Nichols and Knutsson (2016) found that some widely used cluster-based parametric methods had false-positive rates well above the nominal level in resting-state data, which led to changes in practice and a recommendation to use methods with checked assumptions, such as permutation tests. Circular analysis (Kriegeskorte and colleagues, 2009) and flexible analysis choices (Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn, 2011) are further sources of false positives. Registered reports (introduced at the journal Cortex in 2013) and preregistration are procedural responses.

**Effect sizes and p-values.** A p-value is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, nor a measure of an effect's size or importance; a tiny effect can be statistically significant in a large sample. Report estimates with intervals.

### Ethics, rules and data sharing

Rules differ by country, funder and institution, and they change, so use this as a map and check the current texts that apply to you.

- **People.** The Declaration of Helsinki, an ethical statement of the World Medical Association, was last revised on 19 October 2024 (the first revision since 2013); it now speaks of research participants rather than subjects, and stresses community engagement and vulnerability. In the United States, research with people is governed by the Common Rule (45 CFR 46; the revised rule's general compliance date was 21 January 2019) and reviewed by institutional review boards. Informed consent, minimizing risk, privacy and fair selection are core requirements.
- **Animals.** In the United States, animal research is reviewed by institutional animal care and use committees under the Animal Welfare Act and Public Health Service policy; in the European Union, Directive 2010/63/EU applies. The **3Rs**, replacement, reduction and refinement (Russell and Burch, 1959), guide design, and ARRIVE 2.0 (2020) is a reporting guideline for animal studies.
- **Data sharing.** The US National Institutes of Health Data Management and Sharing Policy took effect on 25 January 2023: applications include a plan for managing and sharing scientific data. NIH's 2024 Public Access Policy, effective 1 July 2025, requires papers from NIH-funded research to be publicly available on the date of publication, with no embargo. Other funders and journals set their own rules. FAIR principles (Wilkinson and colleagues, 2016) and CONSORT 2025 for randomized trials (published in April 2025) are further reference points.
- **Neural data.** The UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Neurotechnology was adopted on 12 November 2025; it is not legally binding, and treats neural data as sensitive. The OECD adopted a Recommendation on Responsible Innovation in Neurotechnology on 11 December 2019. Several US states treat neural data as sensitive in their privacy laws, and there is no federal law of this kind; check the current list.

@example
title: Sample size for a two-group comparison
problem: You want 80% power at a two-sided 0.05 level to detect a standardized difference of $d = 0.5$ between two groups. Use $z = 1.96$ and $z = 0.84$. How many participants per group, rounded up?
step: Add the two z values: $1.96 + 0.84 = 2.80$.
step: Apply the formula: $n = 2 \times 2.80^2/0.5^2 = 2 \times 7.84/0.25 = 62.72$.
step: Round up: 63 per group, a total of 126.
step: Note that an exact $t$-based calculation gives about 64 per group, so the approximation is close but slightly low.
answer: About 63 per group (126 in total).

@example
title: The chance a significant result is true
problem: A field tests hypotheses of which 10% are true, with power 0.8 and $\alpha = 0.05$. What is the probability that a significant result is a true effect?
step: True positives: $0.8 \times 0.10 = 0.08$.
step: False positives: $0.05 \times 0.90 = 0.045$.
step: Divide: $\text{PPV} = 0.08/(0.08 + 0.045) = 0.64$.
step: Interpret: about a third of significant findings would be false even with a nominal 5% error rate, and the proportion rises when power falls.
answer: $\text{PPV} = 0.64$.

@mistakes
- Treating BOLD signal as a direct measure of spiking, or an effect of a manipulation as proof that the manipulated cells cause the behavior, without controls.
- Reading a p-value as the probability that the null hypothesis is true, or as the size of the effect.
- Thinking that a nominal 5% level means that 5% of significant results are false.
- Stating legal and policy facts from memory without dates or without saying that they change and differ by place.

answer: 0.71
tol: 0.01
check: 0.5 * 0.2 / (0.5 * 0.2 + 0.05 * 0.8)

@practice self
prompt: Timed answer (about 250 words, 20 minutes): explain why a whole-brain voxelwise fMRI group analysis needs correction for multiple comparisons, describe two approaches, and say what Eklund, Nichols and Knutsson (2016) found and why it matters.
hint: Count the tests, then show how the family-wise error grows, then describe Bonferroni or the false discovery rate and cluster-based or permutation methods.
solution: A whole-brain analysis tests on the order of tens of thousands of voxels. At an uncorrected threshold of 0.05 on, for example, 50,000 independent null voxels, about 2,500 would be significant by chance, so a map of "active" voxels would be mostly noise. The correction controls an error rate over the family of tests. The Bonferroni correction divides the level by the number of tests, controlling the family-wise error rate, but it is conservative when tests are correlated, as neighboring voxels are. Control of the false discovery rate (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995) bounds the expected fraction of false positives among the results declared significant, and is more sensitive. Cluster-based inference uses the spatial extent of suprathreshold regions: spatial smoothness makes false positives cluster, so the null distribution of cluster size is needed, which can be computed from parametric assumptions or by permutation. Eklund, Nichols and Knutsson (2016) tested common software with resting-state data in which no task effect exists, and found false-positive rates for some cluster-based parametric methods well above the nominal 5%, because the assumed spatial smoothness did not hold. This mattered because those methods were used in a very large literature, and it led to recommendations to check assumptions with simulation or to use permutation tests, and to report thresholds and methods in full. It does not imply that all earlier findings are wrong, since the size of the problem depended on the settings used.
rubric: Explains the inflation of false positives with the number of tests, ideally with a number.
rubric: Describes two correction approaches accurately (for example Bonferroni or false discovery rate, and cluster-based or permutation inference).
rubric: States the Eklund, Nichols and Knutsson finding accurately (inflated false positives in some cluster methods because of violated assumptions) and its consequences without overstating them.

@quiz num
prompt: An independent-samples $t$-test with 20 participants per group gives $t = 2.5$. Using $d = t\sqrt{1/n_1 + 1/n_2}$, what is the standardized effect size? Give your answer to two decimal places.
answer: 0.79
tol: 0.01
check: 2.5 * sqrt(1 / 20 + 1 / 20)
hint: With equal groups, $\sqrt{1/20 + 1/20} = \sqrt{0.1}$.
solution: $d = 2.5 \times \sqrt{0.1} = 2.5 \times 0.3162 \approx 0.79$, a medium to large effect, with a wide interval in groups this small.
