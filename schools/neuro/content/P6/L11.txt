@article
Two choices will shape the next several years of your research life more than any exam: what question you work on and with whom. Programs differ greatly in how these choices are made. Some admit you to a program and have you rotate through labs before you choose an advisor; some admit you directly to a lab or a project; some have you choose after a master's year. Timelines for a PhD also vary by country and program, from three or four years to six or more. Learn the rules of your own program before you plan.

### Choosing a direction

A good thesis direction sits at the overlap of four things: a question the field cares about, a question you can actually answer with the methods, data and time available, your own interest (you will spend years with it), and an advisor and lab who can help. The common failure is a question that is important but intractable ("How does the brain create the mind?"). Make it tractable by narrowing the phenomenon, the system and the measure until a specific result would count as an answer: "Does the rate of hippocampal replay during rest after learning predict how well the animal remembers the next day?" can be attacked in a few years; the broad question cannot.

A useful test is the **surprise test**: can you write down two or more plausible results, at least one of which would change what you or the field think? If every outcome would confirm what you expected, or no outcome could be interpreted, the question needs sharpening. A second is the **fallback test**: if your main approach fails (as many do), what would you still have? Build a thesis from three or four aims that are largely independent: if each of three independent aims works with probability $p$, the chance that at least one works is $1 - (1-p)^3$, which is $0.973$ for $p = 0.7$ and $0.875$ for $p = 0.5$. Independence is rarely complete, because aims often share a method or a data set, so treat the figure as an upper bound and design aims that do not fail for the same reason. Your reanalysis project is a template: you took a result, made the question precise and estimated what could be learned from the available data.

### Choosing an advisor and a lab

An advisor is the single most important influence on your training. Gather evidence on:

- **Mentoring.** How often will you meet? Who will teach you the methods? Does the advisor read drafts and give prompt, specific feedback? How are disagreements handled?
- **Science.** Do the lab's papers match the questions and methods you want to learn? Are the data and code shared, and are results reported carefully? A lab's reproducibility culture is visible in its publications and repositories.
- **Support.** Is there funding for your stipend and project for the whole period? What are the rules on conferences, travel and equipment? Funding arrangements differ by program and country, so ask.
- **Culture and outcomes.** Where did former students go, and how do they describe their training? How many people work in the lab? How are authorship and credit decided, and when is that discussed? What expectations exist about hours, vacations and time off?
- **Fit.** Does the lab's style (large collaboration, small team, much independence, much direction) suit how you work best?

The best source is the group's students, past and present. Ask to speak to them without the advisor present, and ask concrete questions ("Tell me about the last time you disagreed with your advisor."). Read the lab's papers, and read the acknowledgments and author order. Be wary of a lab where students are not available to talk with you, where turnover is unexplained, or where expectations are vague. Prestige can matter less to your training than attention and fit, but weigh it as you see fit.

You have an obligation as well: be honest about your interests and your limits, and ask for expectations to be written down. If problems arise later, most universities have an ombudsperson, graduate office or committee to consult, and you should know them before you need them. Joint advising and a thesis committee that includes people outside the lab also provide a safety net.

### Decision matrices

A **weighted decision matrix** makes your reasoning visible: choose criteria, give each a weight that sums to 1, score each option from 1 to 5 on each criterion and compute the weighted sum. The result is only as good as the weights and scores, which are your judgments. Use it to find out which criteria matter to you (change the weights and see if the ranking changes), not to hand the decision to a number. If the ranking flips with small changes of weights, the options are close, and gathering more information is worth more than calculation.

@example
title: A weighted decision matrix
problem: Two labs are scored from 1 to 5 on mentoring, science, funding and location, with weights 0.4, 0.3, 0.2 and 0.1. Lab A scores 5, 3, 4, 2 and lab B scores 3, 5, 3, 4. Which has the higher weighted score, and how robust is the ranking?
step: Compute lab A: $0.4 \times 5 + 0.3 \times 3 + 0.2 \times 4 + 0.1 \times 2 = 2.0 + 0.9 + 0.8 + 0.2 = 3.9$.
step: Compute lab B: $0.4 \times 3 + 0.3 \times 5 + 0.2 \times 3 + 0.1 \times 4 = 1.2 + 1.5 + 0.6 + 0.4 = 3.7$.
step: Test robustness: move 0.2 of weight from mentoring to science (weights 0.2, 0.5, 0.2, 0.1). Lab A: $1.0 + 1.5 + 0.8 + 0.2 = 3.5$; lab B: $0.6 + 2.5 + 0.6 + 0.4 = 4.1$. The ranking flips.
step: Conclude: the options are close and the choice depends on how you weigh mentoring against science, so ask more questions of each lab's students before deciding.
answer: Lab A scores 3.9 and lab B 3.7, but the ranking flips when science is weighted above mentoring.

@example
title: Aims with a fallback
problem: A thesis plan has three largely independent aims, each with a 0.7 chance of working. What is the chance that at least one works, and why is this an upper bound?
step: The chance that one aim fails is $1 - 0.7 = 0.3$.
step: The chance that all three fail is $0.3^3 = 0.027$.
step: So at least one works with probability $1 - 0.027 = 0.973$.
step: This assumes independence. If aims share a method, a data set or a main assumption, they tend to fail together, and the true figure is lower.
answer: About 0.973, an upper bound.

@mistakes
- Choosing a question because it is important, without checking that you can answer any part of it with the data and methods you have.
- Choosing an advisor from the lab's reputation or the advisor's talk alone, without speaking with students.
- Treating a decision matrix as an objective answer, instead of a tool to expose your own weights.
- Planning a thesis with one high-risk aim, so that a single failure would stop the project.

@practice self
prompt: Rewrite the broad question "How does the brain store memories?" as a tractable thesis question for a three-year project, with three largely independent aims (about 150 to 220 words). Say which result would surprise you and what your fallback is if the main method fails.
hint: Narrow the system, the type of memory, the measure and the species or population, and use open data for one aim.
solution: Question: In humans, does the similarity of the multivoxel pattern evoked by an item during encoding and during later rest predict whether it is remembered after a delay? Aim 1: reanalyze an open functional MRI data set of an encoding task with a later memory test to estimate, with an interval, how well encoding-pattern strength predicts later recognition; this uses existing data. Aim 2: test whether the same pattern reappears more strongly during rest after encoding for remembered than for forgotten items, with a matched control period and preregistered thresholds. Aim 3: test the behavioral prediction in a new low-risk experiment with volunteers, manipulating the number of repetitions. A result that would surprise me is no relation between rest-period reactivation and later memory when encoding strength is controlled, because many accounts predict one. If Aim 2's method fails because the signal is too noisy, Aim 1 stands as a result, and Aim 3 does not depend on either, so the thesis would still contain a complete project. The aims share a data type but not a failure mode: Aim 3 depends on neither the open data nor the rest analysis.
rubric: Narrows the system, memory type, measure and population so that a specific result would answer the question.
rubric: Gives three aims that could fail independently, with a stated fallback.
rubric: States a result that would be surprising or informative, and does not rely on a single high-risk aim.
