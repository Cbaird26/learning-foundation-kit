@article
Cognitive and computational neuroscience exams reward two skills: knowing the standard models well enough to compute with them, and knowing the logic of what behavioral and imaging data can support. Both are about exact reasoning. Programs differ in what they emphasize, so check yours.

### Signal detection theory

In a yes/no detection task, the observer's internal evidence is Gaussian with equal variance for noise and for signal, and the means differ by $d'$ in standard deviation units. With hit rate $H$ and false-alarm rate $F$, and $z = \Phi^{-1}$ the inverse of the standard normal distribution function,
$$d' = z(H) - z(F), \qquad c = -\tfrac{1}{2}\big(z(H) + z(F)\big).$$
$d'$ measures sensitivity, independent of the criterion; $c$ measures bias, with $c = 0$ halfway between the two means, $c > 0$ conservative (fewer "yes" responses) and $c < 0$ liberal (Green and Swets, 1966). Rates of exactly 0 or 1 give infinite $z$ and need a correction. A change in payoffs or prior probabilities should move $c$ and leave $d'$ unchanged; if it moves $d'$, the model's assumptions deserve a second look.

### Learning and prediction errors

The **Rescorla–Wagner** model (1972) updates the associative strength of each cue present on a trial by the prediction error shared among all cues: $\Delta V_i = \alpha_i \beta\,(\lambda - \sum_j V_j)$, where $\lambda$ is the outcome's asymptote and $\alpha_i\beta$ a learning rate. It explains **blocking**: when a cue A already predicts the outcome, the sum is near $\lambda$, the error is small, and a new cue B added to A learns little. It cannot explain second-order conditioning, in which a cue is made to predict another cue and not a reward. **Temporal-difference learning** (Sutton, 1988) extends the idea to time: the error is
$$\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t),$$
with discount factor $\gamma$ between 0 and 1. Dopamine neurons recorded in monkeys behave much like $\delta$: they respond to unexpected rewards, shift to the earliest predictive cue as learning proceeds, and dip when an expected reward is omitted (Schultz, Dayan and Montague, 1997). Whether dopamine carries only this signal is debated.

### Decisions

In the **drift-diffusion model** (Ratcliff, 1978), noisy evidence accumulates at a mean rate (drift) $v$ with noise $\sigma$ until it reaches one of two bounds. For symmetric bounds at $\pm A$ and a starting point at 0, the probability of absorbing at the upper bound and the mean decision time are
$$P_{\text{upper}} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-2vA/\sigma^2}}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[T] = \frac{A}{v}\tanh\!\left(\frac{vA}{\sigma^2}\right),$$
and the observed reaction time adds a non-decision time. Wider bounds buy accuracy at the cost of speed, which is the model's account of the speed–accuracy trade-off. Fitting the model to behavior lets one ask whether a manipulation changed drift, bound or non-decision time, but different parameter sets can mimic one another, so a fit is not a proof of the underlying mechanism.

### Combining cues

If two independent cues give Gaussian estimates with variances $\sigma_1^2$ and $\sigma_2^2$, the estimate that minimizes variance weights each by its **precision** $1/\sigma^2$, and its variance is
$$\sigma_{\text{comb}}^2 = \frac{1}{1/\sigma_1^2 + 1/\sigma_2^2} .$$
It is smaller than either cue's variance. Human behavior in several multisensory tasks is close to this prediction, a good example of a model testable by numbers.

### The logic of inference

A **single dissociation** (a lesion impairs task X but not Y) is consistent with X depending on the region, but also with X being harder or more sensitive to damage than Y. A **double dissociation** (a second lesion or patient shows the opposite pattern) is much stronger evidence that the tasks depend on partly separable processes, though not that a region does only one thing. **Reverse inference** reasons from activation in a region to the engagement of a cognitive process; it is only as strong as the region's selectivity, as Bayes's rule shows: $P(\text{process} \mid \text{active})$ depends on $P(\text{active} \mid \text{process})$, on $P(\text{active} \mid \text{no process})$ and on the prior. Marr's three levels (1982), the computational (what and why), algorithmic (how, with which representations) and implementational (in which hardware), help to place a claim: an argument that a model is wrong at one level does not refute the others.

@example
title: A temporal-difference error
problem: A learner has $V(s) = 0.3$ and $V(s') = 0.5$ for the next state, receives reward $r = 1$ on the transition, and uses $\gamma = 0.9$. What is the prediction error?
step: Write the error: $\delta = r + \gamma V(s') - V(s)$.
step: Compute the discounted next value: $0.9 \times 0.5 = 0.45$.
step: Combine: $\delta = 1 + 0.45 - 0.3 = 1.15$.
step: Interpret: the outcome was far better than predicted, so the value of the state should rise by the learning rate times 1.15.
answer: $\delta = 1.15$.

@example
title: Sensitivity and bias from two rates
problem: In a detection task, $H = 0.93$ and $F = 0.16$. The inverse normal distribution values are $z(0.93) = 1.476$ and $z(0.16) = -0.994$. Compute $d'$ and $c$ and interpret them.
step: Compute sensitivity: $d' = 1.476 - (-0.994) = 2.47$.
step: Compute the criterion: $c = -\tfrac{1}{2}(1.476 - 0.994) = -0.241$.
step: Interpret: the signal and noise distributions are separated by about 2.5 standard deviations, and the negative $c$ shows a slight liberal bias, a readiness to say "yes".
answer: $d' \approx 2.47$ and $c \approx -0.24$ (slightly liberal).

@mistakes
- Treating a high hit rate as high sensitivity: a liberal observer has a high hit rate and a high false-alarm rate, and $d'$ may be low.
- Using $\sigma$ in place of $\sigma^2$ in the precision weights for cue combination.
- Concluding from a single dissociation, or from activation in a region alone, that a function is localized there.
- Treating a good fit of a drift-diffusion model as proof that the brain implements accumulation to a bound.

@practice num
prompt: In the Rescorla–Wagner model, cue A has been trained to $V_A = 0.8$ with outcome asymptote $\lambda = 1$. On a compound trial with A and a new cue B (starting at $V_B = 0$), the combined learning rate is $\alpha\beta = 0.25$ for B. What is the change $\Delta V_B$ on that trial?
answer: 0.05
tol: 0.001
check: 0.25 * (1 - (0.8 + 0))
hint: The error uses the sum of the strengths of all cues present, not B alone.
solution: The error is $\lambda - (V_A + V_B) = 1 - 0.8 = 0.2$, so $\Delta V_B = 0.25 \times 0.2 = 0.05$. Had B been trained alone, the error would be 1 and the change 0.25; this is blocking.

@practice self
prompt: Timed answer (about 200 words, 15 minutes): a study reports that a region of the amygdala was more active during the task that evokes fear than during a control task, and concludes that the region's activation shows that participants experienced fear. Critique this inference. Use Bayes's rule with $P(\text{fear}) = 0.2$, $P(\text{active} \mid \text{fear}) = 0.8$ and $P(\text{active} \mid \text{no fear}) = 0.4$, and say what evidence would strengthen the conclusion.
hint: Compute $P(\text{fear} \mid \text{active})$, then ask how selective the region is for fear.
solution: This is a reverse inference: reasoning from the activation back to the psychological process. Its strength depends on how selective the region is. By Bayes's rule, $P(\text{fear} \mid \text{active}) = (0.8 \times 0.2)/(0.8 \times 0.2 + 0.4 \times 0.8) = 0.16/0.48 \approx 0.33$. So even with a region that responds to fear 80% of the time, if it also responds in 40% of other states, active tells us only that fear is more likely than before (from 0.2 to 0.33), not that it is likely. The task may also have differed from the control in arousal, novelty or attention, all of which also engage the amygdala. Stronger evidence would come from showing that activation predicts independent measures of fear (self-report, physiology) within participants, from comparing against several control conditions that match arousal and novelty, from a pattern of activity (not just average level) that classifies fear across tasks, and from causal tests such as lesion studies or other manipulations. The conclusion should be stated as consistent with fear, not as a measurement of experienced fear.
rubric: Identifies the inference as reverse inference and computes the posterior of about 0.33.
rubric: Explains that selectivity and prior probability limit the conclusion and names confounds such as arousal or novelty.
rubric: Proposes stronger evidence (independent measures, multiple controls, pattern analysis or causal tests) and states a modest conclusion.

prompt: Two independent cues estimate the same quantity with variances $\sigma_1^2 = 4$ and $\sigma_2^2 = 1$. What is the variance of the optimal (precision-weighted) combined estimate?
answer: 0.8
check: 1 / (1 / 4 + 1 / 1)

@quiz ms
prompt: Select every statement that is true of the Rescorla–Wagner model.
choice*: Learning on a trial is driven by the difference between the outcome and the summed strengths of all cues present.
choice*: It explains blocking of a new cue by a cue that already predicts the outcome.
choice*: Associative strength approaches an asymptote as the prediction error shrinks.
choice: It accounts for second-order conditioning, in which a cue acquires value by predicting another cue.
hint: Consider what the model does with cues that predict no reward directly.
solution: The shared error term, blocking and approach to an asymptote are central properties. The model has no mechanism for value to pass between cues in the absence of reward, which is why temporal-difference learning, with bootstrapped values, is used for second-order effects.
