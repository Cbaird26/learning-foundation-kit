@review
## Review guide

**Week 1: choosing a result.** Reproducibility (same data and steps), replicability (new data) and robustness (reasonable analysis changes) are different. Screen candidates on open data, a numerical target, a described analysis, modest computing and value. Convert a reported statistic into a target: $d_z = t/\sqrt{n}$, $\text{SE} = \text{estimate}/t$, interval $\pm t_{\text{crit}}\,\text{SE}$.

**Week 2: planning.** A dated plan fixes target, data version, steps, exclusions, uncertainty and success criteria. Same data: use a tolerance and counts. Independent data: $z = (\hat\theta_1 - \hat\theta_2)/\sqrt{s_1^2 + s_2^2}$. Detectable effect at 80% power: $d_{\min} \approx 2.8/\sqrt{n}$; power $\approx \Phi(d\sqrt{n} - 1.96)$.

**Week 3: running.** Raw data stay untouched; scripts generate everything; sanity checks and an exclusion flow; SE $= s/\sqrt{n}$; bootstrap with a fixed seed, resampling participants.

**Week 4: mismatches.** Climb the ladder: counts, descriptives, intermediates, final estimate; one change at a time. Do not fish for agreement: $k$ independent tests at 0.05 give $1 - 0.95^k$ chance of at least one false positive. Report the planned result, the original, the discrepancy and the deviations.

**Week 5: extensions.** Controls (covariates, matching), negative and positive controls, robustness. Partial correlation $r_{xy\cdot z} = (r_{xy} - r_{xz}r_{yz})/\sqrt{(1-r_{xz}^2)(1-r_{yz}^2)}$. Bonferroni $\alpha/m$; Benjamini–Hochberg largest $k$ with $p_{(k)} \le kq/m$. Avoid circular selection.

**Week 6: writing.** One claim; methods repeatable from the text; results with estimates, intervals and $n$; label exploratory work; share code; modest claims. $d = (\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2)/s_p$.

**Weeks 7 to 9: core review.** Nernst $\frac{RT}{zF}\ln\frac{[X]_o}{[X]_i}$; GHK; $\tau = RC$; $V(x) = V_0e^{-x/\lambda}$; $npq$; $d' = z(H) - z(F)$; Rescorla–Wagner; TD error $r + \gamma V(s') - V(s)$; drift-diffusion; precision-weighted cues; sample size $2(z_{1-\alpha/2}+z_{1-\beta})^2/d^2$; PPV; ethics and data-sharing facts with dates, and the habit of checking current rules.

**Week 10: orals.** Five-beat answers (clarify, claim, mechanism, evidence, limits); honest reasoning when you do not know; spaced retrieval; mock exams.

**Weeks 11 and 12: direction, advisor, paths.** Tractable questions with independent aims; evidence about advisors from students and papers; routes differ, so check rules; a one-page statement with a question, evidence, skills and a plan.

**Self-check.** Can you (1) turn a reported test into a target interval, (2) write success criteria before seeing results, (3) debug a mismatch without fishing, (4) correct for many tests, (5) compute $d'$, a TD error, a Nernst potential and a sample size by hand, (6) explain one major idea aloud without notes?

**Common errors.** Choosing the criterion after seeing the result; testing the difference between two estimates computed from the same data; confusing the equilibrium and resting potentials; reading a p-value as the probability that the null is true; reverse inference without selectivity; treating a decision matrix as an answer; stating legal facts without dates and without checking current versions.

@exam num
prompt: An original study (independent data) reports an effect of 0.60 with a standard error of 0.15. Your reanalysis of an independent open data set gives 0.35 with a standard error of 0.12. Compute the two-sided p-value for the difference between the estimates, using $z = (\hat\theta_1 - \hat\theta_2)/\sqrt{s_1^2 + s_2^2}$ and the standard normal distribution. Give your answer to two decimal places.
answer: 0.19
tol: 0.01
check: 2 * (1 - 0.5 * (1 + erf(((0.60 - 0.35) / sqrt(0.15**2 + 0.12**2)) / sqrt(2))))
hint: Compute $z$ first: the standard error of the difference is $\sqrt{0.15^2 + 0.12^2}$.
solution: The difference is 0.25 and its standard error is $\sqrt{0.0225 + 0.0144} = 0.192$, so $z \approx 1.30$ and the two-sided p-value is about 0.19. The estimates are compatible within sampling error; this does not show that the true effects are equal.

choice*: Write the plan, including success criteria and the family of tests, before looking at your own results.
choice*: Report the extension's null results and correct for all the tests you considered.
choice*: Select regions or variables using information independent of the data used to estimate the effect.
choice*: List deviations from the plan with their reasons and label unplanned analyses as exploratory.
choice: Choose the exclusion rule that best matches the published estimate and describe it as the planned rule.
hint: Check that each practice keeps the analysis independent of the outcome and reports what was done.
solution: A plan fixed in advance, complete reporting with correction, independent selection and honest labeling of deviations keep the analysis independent of the result. Choosing the rule that matches the paper and calling it planned misdescribes the process and makes agreement uninformative.

@exam self
prompt: Timed answer (about 300 words, 40 minutes): a published study of 16 volunteers reports a whole-brain functional MRI analysis in which the region of strongest effect shows a correlation of $r = 0.8$ with a behavioral score. The authors selected the region from the whole-brain map of the same participants. Critique the finding (circularity, sample size, multiple comparisons), then outline a reanalysis plan from open data and one extension with a decision rule, and state what a successful or unsuccessful result would and would not show.
hint: Explain why selecting on the same data inflates $r$, what 16 participants can detect, then write the plan in a few specific lines.
solution: The correlation of 0.8 is almost certainly inflated. Selecting the voxel or region from a whole-brain map of the same participants and then estimating its correlation with the behavior is circular: among tens of thousands of voxels, the ones with the largest correlations are selected partly because noise favored them, so the estimate in the selected region is biased upward (Kriegeskorte and colleagues, 2009). With 16 participants, a correlation must exceed about 0.5 in size to reach significance at the 0.05 level, and its interval is very wide, so only a large true effect would be detected reliably; significant results from such samples tend to exaggerate the truth; and many comparisons were made, so the result cannot be trusted unless a correction such as a permutation-based threshold or false discovery rate control was applied. Plan: use an open data set of the same or a similar task with a larger sample (state the data version, license and participant count); specify in advance the region from the original paper, or select it in one half of the participants and test it in the other half; compute the correlation with a bootstrap interval over participants; success criteria: reproduced if the estimate is at least 0.4 (half of the original) and its interval excludes zero, partly reproduced if the estimate is positive but either below 0.4 or has an interval that includes zero, not reproduced if the estimate is zero or negative; deviations will be logged. Extension: partial correlation controlling for head motion and age, with the rule that a drop of more than half means that motion or age explains much of the association; also a negative-control region, with Bonferroni correction over the two tests. A positive result would show that an association of some size is present in independent data, not that the region causes the behavior or that the original estimate was right; a null would show that the original estimate is not supported in this sample, which could reflect inflation, differences between samples or low power, not misconduct.
rubric: Identifies the circular selection and explains why it inflates the correlation, and discusses low power and multiple comparisons in 16 participants.
rubric: Gives a concrete plan with data version, selection on independent information, an interval, and success criteria stated in advance.
rubric: Gives an extension with a covariate or negative control, a decision rule and a correction, and states the limits of a positive or a null result without accusing the original authors.
