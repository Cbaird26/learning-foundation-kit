@article
Your first complete run will probably not match the paper. This is normal, even when both analyses are competent: published methods leave out details, software changes, and errors hide in every pipeline, including the original one and yours. How you respond is what distinguishes research from motivated tinkering.

### Debug by climbing a ladder

Compare your pipeline with the original at the lowest level that can be compared, and move up only when a rung agrees.

1. **Counts.** Participants in the data, participants excluded, trials per participant. A different count is the most common cause of a different result, and it is the easiest to see.
2. **Descriptive statistics.** Condition means, standard deviations or accuracy, if the paper gives a table. These do not depend on the statistical model.
3. **Intermediate quantities.** Per-participant effects, model coefficients, or images after preprocessing, where the paper or shared code gives them.
4. **The final estimate and its uncertainty.**

At each rung, change one thing at a time and record what happened. If two things change together, you cannot tell which mattered.

### The usual suspects

- **Data differences:** a different version of the files, a subset, a participant who was removed later, or a corrected file.
- **Exclusion rules:** thresholds applied at a different stage (before or after averaging), or "outliers" defined differently.
- **Preprocessing:** filter settings, smoothing, baseline windows, software version, reference choice.
- **Model:** random effects, covariates, how factors are coded, one-sided versus two-sided tests, a different estimator.
- **Units and rounding:** milliseconds against seconds, a standard error against a standard deviation, a rounded value compared with an unrounded one.
- **Your own mistakes,** which are more likely than the original's. Check them first, and have someone read your script.
- **The original,** which may also contain a mistake or an unreported choice. That is possible, but it is the last explanation to reach for, and any such claim should be backed by a minimal, shareable demonstration and raised politely with the authors.

A published number that is rounded matches yours if yours falls within half a unit of the last reported digit. A difference beyond that needs an explanation.

### Do not fish for agreement

The tempting shortcut is to try different choices (exclusions, filters, models) until your estimate equals the paper's, then declare success. This is a hazard for two reasons. First, if the pipeline offers many reasonable choices, some combination will land near any number, so agreement carries little information. Second, you have used the data twice, once to choose the analysis and once to test it. The remedy is to treat the plan as the main analysis and to explore alternatives openly, as a **sensitivity analysis**: report the estimate under each reasonable variant, in a table or a plot, and say which choices matter. The number of variants grows quickly: four choices for smoothing, three for motion and two for filtering make $4 \times 3 \times 2 = 24$ pipelines. If those pipelines were independent tests of a true null at the 0.05 level, the chance that at least one is significant would be $1 - 0.95^{24} \approx 0.71$. Real variants are correlated, so the number is smaller, but the lesson stands: reporting only the best variant is not evidence.

### Report honestly

Whatever you find, write it plainly.

- State the planned result first, with its estimate and 95% interval, and then the original's, with the difference between them.
- Classify the outcome by the criteria in your plan: reproduced, partly reproduced or not reproduced.
- List every deviation from the plan with its date and reason.
- Say what you tried to explain the discrepancy, separate what was planned from what was exploratory, and say which explanations remain untested.
- Do not describe an unexplained mismatch as proof that the original is wrong, or a match as proof that it is right: a reproduction shows that the computation can be repeated, not that the conclusion is correct.

A careful report of a failure to reproduce is a useful scientific result. It tells readers which choices matter, and it often leads to better documentation of the original analysis. Journals have procedures for corrections and comments, and they differ between journals, so check the ones that apply before making any public claim about someone else's work.

@example
title: Climbing the ladder
problem: A paper reports a correlation of $r = 0.52$ (to two decimals) between a behavioral score and an average response in 40 participants. Your pipeline gives $r = 0.41$ on 40 participants. Describe how you would debug it.
step: Check the counts: both have 40 participants, so the count is not the cause. Check that the participants are the same ones, using the participant identifiers in the paper's supplementary table.
step: Compare descriptive statistics: the mean score matches the published table, but the mean response differs by a scale factor, so the problem lies in the computation of the response.
step: Find the cause by changing one thing at a time. Your script averaged over all trials; the paper's methods say that trials with reaction times below 200 ms were removed first. Applying that rule changes $r$ to 0.52.
step: Report it: the first run (0.41) was a mistake in my pipeline, found at the intermediate rung; the corrected analysis matches to published precision. The deviation from the first run goes in the log.
answer: The discrepancy came from an unapplied exclusion rule, found by comparing intermediate quantities, not by trying variants until $r$ matched.

@example
title: How many pipelines?
problem: A reanalysis has four smoothing widths, three motion thresholds and two filter settings. How many pipelines are there, and what is the chance of at least one false positive if they were independent tests of a true null at the 0.05 level?
step: Count the combinations: $4 \times 3 \times 2 = 24$.
step: The chance that none of 24 independent tests is significant is $0.95^{24} \approx 0.29$.
step: So the chance of at least one is $1 - 0.29 \approx 0.71$.
step: Variants are correlated, so the true chance is lower, but the point stands: choose one analysis in advance and show the others as a sensitivity analysis.
answer: 24 pipelines, and about a 71% chance of at least one significant result if they were independent tests of a true null.

@mistakes
- Changing several things at once while debugging, so the cause of the change cannot be identified.
- Trying analysis variants until the estimate equals the published one and reporting only that variant.
- Blaming the original authors before checking your own script, the data version and the exclusion rules.
- Reporting a mismatch without the size of the discrepancy, the uncertainty or the list of deviations.

@practice self
prompt: Write a results paragraph of about 120 to 180 words reporting these invented findings: the original study (independent data, 50 participants) reported a standardized effect $d = 0.60$ with interval $[0.31, 0.89]$; your planned analysis of an open data set of 38 participants gave $d = 0.41$ with interval $[0.09, 0.73]$; you used 6 mm smoothing where the paper used 8 mm (a deviation); and a sensitivity analysis with 8 mm smoothing gave $d = 0.43$. Include the uncertainty, the comparison and the deviation, and do not claim more than the data support.
hint: State your result first, then the original, then how they compare, then the deviation and the sensitivity analysis, and finish by saying what the data cannot show.
solution: In the planned analysis of 38 participants, the standardized effect was $d = 0.41$ (95% interval 0.09 to 0.73). The original study of an independent sample of 50 participants reported $d = 0.60$ (0.31 to 0.89). The estimates differ by 0.19, but the two intervals overlap widely, and the difference is small relative to its uncertainty (the standard error of the difference is roughly 0.2), so these data are compatible with the original effect and also with an effect about half its size. By the criteria in the plan (same sign and an interval overlapping the original), the result is classed as partly reproduced. One deviation from the plan should be noted: I used 6 mm smoothing, whereas the paper used 8 mm. A sensitivity analysis with 8 mm gave $d = 0.43$, essentially unchanged, so the choice of smoothing does not explain the smaller estimate. Possible reasons for the difference, including sampling error, differences between the samples and winner's-curse inflation of the original, were not tested here; these data cannot distinguish among them.
rubric: Reports the planned estimate with its interval before the original and states the difference between them.
rubric: Interprets the comparison in terms of uncertainty (overlapping intervals, compatible with a range of effects) without claiming a failure or a success the data cannot show.
rubric: States the deviation and reports the sensitivity analysis as such.
rubric: Avoids causal claims about why the estimates differ, saying what was and was not tested.
