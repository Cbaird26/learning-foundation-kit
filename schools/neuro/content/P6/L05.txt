@article
A reproduction tells you that a computation can be repeated. An **extension** tells you something about the result: whether it survives a control, holds under a different analysis or applies to something new. Your project asks for one extension, and one done well is worth more than five thrown in. The test of a good extension is that you can say, before running it, what outcome would change your interpretation.

### Four kinds of extension

1. **A control for an alternative explanation.** The original effect might be due to something else: head motion in a group comparison, overall reaction time in a comparison of conditions, age in a correlation. Adding the nuisance variable as a covariate, or matching participants on it, asks whether the effect survives.
2. **A negative control.** Analyze something where the effect should be absent (a region or a time window that the theory says is not involved, or condition labels shuffled at random). A "significant" effect there signals bias in the pipeline or inflated false positives. A **positive control**, a case where the effect must be present, checks that the pipeline can detect anything.
3. **A robustness check.** Change one reasonable analysis choice (the model, the metric, the trimming rule) and see whether the conclusion survives; this is the sensitivity analysis of week 4, pre-planned.
4. **A new outcome or subgroup.** Apply the analysis to another measure in the same data. This is the riskiest because the number of possible outcomes is large.

State the extension and its decision rule in a short addendum to your plan, dated before you run it.

### Controlling for a third variable

Suppose a score $x$ correlates with a brain measure $y$, and both may depend on a third variable $z$ (age, say). The **partial correlation** of $x$ and $y$ controlling for $z$ is

$$r_{xy\cdot z} = \frac{r_{xy} - r_{xz}\, r_{yz}}{\sqrt{(1 - r_{xz}^2)(1 - r_{yz}^2)}} .$$

If $z$ explains the whole association, the numerator is near zero and the partial correlation vanishes; if the association is mostly independent of $z$, it changes little. Reporting both the raw and the adjusted estimate lets readers see how much the control matters. A control that changes nothing is also a result.

### Many analyses, many chances

Every additional test is another chance for a false positive. Two standard remedies correct for the **family** of tests you ran. The **Bonferroni correction** divides the significance level by the number of tests $m$: with $m = 20$ tests at 0.05, each p-value must be below $0.05/20 = 0.0025$. It controls the probability of any false positive but is conservative. The **Benjamini–Hochberg procedure** (1995) controls the expected proportion of false discoveries among the results you call significant, when the tests are independent or positively dependent. Sort the $m$ p-values from smallest, $p_{(1)} \le \dots \le p_{(m)}$, find the largest $k$ with $p_{(k)} \le (k/m)\,q$ for the chosen rate $q$, and call the first $k$ results significant. For example, with $m = 6$, $q = 0.05$ and sorted p-values 0.002, 0.010, 0.013, 0.021, 0.040 and 0.30, the thresholds are 0.0083, 0.0167, 0.025, 0.033, 0.0417 and 0.05, and the largest $k$ that passes is 5, so five results are called significant. The family must be defined in advance: it is all tests you considered, not only those you reported.

### Circularity

If you pick the voxels, electrodes or time points with the strongest effect in your data and then estimate the effect in those same data, the estimate is inflated, because the selection chose noise that happened to go your way. This "circular analysis" was named and quantified for neuroscience by Kriegeskorte and colleagues (2009). The remedy is to choose on independent information: select on one half of the data and estimate on the other, use cross-validation, select with an independent localizer, or select using the original paper's region and test it in your data. A related warning applies to model comparison: a model fitted and evaluated on the same data will look better than it is.

### Say what would count against you

Finally, write the interpretation rule. For example: "If the effect shrinks by more than half when motion is controlled, I will report that the original estimate is substantially confounded by motion; if it shrinks by less than 20%, I will report that it is robust to motion." The extension is then a test that your interpretation could fail. Do not forget to report the extension's null results.

@example
title: A partial correlation
problem: A behavioral score $x$ correlates with a brain measure $y$ at $r_{xy} = 0.50$ across participants. Age $z$ correlates with the score at $r_{xz} = 0.40$ and with the brain measure at $r_{yz} = 0.30$. What is the correlation between $x$ and $y$ after controlling for age?
step: Compute the numerator: $r_{xy} - r_{xz} r_{yz} = 0.50 - 0.40 \times 0.30 = 0.38$.
step: Compute the denominator: $\sqrt{(1 - 0.16)(1 - 0.09)} = \sqrt{0.84 \times 0.91} = \sqrt{0.7644} \approx 0.874$.
step: Divide: $r_{xy \cdot z} \approx 0.38/0.874 \approx 0.43$.
step: Interpret: controlling for age shrinks the correlation from 0.50 to 0.43, a modest change, so age explains only a small part of the association. Report both numbers.
answer: $r_{xy\cdot z} \approx 0.43$.

@example
title: A Bonferroni threshold
problem: You test ten regions for an effect at an overall level of 0.05, and obtain p-values 0.003, 0.012, 0.0045 and 0.20 for four of them (the other six are larger than 0.05). Which tests survive a Bonferroni correction?
step: Divide the level by the number of tests: $0.05/10 = 0.005$.
step: Compare each p-value with 0.005: 0.003 passes, 0.0045 passes, 0.012 and 0.20 do not.
step: Report the correction and the family: "ten regions were tested, Bonferroni threshold 0.005; two survive".
answer: The tests with $p = 0.003$ and $p = 0.0045$ survive.

@mistakes
- Adding many extra analyses and reporting the ones that worked, instead of one pre-specified extension and all of its results.
- Choosing regions, time windows or variables from the same data used to estimate the effect.
- Correcting for only the tests reported, instead of every test considered.
- Interpreting a control that leaves the effect unchanged as proof that the effect is causal or valid.

@practice self
prompt: Your planned reanalysis reproduced a correlation of $r = 0.38$ between a memory score and a measure of hippocampal volume in 62 adults (open data, invented). Design one extension with a control, say what would count against the original interpretation (that the association reflects memory-specific hippocampal structure), and say how you will guard against false positives. About 150 to 220 words.
hint: Think of a variable that could drive both measures, such as age or overall head size, and of a specificity check.
solution: Extension: a partial correlation controlling for age and total head size, both available in the participant table, planned before I run it. Because volume depends on head size and both memory and volume change with age, these are plausible common causes. Decision rule: if the partial correlation falls below about 0.19 (half the original), I will say the association is largely explained by these variables; if it remains above about 0.30, I will say it is robust to them, and in either case I will report the raw and adjusted estimates with 95% intervals. A specificity check strengthens the test: I will repeat the analysis with a region the interpretation does not predict, such as a visual cortex volume, as a negative control. A meaningful correlation there would suggest a global factor, not hippocampal specificity. Both checks are specified in advance, apply to all participants, and need no selection from the data, so there is no circularity. Because I run two analyses, I will treat them as one family and apply a Bonferroni threshold of 0.025, and I will report both even if one is null. A remaining limit is that a covariate removes only the confounds I measured.
