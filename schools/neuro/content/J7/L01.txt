@article
Nobody has ever drilled through Earth. The deepest hole ever made reaches only about 12 km, while the distance to Earth's center is about **6,371 km**. So how do we know what is down there? Mostly from **seismic waves**: vibrations from earthquakes that travel through the planet and bend, speed up, slow down or stop depending on what they pass through.

### Four main layers by composition

- **Crust.** The thin, rocky outer skin. Oceanic crust is about 5 to 10 km thick and made mostly of dense basalt. Continental crust is about 30 to 50 km thick (more under mountains) and made mostly of lighter granite-like rock.
- **Mantle.** A thick layer of hot rock about 2,900 km thick that makes up most of Earth's volume. It is solid, but over millions of years it flows very slowly, like extremely thick putty.
- **Outer core.** A **liquid** layer of mostly iron and nickel, about 2,200 km thick, from a depth of about 2,900 km to about 5,150 km.
- **Inner core.** A **solid** ball of iron and nickel with a radius of about 1,220 km. It is hotter than the outer core, but the enormous pressure squeezes the metal into a solid.

### Layers by behavior

Scientists also divide the outer part of Earth by how it behaves. The **lithosphere** is the rigid outer shell: the crust plus the uppermost mantle. It is broken into pieces called plates. Beneath it, the **asthenosphere** is a hot, weak part of the mantle that can flow, so the plates can slowly move on top of it.

### Reading the waves

There are two main kinds of body waves. **P waves** (primary) are compression waves that travel through solids and liquids and arrive first. **S waves** (secondary) shake material side to side and travel only through solids. Seismograph stations on the far side of the planet from an earthquake record P waves but no S waves. That "shadow" shows that something in the middle is liquid: the outer core. Wave speeds also change suddenly at layer boundaries, which is how scientists found where each layer begins.

### Working with the numbers

Depth is measured down from the surface, and radius is measured out from the center. They add up to Earth's full radius: **radius at a boundary = 6,371 km minus its depth**. A layer's thickness is the difference between the depths (or radii) of its top and bottom.

@example
title: How big is the inner core compared with Earth?
problem: The inner core has a radius of about 1,220 km and Earth has a radius of about 6,371 km. What percent of Earth's radius is the inner core's radius?
step: Write the comparison as a fraction: $\frac{1220}{6371}$.
step: Divide: $\frac{1220}{6371} \approx 0.1915$.
step: Multiply by $100$ to turn it into a percent: about $19\%$.
step: Check that it is reasonable: $1220$ is a bit less than one fifth of $6371$, and one fifth is $20\%$.

@mistakes
- Thinking the inner core is liquid because it is the hottest layer. Pressure keeps it solid.
- Confusing the crust with the lithosphere. The lithosphere includes the crust and the rigid top of the mantle.
- Mixing up depth and radius: a boundary at 2,900 km depth is at 3,471 km radius, not 2,900 km.
- Saying the mantle is a liquid ocean of magma. It is solid rock that flows very slowly.
