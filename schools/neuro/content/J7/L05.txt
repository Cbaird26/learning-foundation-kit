@article
The water in your glass may have been in a cloud last week, and the same water has been used and reused for billions of years. Earth's water is always moving between the ocean, the air, the land and living things. This movement is the **water cycle**, and it is powered by **energy from the Sun** and by **gravity**.

### Steps of the cycle

- **Evaporation:** the Sun heats liquid water and it becomes water vapor, mostly from the ocean.
- **Transpiration:** plants release water vapor from their leaves.
- **Condensation:** rising air cools, and the vapor turns into tiny droplets that form clouds.
- **Precipitation:** rain, snow, sleet or hail falls when droplets grow heavy enough.
- **Runoff and infiltration:** water flows downhill over the land into streams and rivers (runoff) or soaks into the ground (infiltration), where it becomes **groundwater**.

### Where the water is

About **97%** of Earth's water is salty ocean water. Only about **2.5%** is fresh, and most of that fresh water is frozen in glaciers and ice sheets (about 69% of the fresh total) or stored as groundwater. Rivers and lakes hold a tiny fraction, yet they are the water most people use.

### Ocean water

Ocean water contains dissolved salts, mostly sodium chloride. **Salinity** is the mass of dissolved salt in a given mass of water. Average ocean salinity is about **35 g of salt per kg of seawater**, or 3.5%. When seawater evaporates, the water leaves and the salt stays behind. That is why salt can be collected from shallow ponds in sunny places.

### Ocean currents

**Surface currents**, such as the Gulf Stream, are pushed by steady winds and bent by Earth's rotation. They carry warm or cold water around the globe, which affects climate: the Gulf Stream helps keep northwestern Europe milder than places at the same latitude elsewhere. **Deep currents** are driven by differences in density. Cold, salty water is dense and sinks, especially near the poles, and flows slowly along the ocean floor. Surface and deep currents form a global "conveyor belt" that mixes the ocean over hundreds to a thousand years.

### Salt math

To find the salt in a sample, multiply the sample's mass by the salinity:
$$\text{mass of salt} = \text{salinity} \times \text{mass of seawater}.$$
For percentages, convert to a decimal first: $3.5\% = 0.035$.

@example
title: Salt left behind by evaporation
problem: A tide pool holds 800 g of seawater with a salinity of 35 g of salt per kg. After all the water evaporates, what mass of salt remains?
step: Convert the mass to kilograms: $800\ \text{g} = 0.8$ kg.
step: Multiply by the salinity: $35 \times 0.8 = 28$ g.
step: Check with percent: $3.5\%$ of $800 = 0.035 \times 800 = 28$ g. Both methods agree.

@mistakes
- Thinking evaporation takes the salt up with the water. Only water vapor leaves; the salt stays behind.
- Forgetting that most of the fresh water is frozen or underground, not in lakes and rivers.
- Saying clouds are made of water vapor. Vapor is invisible; clouds are tiny liquid droplets or ice crystals.
- Believing that all currents are driven by wind. Deep currents are driven by density differences.

@practice self
prompt: Describe the journey of a water molecule that starts in the ocean, falls as snow on a mountain, and returns to the ocean. Name at least five water-cycle processes in order, and say what powers the cycle.
hint: Start with evaporation from the ocean and follow the water step by step until it returns.
solution: The molecule evaporates from the ocean (powered by solar energy), rises and cools, condenses into a cloud, and falls as snow (precipitation). The snow accumulates, then melts in spring. The meltwater either runs off into streams (runoff) or soaks into the ground (infiltration) and moves as groundwater. Rivers carry it back to the ocean. Gravity moves the water downhill and the Sun drives evaporation.
