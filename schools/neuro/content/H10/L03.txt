@article
A play is written to be **performed**, so the meaning lives in speech and action as well as words on a page. When you read drama, imagine the stage: who is present, who moves, what each person wants.

### Conventions of drama

- **Dialogue** is conversation between characters. A **monologue** is one character speaking at length; a **soliloquy** is a speech in which a character, alone, speaks thoughts aloud to the audience. An **aside** is a brief remark heard by the audience but not by other characters.
- **Stage directions** describe action, setting and delivery. A play is divided into **acts** and **scenes**.
- **Dramatic irony** occurs when the audience knows something a character does not.
- A **foil** is a character whose contrast highlights qualities of another.

### Conflict and the scene

Every effective scene has a **conflict**: someone wants something and faces an obstacle. To analyze a scene, ask: What does each character want? What stands in the way? What tactic does each try (flatter, threaten, plead, deceive)? How does the balance of power shift by the end? Tracking these shows why a scene matters to the whole plot.

### Shakespeare's language

Shakespeare wrote mostly in **blank verse**: unrhymed iambic pentameter (ten syllables, five da-DUM beats). Lines that break the pattern are worth noticing: a short line may signal a pause or silence, and a shift from verse to **prose** often marks lower-status characters, comic moments or madness. Rhymed couplets often close a scene or speech.

Early Modern English can be decoded:

1. Read to the punctuation, not the line end.
2. Reorder inverted syntax: "Comes he not?" means "Does he not come?"
3. Look up words that have changed meaning. In the plays, "anon" means "soon," "wherefore" means "why," and "thou/thee/thy" are singular "you."
4. Paraphrase each sentence in plain modern English before interpreting.

Consider "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks? / It is the east, and Juliet is the sun." The speaker compares Juliet to the sun in a **metaphor**; "soft" means "wait, quiet."

### Reading for meaning

Soliloquies show private thought; compare them with what a character says to others to see disguise or doubt. Imagery patterns (light and dark, blood, sleep) repeat across a play and support themes. Always ask how a speech affects the conflict and what it reveals about the speaker.

@example
title: Paraphrasing and analyzing a line
problem: Explain the line "Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow, / Creeps in this petty pace from day to day" from Macbeth (Act 5, Scene 5).
step: Paraphrase: The coming days, one after another, crawl along at a small, slow pace from one day to the next.
step: Identify devices: repetition of "tomorrow" imitates endless sameness; "creeps" personifies time as a slow, almost insect-like creature.
step: Connect to context: the speaker has lost hope and sees life as empty repetition.
step: Claim: The repetition and personification convey exhausted despair, showing that the speaker no longer believes his actions matter.

@mistakes
- Treating a soliloquy as dialogue; no other character hears it.
- Reading line by line without stopping at punctuation, which scrambles the sense.
- Assuming an unfamiliar word means what it means today.
- Describing what a character says but not what he or she wants.
