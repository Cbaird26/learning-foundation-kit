@article
A man who has just been introduced to you cannot, ten minutes later, say that he has met you. He talks fluently, reasons well and repeats a string of seven digits perfectly. Yet he cannot learn your name. Over days he practices a tracing task and gets better at it, while insisting each day that he has never seen it. How can memory be both absent and present? The answer, from patient H.M. and many others, is that memory is not one thing.

### Patient H.M.

In 1953 the surgeon Scoville removed tissue from the medial temporal lobes on both sides of the brain of a young man (known in the literature as H.M.) to relieve severe epilepsy. The removal included the anterior hippocampus and neighboring cortex and the amygdala. Scoville and Milner (1957) reported the result: his seizures eased, his intelligence and language were intact, and he could hold information for seconds, but he could no longer form new lasting memories of events and facts, a severe **anterograde amnesia**. He also lost memories of the years just before the operation (a graded **retrograde amnesia**), while remote childhood memories remained. Later testing showed that he could learn motor skills such as drawing a figure while seeing only its mirror image, improving across days without recalling the sessions.

### The map of memory systems

The findings divide memory roughly like this:

| system | content | typical test | affected by H.M.-type lesions |
|---|---|---|---|
| **working memory** (short-term) | information held and used for seconds (Baddeley and Hitch, 1974) | digit span | spared |
| **episodic** (Tulving, 1972) | personal events, with time and place | story or word-list recall, recognition | impaired |
| **semantic** | facts and word meanings | vocabulary, naming | impaired for new facts; old facts largely spared |
| **procedural** | skills and habits | mirror tracing, serial reaction time task | largely spared |
| **priming** | faster processing of repeated items | word-stem completion | largely spared |
| **conditioning** | simple associations | eyeblink conditioning | spared in simple forms |

The first two rows of the right half form the **declarative** (explicit) group, which can be consciously recalled and stated; the rest are **nondeclarative**, expressed in performance. The classic evidence is the **dissociation**: damage to one region impairs one kind of test but not another. A **single dissociation** can be explained by one test being harder than the other. A **double dissociation** (patient A impaired on task 1 but not task 2, patient B on task 2 but not task 1) rules out difficulty and supports separate processes; it still does not show which brain region does the work, nor that the systems share no component.

### Classifying findings in code

Clinical tests are compared with a control group. The **z-score** $z = (x - \bar{x}_{\text{control}})/s_{\text{control}}$ says how many control standard deviations a score lies from the control mean; a common convention is to call performance impaired when $z \le -2$. The data below are invented for practice, with each row a task and control mean and SD given:

| task | system | control mean | control SD | patient P1 | patient P2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| word recall (of 15) | episodic | 10 | 2 | 3 | 9 |
| face recognition (of 30) | episodic | 25 | 2.5 | 15 | 24 |
| vocabulary | semantic | 40 | 4 | 38 | 41 |
| mirror-tracing gain (s) | procedural | 30 | 8 | 28 | 8 |
| stem completion (%) | priming | 20 | 5 | 19 | 17.5 |
| digit span | working | 7 | 1 | 7 | 7 |

```python
def z_scores(scores, mean, sd):
    """z-score of each task: (score - control mean) / control SD."""
    return {t: (scores[t] - mean[t]) / sd[t] for t in scores}

def impaired_tasks(scores, mean, sd, cutoff=-2.0):
    """Sorted names of the tasks with z at or below the cutoff."""
    z = z_scores(scores, mean, sd)
    return sorted(t for t in z if z[t] <= cutoff)

mean = {"recall": 10, "recog": 25, "vocab": 40, "mirror": 30, "prime": 20, "digit": 7}
sd = {"recall": 2, "recog": 2.5, "vocab": 4, "mirror": 8, "prime": 5, "digit": 1}
p1 = {"recall": 3, "recog": 15, "vocab": 38, "mirror": 28, "prime": 19, "digit": 7}
p2 = {"recall": 9, "recog": 24, "vocab": 41, "mirror": 8, "prime": 17.5, "digit": 7}
print(impaired_tasks(p1, mean, sd), impaired_tasks(p2, mean, sd))
```

This prints `['recall', 'recog'] ['mirror']`. P1 is impaired on episodic tests (z = −3.5 and −4.0) but spared on skill, priming and span; P2 is the reverse on skill learning. Together they form a double dissociation between episodic recall and skill learning, much like the contrast reported between patients with medial temporal damage and patients with basal ganglia disease such as Parkinson's or Huntington's disease.

### Cautions

The map is not tidy. Recognition memory may combine a sense of familiarity and recollection of details, and researchers disagree whether the hippocampus is needed for both or mainly for recollection. Whether the hippocampus supports memory for events specifically, or relations among items more broadly (including some tasks outside conscious recall), is debated. Amnesia from lesions is also rarely confined to one structure. Finally, a patient's lesion tells us what a damaged brain can no longer do, not simply what the missing region does in an intact brain.

@example
title: Is a patient impaired?
problem: A control group scores a mean of 24 with a standard deviation of 3 on a recognition test (out of 30). A patient scores 15. Compute the z-score and decide with the cutoff $z \le -2$.
step: The difference from the control mean is $15 - 24 = -9$.
step: Divide by the control SD: $z = -9/3 = -3$.
step: $-3 \le -2$, so the patient is impaired on this test.
answer: $z = -3$: impaired.

@example
title: A double dissociation from two patients
problem: Using control means and SDs of 24 and 3 (recognition) and 30 and 8 (mirror-tracing gain), patient A scores 15 and 29, and patient B scores 23 and 10. With the cutoff $z \le -2$, do A and B form a double dissociation?
step: Patient A: recognition $z = (15-24)/3 = -3$ (impaired); tracing $z = (29-30)/8 = -0.125$ (not impaired).
step: Patient B: recognition $z = (23-24)/3 = -0.33$ (not impaired); tracing $z = (10-30)/8 = -2.5$ (impaired).
step: A is impaired on recognition but not tracing; B is impaired on tracing but not recognition: the pattern is a double dissociation.
step: It suggests partly separate processes for the two tasks, though it does not say which structure supports each.
answer: Yes: A is impaired only on recognition, B only on tracing.

@mistakes
- Treating H.M. as having lost all memory: he kept working memory, skills, old memories and priming; the loss was of new episodic and semantic learning.
- Concluding from a single dissociation that two processes are separate; a harder test can show impairment alone.
- Calling a deficit on a task a deficit of a whole system; each task mixes processes, so conclusions need several tasks per system.
- Using a cutoff mechanically: a z-score of $-1.9$ and one of $-2.1$ are not different kinds of performance, and control groups differ in size and age.

```python
def classify_pattern(scores, mean, sd, task_system, cutoff=-2.0):
    """Impaired, spared or mixed status of each memory system."""
    flags = {}
    for task, score in scores.items():
        z = (score - mean[task]) / sd[task]
        flags.setdefault(task_system[task], []).append(z <= cutoff)
    result = {}
    for system, flag_list in flags.items():
        if all(flag_list):
            result[system] = "impaired"
        elif any(flag_list):
            result[system] = "mixed"
        else:
            result[system] = "spared"
    return result
```
hint: Compute each task's z-score, group the true/false "impaired" flags by system, and decide with `all` and `any`.
solution: The patient in test `c` scores exactly $z = -2.0$ on the story task, so the cutoff must be inclusive, and $z = -0.2$ on faces, so the episodic system is mixed: one impaired task and one spared. Grouping by system, not by task, is the step that turns raw scores into a statement about memory systems.

```python
def double_dissociation(pa, pb, mean, sd, task1, task2, cutoff=-2.0):
    """True if A is impaired only on task1 and B only on task2."""
    def impaired(patient, task):
        return (patient[task] - mean[task]) / sd[task] <= cutoff

    return (impaired(pa, task1) and not impaired(pa, task2)
            and impaired(pb, task2) and not impaired(pb, task1))
```
hint: Write a small helper that says whether a patient is impaired on a task, then combine four conditions with `and`.
solution: The pattern needs all four conditions. The test on `"story"` and `"faces"` fails because patient A is impaired on both episodic tests, and the test on `"story"` and `"span"` fails because patient B, with $z = -1$ on span, is not impaired there. Swapping the patients reverses which task each is impaired on, so the result is `False`.

```python
def gain_from_first(times):
    """Percent improvement of each session relative to the first session."""
    if not times:
        return []
    first = times[0]
    return [100.0 * (first - t) / first for t in times]
```
hint: Take the first time as the reference and apply the formula to every session, including the first; handle the empty list separately.
solution: Gain is relative to the first session, so session 1 always has gain 0, and a session slower than the first gives a negative gain (the second test). Such curves in H.M.-type patients and controls both rise across sessions, which is what shows that procedural learning is intact.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain what patient H.M.'s case showed about memory, and why researchers looked for a second patient with the opposite pattern. Describe what a double dissociation between a recognition test and a skill-learning test would support, and give one reason it would not settle which brain regions underlie each system.
hint: Contrast a single dissociation with a double dissociation in terms of task difficulty.
solution: H.M. showed that a lesion of the medial temporal lobes can destroy the ability to form new episodic and semantic memories while leaving working memory, intelligence, language and motor-skill learning largely intact, so memory is not a single faculty and the medial temporal lobe is important for forming declarative memories. A single dissociation could be explained by the spared tests being easier or less sensitive; a second patient impaired on the skill task but normal on recognition would show that task difficulty alone cannot explain the pattern. A double dissociation then supports at least partly separate processes for the two kinds of learning. It does not settle which regions underlie each system because lesions are often large and affect fibers and several structures, because tests rely on mixtures of processes, and because a lesion shows what the rest of the brain cannot do without a region and not what the region does by itself.
