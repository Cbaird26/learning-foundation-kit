@article
In classical conditioning, the animal's behavior does not change what happens next: the food arrives whether or not the dog salivates. In **instrumental** (or **operant**) **conditioning**, behavior produces the outcome. A rat presses a lever and a food pellet drops; a pigeon pecks a key and a light turns on. Thorndike's law of effect and Skinner's operant chamber studies share one idea: behavior followed by a good outcome becomes more likely, and behavior followed by a bad one becomes less likely. The questions here are quantitative. How does the pattern of responding depend on the rule that links responses to rewards, and can a simple learning rule predict choices?

### Four consequences

A consequence can be added or removed, and it can be pleasant or aversive:

| | outcome added | outcome removed |
|---|---|---|
| appetitive (food, praise) | **positive reinforcement**: behavior increases | **negative punishment** (omission): behavior decreases |
| aversive (shock, noise) | **positive punishment**: behavior decreases | **negative reinforcement** (escape): behavior increases |

"Reinforcement" always means that behavior increases; "negative" means that something is removed. When a reinforcer stops being delivered, responding declines: **extinction**, which, as in classical conditioning, is not the erasure of the learning.

### Schedules of reinforcement

A **schedule** is the rule that decides which responses are reinforced. The four basic ones:

- **Fixed ratio (FR $n$)**: every $n$th response is reinforced. Steady fast responding with a brief pause after each reinforcer.
- **Variable ratio (VR $n$)**: the number of responses required varies around a mean of $n$. Very high, steady rates; slot machines are the familiar analogy.
- **Fixed interval (FI $t$)**: the first response after $t$ seconds since the last reinforcer is reinforced. Responses before that earn nothing. Responding is slow after a reinforcer and speeds up as the interval ends (a "scallop" in a cumulative record).
- **Variable interval (VI $t$)**: the interval varies around a mean; moderate, steady responding.

Ratio schedules tie reward to the amount of work, so faster responding earns more; interval schedules cap the reward rate, so extra responses are wasted. That explains why ratio schedules usually produce higher response rates. Behavior learned on a variable (partial) schedule typically extinguishes more slowly than behavior learned with every response rewarded, the **partial reinforcement extinction effect**.

In code, a schedule is a few lines that scan a list of response times (in seconds):

```python
def fixed_ratio(response_times, n):
    """Times (s) at which an FR-n schedule delivers a reinforcer."""
    reinforcers, count = [], 0
    for t in response_times:
        count += 1
        if count == n:
            reinforcers.append(t)
            count = 0
    return reinforcers

times = [0.8, 1.5, 2.1, 3.0, 3.4, 4.6, 5.2, 6.1, 7.3, 8.0, 8.8, 9.5]
print(fixed_ratio(times, 4))
```

This prints `[3.0, 6.1, 9.5]`: the 4th, 8th and 12th responses.

### Matching and learning from outcomes

When a pigeon chooses between two keys on concurrent VI schedules, the share of responses on key 1 approximately equals its share of the reinforcers (Herrnstein, 1961):

$$\frac{B_1}{B_1 + B_2} = \frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_2}.$$

If key 1 pays 20 reinforcers per hour and key 2 pays 60, the strict matching law predicts $20/80 = 25\%$ of responses on key 1. Animals often show *undermatching*, a less extreme split.

A simple learning model connects this to week 1. Keep an action value $Q_a$ for each action and update the chosen one after each outcome $r$ with the **delta rule**

$$Q_a \leftarrow Q_a + \alpha\,(r - Q_a),$$

the same error-correcting form as Rescorla–Wagner, with $\alpha$ the learning rate. To choose, a **softmax** converts values into probabilities, $P(a) = e^{\beta Q_a}/\sum_b e^{\beta Q_b}$, where the inverse temperature $\beta$ sets how strongly the larger value is favored. With two actions, $P(1) = 1/(1 + e^{-\beta(Q_1 - Q_2)})$. This model is a description of choice, not a claim that the animal computes such numbers; whether instrumental behavior is driven by values at all is the topic of week 5.

@example
title: A fixed-interval schedule from response times
problem: On an FI 10 s schedule the clock starts at time 0 and restarts at each reinforcer. A rat's lever presses occur at 4, 11, 13, 19, 24 and 25 seconds. Which presses are reinforced?
step: Presses at 4 s: only 4 s have elapsed (less than 10), so nothing happens and the clock keeps running.
step: Press at 11 s: 11 s have elapsed, so this press is reinforced; the clock restarts at 11 s.
step: Presses at 13 s and 19 s are 2 s and 8 s after the reinforcer, so they earn nothing. The press at 24 s is 13 s after the reinforcer and is reinforced; the clock restarts at 24 s.
step: The press at 25 s is 1 s after the reinforcer, so it earns nothing.
answer: The presses at 11 s and 24 s are reinforced.

@example
title: One delta-rule update and a softmax choice
problem: A rat's value for the left lever is $Q_L = 0.5$ and for the right lever $Q_R = 0.2$. It chooses left and gets food ($r = 1$). With $\alpha = 0.2$ and $\beta = 3$, find the new $Q_L$ and the probability of choosing left on the next trial.
step: The prediction error is $r - Q_L = 1 - 0.5 = 0.5$.
step: The update is $Q_L \leftarrow 0.5 + 0.2 \times 0.5 = 0.6$; $Q_R$ is unchanged because it was not chosen.
step: The value difference is $0.6 - 0.2 = 0.4$, so $P(\text{left}) = 1/(1 + e^{-3 \times 0.4}) = 1/(1 + e^{-1.2}) \approx 0.769$.
answer: $Q_L = 0.6$ and $P(\text{left}) \approx 0.77$.

@mistakes
- Treating "negative reinforcement" as punishment: it is the removal of an aversive event that increases behavior, such as pressing a lever to stop a noise.
- Restarting a fixed-interval clock at every response instead of only at reinforcers; early responses neither earn a reward nor reset the timer.
- Updating the value of every action after each outcome; the delta rule changes only the action that was taken.
- Expecting the same response rate on ratio and interval schedules; the reward rate depends on responding only for ratio schedules.

```python
def fixed_interval(response_times, interval):
    """Reinforcer times (s) on a fixed-interval schedule."""
    reinforcers, last = [], 0.0
    for t in response_times:
        if t - last >= interval:
            reinforcers.append(t)
            last = t
    return reinforcers
```

model:
```python
def delta_rule_values(choices, rewards, alpha, n_actions, q0=0.0):
    """Final action values after delta-rule learning."""
    Q = [q0] * n_actions
    for a, r in zip(choices, rewards):
        Q[a] += alpha * (r - Q[a])
    return Q
```
hint: Start with a list of `n_actions` copies of `q0`, then loop over the trials with `zip`, changing only the entry for the chosen action.
solution: Only the chosen action's value moves toward the reward. With $\alpha = 1$ the value jumps straight to the last reward, a useful edge case; unchosen actions keep their starting value, as the second test (which starts at 0.5) shows.

model:
```python
def variable_ratio(response_times, requirements):
    """Reinforcer times on a VR schedule with cycling requirements."""
    reinforcers, index, count = [], 0, 0
    for t in response_times:
        count += 1
        if count == requirements[index % len(requirements)]:
            reinforcers.append(t)
            index += 1
            count = 0
    return reinforcers
```
hint: Keep a response counter and an index into the requirements; when the counter reaches the current requirement, record the time, reset the counter and advance the index with a modulo.
solution: This is the fixed-ratio loop with the target changing after each reinforcer. The requirement list `[5, 2]` gives reinforcers on the 5th response (3.1 s) and then the 2nd after that (the 7th response overall, 4.2 s); the next requirement of 5 is never completed because only three responses remain.

@quiz self
prompt: Two groups of rats press a lever for food. Group 1 gets a pellet after every press (continuous reinforcement); group 2 gets one after a variable number of presses averaging 5 (VR 5). After the same number of reinforcers, food is stopped for both. Predict which group presses longer during extinction, describe the cumulative record on each schedule before extinction, and give one proposed explanation for the difference.
hint: Think about how easy it is for each group to detect that the rule has changed.
solution: Group 2 should keep pressing longer: this is the partial reinforcement extinction effect. Before extinction the continuous group presses steadily, with brief pauses to eat; the VR group shows a high, steady rate with few pauses, a straight line of steep slope. One explanation is discrimination: for the continuous group, a string of unrewarded presses is quickly distinguishable from training, while for the VR group long unrewarded runs were common, so extinction looks like more of the same. A related account says the VR animal has learned to expect runs of non-reward followed by a reward. Both explanations are debated, and the effect is a reliable behavioral result whose cause is still studied.

rubric: Describes steady high-rate responding on VR and brief post-reinforcement pauses on continuous reinforcement.
rubric: Gives a reasonable mechanism (discrimination of the change, or learned expectation of non-reward runs) and notes the explanations are debated.
