@article
A rat explores a box. You record one hippocampal neuron. It is silent almost everywhere, but fires reliably whenever the rat is in one particular corner, whichever direction it faces. Move the cue cards and the field may move too. O'Keefe and Dostrovsky (1971) reported such **place cells** in the rat hippocampus, and the discovery led to the idea that the hippocampus provides a **cognitive map** of the environment, a notion that Tolman had proposed from behavior in maze experiments.

### Cells of the spatial system

- **Place cells** (hippocampus, mainly CA1 and CA3) fire in one or a few compact **place fields**. Across a population the fields tile the environment. In a new environment the fields reorganize (**remapping**), so the map is specific to a context. Place cells also carry non-spatial information such as objects and events, so "place" is only part of what they encode.
- **Grid cells** (medial entorhinal cortex), reported by Hafting, Fyhn, Molden, Moser and Moser (2005), fire at multiple locations arranged on a triangular lattice that tiles the whole environment. Different cells have different spacings (the **grid period**), orientations and offsets (**phases**); the period increases along the dorsal-to-ventral axis of the cortex, from tens of centimeters to meters.
- **Head-direction cells** fire when the head points in one direction, and **border cells** fire near boundaries. Together with grid cells they provide inputs for the hippocampal map and for path integration, the updating of position from self-motion.

Whether grid cells give place cells their fields, or place fields can arise without grid input, is debated, as is the extent to which both systems also represent non-spatial relations between experiences.

### A model of a place-cell population

On a linear track (positions $x$ in cm), model place cell $i$ with a Gaussian field centered at $c_i$:

$$f_i(x) = r_{\max}\,\exp\!\Big(-\frac{(x - c_i)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big),$$

with peak rate $r_{\max}$ in hertz and width $\sigma$ in cm. In a window of $T$ seconds the cell fires a Poisson-distributed count $n_i$ with mean $f_i(x)T$. If cells fire independently, the likelihood of the counts $n_1, \ldots, n_N$ at position $x$ is a product of Poisson terms. Taking logarithms and dropping the terms that do not depend on $x$:

$$\log L(x) = \sum_i \big[n_i \log f_i(x) - f_i(x)\,T\big].$$

The **maximum-likelihood decoder** returns the candidate position $\hat{x}$ with the largest $\log L$. A simpler **population-vector** decoder returns the count-weighted average of the field centers, $\hat{x} = \sum_i n_i c_i / \sum_i n_i$. Take four cells with centers 20, 60, 100 and 140 cm, $\sigma = 20$ cm, $r_{\max} = 20$ Hz, $T = 0.5$ s, and observed counts $(1, 6, 5, 0)$:

```python
import math

def place_rate(x, center, sigma, r_max):
    """Firing rate (Hz) of a Gaussian place field at position x (cm)."""
    return r_max * math.exp(-(x - center) ** 2 / (2 * sigma ** 2))

def decode_ml(counts, centers, sigma, r_max, T, candidates):
    """Candidate position (cm) with the highest Poisson log-likelihood."""
    best, best_ll = None, None
    for x in candidates:
        ll = 0.0
        for n, c in zip(counts, centers):
            f = place_rate(x, c, sigma, r_max)
            ll += n * math.log(f) - f * T
        if best_ll is None or ll > best_ll:
            best, best_ll = x, ll
    return best

centers = [20, 60, 100, 140]
print(decode_ml([1, 6, 5, 0], centers, 20, 20, 0.5, range(0, 161, 5)))
```

This prints `75`. The population-vector estimate is $(1 \cdot 20 + 6 \cdot 60 + 5 \cdot 100)/12 = 73.3$ cm: close, but the two estimators need not agree. Both ignore the animal's earlier positions; real decoders add a prior and a model of movement.

### Grid cells and a position code

One grid cell is ambiguous because it fires at many places. Cells with different periods resolve each other, like the hands of a clock. In one dimension, a cell with period $p$ and phase $\phi$ is "on" at positions with $x \bmod p = \phi$. Two cells with periods 30 and 40 cm together single out a position until the pattern repeats at the least common multiple, 120 cm; a cell pair with phases 10 and 10 is consistent with $x = 10$ cm and 130 cm on a longer track. Real grid cells fall in a small number of discrete modules of similar period, and the combined code can cover a distance much larger than any single period, a point made in theoretical work.

### What decoding shows

That a position can be decoded from spikes shows that the population contains information about position. It does not show that the brain uses the same decoder, nor that cells encode only position. A good decoding result also depends on the number of cells and the window length; a short window gives few spikes, and the error grows.

@example
title: A place field as a firing rate and a count
problem: A cell has its field centered at 60 cm with $\sigma = 20$ cm and $r_{\max} = 20$ Hz. A rat is at 80 cm. What is the cell's rate, the expected spike count in a 0.5 s window, and the probability of exactly 6 spikes?
step: The distance is 20 cm, one $\sigma$, so the rate is $20\,e^{-0.5} = 12.13$ Hz.
step: The expected count is $12.13 \times 0.5 = 6.07$ spikes.
step: For a Poisson count with mean $\mu = 6.07$: $P(6) = e^{-\mu}\mu^6/6! = 0.1606$.
answer: Rate 12.13 Hz, expected count 6.07, and a 16% chance of exactly 6 spikes.

@example
title: Population-vector decoding
problem: Three place cells have centers 30, 60 and 90 cm and fire 2, 5 and 3 spikes in a window. Estimate the position with the population vector.
step: Weight each center by its count: $2 \times 30 = 60$, $5 \times 60 = 300$, $3 \times 90 = 270$.
step: The weighted sum is $630$ and the total number of spikes is $10$.
step: The estimate is $630/10 = 63$ cm.
answer: About $63$ cm.

@mistakes
- Treating a place cell as a sensor of a landmark: fields depend on the animal's position in the environment, can persist after cues are removed for a short time, and remap between environments.
- Describing a grid cell's field as a single bump, or place cells as having a lattice; the lattice of several fields is the grid-cell signature.
- Dropping the $-f_i(x)T$ term of the log-likelihood; it is constant only when the summed rates are the same at every candidate position.
- Believing that successful decoding proves the brain decodes in the same way.

```python
def population_vector(counts, centers):
    """Count-weighted mean of the field centers, or None with no spikes."""
    total = sum(counts)
    if total == 0:
        return None
    return sum(n * c for n, c in zip(counts, centers)) / total
```

model:
```python
import math


def decode_ml_2d(counts, centers, sigma, r_max, T, candidates):
    """Candidate (x, y) with the highest Poisson log-likelihood."""
    best, best_ll = None, None
    for x, y in candidates:
        ll = 0.0
        for n, (cx, cy) in zip(counts, centers):
            d2 = (x - cx) ** 2 + (y - cy) ** 2
            f = r_max * math.exp(-d2 / (2 * sigma ** 2))
            ll += n * math.log(f) - f * T
        if best_ll is None or ll > best_ll:
            best, best_ll = (x, y), ll
    return best
```
hint: Use squared Euclidean distance in the exponent, and keep the first candidate with the highest log-likelihood (strict comparison).
solution: This is the one-dimensional decoder with the squared distance computed in two dimensions. In the third test the cells at `(50, 0)` and `(50, 50)` fire most, so the estimate lies near `x = 50`, pulled toward the cell with nine spikes; the $-fT$ term makes the best `y` equal 40 and not 50, because a position at the exact center of a field would predict more spikes from that cell than the 9 observed in one second.

model:
```python
import math


def spatial_information(rates, occupancy):
    """Spatial information in bits per spike."""
    mean_rate = 0.0
    for p, r in zip(occupancy, rates):
        mean_rate += p * r
    if mean_rate == 0:
        return 0.0
    info = 0.0
    for p, r in zip(occupancy, rates):
        if r > 0:
            info += p * (r / mean_rate) * math.log2(r / mean_rate)
    return info
```
hint: Compute the occupancy-weighted mean rate first; skip bins with zero rate (the limit of $x\log x$ at 0 is 0) and handle a cell that never fires.
solution: A cell that fires only in one quarter of the space tells you 2 bits per spike (it halves the space twice), a cell that fires equally everywhere tells you nothing, and one that fires in half the space gives 1 bit. A silent cell has no spikes and returns 0.0 by convention, which avoids division by zero.

solution: The decoding shows that these 40 neurons collectively carry information about position, since an observer with the model can read it out to within 8 cm.
