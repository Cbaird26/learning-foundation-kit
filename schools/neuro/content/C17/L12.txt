@article
A three-year-old learns the names of hundreds of objects, yet adults remember almost nothing of their own early years. An eighty-year-old can define rare words better than a twenty-year-old, yet struggles to recall what happened at breakfast. And a person with a dementia may remember a song from youth while forgetting this morning's visit. Memory changes with age, and the changes differ by system, as the maps of weeks 6 and 7 would predict.

### Development

Plasticity is high early in life and often declines. In a **critical period** (or sensitive period) experience has an outsized effect on the circuit. In the visual system, closing one eye of a kitten for a few days during a sensitive period shifts cortical responses toward the open eye, a classic result of Hubel and Wiesel, while the same deprivation in an adult cat has little effect. Infants show **perceptual narrowing**: young infants discriminate speech sounds of languages they have never heard, and by the end of the first year they lose some of those contrasts while sharpening the contrasts of their own language (Werker and Tees, 1984). Infants also learn the statistics of sound sequences within minutes (Saffran, Aslin and Newport, 1996). Mechanisms proposed for the closing of critical periods include the maturation of inhibition and changes in the extracellular matrix; this is an active area of study.

**Infantile amnesia** is the fact that adults recall very few events from before about age 3 or 4, although infants and toddlers do learn and remember over days to weeks, and show implicit learning from the first months. Explanations include the late maturation of the hippocampus and its inputs, rapid forgetting in the early years, the later development of language and of a sense of self, and the high rate of cell turnover in the dentate gyrus; these accounts are not mutually exclusive and none is settled. The extent of new neuron formation (neurogenesis) in the adult human hippocampus is itself debated, although it is well established in rodents.

### Aging

On average, healthy aging brings gradual declines in processing speed, working memory and, especially, episodic memory, with associated changes in the prefrontal cortex and the hippocampus. Semantic knowledge such as vocabulary is often preserved or increases into older age, and so does much procedural skill. Individual differences are large: some older adults perform like younger ones. Education, activity and other factors are associated with better outcomes, though the direction of causation is hard to establish. **Dementia** is not normal aging; it is a decline severe enough to interfere with daily life, with many causes.

**Alzheimer's disease** is the most common cause of dementia. It is characterized by extracellular plaques of amyloid-beta and intracellular tangles of tau protein, with loss of synapses and neurons, and it typically begins in the medial temporal lobe (entorhinal cortex and hippocampus) before spreading through the neocortex. The first symptoms are usually difficulty forming new episodic memories, then widening deficits. How much amyloid, tau and other processes each contribute to the damage, and which should be targeted, is under debate. Diagnosis and treatment belong to clinicians; this lesson describes the science only.

### A forgetting curve

A common model of forgetting is exponential: the proportion retained after $t$ days is $R(t) = e^{-t/S}$, where $S$ is the **stability** of the memory in days. A larger $S$ means slower forgetting, and the half-life is $S\ln 2$. Taking logarithms, $\ln R = -t/S$ is a line through the origin with slope $-1/S$. Fit it by least squares through the origin, which minimizes $\sum (\ln R_i + b\,t_i)^2$ over the slope $b$ and gives $b = -\sum t_i \ln R_i/\sum t_i^2$, so $S = -\sum t_i^2 / \sum t_i \ln R_i$. For retention $0.80, 0.66, 0.45, 0.30$ at $t = 1, 2, 4, 7$ days, $S = 5.52$ days, the fitted retention is $0.83, 0.70, 0.48, 0.28$ and the half-life is $3.83$ days. Whether forgetting is truly exponential is debated, and power-law curves often fit group data better, so $S$ is a description, not a mechanism.

### Losing connections

Synapse loss is a major correlate of cognitive decline in Alzheimer's disease. In a Hopfield network (week 11) it can be modeled by setting some weights to zero. Take $N = 8$ and three orthogonal patterns $(+,+,-,-,+,+,-,-)$, $(+,-,+,-,+,-,+,-)$ and $(+,+,+,+,-,-,-,-)$. List the 28 neuron pairs $(i,j)$ with $i<j$ in order, delete every $m$th pair's connections starting with the first (list positions $0, m, 2m, \ldots$; both $W_{ij}$ and $W_{ji}$; $m = 4, 3, 2, 1$ give 7, 10, 14 and 28 deleted pairs), cue each pattern with its first bit flipped and recall by asynchronous sweeps. The mean **overlap** $m = \frac{1}{N}\,\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\mathbf{s}$ between the recalled state and the stored pattern is:

| pairs deleted | 0 | 7 | 10 | 14 | 28 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| mean overlap | 1.00 | 1.00 | 0.67 | 0.67 | 0.75 |

Recall is intact after losing a quarter of the connections (**graceful degradation**), then fails: the remaining weights are unbalanced and pull the state toward other patterns. With every connection gone the state stays at the cue, which has overlap 0.75 (7 of 8 bits correct). So moderate damage can leave performance normal while greater damage makes it worse than the cue itself; this tolerance of moderate loss before performance suffers is analogous to the idea of reserve in aging research, although this toy model is not a model of reserve.

```python
import math

def fit_stability(times, retention):
    """Least-squares stability S (days) of R = exp(-t / S), through the origin."""
    num = sum(t * math.log(r) for t, r in zip(times, retention))
    den = sum(t * t for t in times)
    return -den / num

S = fit_stability([1, 2, 4, 7], [0.80, 0.66, 0.45, 0.30])
print(round(S, 2), round(S * math.log(2), 2))
```

This prints `5.52 3.83`.

problem: A memory has stability $S = 5$ days under the model $R = e^{-t/S}$. What fraction is retained after 3 days, and after how many days has half been forgotten?
step: After 3 days $R = e^{-3/5} = e^{-0.6} = 0.549$.

step: At 3 days the model gives $0.549 > 0.5$, consistent with the half-life of 3.47 days being later.
answer: About $55\%$ is retained after 3 days, and the half-life is $3.47$ days.

@mistakes
- Equating dementia with normal aging; normal aging produces small, gradual changes, whereas dementia is a decline severe enough to disrupt daily life.
- Saying that aging impairs all memory equally; semantic knowledge and procedural skills are relatively preserved while episodic memory and processing speed decline on average.
- Taking the best-fit exponential for the true form of forgetting; several curves fit sparse data, and the fit depends on the intervals tested.
- Assuming that more connection loss always gives proportionally worse recall; the network can tolerate moderate loss and then fail suddenly.

```python
import math


def best_stability(times, retention, candidates):
    """Candidate S minimizing the squared error of R = exp(-t / S)."""
    best, best_sse = None, None
    for S in candidates:
        sse = 0.0
        for t, r in zip(times, retention):
            sse += (r - math.exp(-t / S)) ** 2
        if best_sse is None or sse < best_sse:
            best, best_sse = S, sse
    return best
```
hint: For each candidate, compute the sum of squared differences between the measured retention and the model's prediction; keep the candidate with the lowest value, using a strict comparison so that ties keep the first.
solution: This is a grid search on the original scale. It need not agree with the log-linear fit of the article, because squared errors in retention weight the early, high-retention points differently from squared errors in its logarithm (here the grid gives 5 days and the log fit gives 5.5 days). With exact data the true value gives zero error and is chosen.

```python
def lesion_overlap(patterns, drop_pairs, cue, target):
    """Overlap of the recalled state with the target after deleting weights."""
    n = len(patterns[0])
    W = [[0.0] * n for _ in range(n)]
    for p in patterns:
        for i in range(n):
            for j in range(n):
                if i != j:
                    W[i][j] += p[i] * p[j] / n
    for i, j in drop_pairs:
        W[i][j] = 0.0
        W[j][i] = 0.0
    s = list(cue)
    changed = True
    while changed:
        changed = False
        for i in range(n):
            h = sum(W[i][j] * s[j] for j in range(n))
            new = s[i] if h == 0 else (1 if h > 0 else -1)
            if new != s[i]:
                s[i] = new
                changed = True
    return sum(t * x for t, x in zip(target, s)) / n
```
hint: Build the weights, zero out both entries of each dropped pair, run asynchronous sweeps until nothing changes, and compute the average of the products of target and final state.
solution: Both $W_{ij}$ and $W_{ji}$ must be zeroed to keep the weights symmetric; zeroing only one breaks the energy argument and can make the sweeps cycle. With all 15 pairs deleted the network leaves the cue unchanged, so the overlap is $4/6$; with partial deletion the remaining weights can pull the state away from the stored pattern, as in the third test.

```python
def first_failure(patterns, target, cue, pair_order):
    """Smallest number of deleted pairs at which recall of target fails."""
    n = len(patterns[0])
    W = [[0.0] * n for _ in range(n)]
    for p in patterns:
        for i in range(n):
            for j in range(n):
                if i != j:
                    W[i][j] += p[i] * p[j] / n

    def recall(weights):
        s = list(cue)
        changed = True
        while changed:
            changed = False
            for i in range(n):
                h = sum(weights[i][j] * s[j] for j in range(n))
                new = s[i] if h == 0 else (1 if h > 0 else -1)
                if new != s[i]:
                    s[i] = new
                    changed = True
        return s

    for k in range(len(pair_order) + 1):
        damaged = [row[:] for row in W]
        for i, j in pair_order[:k]:
            damaged[i][j] = 0.0
            damaged[j][i] = 0.0
        if recall(damaged) != list(target):
            return k
    return None
```
hint: Rebuild the damaged matrix from the intact one for each k (do not accumulate errors), run the recall, and compare the whole state to the target.
solution: The loop starts at k = 0, so a network that already fails with all connections intact returns 0 (the last test: the three patterns are orthogonal, but three patterns in eight neurons is far above the capacity of the model, and the cross-talk after one flipped bit sends this cue to a mixture state). Deleting pairs in a different order changes when recall breaks (16 against 15 deletions in the first two tests), which shows that which connections are lost matters as well as how many; the ordering that deletes the last pairs first is hit sooner here.

@quiz self
prompt: A researcher claims that infantile amnesia shows that infants do not form memories. Evaluate this claim using findings about infant learning, and describe two hypotheses for why adults rarely recall early childhood events, with one observation each hypothesis explains well and one it explains poorly.
hint: Distinguish forming a memory from retrieving it years later, and implicit from episodic memory.
solution: The claim confuses forming memories with retrieving them as an adult. Infants learn and show memory over days to weeks (for example conditioning, imitation, and statistical learning of sound sequences within minutes), and implicit learning begins in the first months, so memory formation is present; what is missing is later conscious recall of episodes. One hypothesis is the late maturation of the hippocampus and its cortical inputs, which would explain the rise in episodic memory through early childhood, but explains poorly why children aged 3 to 5 can describe events that they later lose. A second hypothesis is that rapid forgetting in the early years, perhaps linked to high turnover of dentate gyrus neurons, erases traces; it explains the fast loss of early memories in children, but the human neurogenesis evidence is debated and it does not explain why some memories last. A third idea based on language and self-concept is also plausible. Because the hypotheses make different predictions about the shape of the forgetting curve at different ages, longitudinal studies of the same children are the way to compare them.
