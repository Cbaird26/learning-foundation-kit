@article
Hebb (1949) suggested that when one cell repeatedly takes part in firing another, the connection between them strengthens. A quarter of a century later, Bliss and Lømo (1973) showed in anesthetized rabbits that a brief, high-frequency train of stimuli to the perforant path produced an increase in the response of dentate granule cells that lasted hours. They had found **long-term potentiation** (LTP), and with it a mechanism that has the right properties to store information in synapses.

### Induction and properties

LTP is studied most often in the Schaffer collateral synapses onto CA1 pyramidal cells in hippocampal slices. The experimenter records the field excitatory postsynaptic potential (fEPSP) and measures its **slope**, a proxy for synaptic strength, once a minute at low frequency. A **tetanus** (for instance 100 Hz for 1 s) or **theta-burst stimulation** then raises the slope, often to 150% or more of the baseline, for as long as the slice is healthy. Three properties make LTP a candidate for Hebbian learning:

- **Cooperativity**: enough inputs must be active together; a weak input alone does not potentiate.
- **Associativity**: a weak input potentiates if it is active while a strong input depolarizes the cell.
- **Input specificity**: only the synapses that were active are potentiated, not their neighbors.

**Long-term depression** (LTD), a lasting decrease in synaptic strength, is induced in the same synapses by prolonged low-frequency stimulation, such as 1 Hz for several minutes.

### The NMDA receptor as a coincidence detector

At the typical resting potential, glutamate released by the presynaptic terminal binds to AMPA receptors (which carry most of the current) and also to **NMDA receptors**. The NMDA channel is blocked by an extracellular magnesium ion ($\text{Mg}^{2+}$) at hyperpolarized voltages. Depolarization of the postsynaptic cell expels the ion. So the channel passes current, including calcium, only when glutamate is bound (presynaptic activity) and the membrane is depolarized (postsynaptic activity): a **coincidence detector** in Hebb's sense. A widely used empirical description of the fraction of unblocked channels is

$$B(V) = \frac{1}{1 + \dfrac{[\text{Mg}^{2+}]}{3.57\ \text{mM}}\,e^{-0.062\,V}}, \qquad V \text{ in mV}, \ [\text{Mg}^{2+}] \text{ in mM}.$$

With 1 mM magnesium: $B(-70) = 0.044$, $B(-40) = 0.230$, $B(-20) = 0.508$, $B(0) = 0.781$. The NMDA current $I = g\,B(V)\,(V - E_{\text{rev}})$ with $E_{\text{rev}} \approx 0$ mV is small near rest because the channel is blocked, grows as the block lifts, and falls to zero at 0 mV because the driving force vanishes, so the largest inward current is at an intermediate voltage.

### From calcium to plasticity

Calcium entering through NMDA receptors triggers the change. Large, fast calcium rises activate kinases such as CaMKII, leading to the phosphorylation and insertion of AMPA receptors in the postsynaptic membrane (LTP). Smaller, prolonged rises activate phosphatases and lead to the removal of AMPA receptors (LTD). This **calcium hypothesis** can be written as a rule that depends on the calcium level $Ca$ reached during a protocol, with two thresholds $\theta_d < \theta_p$: no change below $\theta_d$, depression between them, potentiation above $\theta_p$. A toy version, with invented numbers, multiplies the weight on each protocol by $1 + \Omega(Ca)$, where $\Omega = 0$ when $Ca$ is below 0.2 µM, $\Omega = -0.30$ when $Ca$ is at least 0.2 µM but below 0.5 µM, and $\Omega = +0.60$ when $Ca$ is at least 0.5 µM:

```python
import math

def mg_unblocked(V, mg=1.0):
    """Fraction of NMDA channels not blocked by Mg at V (mV), mg in mM."""
    return 1.0 / (1.0 + mg / 3.57 * math.exp(-0.062 * V))

def omega(ca):
    """Toy calcium rule: relative weight change for a calcium level (uM)."""
    if ca < 0.2:
        return 0.0
    if ca < 0.5:
        return -0.30
    return 0.60

print(round(mg_unblocked(-40), 3), omega(0.3), omega(0.8))
```

This prints `0.23 -0.3 0.6`. Three protocols with calcium levels of 0.3, 0.8 and 0.3 µM change a weight of 1 to $0.7 \to 1.12 \to 0.784$: a depotentiation by low-frequency stimulation after LTP is possible in this toy rule.

### Evidence and debate

The link of LTP to memory is supported but not complete. Blocking NMDA receptors in the hippocampus impairs some forms of learning and LTP, and manipulations that alter the molecules of LTP change memory in animals; neurons that are active during learning can have their synapses strengthened. But NMDA-independent forms of LTP exist, blocking LTP does not always block learning, and showing that LTP is both necessary and sufficient for a specific memory is difficult. Many researchers see LTP and LTD as the best candidate mechanism for memory storage, while stressing that other changes (in intrinsic excitability, in circuits, in structure) also contribute.

@mistakes
- Saying that NMDA receptors carry the baseline synaptic current; AMPA receptors do most of it, and NMDA receptors contribute mainly when the cell is depolarized.
- Reading "percent of baseline" as "percent increase": 155% of baseline is a 55% increase, and 70% of baseline is a 30% decrease.
- Assuming that LTP in a slice is the same as a memory; slices show a mechanism, and the link to behavior needs separate evidence.
- Treating high-frequency stimulation as the way neurons are normally active; tetani are strong, artificial inputs, and patterns like spike timing matter (week 10).

```python
def ltp_percent(slopes, n_base, start, end):
    """Post-protocol mean slope as a percent of the baseline mean."""
    baseline = sum(slopes[:n_base]) / n_base
    if baseline == 0:
        return None
    post = sum(slopes[start:end]) / (end - start)
    return 100.0 * post / baseline
```
hint: Slice the list twice, average each slice, and divide; check for a zero baseline before dividing.
solution: Only the first `n_base` values define the baseline, and the response is averaged over the window `start` to `end` (not the whole rest of the experiment); the potentiation immediately after the tetanus (0.95) is excluded in the second test because it is outside the window. A baseline of zero has no meaningful ratio, so the function returns `None`.

model:
```python
def apply_protocols(w0, ca_list, theta_d, theta_p, ltd, ltp, w_max):
    """Final weight after a sequence of calcium-threshold plasticity protocols."""
    w = w0
    for ca in ca_list:
        if ca >= theta_p:
            w *= 1 + ltp
        elif ca >= theta_d:
            w *= 1 - ltd
        w = min(max(w, 0.0), w_max)
    return w
```
hint: Check the higher threshold first, then the lower one; clip the weight after every protocol, not only at the end.
solution: A calcium level exactly at a threshold belongs to the higher band (fourth test: 0.3 depresses, 0.6 potentiates, 0.2 does nothing). The weight saturates at `w_max` after each step, so in the third test the first two potentiations leave 1.2 and the depression then gives 0.9; clipping only at the end would give a different value.

```python
import math


def nmda_current(V, g, mg=1.0):
    """NMDA current (pA) with a voltage-dependent magnesium block."""
    unblocked = 1.0 / (1.0 + mg / 3.57 * math.exp(-0.062 * V))
    return g * unblocked * V
```
hint: Compute the unblocked fraction first, then multiply conductance, fraction and voltage (the reversal potential is 0 mV).
solution: With no magnesium the current is linear in $V$, $g \times V = -30$ pA at $-30$ mV. With magnesium it is $-10.7$ pA there but only $-3.1$ pA per nanosiemens at $-70$ mV, so most of the current is lost at rest. The current is negative (inward) below 0 mV and is zero at 0 mV.

solution: Stimulate two independent sets of Schaffer collaterals, S (weak) and C (control, also weak), onto the same CA1 population while recording fEPSP slopes from each, alternating the test pulses once a minute for a stable baseline. Pair S with a strong input path, such as a third pathway (strong) stimulated at 100 Hz, so that S is active in the time window when the cells are strongly depolarized, while C is not stimulated during the strong burst. Associativity predicts that S potentiates, although alone it was too weak to induce LTP, because the depolarization from the strong input relieved the magnesium block. Input specificity is shown by C, which was not active during the burst and does not potentiate, even though it contacts the same cells. Controls include a group in which S is stimulated weakly without the strong input (no LTP expected), and an NMDA receptor antagonist group (LTP prevented), to show that the induction depends on NMDA receptors.
