@article
A thirsty monkey sits in front of a spout. A light flashes, and a second later a drop of juice arrives. The first time, dopamine neurons in the midbrain fire a burst when the juice arrives. After many trials they stay quiet at the juice and fire at the light instead. If the juice is then withheld, they fall silent exactly when it was due. Schultz, Dayan and Montague (1997) pointed out that this three-part pattern is what the TD error $\delta$ of week 3 should do, and proposed that phasic dopamine signals a **reward prediction error**.

### The recordings

Dopamine neurons of the ventral tegmental area (VTA) and substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc) project widely, with dense projections to the striatum and prefrontal cortex. Recorded in monkeys during conditioning tasks, they show a brief **phasic** change (a burst on the order of 100 ms, rising from a baseline of a few hertz, or a dip below baseline) in three cases:

| situation | dopamine response | TD error |
|---|---|---|
| unpredicted reward | burst at reward | $\delta > 0$ |
| reward fully predicted by a cue | burst at the cue, none at reward | $\delta > 0$ at cue, $\approx 0$ at reward |
| predicted reward omitted | dip below baseline at the expected time | $\delta < 0$ at that time |

Later work found that the size of the response scales with the difference between the received and the expected reward (with larger rewards giving larger bursts, and a higher reward probability shrinking the response to the reward). Causal tests, in which dopamine neurons are stimulated or inhibited with light in genetically targeted animals, support a role as a teaching signal for learning about cues, though they do not by themselves establish the full computation.

### Simulating the task with time steps

The TD error needs a representation of time within a trial. The simplest is a **complete serial compound**: one state per time step since the cue. Let the cue appear at step 0 and the reward arrive at step $R$. The values $V_0, \ldots, V_R$ belong to the $R+1$ states after the cue. The state before the cue has value 0 because the cue's onset is not predictable. At each trial:

- at cue onset, $\delta_{\text{cue}} = \gamma V_0 - 0$;
- at each step $t = 0, \ldots, R$: $\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V_{t+1} - V_t$, with $r_t = r$ (the reward size) at $t = R$ and 0 earlier, and $V_{R+1} = 0$;
- then $V_t \leftarrow V_t + \alpha\delta_t$.

With $R = 3$, $\alpha = 0.5$ and $\gamma = 0.9$ the errors evolve like this:

| trial | $\delta$ at cue | $\delta$ at reward ($t = 3$) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.000 | 1.000 |
| 2 | 0.000 | 0.500 |
| 3 | 0.000 | 0.250 |
| 10 | 0.490 | 0.002 |
| 30 | 0.656 | 0.000 |
| 31, reward omitted | 0.656 | $-1.000$ |

The error moves from the reward to the cue. It cannot go earlier than the cue, because the model has no prediction of when the cue will occur, and the asymptotic cue response is $\gamma V_0 = \gamma^{R+1} = 0.656$, which shrinks with the delay: a longer delay or a smaller $\gamma$ means a weaker cue response. The dip on omission equals $-V_R \approx -r$.

```python
def train_cue_reward(n_trials, reward_step, alpha, gamma):
    """TD(0) on a cue-reward task with one state per step after the cue.

    Returns (V, profiles): the values and, for each trial, the list
    [delta_cue, delta_0, ..., delta_R].
    """
    V = [0.0] * (reward_step + 1)
    profiles = []
    for _ in range(n_trials):
        deltas = [gamma * V[0]]
        for t in range(reward_step + 1):
            last = t == reward_step
            r = 1.0 if last else 0.0
            v_next = 0.0 if last else V[t + 1]
            delta = r + gamma * v_next - V[t]
            V[t] += alpha * delta
            deltas.append(delta)
        profiles.append(deltas)
    return V, profiles

V, profiles = train_cue_reward(30, 3, 0.5, 0.9)
print([round(d, 3) for d in profiles[-1]])
```

This prints `[0.656, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0]`.

### Debates and limits

The prediction-error hypothesis is one of the best-supported links between a computation and a neural signal, but it is not the whole story. Many dopamine neurons also respond to novel, surprising or aversive events, and some activity relates to movement and to ramping toward a goal; whether these are separate signals, errors of other kinds, or reflect a population that is more diverse than a single scalar error is under active study. It is debated whether dopamine mainly teaches (changes values), motivates (energizes behavior and the "wanting" of rewards), or does both on different timescales. The TD account also needs assumptions: a time representation, a fixed delay, and a known cue onset. The evidence links phasic dopamine to the mismatch between expected and received reward, not to pleasure itself.

@example
title: Error profiles by hand
problem: A cue is followed one step later by a reward ($R = 1$, two states). Use $\alpha = 0.5$, $\gamma = 1$ and reward size 1. Find the errors at the cue and in each state on trials 1 and 2.
step: Trial 1, with all $V = 0$: $\delta_{\text{cue}} = \gamma V_0 = 0$. At $t = 0$: $\delta_0 = 0 + V_1 - V_0 = 0$. At $t = 1$ (the reward): $\delta_1 = 1 - 0 = 1$, so $V_1 = 0.5$.
step: Trial 2: $\delta_{\text{cue}} = V_0 = 0$. At $t = 0$: $\delta_0 = 0 + 0.5 - 0 = 0.5$, so $V_0 = 0.25$. At $t = 1$: $\delta_1 = 1 - 0.5 = 0.5$, so $V_1 = 0.75$.
step: The error at reward fell from 1 to 0.5 while an error appeared at the earlier step: the beginning of the shift from reward to cue.
answer: Trial 1: $(0, 0, 1)$. Trial 2: $(0, 0.5, 0.5)$ for the cue, $t=0$ and $t=1$.

@example
title: Error profile on an omission trial
problem: A cue-reward task has $R = 2$ and $\gamma = 0.8$, and learning has converged, so $V = [0.64, 0.8, 1.0]$. On a test trial the reward is omitted. Find the error at the cue and in each state.
step: At the cue, $\delta_{\text{cue}} = 0.8 \times 0.64 = 0.512$.
step: At $t = 0$: $0 + 0.8 \times 0.8 - 0.64 = 0$. At $t = 1$: $0 + 0.8 \times 1.0 - 0.8 = 0$.
step: At $t = 2$, the reward step: $\delta_2 = 0 + 0 - 1.0 = -1$, a large negative error at the time the reward was due.
answer: $(0.512, 0, 0, -1)$: a response at the cue, then a dip when the reward fails to arrive.

@mistakes
- Saying dopamine neurons signal reward: the burst follows a reward only when it is unexpected, and a fully predicted reward produces almost none.
- Expecting the error to move to a time before the cue; with an unpredictable cue onset the model keeps a positive error at the cue itself.
- Ignoring the negative side: firing cannot go below zero, so a dip is a modest decrease from a baseline of a few spikes per second, which makes large negative errors hard to read from single neurons.
- Treating the reward-prediction-error hypothesis as settled in every detail; dopamine's links to novelty, movement, motivation and aversive events are still debated.

```python
def delta_profile(V, reward_step, gamma, reward):
    """TD errors at the cue and at each state of one trial (no learning)."""
    deltas = [gamma * V[0]]
    for t in range(reward_step + 1):
        r = reward if t == reward_step else 0.0
        v_next = V[t + 1] if t < reward_step else 0.0
        deltas.append(r + gamma * v_next - V[t])
    return deltas
```

solution: The loop is the cue-reward error computation of the article without the value update. The single-state case checks that the last step does not read past the end of the list.

```python
def first_cue_response(reward_step, alpha, gamma, threshold, max_trials=200):
    """First trial whose cue-onset TD error reaches the threshold."""
    V = [0.0] * (reward_step + 1)
    for trial in range(1, max_trials + 1):
        if gamma * V[0] >= threshold:
            return trial
        for t in range(reward_step + 1):
            last = t == reward_step
            r = 1.0 if last else 0.0
            v_next = 0.0 if last else V[t + 1]
            V[t] += alpha * (r + gamma * v_next - V[t])
    return None
```
hint: On each trial, first test `gamma * V[0]` against the threshold, then run the updates for the trial.
solution: The cue response must first wait for value to propagate backward from the reward to state 0, which takes about one trial per step, and then grows toward its asymptote $\gamma^{R+1}$. With $R = 1$ and $\gamma = 0.9$ the asymptote is $0.81$, below the threshold $0.95$, so the answer is `None`. Checking before updating matters: with threshold 0 the cue error already qualifies on trial 1.

model:
```python
def omission_dip(V_reward, rewards):
    """Reward-time prediction errors with a delta-rule predicted value."""
    V, errors = V_reward, []
    for r in rewards:
        delta = r - V
        V += 0.5 * delta
        errors.append(delta)
    return errors
```
hint: For each reward compute the error from the current prediction, store it, then move the prediction halfway toward the reward.
solution: The error is stored before the update, so a surprise shows in full. After two rewards equal to the prediction the errors are zero; an omission gives an error equal to minus the prediction, and repeated omissions produce dips that shrink as the prediction falls, the extinction-like decline of the reward-time response.

@quiz self
prompt: A colleague says "dopamine is the brain's pleasure chemical, and the monkey data show it." Using the cue-reward recordings, explain why the data do not support that claim as stated, describe what the prediction-error hypothesis says instead, and name one observation about dopamine neurons that the simple hypothesis does not explain.
hint: Compare the response to a predicted reward with the response to an unpredicted one.
solution: If dopamine neurons signaled pleasure or reward value, they would fire whenever a reward is received, yet after training the burst at a fully predicted juice reward is nearly absent while the reward is just as pleasant; the burst moves to the cue, and a dip occurs when the reward is withheld, when nothing pleasant happens. The prediction-error hypothesis says phasic dopamine reports the difference between the reward received and the reward predicted, as the TD error $\delta$ does, and serves as a teaching signal for values. The simple scalar-error account does not by itself explain responses to novel or aversive events, ramping activity toward a goal, or the diversity among dopamine neurons; researchers debate whether these reflect other computations or a richer error code. The hypothesis also says little about pleasure itself, which may involve other systems.
