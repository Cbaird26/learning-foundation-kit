@article
A person falls, hits their head, and wakes in a hospital unable to recall the accident or the last few days before it, but remembers their childhood perfectly. Recent memories seem to be fragile and old ones sturdy. This **temporal gradient** of retrograde amnesia, noticed in the nineteenth century, suggests that memories change over time from a vulnerable form to a stable one. **Consolidation** is the name for that process.

### The hippocampal circuit

The **hippocampus** lies in the medial temporal lobe and receives its input mostly from the **entorhinal cortex**. The main route, the trisynaptic loop, runs from entorhinal cortex through the perforant path to the **dentate gyrus**, then through mossy fibers to **CA3**, then through the Schaffer collaterals to **CA1**, and from there to the subiculum and back to the entorhinal cortex and neocortex. CA3 has many recurrent connections between its own neurons, which makes it a natural candidate for the associative networks of week 11. Influential theories assign the dentate gyrus a role in **pattern separation** (making similar inputs more distinct) and CA3 a role in **pattern completion** (recovering a whole memory from a partial cue); direct evidence for these roles is supported but still debated.

### Two accounts of the gradient

The **standard consolidation** account says the hippocampus rapidly binds together the cortical areas that represent an experience. Over days to years, by repeated reactivation, the cortical areas become linked directly and the memory no longer needs the hippocampus. It predicts a temporal gradient: a hippocampal lesion removes recent memories but spares remote ones. The **multiple-trace** (or transformation) account holds that the hippocampus stays involved whenever a memory is rich in episodic detail, while the gist of old memories becomes independent of it. The two make different predictions about detailed old memories, and data on that point are mixed and interpreted differently, so the question remains open.

### Sleep and replay

During **slow-wave sleep** three rhythms are coordinated: slow oscillations of the neocortex (about 1 Hz), thalamocortical **spindles** (about 12 to 15 Hz), and hippocampal **sharp-wave ripples**, brief bursts of about 150 to 250 Hz lasting tens of milliseconds. In rodents, sequences of place-cell firing from a recent trip along a track recur during ripples, compressed in time, a phenomenon called **replay**; disrupting ripples in rats after learning impairs later performance, which supports a causal role. In humans, presenting a sound during sleep that was linked to learning can improve later recall of the material (targeted memory reactivation). Alternative proposals say sleep helps memory by scaling down synapses or by protecting memories from interference; these accounts are not mutually exclusive, and how much replay contributes in humans is debated.

### A two-store model

A toy model shows the gradient. Let $h_t$ be the strength of a memory's hippocampal trace $t$ days after learning, with $h_0 = 1$. The trace fades by a fraction $d_h$ per day, and each day the hippocampus transfers a fraction $k$ of its current strength to a cortical trace $c_t$ (replay during sleep), with $c_0 = 0$:

$$h_{t+1} = (1 - d_h)\,h_t, \qquad c_{t+1} = c_t + k\,h_t .$$

The cortical trace grows toward $c_\infty = k/d_h$. With $d_h = 0.3$ and $k = 0.2$:

| age (days) | $h$ | $c$ | fraction surviving a hippocampal lesion, $c/(h+c)$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1.0000 | 0.0000 | 0.000 |
| 1 | 0.7000 | 0.2000 | 0.222 |
| 2 | 0.4900 | 0.3400 | 0.410 |
| 3 | 0.3430 | 0.4380 | 0.561 |
| 5 | 0.1681 | 0.5546 | 0.767 |
| 10 | 0.0282 | 0.6478 | 0.958 |

A lesion at a given age removes $h$ and leaves $c$. Recent memories are mostly lost and old ones are mostly spared, so the model reproduces the gradient. Note that its $h + c$ is not constant: the total first falls and then plateaus near $c_\infty = 0.667$, so even remote memories are weaker than at first.

```python
def consolidate(days, d_h, k):
    """Return [(h, c), ...] for ages 0..days in the two-store model."""
    h, c = 1.0, 0.0
    trace = [(h, c)]
    for _ in range(days):
        c = c + k * h
        h = (1 - d_h) * h
        trace.append((h, c))
    return trace

trace = consolidate(5, 0.3, 0.2)
print([round(x, 4) for x in trace[5]])
```

This prints `[0.1681, 0.5546]`. Note the order of the two updates: the transfer must use the old value of $h$.

@mistakes
- Saying that consolidation moves a memory from the hippocampus to the cortex like a file; the standard account says the cortical trace is gradually strengthened by replay, and the multiple-trace account says the hippocampus can stay involved.
- Taking correlations as proof: ripple-locked replay shows a relationship to learning; the causal evidence comes from disrupting ripples.
- Calling sleep a single state: replay and ripples occur mostly in slow-wave sleep, whereas other stages have different rhythms.
- Updating the cortical trace after reducing the hippocampal trace in the same step, which transfers a smaller amount than the model says.

```python
def crossover_age(d_h, k, max_days=1000):
    """First age at which the cortical trace exceeds the hippocampal one."""
    h, c = 1.0, 0.0
    for age in range(max_days + 1):
        if c > h:
            return age
        c = c + k * h
        h = (1 - d_h) * h
    return None
```
hint: Test the condition at each age before taking the daily step, and do the transfer with the old value of $h$.

With $k = 0$ nothing is ever transferred, so the answer is `None`; with `max_days=5` the search stops before the true crossover at age 11. Updating $h$ before $c$ would transfer too little and shift some answers by a day.

model:
```python
def detect_ripples(envelope, threshold, dt_ms, min_ms):
    """(onset_ms, duration_ms) of each run above threshold that is long enough."""
    events = []
    i, n = 0, len(envelope)
    while i < n:
        if envelope[i] >= threshold:
            j = i
            while j < n and envelope[j] >= threshold:
                j += 1
            if (j - i) * dt_ms >= min_ms:
                events.append((i * dt_ms, (j - i) * dt_ms))
            i = j
        else:
            i += 1
    return events
```
hint: Scan with an index; when a sample is at or above threshold, advance a second index to the end of the run, record the run if it is long enough, and continue from the end of the run.
solution: The envelope contains runs of 4, 2, 4 and 2 samples (20, 10, 20 and 10 ms). The threshold comparison is inclusive (a sample of exactly 3.0 counts, as in the fifth test), and a run that ends at the end of the data must still be reported. Lowering the minimum duration to 10 ms admits the two short runs.

model:
```python
def ripples_after_spindles(spindle_ms, ripple_ms, window_ms):
    """Number of ripples that follow a spindle peak within the window."""
    count = 0
    for r in ripple_ms:
        if any(0 <= r - s <= window_ms for s in spindle_ms):
            count += 1
    return count
```
hint: Loop over ripples, not spindles, and ask whether any spindle precedes the ripple by between 0 and the window; both ends are inclusive.
solution: Looping over ripples counts each at most once; looping over spindle-ripple pairs would count the ripple at 1400 twice in the fifth test. The window is inclusive at both ends: ripples at exactly 500 ms (5500 and 9500) count, and a ripple that comes before the spindle (900 against 1000) does not.

@quiz self
prompt: Suppose you want to test whether hippocampal ripples after learning causally contribute to later memory in rats. Describe a design with an experimental and a control manipulation, the behavioral measure, and what a positive and a null result would each mean. Mention one way the result could be misinterpreted.
hint: Think about disrupting ripples at the right time, and include a control for the disruption itself.
solution: Train rats on a spatial task, and detect ripples in real time in the sleep or rest period after each session. In the experimental group, deliver a brief disruption (for example, electrical stimulation) triggered by each detected ripple; in the control group, deliver the same number of stimulations at the same times delayed so that they do not coincide with ripples, so that the disruption itself is matched. Test learning on later days (the number of errors or the time to reach a goal). A positive result, worse learning in the ripple-disrupted group, supports a causal role of ripples in consolidation. A null result would mean either that ripples are not needed, or that the disruption was incomplete or that other mechanisms compensated, so the interpretation depends on the verification of ripple suppression. A way to misinterpret a positive result is to conclude that replay of a specific sequence is the cause, because disrupting ripples also removes other ripple-related processes.
