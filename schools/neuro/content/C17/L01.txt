@article
A dog hears a bell and then gets food. After enough pairings the bell alone makes the dog salivate. Pavlov's finding is the template for **classical conditioning**: a neutral **conditioned stimulus** (CS, the bell) comes to predict a biologically significant **unconditioned stimulus** (US, the food), and the animal develops a **conditioned response** (CR) to the CS. Two puzzles show that pairing alone is not the whole story. First, in **blocking** (Kamin, 1969) an animal first learns that a light predicts a shock, then experiences light and tone together followed by the shock; later the tone alone evokes almost no fear, although it was paired with shock many times. Second, learning curves are negatively accelerated: early trials change behavior a lot, later ones very little. Both follow if learning is driven by **surprise**, not by pairing.

### The Rescorla–Wagner rule

Rescorla and Wagner (1972) proposed that on each trial, every cue $X$ that is present changes its **associative strength** $V_X$ in proportion to the shared prediction error:

$$\Delta V_X = \alpha\,(\lambda - V_{\text{total}}), \qquad V_{\text{total}} = \sum_{Y \text{ present}} V_Y .$$

Here $\alpha$ is the learning rate ($0 < \alpha \le 1$; in the full model it is a product $\alpha_X\beta$ of a cue salience and a US-dependent rate), and $\lambda$ is the **asymptote**, the largest strength the US can support: $\lambda = 1$ on a reinforced trial and $\lambda = 0$ on a trial where the US is omitted. Cues that are absent do not change. Strengths start at 0 unless stated.

For one cue with $\lambda = 1$ the error shrinks by a factor $1-\alpha$ each trial, so $V_n = 1 - (1-\alpha)^n$. With $\alpha = 0.3$:

| trial | error before | $V$ after |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1.000 | 0.300 |
| 2 | 0.700 | 0.510 |
| 3 | 0.490 | 0.657 |
| 4 | 0.343 | 0.760 |

That is the negatively accelerated curve. If the US is then withheld ($\lambda = 0$), the error is $-V$ and $V$ shrinks by $1-\alpha$ per trial: **extinction**. The rule says extinction reduces the strength; later evidence (spontaneous recovery, reinstatement) shows the original learning is not simply erased, a limit of the model.

### Compounds: overshadowing and blocking

When tone and light are presented together, they share one error term. Two equal cues starting from zero split the asymptote: each approaches $\lambda/2$, so each is weaker than if trained alone (**overshadowing**). With unequal salience, the more salient cue wins a larger share. In blocking, cue A is first trained alone until $V_A \approx 1$. On the compound trials AB+, the error $\lambda - V_A - V_B$ is already near zero, so B gains almost nothing. Simulating 10 trials of A+ followed by 10 trials of AB+ with $\alpha = 0.3$ gives $V_A = 0.986$ and $V_B = 0.014$, while a control group trained with AB+ for 10 trials ends at $V_A = V_B = 0.500$.

Blocking supports the idea that learning depends on the prediction error, not on contiguity. Limits matter: the model has no time (no difference between a CS that precedes the US by 1 s or 10 s), it predicts the CR from $V$ directly, and some effects (latent inhibition, the effect of CS-alone preexposure) need changes in $\alpha$ that the basic rule lacks. Temporal-difference models (week 3) add time.

### In code

```python
def rw_train(trials, alpha, lam=1.0):
    """Rescorla-Wagner. trials: list of (set_of_cues, reinforced 0/1).

    Returns (V, errors): final strengths and the error on each trial.
    """
    V, errors = {}, []
    for cues, reinforced in trials:
        total = 0.0
        for c in cues:
            total += V.get(c, 0.0)
        error = (lam if reinforced else 0.0) - total
        for c in cues:
            V[c] = V.get(c, 0.0) + alpha * error
        errors.append(error)
    return V, errors

blocking = [({"A"}, 1)] * 10 + [({"A", "B"}, 1)] * 10
V, errors = rw_train(blocking, 0.3)
print(round(V["A"], 3), round(V["B"], 3))
```

This prints `0.986 0.014`. The key line is that the error is computed once from the total prediction and then applied to every present cue.

@example
title: Overshadowing by hand
problem: Cues A and B are always presented together and always followed by the US. Use $\alpha = 0.2$ and $\lambda = 1$, starting from $V_A = V_B = 0$. Find the strengths after two trials and the strength both approach in the long run.
step: Trial 1: the total prediction is $0$, so the error is $1 - 0 = 1$ and each cue changes by $0.2 \times 1 = 0.2$. Now $V_A = V_B = 0.2$.
step: Trial 2: the total is $0.2 + 0.2 = 0.4$, so the error is $0.6$ and each cue gains $0.2 \times 0.6 = 0.12$. Now $V_A = V_B = 0.32$.
step: In the long run the error goes to zero, so $V_A + V_B = \lambda = 1$. By symmetry the cues share equally: $V_A = V_B = 0.5$.
step: Compare with training A alone: its strength would approach $1$, so presenting B alongside A overshadowed A by half.
answer: After two trials $V_A = V_B = 0.32$; both approach $0.5$.

@example
title: A single extinction trial after blocking-style pretraining
problem: Cue A has $V_A = 0.9$ and is presented in compound with a new cue B, followed by the US ($\lambda = 1$), with $\alpha = 0.2$. How much does B learn on this trial?
step: The total prediction is $0.9 + 0 = 0.9$, so the error is $1 - 0.9 = 0.1$.
step: B changes by $\alpha \times 0.1 = 0.02$, and A also rises by $0.02$ to $0.92$.
step: B has gained only $0.02$ of the possible $1$: the US was already predicted, so there was little surprise to learn from.
answer: B gains $0.02$.

@mistakes
- Updating each cue from its own error ($\lambda - V_X$) instead of the shared error ($\lambda - V_{\text{total}}$); that version cannot produce blocking or overshadowing.
- Changing the strengths of cues that were not presented on the trial; in the Rescorla–Wagner rule absent cues keep their value.
- Treating $\lambda = 0$ on an extinction trial as "reset to zero": the error is $-V_{\text{total}}$, so strength shrinks by a factor $1-\alpha$ per trial and never jumps to zero.
- Reading blocking as proof that the animal did not notice the tone; the model explains it with a small prediction error, and a cue can still be perceived.

model:
```python
def trials_to_criterion(alpha, lam, criterion):
    """Trials of reinforced training until V reaches the criterion."""
    if criterion <= 0:
        return 0
    if criterion >= lam:
        return None
    V, n = 0.0, 0
    while V < criterion:
        V += alpha * (lam - V)
        n += 1
    return n
```
hint: Loop: apply $V \leftarrow V + \alpha(\lambda - V)$, count the trial, and stop when $V$ is at least the criterion. Handle the two special cases before the loop.
solution: The loop is the single-cue rule applied until the criterion is reached. The test with criterion $0.875$ is exactly reached on trial 3 (values 0.5, 0.75, 0.875 are exact in binary), so the comparison must be `>=`, not `>`; the test with $\lambda = 2$ catches code that hard-codes $\lambda = 1$.

model:
```python
def rw_salience(trials, alphas, lam=1.0):
    """Rescorla-Wagner with a separate learning rate for each cue."""
    V = {}
    for cues, reinforced in trials:
        total = 0.0
        for c in cues:
            total += V.get(c, 0.0)
        error = (lam if reinforced else 0.0) - total
        for c in cues:
            V[c] = V.get(c, 0.0) + alphas[c] * error
    return V
```
hint: Compute the error once from the total, then update each cue with its own rate; do not recompute the total inside the update loop.
solution: With unequal rates the cues share the asymptote in proportion to their rates ($0.8$ and $0.2$ for rates $0.4$ and $0.1$), so the salient cue overshadows the weak one. In the fourth test the first trial gives $V_A = 0.5$; the compound extinction trial has error $-0.5$, so $A$ falls to $0.25$ and the new cue $B$ becomes $-0.25$ (a conditioned inhibitor). Recomputing the total mid-update would give different values.

model:
```python
def inhibition_strengths(n_blocks, alpha):
    """Final (V_A, V_X) after A+ / AX- training blocks."""
    V_A, V_X = 0.0, 0.0
    for _ in range(n_blocks):
        V_A += alpha * (1.0 - V_A)
        error = 0.0 - (V_A + V_X)
        V_A += alpha * error
        V_X += alpha * error
    return (V_A, V_X)
```
hint: On the A trial only A changes; on the AX trial compute one error from $V_A + V_X$ and add it, times alpha, to both.
solution: The compound trial makes the total prediction too high, so the negative error pushes X below zero. In the long run $V_A \to 1$ and $V_X \to -1$, so that $V_A + V_X = 0$ on the compound trials and A alone still predicts the US; a negative $V_X$ is a conditioned inhibitor.
