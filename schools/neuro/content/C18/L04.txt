@article
Sex differences in the brain are a fact of animal biology and a source of exaggerated claims about people. Both statements need evidence. This lesson separates what is well established in rodents, where hormones can be manipulated, from what is known in humans, where evidence is mostly indirect and effects are small.

### From chromosomes to gonads

In mammals, an embryo with a Y chromosome usually develops testes because of the *SRY* gene on the Y (Sinclair and colleagues, 1990); XX mice carrying a copy of the mouse gene as a transgene develop as males (Koopman and colleagues, 1991). The fetal testes then make **testosterone**, which supports male internal structures, and **anti-Müllerian hormone**, which regresses the female duct. Jost's experiments in the 1940s on rabbit fetuses showed that the gonad's hormones, not the chromosomes directly, drive the body's differentiation: removing the fetal testes before differentiation led to female-typical structures.

### The brain in rodents

In rats and mice, the same hormones act on the brain in a perinatal sensitive period (the organizational effect from the previous lesson). A twist is that in the rodent brain much of the masculinizing action of testosterone follows its conversion to estradiol by the enzyme aromatase; a fetal protein, alpha-fetoprotein, binds circulating estradiol and is thought to protect the female brain from her own mother's and her own estrogens. The **sexually dimorphic nucleus of the preoptic area** (SDN-POA), described by Gorski and colleagues in 1978, is several-fold larger in male rats, and its size depends on perinatal hormones.

Behavior circuits follow the same plan. In male rodents, the **medial preoptic area** is required for male copulatory behavior: lesions greatly reduce it, and stimulating it increases it. In female rodents, the **ventromedial hypothalamus** (ventrolateral part) contributes to receptivity, which in rats appears as a **lordosis** posture that depends on estrogen and progesterone and occurs at the fertile phase of a cycle of about four to five days. In male mice, activating neurons in the ventrolateral part of the ventromedial hypothalamus can evoke attack (Lin and colleagues, 2011). Many of these neurons express hormone receptors, so hormones change what circuits do, over minutes to days.

### What is known in people

Controlled hormone manipulation in human fetuses is impossible and unethical, so human evidence comes from natural experiments. In **congenital adrenal hyperplasia** (CAH), the adrenal gland makes excess androgens before birth; girls and women with CAH show on average more male-typical childhood toy and activity choices (Berenbaum and Hines, 1992), a finding consistent with a prenatal hormone influence. But the groups also differ in treatment, body appearance, medical history and parents' responses, so this evidence is suggestive, not conclusive. In **complete androgen insensitivity syndrome**, people with XY chromosomes have testes but nonfunctional androgen receptors; they usually develop female-typical bodies and most identify as women. This shows that androgen action, not the Y chromosome alone, is needed for typical male development of the body, and that the gender identity of most such people matches their rearing and body. Neither condition shows that hormones alone determine gender identity or sexual orientation: the causes of both are not established, studies are limited and no single factor explains them. People vary, and each person is the best authority on their own identity.

Average sex differences in the human brain and behavior exist but are mostly small, and the groups overlap widely. Much of the group-average difference in brain structure is related to overall body and brain size, though smaller differences remain after adjustment and are debated. One analysis of brain images (Joel and colleagues, 2015) argued that individual brains are mosaics of features more common in one sex or the other, with few brains fully consistent with an "all male" or "all female" pattern; critics replied that multivariate patterns across many features can classify sex with high accuracy, so researchers still disagree about how to interpret the overlap. A standardized effect size makes the overlap concrete. If two groups have equal variances and normal distributions with difference $d$ in standard-deviation units, the probability that a randomly chosen member of group 1 scores above a randomly chosen member of group 2 (the probability of superiority) is

$$P = \Phi\!\left(\frac{d}{\sqrt{2}}\right) = \tfrac{1}{2}\left[1 + \text{erf}\!\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)\right].$$

For $d = 0.5$, $P \approx 0.64$: even a medium difference means that about 36% of random pairs go the "other" way. Mental rotation tasks give one of the larger cognitive sex differences, of moderate size: meta-analyses report $d$ of roughly 0.5 to 0.9 depending on the test and the era. The distributions still overlap widely, and practice and experience change performance; most other cognitive differences are smaller.

### Limits

Rodent circuits are well described but differ from human ones: rodents use pheromones and stereotyped postures, and human sexual behavior is shaped far more by cortex, culture and learning. Findings of "a nucleus larger in males" should not be extended to people without human evidence.

@example
title: Overlap from an effect size
problem: Two normally distributed groups have the same variance and a mean difference of $d = 0.8$ standard deviations. What is the probability that a random member of the higher group scores above a random member of the lower group?
step: Use $P = \tfrac12[1 + \text{erf}(d/2)]$.
step: With $d = 0.8$, the argument is $0.4$, and $\text{erf}(0.4) \approx 0.4284$.
step: $P = 0.5 \times 1.4284 \approx 0.714$.
answer: About $0.71$; even a large difference leaves roughly 29% of random pairs reversed.
@mistakes
- Calling a nucleus "the male brain region" in people: the SDN-POA result is from rats and was shown to depend on perinatal hormones there.
- Treating small average differences as categorical: with $d \approx 0.5$ the distributions overlap by a large margin.
- Inferring a hormonal cause for a human trait from a natural experiment alone: treatment, appearance and rearing differ along with hormone exposure.

@practice self
prompt: Evaluate this supplied study. Researchers compare 24 girls with congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH; high prenatal androgens, treated from infancy) with 24 unaffected sisters. Parents rate each child on a "male-typical play" questionnaire. The CAH group scores higher (mean 7.5 vs 6.0; standard deviations 1.8 and 2.2). The authors conclude that prenatal testosterone programs children's play. Give strengths, weaknesses, the effect size, and what further evidence would be needed.
hint: Think about who rates the children, what else differs between the groups, and the size of the effect.
solution: Strengths: a natural experiment with prenatal hormone exposure not chosen by the family, and unaffected sisters as a partly matched comparison group. Weaknesses: parents rate the children and know the diagnosis, so expectations and reporting bias could affect scores; CAH also involves medical treatment, body differences and parental behavior; 24 per group gives an imprecise estimate; the questionnaire is a proxy for behavior; and the exposure is unknown in amount and timing. The effect size is $d = 1.5/\sqrt{(1.8^2 + 2.2^2)/2} \approx 0.75$, medium to large with a wide confidence interval and a lot of overlap. More evidence: observed play behavior by raters blind to diagnosis, larger and independent samples, measures of dose (severity grades), comparisons with other conditions that change hormones but not appearance, and convergence with animal experiments. The study supports an influence consistent with the hormone hypothesis but not "programming" as the full explanation.
