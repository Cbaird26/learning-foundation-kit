@article
A mouse that lacks the gene for a single hormone, leptin, eats until it weighs three times a normal mouse. A human who loses 10% of body weight usually finds it hard to stay there. Yet most adults keep their weight within a few kilograms for years, though they eat a million kilocalories a year. What measures the body's energy stores, and how does the brain use the measurement?

### Energy balance

Body energy stores change by the difference between intake and expenditure:

$$\frac{dE_{\text{store}}}{dt} = \text{intake} - \text{expenditure}.$$

Body fat holds roughly 7,000 to 7,700 kcal per kilogram (it is not pure lipid), so a steady surplus of 200 kcal per day would add 1 kg in about $7700/200 = 38.5$ days if nothing else changed. That "rule" is a crude first approximation. Expenditure rises with body mass and adapts to eating less, and appetite also adjusts, so real weight change slows and levels off. The equation says what must hold, not how the brain does the regulation.

### Signals of stored energy

- **Leptin**, a peptide made by fat cells in proportion to fat mass (cloned by Zhang and colleagues in 1994 as the product of the *ob* gene). It acts on neurons in the arcuate nucleus. Mice lacking leptin (*ob/ob*) or its receptor (*db/db*) are hyperphagic and obese. In **parabiosis**, in which the circulations of two animals are joined (Coleman, 1973), an *ob/ob* mouse joined to a normal mouse ate less and gained less weight, while a normal mouse joined to a *db/db* mouse stopped eating. The interpretation: the normal animal makes a circulating factor, *ob/ob* lacks it, and *db/db* makes plenty but cannot respond. Rare people with congenital leptin deficiency are severely obese, so the system matters in humans. In common obesity leptin levels are high, not low, and the response to it is weakened ("leptin resistance"), and how much this explains is debated.
- **Insulin** also reflects fat stores and acts in the brain.
- **Ghrelin**, made in the stomach, rises before expected meals and promotes eating. **Cholecystokinin** and **GLP-1**, made by the gut during a meal, promote satiety, partly through the vagus nerve to the brainstem.

### The arcuate circuit

The arcuate nucleus contains two opposing populations. **AgRP/NPY neurons** are excited by low leptin and by ghrelin and promote feeding; activating them in a fed mouse with light or a drug-activated channel makes it eat promptly (Aponte, Atasoy and Sternson, 2011; Krashes and colleagues, 2011). **POMC neurons** are excited by leptin and release $\alpha$-MSH, which acts on **melanocortin-4 receptors** (MC4R) in downstream areas, including the paraventricular nucleus, to reduce eating. AgRP blocks MC4R. In humans, loss-of-function mutations in MC4R are the most common single-gene cause of severe obesity, which supports the circuit's role in people.

### Thirst

Two stimuli cause thirst. **Osmotic thirst**: when plasma osmolality rises by a percent or two, osmoreceptors near the third ventricle (in the organum vasculosum of the lamina terminalis and subfornical organ, which lack a tight blood-brain barrier) increase vasopressin release, which makes the kidney conserve water, and drive drinking. **Volumetric thirst**: a fall in blood volume (for example by bleeding) raises renin and angiotensin II, which acts on the subfornical organ to drive drinking and salt appetite. Plasma osmolality in mOsm/kg can be estimated from lab values (mg/dL for glucose and urea nitrogen, mmol/L for sodium):

$$\text{Osm} \approx 2\,[\text{Na}^+] + \frac{[\text{glucose}]}{18} + \frac{[\text{BUN}]}{2.8}.$$

### A slow feedback loop in code

Suppose, as a toy model with illustrative constants (not measurements), that daily intake falls below expenditure by `gain` kcal per day for every kilogram of fat above a set point, and that 7700 kcal is one kilogram:

```python
def simulate_store(F0, days, gain, F_set=15.0, E=2300.0):
    """Body fat (kg) after `days` days of feedback on daily intake."""
    F = F0
    for _ in range(days):
        intake = max(0.0, E - gain * (F - F_set))
        F += (intake - E) / 7700.0
    return F

print(round(simulate_store(18.0, 300, 25.0), 2))
```

This prints `16.13`: starting 3 kg above the set point, the store has recovered only about two thirds of the gap after 300 days. Each day removes the fraction $\text{gain}/7700$ of the excess, so the time constant is $7700/\text{gain}$, about 308 days for a gain of 25 kcal/day per kg. Weak feedback on intake can still hold weight steady against tiny daily errors, but it corrects a large perturbation slowly. Two views are debated: a **set-point** view (a defended target) and a **settling-point** view (weight settles where the environment, behavior and physiology balance, with no fixed target). Evidence for set points includes defended weight after dieting; evidence for settling points includes the rise in average weight when calorie-dense food became cheap.

step: The daily surplus is $2700 - 2400 = 300$ kcal.
step: Days per kilogram: $7700/300 \approx 25.7$ days (an upper bound on how fast weight would rise, since expenditure also rises as mass grows).
step: Osmolality: $2 \times 140 = 280$, glucose adds $90/18 = 5$, BUN adds $14/2.8 = 5$.
step: Total: $280 + 5 + 5 = 290$ mOsm/kg.
answer: About $25.7$ days per kilogram, and an osmolality of $290$ mOsm/kg.
@mistakes
- Treating 7,700 kcal per kg as an exact law for weight change: expenditure and appetite adapt, so weight change slows.
- Reading parabiosis results backward: the *db/db* partner makes leptin but cannot respond, so its normal partner is the one that stops eating.
- Equating hunger with an empty stomach: eating is controlled by many signals, including leptin, ghrelin and gut peptides, and the brain's circuits for them act before and after meals.

model:
```python
def days_to_recover(F0, gain, tol, F_set=15.0, E=2300.0, max_days=2000):
    """Days until the fat store is within `tol` kg of the set point."""
    F = F0
    for day in range(max_days + 1):
        if abs(F - F_set) <= tol:
            return day
        intake = max(0.0, E - gain * (F - F_set))
        F += (intake - E) / 7700.0
    return None
```
hint: Check the condition before each update, so a store that starts inside the tolerance returns 0, and keep applying the daily update with the clipped intake until the condition holds or the days run out.
solution: The loop counts days, testing the tolerance at the start of each day. The case with gain 400 and a store of 30 kg shows why the intake must be clipped at zero: unclipped feedback would call for negative intake, while the clipped model loses fat at the maximum rate of 2300/7700 kg per day until it gets close to the set point.

prompt: Some researchers describe body weight as regulated around a set point; others say it settles at a point determined by the environment and physiology, with no fixed target. Give the best evidence for each view, and describe one observation that the other view finds harder to explain.
hint: Ask what each view predicts about weight after dieting and across populations over decades.
solution: For a set point: after weight loss, hunger rises and energy expenditure falls somewhat below what the new body mass predicts, and weight tends to drift back; parabiosis and leptin-deficiency studies show a circulating signal that corrects intake. For settling points: average body weight has risen across populations as energy-dense food became cheap and abundant, which a fixed biological target would not predict, and a feedback loop that is weak (long time constant, as in the toy model) can be overwhelmed by a persistent environmental push. A strict set point has trouble explaining the population-wide rise; a pure settling point has trouble explaining the defended weight after dieting and the severe obesity in single-gene defects of the leptin-melanocortin pathway. Both views agree that the arcuate circuit senses stores; they differ on whether a fixed reference value exists.
