@article
Your core body temperature stays within a degree or so of $37\,^\circ$C whether you sit in a cold library or run in the sun, yet the heat your body makes and loses changes many-fold. Something is measuring temperature, comparing it with a target and adjusting heat production and loss. Physiologists in the early twentieth century called such regulation **homeostasis** (Cannon's term, from the 1920s), extending Claude Bernard's idea that the body keeps its internal environment constant.

### The logic of a feedback loop

A homeostatic loop has four parts: a **regulated variable** (temperature, blood osmolality, blood glucose), a **sensor** that measures it, a **comparator** that sets the error relative to a **set point**, and **effectors** (sweat glands, blood vessels, muscles, hormones, behavior) that push the variable back. In **negative feedback** the effector's action opposes the error.

Take the simplest quantitative version. Let $e = x - x_0$ be the deviation of the variable from its set point. A constant disturbance $d$ (heat load per hour, in the variable's units) pushes $e$ up. The body's passive losses remove a fraction $a$ of the deviation per hour, and a feedback controller removes a further amount proportional to the error, with gain $G$ per hour:

$$\frac{de}{dt} = d - (a + G)\,e .$$

Setting $de/dt = 0$ gives the steady-state error and the time constant:

$$e_\infty = \frac{d}{a + G}, \qquad \tau = \frac{1}{a + G}.$$

Without feedback ($G = 0$) the error would settle at $d/a$, so feedback shrinks the error by the factor $a/(a+G)$ and speeds recovery. Two lessons follow. First, a **proportional** loop never removes the error completely: a nonzero error is what drives the effectors, so $e_\infty > 0$ for any finite $G$. Second, higher gain means a smaller error but also a faster loop, which, when delays are present, can start to oscillate; real body temperature and blood pressure do oscillate gently around their means.

### Is the set point fixed?

The set point can move. In **fever**, prostaglandin E2, produced in response to infection, acts on neurons of the preoptic area and raises the temperature the system defends: the person feels cold and shivers at normal temperature until the new, higher target is reached. Some physiologists therefore prefer **allostasis** (Sterling and Eyer, 1988): stability through change, with targets that shift with predicted demands. Whether that adds explanatory power beyond a flexible set point is debated.

### The hypothalamus

The **hypothalamus** is a small region (a few grams in humans) below the thalamus, on each side of the third ventricle, above the pituitary. Its main nuclei, by location and role:

| nucleus or area | main roles (textbook level) |
|---|---|
| preoptic area | temperature sensing and control, sleep, reproductive behavior |
| paraventricular nucleus (PVN) | oxytocin and vasopressin; corticotropin-releasing hormone; autonomic output |
| supraoptic nucleus (SON) | vasopressin and oxytocin for the posterior pituitary |
| suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) | circadian clock |
| arcuate nucleus | energy balance neurons; releasing hormones for the portal system |
| ventromedial hypothalamus | feeding, aggression, female receptivity (rodents) |
| lateral hypothalamic area | arousal (orexin neurons), feeding |

It acts through four channels. (1) **Autonomic**: descending paths to brainstem and spinal autonomic neurons adjust heart rate, sweating and blood vessels. (2) **Neuroendocrine, posterior pituitary**: large (magnocellular) neurons in PVN and SON send axons down the pituitary stalk and release vasopressin and oxytocin into the blood. (3) **Neuroendocrine, anterior pituitary**: small (parvocellular) neurons release **releasing hormones** into a capillary bed at the base of the hypothalamus; short **portal vessels** carry them to the anterior pituitary, where they control the release of its hormones. (4) **Behavior**: drives such as eating, drinking and seeking warmth. Sensors for blood osmolality lie in regions near the third ventricle that lack a tight blood-brain barrier, so they can sample the blood directly.

### What lesions show

Hetherington and Ranson (1940) found that damage in the ventromedial hypothalamus made rats overeat and become obese, and Anand and Brobeck (1951) found that lateral hypothalamic lesions made rats stop eating. This suggested a "satiety center" and a "feeding center". Later work showed the limits of the inference: the lesions also cut fibers of passage, such as dopamine axons running through the lateral hypothalamus, and the ventromedial lesions disturbed autonomic and endocrine systems (more insulin release, for example). A lesion's effect is not the function of the damaged region: it shows what the animal can do without the whole mixed collection of cells and axons that were destroyed. Modern cell-type-specific methods (optogenetics, chemogenetics) test the individual cell populations; the next lesson uses them.

@example
title: How much does feedback shrink a disturbance?
problem: A regulated variable has a constant disturbance $d = 2.0$ units per hour, passive loss $a = 0.5$ per hour and a feedback gain $G = 4.5$ per hour. Find the steady-state error with and without feedback, and the time constant of the loop.
step: Without feedback, $e_\infty = d/a = 2.0/0.5 = 4.0$ units.
step: With feedback, the total loss rate is $a + G = 5.0$ per hour, so $e_\infty = 2.0/5.0 = 0.4$ units: ten times smaller, since $a/(a+G) = 0.5/5.0 = 0.1$.
step: The time constant is $\tau = 1/(a+G) = 0.2$ h, which is 12 minutes. Without feedback it would be $1/a = 2$ h.
answer: The error is $0.4$ units with feedback against $4.0$ without, and the loop recovers with a time constant of 12 minutes.
@mistakes
- Treating the set point as an exact value that feedback always reaches: a proportional loop leaves a residual error $d/(a+G)$ under a sustained disturbance.
- Reading a lesion effect as the function of the region: lesions also destroy fibers of passage and cells with other roles, and the animal can compensate.
- Mixing up the two pituitary routes: posterior pituitary hormones are made in hypothalamic neurons and released from their axon endings, while the anterior pituitary makes its own hormones under the control of releasing hormones carried in portal blood.

@practice self
prompt: A proportional feedback loop under a sustained disturbance holds a variable slightly away from its set point. Explain why, and say how a second mechanism that responds to the accumulated error could remove the offset. Then give one reason that a moving set point (as in fever) is not the same as a failure of regulation.
solution: The effector acts only in proportion to the error, so a steady disturbance can be balanced only if the error stays nonzero: $e_\infty = d/(a+G)$. A mechanism that adds up the error over time (an integral term, for example a slower hormonal or adaptive process) keeps changing its output until the error is zero, which removes the steady offset. In fever the regulated target itself is raised by a signal acting on preoptic neurons, and the loop still defends the new value with the usual feedback, so regulation is working, around a different target.
rubric: Derives or states that a nonzero error is needed to drive a proportional controller.
rubric: Explains that accumulation of error (integral action) removes the offset.
rubric: Distinguishes a shifted target that is still defended from a failure of feedback.
