@article
Hamsters kept in constant darkness still run on their wheels about every 24 hours, but a mutant strain, found by Ralph and Menaker in 1988, cycles every 20 hours. When the suprachiasmatic nucleus of a normal hamster was destroyed, its rhythm vanished; when SCN tissue from the mutant was transplanted in, the animal's rhythm came back with the *donor's* 20-hour period (Ralph and colleagues, 1990). A clock lives in that small piece of tissue.

### A clock in the hypothalamus

The **suprachiasmatic nucleus** (SCN) sits above the optic chiasm in the anterior hypothalamus. Lesions abolish daily rhythms of activity, sleep and corticosterone in rodents (Stephan and Zucker, 1972; Moore and Eichler, 1972), and transplants restore them with the donor's period. Humans kept in time-free conditions show an average **free-running period** of about 24.2 hours (Czeisler and colleagues, 1999), so the internal clock is close to, not exactly, 24 hours, and most healthy adults lie within a few tenths of an hour of that value. A rhythm that persists without time cues and has a period near 24 hours is called **circadian**.

Inside each SCN cell runs a **transcription-translation feedback loop**. The proteins CLOCK and BMAL1 activate transcription of the *Per* and *Cry* genes; PER and CRY proteins accumulate, enter the nucleus and inhibit CLOCK:BMAL1, shutting off their own genes; then they are degraded (with phosphorylation by casein kinase 1 setting the pace) and the cycle restarts, taking about 24 hours. The hamster tau mutation lies in casein kinase 1 epsilon (Lowrey and colleagues, 2000), which explains the 20-hour period, and a *PER2* variant in a family with very early sleep timing (Toh and colleagues, 2001) shows that clock genes affect human timing. Many cells outside the brain, such as in liver, carry the same clock; the SCN coordinates them. The 2017 Nobel Prize recognized Hall, Rosbash and Young for the molecular mechanism, first suggested by work on the *period* gene in flies (Konopka and Benzer, 1971).

### Entrainment and the phase response curve

Because the internal period is not exactly 24 h, the clock must be reset daily, or **entrained**, by a **zeitgeber**, chiefly light. Light is detected for the clock by intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells containing melanopsin (Berson, Dunn and Takao, 2002; Hattar and colleagues, 2002) and sent along the retinohypothalamic tract to the SCN. The effect of light depends on when it occurs, summarized by the **phase response curve** (PRC): light in the early subjective night delays the clock, light in the late subjective night advances it, and light during the subjective day has little effect. In humans the crossover is near the minimum of core body temperature, usually a few hours before habitual waking. So evening light tends to delay and morning light to advance the rhythm. The SCN controls the pineal gland through a multi-synaptic path, and **melatonin** release rises in the evening; its onset in dim light is a standard marker of internal phase. Mismatch between the internal rhythm and social schedules is called **social jetlag** (Wittmann and colleagues, 2006); health associations with shift work come from observational studies, where confounding is hard to remove.

### Modeling a rhythm

A simple model of a circadian variable is a cosine:

$$x(t) = M + A\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi (t - \phi)}{\tau}\right),$$

with mesor (mean) $M$, amplitude $A$, period $\tau$ and **acrophase** $\phi$ (the clock time of the peak). A free-running rhythm with $\tau > 24$ h drifts later each day by $\tau - 24$ hours. After $k$ days the peak occurs at clock time $(\phi + k\tau) \bmod 24$.

```python
import math

def cosine_rhythm(t, mesor, amp, period, acro):
    """Value of a cosine rhythm at clock time t (hours)."""
    return mesor + amp * math.cos(2 * math.pi * (t - acro) / period)

for t in (5.0, 11.0, 17.0):
    print(t, round(cosine_rhythm(t, 36.9, 0.4, 24.0, 17.0), 3))
```

This prints `5.0 36.5`, `11.0 36.9` and `17.0 37.3` (core temperature in degrees Celsius, with illustrative constants): the peak at 17:00, the mean six hours before and after, and the trough at 05:00.

The deliverable for these two weeks is a model with two curves. Sleep pressure $S(t)$ rises while awake from the wake-up time; a circadian threshold $H(t) = H_0 + a\cos(2\pi(t - t_{\text{peak}})/24)$ is highest in the early evening. Sleep begins when $S(t) \ge H(t)$. The model reproduces that sleep begins at night, when $H$ has fallen, that a higher starting pressure brings onset earlier, and that a threshold peaking in the evening opposes sleep then. It also omits much: the thresholds are illustrative, light and activity feed back on the clock, and people differ in $\tau$.

problem: A person in time-free conditions has a period of $\tau = 24.3$ h, and the temperature peak is at 17:00 on day 0. At what clock time is the peak on day 20, and how many hours has it drifted?

step: The peak is at $(17 + 20 \times 24.3) \bmod 24 = 503 \bmod 24 = 23$.
step: Check: $17 + 6 = 23$ hours, which is 23:00.
answer: The peak is at 23:00 on day 20, six hours later than on day 0.
@mistakes
- Saying that circadian means exactly 24 hours: the free-running period is about 24.2 hours in humans and differs between people and species.
- Mixing up the direction of the light effect: light before the temperature minimum delays the clock, light after it advances it.
- Calling the pineal gland the clock: the SCN generates the rhythm, and the pineal gland, through melatonin, passes on its timing.

model:
```python
import math


def sleep_onset(wake, S0, tau_i, H0, a, t_peak, dt=0.1, max_hours=48.0):
    """First time (clock hours, not wrapped) when S reaches the threshold H."""
    for k in range(int(round(max_hours / dt)) + 1):
        h = k * dt
        t = wake + h
        S = 1.0 - (1.0 - S0) * math.exp(-h / tau_i)
        H = H0 + a * math.cos(2 * math.pi * (t - t_peak) / 24.0)
        if S >= H:
            return t
    return None
```
hint: Loop over the steps starting at `k = 0` (so a person who is already above threshold at waking gets `wake`), and return as soon as the pressure is at or above the threshold.
solution: The loop compares a rising exponential with a cosine threshold that peaks at `t_peak`. Starting at k = 0 handles a high starting pressure, and returning `None` handles a threshold that pressure never reaches. The returned time is not wrapped, so a value above 24 means after midnight on the next day.
