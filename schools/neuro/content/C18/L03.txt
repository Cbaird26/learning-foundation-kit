@article
In 1849 Berthold removed the testes from young roosters. The birds grew small combs and stopped crowing and fighting. When he put testes back into the body cavity at another site, with no nerve connection to the original site, the comb grew and the behavior returned. Something carried in the blood by the testes controls behavior. That experimental logic, **remove, replace, measure**, is still the foundation of behavioral endocrinology.

### Three classes of hormone

- **Peptide and protein hormones** (oxytocin, vasopressin, insulin, leptin, corticotropin-releasing hormone) are water soluble, circulate free, and bind **membrane receptors**, often G-protein-coupled, with effects within seconds to minutes.
- **Steroid hormones** (testosterone, estradiol, progesterone, cortisol) are made from cholesterol, are fat soluble and bind **intracellular receptors**, which act as ligand-activated transcription factors that change gene expression over hours. Steroids also have rapid actions at membrane sites.
- **Amine hormones** include thyroid hormone (which also acts through nuclear receptors) and epinephrine (membrane receptors).

Many hormones are released in **pulses**, with strong rhythms across the day and across the reproductive cycle, so a single blood or saliva sample is a noisy measure of "the level".

### Organizational and activational effects

Phoenix, Goy, Gerall and Young (1959) gave testosterone to pregnant guinea pigs. Their female offspring, as adults, showed less female-typical receptive behavior and more male-typical mounting, even when later given the hormones that normally make females receptive. The proposal that followed is the **organizational-activational hypothesis**:

- **Organizational effects** act during a sensitive period of development and permanently shape neural circuits. They are not reversed by later removal of the hormone.
- **Activational effects** act on already-built circuits and are reversible: they come and go as the hormone level rises and falls (castration of an adult reduces male behavior; replacement restores it).

The test for an activational effect is replacement in the adult; the test for an organizational effect is manipulation in the sensitive period with a test in adulthood under matched adult hormone. The distinction is a useful simplification; sensitive periods also occur at puberty and adolescence, and hormones can alter adult structure too.

### Receptor occupancy

A hormone $L$ binds a receptor with dissociation constant $K_d$ (the concentration at which half the receptors are occupied). For one ligand at equilibrium (mass action), the fraction of receptors occupied is

$$\theta = \frac{[L]}{[L] + K_d}.$$

At $[L] = K_d$, $\theta = 0.5$; at $[L] = 9K_d$, $\theta = 0.9$. If a competitive antagonist $I$ with dissociation constant $K_i$ is present, it takes up receptors without activating them, and the fraction occupied by $L$ falls to

$$\theta_L = \frac{[L]/K_d}{1 + [L]/K_d + [I]/K_i}.$$

Response is not always proportional to occupancy: tissues with many spare receptors reach a full response at low occupancy.

```python
def occupancy(conc, kd):
    """Fraction of receptors occupied by one ligand (same units for both)."""
    return conc / (conc + kd)

print(round(occupancy(30.0, 10.0), 2))
```

This prints `0.75`.

### What a correlation does and does not show

Suppose men with higher testosterone are, on average, more aggressive on a questionnaire. Several explanations compete: the hormone raises aggression; aggression and its outcomes raise the hormone (winning contests raises testosterone in many species, including humans in some studies); a third factor (age, stress, sleep, sport) drives both; or the measure is unreliable. The **challenge hypothesis** (Wingfield and colleagues, 1990) was developed from bird studies: testosterone rises with social challenge, so the hormone-behavior arrow runs in both directions. Evaluating a study means asking: what was manipulated, what was only measured, what is the effect size, and how large and diverse was the sample? In human studies, giving hormones to volunteers requires clinical oversight and is not something an individual can do safely on their own; most human evidence is observational or comes from natural experiments (clinical conditions, genetic variants).

@example
title: Occupancy with and without a competitor
problem: A receptor has $K_d = 8$ nM for its hormone. (a) What fraction is occupied at $[L] = 24$ nM? (b) A competitive antagonist with $K_i = 4$ nM is added at 12 nM. What fraction of receptors is now occupied by the hormone?
step: (a) $\theta = 24/(24 + 8) = 0.75$.
step: (b) Express concentrations as multiples of the constants: $[L]/K_d = 24/8 = 3$ and $[I]/K_i = 12/4 = 3$.
step: Apply the competitive formula: $\theta_L = 3/(1 + 3 + 3) = 3/7 \approx 0.43$.
step: The antagonist cut occupancy from 0.75 to 0.43 without changing the hormone concentration. Raising the hormone would restore occupancy.
answer: $0.75$ without the antagonist, $3/7 \approx 0.43$ with it.
@mistakes
- Assuming a higher circulating level always means a larger behavioral effect: receptor number, spare receptors and local synthesis in the brain matter as much as blood levels.
- Calling every sex-hormone effect "organizational" or "activational" by default: the label depends on timing and reversibility, which must be tested.
- Reading a correlation between a hormone and a behavior as one-way causation: behavior and social experience change hormone levels too.

```python
def occupancy_competitive(conc, kd, inh, ki):
```

```python
    a = conc / kd

```
hint: Convert each concentration to a multiple of its own dissociation constant; the denominator counts empty receptors (1), hormone-bound ($a$) and blocker-bound ($I/K_i$).
solution: Mass action gives one share for free receptors, $c/K_d$ for hormone-bound and $I/K_i$ for blocker-bound ones; the hormone's fraction is its share of the total. A common error is to leave out the blocker term, which gives the single-ligand answer.

A news story says testosterone "causes" aggression. Evaluate the claim: give at least three problems or alternative explanations, say what the statistics show, and describe one study design (without giving participants hormones) that would be more informative.
hint: Check the sample size, the p-value and the direction of causation before the news claim.
solution: With 40 participants and $p = 0.12$ the result is not statistically significant, and a correlation of 0.25 is small and imprecise (the confidence interval, roughly $-0.07$ to $0.52$, includes zero and moderate values). A more informative design measures hormone repeatedly in each person (within-person change across days or across contests) and behavior in a task, preregisters the analysis and uses a larger sample; natural experiments (clinical conditions or genetic variants affecting hormone action, studied with ethics oversight) give causal evidence more directly than a cross-sectional correlation.
