@article
In 1954 Olds and Milner implanted electrodes in the brains of rats and let the rats stimulate themselves by pressing a lever. Rats with electrodes in the septal area pressed the lever hundreds of times an hour; later work showed that sites along the medial forebrain bundle, through which dopamine axons run, were especially effective. Did the rats feel pleasure, or were they driven to repeat a behavior? The question is still debated.

### The dopamine system and prediction error

Dopamine neurons in the **ventral tegmental area** (VTA) project to the **nucleus accumbens** (ventral striatum) and to prefrontal cortex (the mesolimbic and mesocortical pathways); neurons in the substantia nigra project to the dorsal striatum. Schultz, Dayan and Montague (1997) interpreted recordings in monkeys as showing that dopamine neurons signal a **reward prediction error**: an unexpected juice reward makes them fire; once a cue predicts the reward, they fire at the cue and no longer at the reward; when the predicted reward is omitted, firing dips below baseline at the expected time. This matches the **temporal-difference (TD) error** of Sutton (1988):

$$\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t), \qquad V(s_t) \leftarrow V(s_t) + \alpha\,\delta_t,$$

with reward $r_t$, discount factor $\gamma \le 1$ and learning rate $\alpha$. Value $V$ at each time step after a cue is learned from the error. Consider a cue followed by three time steps (states 0, 1, 2) with reward after the last one. The first element of the output below is the error at cue onset, assuming the cue arrives unpredictably (value before the cue is zero), and the other three are the errors at the three states:

```python
def td_trial(V, reward, alpha, gamma=1.0):
    """One trial; returns [cue-onset error, error at each state]."""
    n = len(V)
    deltas = [gamma * V[0]]
    for s in range(n):
        r = reward if s == n - 1 else 0.0
        v_next = V[s + 1] if s < n - 1 else 0.0
        d = r + gamma * v_next - V[s]
        V[s] += alpha * d
        deltas.append(d)
    return deltas

V = [0.0, 0.0, 0.0]
print([round(x, 2) for x in td_trial(V, 1.0, 0.5)])
for _ in range(59):
    d = td_trial(V, 1.0, 0.5)
print([round(x, 2) for x in d])
print([round(x, 2) for x in td_trial(V, 0.0, 0.5)])
```

The three lines print `[0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 1.0]` (the first trial: error only at the reward), `[1.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0]` (after learning: error at the cue, none at the reward) and `[1.0, 0.0, 0.0, -1.0]` (omission: a negative error at the time of the missing reward). This reproduces the three response patterns. The account has limits: dopamine neurons also respond to novelty, aversive events in some subpopulations, and movement, and not every dopamine signal is a scalar error.

### Wanting and liking

Berridge and Robinson distinguish **liking** (hedonic impact) from **wanting** (incentive salience, the motivational pull of a cue). Rats with most of their dopamine in the nucleus accumbens depleted still show positive facial reactions to sweet tastes but do little to obtain food, which suggests dopamine is not necessary for the hedonic reaction as measured and is important for motivation. Other researchers emphasize prediction error learning, effort and vigor; these accounts may describe different functions of different dopamine subpopulations.

### How drugs act (textbook level)

Most drugs that people use compulsively raise dopamine in the nucleus accumbens (Di Chiara and Imperato, 1988, in rats), by different means. Cocaine blocks the dopamine transporter, which clears dopamine from the synapse; amphetamine reverses and blocks it. Opioids act on $\mu$ receptors, among other places on inhibitory interneurons in the VTA, which releases dopamine neurons from inhibition. Nicotine activates nicotinic acetylcholine receptors on VTA neurons and their inputs. Alcohol has several targets. The shared effect is a larger and longer dopamine signal than natural rewards usually give, bypassing the normal prediction-error calibration, so the drug acts as an error that cannot be predicted away.

### Accounts of addiction

- **Incentive sensitization** (Robinson and Berridge, 1993): repeated drug use sensitizes wanting, not liking, so cues trigger intense craving even as pleasure falls.
- **Habit and compulsion** (Everitt and Robbins, 2005): control shifts from the ventral to the dorsal striatum, from goal-directed to habitual to compulsive use.
- **Opponent process and allostasis** (Solomon and Corbit, 1974; Koob and Le Moal): each drug effect is followed by an opposing process that grows with use, causing tolerance and withdrawal, so use shifts toward relief of a negative state.
- **Choice accounts** point out that many people stop without treatment and that use responds to alternatives and incentives, and criticize the "brain disease" label; supporters of the disease model reply that changes in brain circuits and the persistent risk of relapse justify it. In animals, only a minority of rats given long access to cocaine show compulsive use despite punishment, and in people only a minority of those who try a drug become dependent.

Human imaging shows lower dopamine D2 receptor availability in the striatum of people with several addictions (Volkow and colleagues), but this is correlational: it may be a risk factor, a consequence or both. Treatment is a clinical matter.

problem: A state has value $V(s) = 0.2$ and the next state has value $V(s') = 0.6$. The reward on this step is $r = 0$, with $\gamma = 0.9$ and $\alpha = 0.5$. Compute the TD error and the updated $V(s)$.
step: The error is $\delta = r + \gamma V(s') - V(s) = 0 + 0.9 \times 0.6 - 0.2$.
step: $0.9 \times 0.6 = 0.54$, so $\delta = 0.54 - 0.2 = 0.34$.
step: Update: $V(s) \leftarrow 0.2 + 0.5 \times 0.34 = 0.37$.
answer: $\delta = 0.34$, and the new value is $V(s) = 0.37$: the earlier state inherits value from the better state that follows it.

model:
```python
def learn_values(n, rewards, alpha, gamma):
    """Tabular TD(0) values for a chain of n states, one reward per trial."""
    V = [0.0] * n
    for reward in rewards:
        for s in range(n):
            r = reward if s == n - 1 else 0.0
            v_next = V[s + 1] if s < n - 1 else 0.0
            V[s] += alpha * (r + gamma * v_next - V[s])
    return V
```
hint: Use the current value of the next state before it is updated on this trial, and use a different reward on each trial from the list.
solution: With reward always 1, values converge to $V(s) = \gamma^{n-1-s}$, so earlier states are discounted copies of the reward: that is how the error migrates back to the cue. An omission trial lowers only the last state at first and then the earlier ones, in later trials.
