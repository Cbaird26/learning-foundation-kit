@article
After a night of no sleep your reaction times lengthen, attention lapses and mood suffers, and a night of long sleep restores them. Every mammal and bird studied sleeps, and so do fish, flies and worms in some form. Yet the function of sleep is among the most debated questions in neuroscience. What we do know in detail is how sleep looks, how it is switched, and how the need for it builds up.

### Stages of sleep

In 1953 Aserinsky and Kleitman reported periods of rapid eye movements during sleep, with an active EEG. Human sleep is scored in 30-second epochs from the EEG, eye movements and chin muscle tone:

| stage | EEG | eyes and muscle |
|---|---|---|
| wake | alpha rhythm (8 to 12 Hz) with eyes closed, beta when alert | normal tone |
| N1 | slow, low-amplitude activity; the alpha rhythm fades | slow rolling eye movements |
| N2 | sleep spindles (11 to 16 Hz bursts) and K-complexes | little eye movement |
| N3 (slow-wave sleep) | high-amplitude slow waves; scored when waves of 0.5 to 2 Hz above 75 microvolts fill at least a fifth of the epoch | low tone |
| REM | low-amplitude, mixed-frequency, resembling wake | rapid eye movements; strong loss of muscle tone (atonia) |

A cycle of N1, N2, N3 then REM lasts about 90 minutes, and an adult has four to six per night. N3 is concentrated in the first third of the night and REM in the last third, with REM periods lengthening. In young adults roughly a fifth to a quarter of sleep is REM and a larger part N2; N3 declines with age. Muscle atonia in REM prevents the acting-out of dreams; when atonia fails in REM sleep behavior disorder, people move vigorously during dreams.

### The two-process model

Borbély (1982) proposed that sleep timing is set by two processes. **Process S** is homeostatic sleep pressure: it rises during wake and falls during sleep. **Process C** is the circadian signal, which sets upper and lower thresholds for S. We fall asleep when S reaches the upper threshold and wake when it falls to the lower one. A simple version has S rise toward 1 while awake and decay toward 0 while asleep:

$$S(t) = 1 - (1 - S_0)\,e^{-t/\tau_i} \;\;(\text{wake}), \qquad S(t) = S_0\,e^{-t/\tau_d} \;\;(\text{sleep}),$$

where $\tau_i$ is the rise time constant and $\tau_d$ the decay time constant. Fits to human data (Daan, Beersma and Borbély, 1984) gave values of the order of 18 hours for $\tau_i$ and 4 hours for $\tau_d$, so pressure builds slowly and is discharged faster, which matches that we need about a third of the day asleep. Sleep pressure is measured by the **slow-wave activity** (EEG power at about 0.5 to 4.5 Hz) in non-REM sleep, which is highest early in sleep and falls across the night, and rises after sleep deprivation.

```python
import math

def process_s(S0, hours, awake, tau_i=18.2, tau_d=4.2):
    """Sleep pressure S (0 to 1) after `hours` of wake or sleep."""
    if awake:
        return 1.0 - (1.0 - S0) * math.exp(-hours / tau_i)
    return S0 * math.exp(-hours / tau_d)

S = process_s(0.2, 16.0, True)
print(round(S, 3), round(process_s(S, 8.0, False), 3))
```

This prints `0.668 0.099`: sixteen waking hours from $S = 0.2$ raise pressure to 0.668, and eight hours of sleep nearly clear it. The model is a simplification fitted to data, and the constants are descriptive.

### Switching and neurobiology

Sleep-active GABAergic neurons in the **ventrolateral preoptic area** (VLPO; Sherin and colleagues, 1996) inhibit wake-promoting groups: the locus coeruleus (norepinephrine), raphe nuclei (serotonin), the tuberomammillary nucleus (histamine) and cholinergic nuclei. Those groups inhibit the VLPO in turn. Saper and colleagues described this mutual inhibition as a **flip-flop switch** that favors fast, complete transitions. **Orexin** (hypocretin) neurons in the lateral hypothalamus excite the wake-promoting groups and stabilize waking: orexin knockout mice show sudden sleep attacks (Chemelli and colleagues, 1999) and people with narcolepsy type 1 lack most orexin neurons (Peyron and colleagues; Thannickal and colleagues, 2000). REM sleep depends on circuits in the pons that switch on cortical activation and suppress muscle tone; lesions in cats that remove atonia produce REM sleep with movement (Jouvet's work). **Adenosine**, a by-product of energy use, rises in the basal forebrain of cats during prolonged waking (Porkka-Heiskanen and colleagues, 1997) and acts on receptors that caffeine blocks; it is a candidate for part of process S, though not the only one.

### Why do we sleep?

- **Memory consolidation.** People remember more after sleep than after an equal period awake (Jenkins and Dallenbach, 1924), and hippocampal place-cell sequences from a task replay in rats' subsequent slow-wave sleep (Wilson and McNaughton, 1994). Limits: effects depend on the task and the control, and causation from replay to memory benefit is shown only in some experiments.
- **Synaptic homeostasis** (Tononi and Cirelli): net synaptic strength rises in wake, and sleep downscales it to save energy and restore learning capacity; supported by cortical measurements in rodents, including microscopy of synapses (de Vivo and colleagues, 2017) and by slow-wave activity changes, but disputed in its extent and in which synapses it affects.
- **Clearance** of metabolites: interstitial space in the mouse brain expanded in sleep and anesthesia, increasing fluid exchange (Xie and colleagues, 2013, the "glymphatic" proposal); later work has questioned its size and direction, and its relevance to humans is uncertain.
- **Energy conservation and restoration**, and **adaptive inactivity** (sleep as safe, quiet time). None excludes the others, and none yet explains why consciousness is lost.

Healthy adults generally need around seven hours or more; consult current guidelines and a clinician for sleep problems.

@mistakes

```python
def sleep_summary(epochs):
    """Total sleep time (min), latency (min) and REM percent of sleep."""
    asleep = [e for e in epochs if e != 'W']
    if not asleep:
        return (0.0, len(epochs) * 0.5, 0.0)
    latency = next(i for i, e in enumerate(epochs) if e != 'W') * 0.5
    rem = 100.0 * asleep.count('R') / len(asleep)
    return (len(asleep) * 0.5, latency, rem)
```
hint: Wake epochs after sleep onset are not sleep time, and REM percent is relative to sleep time, not to the whole record.
solution: Count non-wake epochs for sleep time, find the index of the first one for latency, and divide REM epochs by non-wake epochs. A common mistake is to use all epochs as the denominator for REM percent, which would understate it when there is wake after sleep onset.
