@article
You see a bear on the trail, your heart pounds, and you feel afraid. Which came first, the feeling or the pounding? William James (1884) and Carl Lange (1885) argued that the order is perception, then bodily change, then the feeling, which *is* the perception of the bodily change. Common sense says the opposite. The disagreement has driven a century of experiments.

### Theories and what they predict

| theory | claim | key prediction |
|---|---|---|
| James-Lange | the feeling is the perception of bodily changes | cut off bodily feedback and feelings weaken |
| Cannon-Bard (1927, 1931) | thalamic and hypothalamic circuits produce feeling and bodily response in parallel | bodily changes are not needed for feeling |
| Schachter-Singer two-factor (1962) | undifferentiated arousal plus a cognitive label from the situation gives the emotion | the same arousal can feel different with different labels |
| Appraisal (Arnold, Lazarus, Scherer) | emotion arises from evaluating an event's relevance to one's goals | change the appraisal and the emotion changes |
| Basic emotions (Ekman) | a few emotions have innate programs and universal expressions | the same expressions are recognized across cultures |
| Constructionist (Barrett) | emotions are built from core affect and concepts in context | no fixed signature per emotion category |

**Evidence.** Cannon argued that the body responds too slowly and in too similar a way across emotions and that animals with the viscera disconnected from the brain still show emotional behavior. Against Cannon, later studies find that peripheral and bodily signals do bias feelings and that some autonomic patterns differ across emotions, although the extent is debated. People with spinal cord injury or loss of autonomic feedback still report feelings, in some reports weaker, and the interpretation is contested. Schachter and Singer injected epinephrine and manipulated information and a confederate's behavior; the data are usually cited in textbooks but were weak and have not been clearly replicated. The **facial feedback hypothesis** (Strack, Martin and Stepper, 1988: holding a pen in the teeth makes cartoons seem funnier) was not reproduced in a large registered replication across many labs (Wagenmakers and colleagues, 2016), though the question remains disputed. Ekman and Friesen (1971) found recognition of posed facial expressions across cultures, including a small-scale society; critics note that recognition depends on the task format and context. **Dimensional** views (Russell, 1980) place affect on valence and arousal axes. LeDoux separates **survival circuits** that detect and react to threats from the conscious *feeling* of fear, which he argues is constructed in cortical circuits; Panksepp argued for subcortical affective systems that themselves generate feeling. Which view is best is unresolved, and they may describe different levels.

### The amygdala

The **amygdala** is a group of nuclei in the anterior temporal lobe, next to the hippocampus. The **lateral** and **basal** nuclei (the basolateral complex) receive sensory input from thalamus and cortex and from the hippocampus; the **central nucleus** sends output to the hypothalamus and brainstem (periaqueductal gray, vagal and other autonomic centers); **intercalated cells** form inhibitory gates between them.

Klüver and Bucy (1939) found that monkeys with bilateral temporal lobe removal became unusually tame, examined objects orally and lost the normal fear response; later work implicated the amygdala. Patient **S.M.**, with rare bilateral amygdala damage from Urbach-Wiethe disease, has trouble recognizing fear in faces (Adolphs, Tranel and Damasio, 1994) and reported little fear in many situations, yet she did experience panic when breathing air with extra carbon dioxide (Feinstein and colleagues, 2013). So the amygdala is not needed for every form of fear.

Is the amygdala a "fear center"? Evidence for threat detection: lesion and conditioning results in animals and people, and activation by fearful faces. Evidence against a narrow view: amygdala responses also occur for novel, ambiguous and strongly positive stimuli, which led to the proposal that it is a **relevance detector** (Sander, Grafman and Zalla, 2003) that tags stimuli important to the organism; and in mice, different basolateral neurons respond to positive and negative cues and project to different targets (Namburi and colleagues, 2015). Most researchers now view it as a hub for the significance of stimuli and for linking them to responses, with fear a prominent but not exclusive case.

### Reverse inference

If fear activates the amygdala, does amygdala activation in a scan show fear? Bayes' rule gives

$$P(\text{fear} \mid \text{act}) = \frac{P(\text{act}\mid\text{fear})\,P(\text{fear})}{P(\text{act}\mid\text{fear})\,P(\text{fear}) + P(\text{act}\mid\text{not fear})\,(1 - P(\text{fear}))}.$$

If the amygdala also responds to non-fear conditions, the likelihood $P(\text{act}\mid\text{not fear})$ is large and the posterior stays modest even when $P(\text{act}\mid\text{fear})$ is high.

@example
title: How informative is amygdala activation?
problem: In a task, the prior probability that a trial evokes fear is $0.2$. The amygdala is active on 80% of fear trials and on 50% of non-fear trials. If the amygdala is active, what is the probability that the trial evoked fear?
step: Compute the joint probability of activation and fear: $0.8 \times 0.2 = 0.16$.
step: Compute the joint probability of activation and no fear: $0.5 \times 0.8 = 0.40$.
step: The total probability of activation is $0.16 + 0.40 = 0.56$.
step: Divide: $P(\text{fear}\mid\text{act}) = 0.16/0.56 \approx 0.286$.
answer: About $0.29$: activation raises the probability from 0.20 to 0.29, a small change, because the region is also active in many non-fear trials.
@mistakes
- Treating the amygdala as the fear center: it also responds to novelty, ambiguity and positive significance, and a patient without amygdalae can still feel panic.
- Reasoning that activation of a region proves a mental state (reverse inference): the strength of inference depends on how selective the region's response is.
- Picking a winner among the theories from a single experiment: theories differ in what they explain (a feeling, a bodily response, a label) and in what they would count as evidence.

@practice self
prompt: A scan study reports that viewing an unfamiliar social video activated the amygdala, and concludes that the video made people afraid. Write a critique that uses reverse inference, gives the probabilities that would make the conclusion strong, and suggests what extra evidence would help.
solution: The inference runs from activation to a mental state, which is valid only to the extent that the region is selective for that state: $P(\text{fear}\mid\text{act})$ depends on the prior probability of fear in the situation and on how often the amygdala is active for non-fear reasons. Since the amygdala also responds to novelty, ambiguity and social significance, $P(\text{act}\mid\text{not fear})$ is not small, and the posterior probability of fear is modest unless the prior is already high. The conclusion would be strong if activation were rare outside fear, for example if $P(\text{act}\mid\text{fear}) = 0.9$ and $P(\text{act}\mid\text{not fear}) = 0.05$. Extra evidence: self-report and autonomic measures of fear collected in the same task, a control condition matched for novelty and arousal, a pattern-based test of fear that has been validated across tasks, and replication.
rubric: Names reverse inference and explains the role of selectivity ($P(\text{act}\mid\text{not fear})$).
rubric: Mentions amygdala responses to novelty, ambiguity or positive stimuli as alternative explanations.
rubric: Suggests specific convergent measures or matched controls.
