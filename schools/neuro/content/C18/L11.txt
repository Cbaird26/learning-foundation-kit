@article
Of the 4,000 or so mammal species, a small minority form long-lasting pair bonds. Among voles, the prairie vole does and its close relative the meadow vole does not. Because the two species are so similar, and the difference in behavior is large, they offered a rare chance to ask which neural differences matter for a social bond.

### Pair bonds in voles

Prairie voles (*Microtus ochrogaster*) form partnerships after mating, share a nest and both care for young. The standard laboratory measure is the **partner preference test**: after cohabitation, the animal chooses between spending time with its partner and with an unfamiliar vole, and a preference means more time huddling with the partner. Findings from the 1990s onward:

- Infusing oxytocin (in females) or vasopressin (in males) into the brain could induce partner preference without mating, and blocking the receptors prevented it after mating (Williams and colleagues, 1992; Winslow and colleagues, 1993).
- The *distribution* of receptors differs between species: prairie voles have oxytocin receptors in the nucleus accumbens and vasopressin V1a receptors in the ventral pallidum, where promiscuous species have far fewer (Insel and Shapiro, 1992). Increasing V1a receptor expression in the ventral pallidum of meadow voles by gene transfer raised their partner preference (Lim and colleagues, 2004).
- Dopamine D2 receptors in the nucleus accumbens are involved in forming the bond, linking pair bonding to the reward circuit of the previous lesson (Aragona and colleagues, 2006).

The picture is of a social bond as a form of learned reward. It is not the whole story: CRISPR knockout of the oxytocin receptor in prairie voles, reported in 2023, left pair-bond formation largely intact, so other mechanisms can compensate and the receptor is not strictly required. Evidence that these mechanisms generalize beyond voles is much thinner; people are not voles.

### Parental care and social recognition

In rodents, the **medial preoptic area** is central for parental care: lesions disrupt it, and galanin-expressing cells in the area are required for parenting in mice (Wu and colleagues, 2014). Oxytocin helps mothers respond to pup calls (Marlin and colleagues, 2015). Oxytocin knockout mice fail to recognize previously met animals, and oxytocin returned to the **medial amygdala** restores recognition (Ferguson, Young and Insel, 2000).

### The social brain in people

Human imaging studies find a set of regions engaged when we think about others' minds (mentalizing): the medial prefrontal cortex, the temporoparietal junction and the posterior superior temporal sulcus, with the amygdala and the fusiform face area in face processing and the striatum in social reward. Being excluded in a game activated the dorsal anterior cingulate (Eisenberger and colleagues, 2003), which was read as "social pain overlaps physical pain"; critics note that this is a reverse inference, since the region responds to many kinds of salient events. Social support is associated with smaller cortisol responses in lab stress tests, although studies are mostly correlational.

### Two claims to weigh

**Intranasal oxytocin.** Kosfeld and colleagues (2005) reported that oxytocin sprayed into the nose increased trust in a game. Later studies found smaller and inconsistent effects; meta-analyses suggest small average effects with publication bias, and there is uncertainty about how much peptide reaches the brain and in what form (Walum, Waldman and Young, 2016; Leng and Ludwig, 2016). The hormone is clearly important in animals; whether nasal oxytocin reliably changes human social behavior is unresolved.

**Mirror neurons.** In macaque premotor area F5, neurons fire both when the monkey performs a grasp and when it sees one (di Pellegrino and colleagues, 1992; Gallese and colleagues, 1996). In humans, direct evidence is rare (single neurons in patients, Mukamel and colleagues, 2010) and most evidence is from imaging of regions with overlapping activity. Claims that mirror neurons explain understanding of others' intentions, language or autism go far beyond the evidence and are debated.

### How big a study do we need?

For a two-group comparison, with standardized effect size $d$, two-sided significance level $\alpha$ and power $1 - \beta$, the normal approximation to the number per group is

$$n \approx \frac{2\,(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})^2}{d^2}.$$

With $\alpha = 0.05$ ($z = 1.96$) and 80% power ($z = 0.8416$), $(1.96 + 0.8416)^2 \approx 7.85$, so $n \approx 15.7/d^2$. A study of 20 per group has only about 35% power for $d = 0.5$, and small underpowered studies both miss real effects and, among those that reach significance, exaggerate them.

@example
title: Sample size for a small effect
problem: How many participants per group are needed to detect $d = 0.4$ with 80% power at $\alpha = 0.05$ (two-sided), using the normal approximation?
step: The constant is $(1.96 + 0.8416)^2 = 7.849$.
step: $n = 2 \times 7.849/0.4^2 = 15.698/0.16 = 98.1$.
step: Round up to whole participants.
answer: 99 participants per group (a $t$ test needs slightly more, about 100).
@mistakes
- Treating the vole result as a theory of human love: it shows mechanisms of partner preference in one species; the human evidence is indirect.
- Calling oxytocin "the love" or "trust" hormone: its effects depend on context, species and site of action, and the human nasal-spray effects are small and inconsistent.
- Treating mirror neurons as an established explanation of empathy or autism: the single-neuron evidence is from monkeys and a few patients.

@practice self
prompt: A study of 40 volunteers (20 per group) reports that a nasal spray of oxytocin raised trust-game investments compared with placebo ($d = 0.5$, $p = 0.04$). A headline says "oxytocin boosts trust". Evaluate the claim: discuss power and the winner's curse, what a replication should do, and what animal evidence adds and does not add.
hint: Compute roughly what power a study of this size has for $d = 0.5$, then ask what significant results from low-powered studies look like.
solution: With 20 per group and a true effect of $d = 0.5$, power is about one third, so most studies of this size would miss the effect, and those that reach $p < 0.05$ tend to overestimate it (winner's curse). A single $p = 0.04$ is weak evidence, especially if many outcomes were tested. A replication should be preregistered, use a sample large enough for a smaller effect (about 175 per group for $d = 0.3$), define the outcome and analysis in advance, and report effects with confidence intervals. Animal evidence shows that oxytocin acts in social recognition and parenting circuits, but it does not show that a nasal spray raises trust in people, because delivery, dose, context and species all differ. The headline overstates a single small study.
