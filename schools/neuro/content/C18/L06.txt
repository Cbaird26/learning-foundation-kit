@article
A rat hears a tone, then receives a mild foot shock. After a few pairings it freezes at the tone alone. Two questions follow: where in the brain is that association made, and what happens when the tone is later played repeatedly with no shock? The second question matters most for people: exposure-based therapy for phobias and trauma-related disorders builds on the same principle, which clinicians apply.

### The conditioning circuit

In **Pavlovian fear conditioning**, a neutral **conditioned stimulus** (CS, the tone) is paired with an aversive **unconditioned stimulus** (US, the shock). Measures are freezing in rodents, potentiation of the startle reflex, and the skin-conductance response in people. The circuit, worked out largely by LeDoux and colleagues, is:

1. Auditory information reaches the **lateral amygdala** (LA) from the auditory thalamus (a fast, coarse route) and from auditory cortex (a slower, more detailed route).
2. The LA connects to the basal nucleus and to the **central nucleus** of the amygdala (CeA).
3. The CeA drives the **periaqueductal gray** (freezing) and the hypothalamus and brainstem (autonomic and hormonal responses).

Evidence: lesions of the amygdala before training abolish the learned response; pairing alters the responses of LA neurons to the tone; and conditioning strengthens the thalamic input to the LA (Rogan, Stäubli and LeDoux, 1997), which parallels long-term potentiation and depends on NMDA receptors in the LA. Whether the amygdala *stores* the memory or *modulates* storage elsewhere has been debated; most evidence favors plasticity within the amygdala, with other regions contributing.

In people, Bechara and colleagues (1995) described a **double dissociation**: a patient with bilateral amygdala damage failed to show conditioned skin-conductance responses but could state the CS-US pairing, while a patient with hippocampal damage showed conditioned responses but could not state the pairing. So the amygdala is needed for the emotional response and the hippocampus for declarative knowledge.

### Extinction

When the CS is presented repeatedly without the US, the conditioned response declines: **extinction**. Pavlov and later Bouton argued that extinction is *new learning* (the CS now signals no US) that competes with the old memory, not erasure of it, because:

- **Spontaneous recovery**: after a delay the response partly returns.
- **Renewal**: if extinction was learned in context B and the CS is tested in the original context A (or a third context), fear returns.
- **Reinstatement**: unsignaled US presentations restore fear to the CS.
- **Rapid reacquisition**: re-pairing leads to faster relearning than the first time.

In the circuit, the **infralimbic** part of rodent medial prefrontal cortex (often compared with the ventromedial prefrontal cortex in people, a correspondence that is debated) and the hippocampus (context) act on the amygdala, partly through the inhibitory **intercalated cells**, to suppress CeA output. Another route is **reconsolidation**: a reactivated memory becomes labile for a time. In rats, injecting a protein-synthesis inhibitor into the amygdala after a reminder reduced later fear (Nader, Schafe and LeDoux, 2000). Attempts to use this principle in people with drugs have produced mixed results, and the conditions under which it works are still being mapped. Lab fear conditioning is a model of one learning process; it is not the same as an anxiety disorder.

### The Rescorla-Wagner rule

Rescorla and Wagner (1972) described associative strength $V$ of each cue present on a trial as changing in proportion to the prediction error, the difference between the outcome $\lambda$ and the **summed** strength of all cues present:

$$\Delta V_i = \alpha_i \beta\,\bigl(\lambda - \textstyle\sum_j V_j\bigr).$$

With one cue and $\alpha\beta = 0.2$, $\lambda = 1$ for pairing and $\lambda = 0$ for extinction trials:

```python
def rw_single(V0, ab, lams):
    """Associative strength after each trial, for one cue."""
    V, out = V0, []
    for lam in lams:
        V += ab * (lam - V)
        out.append(V)
    return out

print([round(v, 4) for v in rw_single(0.0, 0.2, [1] * 5)])
```

This prints `[0.2, 0.36, 0.488, 0.5904, 0.6723]`: a negatively accelerated curve, $V_n = \lambda\bigl(1 - (1 - \alpha\beta)^n\bigr)$. Because the summed prediction sets the error, the rule explains **blocking** (Kamin, 1969): after cue A alone predicts the US, adding cue B yields little learning about B since the error is already near zero. But the rule treats extinction as unlearning, so it cannot explain spontaneous recovery or renewal; later models add context and competing associations.

title: Blocking by hand
problem: Cue A has been conditioned to $V_A = 0.9$. Now compound AB is paired with the US ($\lambda = 1$) for one trial, with $\alpha\beta = 0.2$ and $V_B = 0$. Find the new values of $V_A$ and $V_B$, and compare with the case $V_A = 0$.
step: The summed prediction is $V_A + V_B = 0.9$, so the error is $1 - 0.9 = 0.1$.
step: Each cue present changes by $0.2 \times 0.1 = 0.02$, so $V_A = 0.92$ and $V_B = 0.02$.
step: If $V_A$ had been 0, the error would be $1$ and each cue would change by $0.2$: $V_B = 0.2$.
step: Cue B gains ten times less after A has already been trained: A "blocks" learning about B.
answer: $V_A = 0.92$, $V_B = 0.02$ (versus $V_B = 0.2$ without pretraining of A).
@mistakes
- Saying extinction erases the memory: spontaneous recovery, renewal, reinstatement and rapid reacquisition show that the original association remains.
- Using each cue's own strength to compute the error: in the Rescorla-Wagner rule the error uses the sum over all cues present.
- Treating a single patient's dissociation as a double dissociation: both patterns, in different patients, are needed.

model:
```python
def rw_compound(V, ab, trials):
    """Rescorla-Wagner updates for two cues, with a summed prediction."""
    V = list(V)
    for present, lam in trials:
        total = sum(V[i] for i in range(2) if present[i])
        error = lam - total
        for i in range(2):
            if present[i]:
                V[i] += ab * error
    return V
```
hint: Compute the sum of strengths over present cues first, take one error, then update every present cue by the same amount.
solution: The same error is shared by all cues present, so a well-trained cue leaves little error for its partner (blocking), and on a compound trial with no outcome from strongly positive cues the error is negative for both. A common wrong solution computes a separate error for each cue from its own strength, which removes blocking.
