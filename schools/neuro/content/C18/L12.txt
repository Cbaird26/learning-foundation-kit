@article
Almost everything in this course came from animals first: the hypothalamic control of eating, fear conditioning, the HPA axis, the sleep switch, the clock and the vole bond. How much can we trust each result for people? The answer is neither "all" nor "none"; it depends on the question, the species and what evidence converges.

### Homology and analogy

A trait is **homologous** if two species inherited it from a common ancestor (a mammalian hypothalamus), and **analogous** if similar function arose independently (the wing of a bat and of a bird). Homology makes transfer plausible; it does not guarantee that the function is the same. Some mechanisms are deeply conserved:

- The circadian clock's transcription-translation loop works in flies and in mammals with homologous genes (*period*/*Per*, *Clock*, *cry*/*Cry*).
- Sleep-like states, with reduced responsiveness and homeostatic rebound after deprivation, occur in flies (Hendricks and colleagues, 2000; Shaw and colleagues, 2000) and in zebrafish, and the orexin system is conserved across mammals and fish.
- The amygdala's role in fear conditioning is shared by rodents and people, shown by lesion and imaging work, including the double dissociation in the previous lessons.
- Dopamine's role in reward learning and in drug action is conserved across mammals.

Others differ. The primate prefrontal cortex has regions (granular cortex) without clear rodent counterparts, so the "infralimbic cortex equals ventromedial prefrontal cortex" mapping is a hypothesis. Mice are nocturnal and sleep in many short bouts across 24 hours, so the *timing* of sleep relative to the SCN rhythm is reversed and the structure of sleep differs. Rodents' main glucocorticoid is corticosterone and not cortisol. Human social behavior, language and culture have no close animal counterparts.

### Validity of an animal model

Willner (1984) proposed three criteria. **Face validity**: the model resembles features of the human condition. **Construct validity**: it rests on the same mechanism or cause. **Predictive validity**: it predicts what works in people, for example that a drug effective in people also works in the model. A test can have one without the others: a rodent test in which immobility in a swim tank is reduced by antidepressants has predictive validity for some drugs but weak construct validity for depression, since what causes immobility is unclear. Fear conditioning is a strong model of learned fear but not of the whole of anxiety disorders.

### Why findings fail to translate

- **Biology differs**: species differences in receptors, metabolism and circuits. Drugs that blocked the corticotropin-releasing hormone receptor type 1 and reduced stress responses in rodents did not show clear benefit in several human trials for anxiety and depression.
- **Design**: small samples, lack of blinding and randomization, and publication bias inflate animal effects. Until recently most animal studies used only males (Beery and Zucker, 2011); the US National Institutes of Health began requiring consideration of sex as a biological variable in 2016. Check the current policy.
- **Husbandry**: laboratory mice at ordinary room temperature are below their thermoneutral zone (near 30 degrees Celsius), which changes their metabolism and stress physiology; housing, handling and strain all affect behavior.
- **Model mismatch**: a single inbred strain and a short, controlled lifespan differ from diverse people.

### Building an argument: the evidence ladder

Confidence rises with converging, independent evidence: (1) a homologous circuit or molecule; (2) a causal manipulation in animals (lesion, optogenetics, knockout); (3) correlational human evidence (imaging, hormone measures); (4) human causal evidence (lesion patients, genetic variants, pharmacology within ethics oversight, trials); (5) a replicated clinical effect. A claim resting on rung 2 alone is a hypothesis for humans. A rough way to combine pieces of evidence is to multiply likelihood ratios with the prior odds: prior odds 1:3 with likelihood ratios of 3 and 2 from independent findings give odds $\tfrac13 \times 3 \times 2 = 2$, a probability of $2/3$ (illustrative numbers, not a rule).

### Screening arithmetic

If a fraction $b$ of tested compounds truly work, and a test has sensitivity $Se$ and specificity $Sp$, the **positive predictive value** is

$$\text{PPV} = \frac{Se \cdot b}{Se \cdot b + (1 - Sp)(1 - b)}.$$

### Views on animal research

Research with animals is regulated: institutional animal care and use committees review it in the United States, and Directive 2010/63/EU applies in the European Union; the 3Rs of Russell and Burch (1959) call for replacement, reduction and refinement. Whether animal research is justified is a question of values, not only of evidence. Defenders emphasize that many medical advances depended on it and that mechanisms such as those in this course cannot yet be studied otherwise; critics emphasize animal suffering, poor translation in some fields and the growth of alternatives such as human cell models and imaging. Reasonable people weigh these differently, and rules differ between countries and change over time.

@example
title: How informative is a positive screen?
problem: A rodent screen has sensitivity 0.8 and specificity 0.7. Ten percent of compounds tested truly work. If a compound passes the screen, what is the probability that it works?
step: True positives per compound tested: $0.8 \times 0.1 = 0.08$.
step: False positives: $(1 - 0.7) \times 0.9 = 0.27$.
step: $\text{PPV} = 0.08/(0.08 + 0.27) = 0.08/0.35$.
answer: About $0.23$: most compounds that pass the screen do not work, because true hits are rare.
@mistakes
- Equating "homologous" with "same function": a conserved structure can have different functions or different inputs in another species.
- Judging an animal model on face validity alone: it should resemble the condition, but it also needs construct or predictive validity.
- Treating failed translation as proof that animal work is worthless, or successful translation as proof that all animal findings apply: transfer depends on the specific mechanism and the converging evidence.

prompt: Choose fear conditioning and extinction as the animal model and human anxiety-related fear as the target behavior. Weigh the evidence from animal models for how far findings apply to people, using the evidence ladder. hint: Go up the evidence ladder from homologous circuit to human causal evidence, and name the weakest rung.
solution: Established: the amygdala circuit for fear conditioning was worked out in rodents, with causal evidence (lesions, plasticity, manipulations); in people, lesion patients (a double dissociation between amygdala and hippocampus), imaging of amygdala responses to a conditioned stimulus, and skin conductance show a homologous system. The strongest rungs are 1 to 3; rung 4 is partial and rung 5 (clinical benefit from mechanism-based interventions) is limited to extinction-based therapy, which is practiced and studied by clinicians.
