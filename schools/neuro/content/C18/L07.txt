@article
Within a second of a sudden loud noise your heart speeds up. Over the next few minutes a second wave begins, and it peaks in the half hour that follows: cortisol rises in the blood and stays elevated for an hour or two. Why does the body use two systems, and what stops the second from running forever?

### Two systems

Hans Selye (1936) described a general response to diverse harmful agents, which he called the general adaptation syndrome (alarm, resistance, exhaustion). Today researchers separate two arms:

| | sympathetic-adrenal-medullary (SAM) | HPA axis |
|---|---|---|
| speed | seconds | minutes to hours |
| path | hypothalamus and brainstem to sympathetic neurons and the adrenal medulla | hypothalamus to pituitary to adrenal cortex |
| hormones | norepinephrine and epinephrine | cortisol in humans (corticosterone in rats and mice) |
| action | heart rate, blood pressure, blood flow, energy release | glucose mobilization, immune modulation, changes in brain function |

### The HPA axis, step by step

1. A threat is detected by sensory and limbic structures; the amygdala (central nucleus and bed nucleus of the stria terminalis) activates the hypothalamus, while the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex tend to restrain it for stressors that can be controlled or are not real threats.
2. Parvocellular neurons of the **paraventricular nucleus** release **corticotropin-releasing hormone** (CRH; characterized by Vale and colleagues in 1981) and vasopressin into the portal vessels.
3. CRH acts on **corticotroph** cells of the anterior pituitary, which release **ACTH** (adrenocorticotropic hormone, cleaved from the precursor pro-opiomelanocortin).
4. ACTH travels in the blood to the **adrenal cortex** (zona fasciculata), which releases cortisol within minutes.
5. Cortisol acts on the **glucocorticoid receptor** (GR, widely expressed) and the **mineralocorticoid receptor** (MR, higher affinity and largely occupied at baseline levels) in nearly every tissue, including the brain. Its effects over minutes to hours include mobilizing glucose, raising the vascular response to catecholamines and suppressing immune and inflammatory responses.
6. **Negative feedback**: cortisol acts at the pituitary, the paraventricular nucleus, the hippocampus and the prefrontal cortex to reduce CRH and ACTH. A fast phase (seconds to minutes) is non-genomic; a delayed phase depends on gene expression. Feedback is tested clinically with a synthetic glucocorticoid that suppresses ACTH release when feedback works.

Cortisol also has a strong **daily rhythm**: it peaks in the first half hour or so after waking (the cortisol awakening response) and is lowest around midnight, driven by the circadian clock. Its half-life in plasma is on the order of an hour or two. If $C_0$ is the concentration at time zero and the half-life is $t_{1/2}$ (minutes), then

$$C(t) = C_0\, 2^{-t/t_{1/2}} .$$

What counts as stressful is psychological as much as physical: a meta-analysis (Dickerson and Kemeny, 2004) found the largest and longest cortisol responses to tasks that combine uncontrollability with social-evaluative threat, such as speaking before a panel. Correlations between felt stress and measured cortisol are often modest.

### Chronic stress and the brain

In rodents, repeated stress shrinks dendrites in hippocampal CA3 neurons and prefrontal cortex and enlarges them in the amygdala (work associated with McEwen's laboratory), and impairs hippocampus-dependent memory and feedback. The **glucocorticoid cascade hypothesis** (Sapolsky and colleagues, 1986) proposed that exposure to high cortisol damages the hippocampus, weakening feedback and driving more cortisol. In people, smaller hippocampal volumes are reported in post-traumatic stress disorder and depression. But a twin study of Vietnam veterans (Gilbertson and colleagues, 2002) found that the combat-unexposed identical twins of veterans with PTSD also had smaller hippocampi, so a small hippocampus may be a pre-existing risk factor and not only a result of trauma. The causal direction is probably mixed. **Allostatic load** (McEwen and Stellar, 1993) names the cumulative wear from repeated or prolonged activation. The "exhaustion" stage proposed by Selye has little direct support, and the inverted-U relation between arousal and performance (Yerkes and Dodson, 1908) is better treated as a rough rule than a law. Treatment of stress-related disorders is a matter for clinicians.

@example
title: Cortisol after a stressor
problem: After a stressor, plasma cortisol peaks at 600 nmol/L (an illustrative value). If its half-life is 70 minutes and no further release occurs, what is its concentration 3 hours after the peak?
step: Convert 3 hours to minutes: $180$ min.
step: Count half-lives: $180/70 \approx 2.571$.
step: Apply $C = 600 \times 2^{-2.571}$.
step: $2^{-2.571} \approx 0.168$, so $C \approx 101$ nmol/L.
answer: About $101$ nmol/L, about one sixth of the peak.
@mistakes
- Treating cortisol as the immediate fight-or-flight hormone: the rapid response is sympathetic (epinephrine and norepinephrine); cortisol rises over minutes.
- Concluding that a small hippocampus in PTSD shows that trauma shrinks it: the twin evidence suggests that smaller volume can also precede trauma.
- Thinking negative feedback stops the response only at the adrenal gland: cortisol also acts at the pituitary, hypothalamus, hippocampus and prefrontal cortex.

solution: The threat is detected by sensory cortex and the amygdala, which activates the hypothalamus and brainstem. The sympathetic arm acts within seconds: preganglionic neurons stimulate the adrenal medulla to release epinephrine, and sympathetic nerves raise heart rate and blood pressure. A fast non-genomic feedback phase and a slower genomic phase bring release down. A stressor that continues keeps the axis active; feedback can then become less effective.
