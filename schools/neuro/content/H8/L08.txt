@article
A balanced equation is a recipe. Its coefficients say how many moles of each substance react or form, so they let you predict how much product a given amount of reactant can make.

### The mole ratio

For $\text{N}_2+3\,\text{H}_2\rightarrow2\,\text{NH}_3$, the coefficients give the **mole ratios**: $1\ \text{mol N}_2$ reacts with $3\ \text{mol H}_2$ to give $2\ \text{mol NH}_3$. As conversion factors: $\dfrac{2\ \text{mol NH}_3}{3\ \text{mol H}_2}$ and so on. Coefficients compare *moles*, never grams.

### The mass-to-mass pathway

To go from the mass of one substance to the mass of another:

$$\text{g A}\xrightarrow{\ \div M_A\ }\text{mol A}\xrightarrow{\ \text{mole ratio}\ }\text{mol B}\xrightarrow{\ \times M_B\ }\text{g B}.$$

The mole ratio from the balanced equation is the only step that depends on the reaction. If gases are involved, you can also use the ideal gas law (week 9) to move between moles and volume.

### Limiting reagent

Reactants are rarely mixed in exact stoichiometric proportion. The **limiting reagent** (limiting reactant) is the one that is used up first; it determines how much product forms. The others are **in excess**.

To find it, compute for each reactant the amount of product it could make (or compare mole amounts divided by their coefficients). The reactant giving the **smaller** amount of product is limiting, and that smaller amount is the **theoretical yield**: the maximum product possible. The leftover excess is the starting amount minus the amount used.

### Percent yield

The **actual yield** is what you really collect, usually less than the theoretical yield because of incomplete reactions, side reactions and losses. Compare them in the same units:

$$\text{percent yield}=\frac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}}\times100.$$

A percent yield above $100\%$ usually means the product is impure or wet.

### Strategy checklist

1. Balance the equation.
2. Convert every given quantity to moles.
3. Divide each mole amount by its coefficient; the smallest quotient marks the limiting reagent.
4. Use the limiting reagent's moles and the mole ratio to get moles of product, then convert to grams if needed.
5. Compare the actual yield to the theoretical yield for percent yield.

@example
title: Limiting reagent and percent yield
problem: A mixture of $6.0\ \text{mol H}_2$ and $4.0\ \text{mol O}_2$ reacts by $2\,\text{H}_2+\text{O}_2\rightarrow2\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$. Find the limiting reagent, the theoretical yield of water, the amount of excess reagent left, and the percent yield if $4.5\ \text{mol}$ of water is collected.
step: Moles of water each reactant could make: $6.0\ \text{mol H}_2\times\dfrac{2\ \text{mol H}_2\text{O}}{2\ \text{mol H}_2}=6.0\ \text{mol}$ and $4.0\ \text{mol O}_2\times\dfrac{2\ \text{mol H}_2\text{O}}{1\ \text{mol O}_2}=8.0\ \text{mol}$.
step: The smaller amount comes from $\text{H}_2$, so hydrogen is the limiting reagent and the theoretical yield is $6.0\ \text{mol H}_2\text{O}$.
step: Oxygen used: $6.0\ \text{mol H}_2\times\dfrac{1\ \text{mol O}_2}{2\ \text{mol H}_2}=3.0\ \text{mol}$, so $4.0-3.0=1.0\ \text{mol O}_2$ is left over.
step: Percent yield: $\dfrac{4.5}{6.0}\times100=75\%$.

@mistakes
- Using the ratio of grams, or the coefficients, directly without converting to moles.
- Calling the reactant with the smaller mass or smaller mole count the limiting reagent without accounting for the coefficients.
- Using the actual yield as the starting point for a calculation instead of the theoretical yield.
- Mixing units, such as dividing grams of actual yield by moles of theoretical yield.

@practice num
prompt: In $\text{N}_2+3\,\text{H}_2\rightarrow2\,\text{NH}_3$, how many moles of $\text{NH}_3$ form from $3.0\ \text{mol H}_2$ with excess nitrogen?
answer: 2.0
check: 3.0 * 2 / 3
tol: 0.02
unit: mol
hint: Use the mole ratio of $\text{NH}_3$ to $\text{H}_2$ from the coefficients.
hint: $3.0\ \text{mol H}_2\times\dfrac{2\ \text{mol NH}_3}{3\ \text{mol H}_2}$.
solution: $3.0\times\dfrac{2}{3}=2.0\ \text{mol NH}_3$.

@practice num
prompt: Hydrogen burns by $2\,\text{H}_2+\text{O}_2\rightarrow2\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$. How many grams of water form from $4.00\ \text{g}$ of $\text{H}_2$ with excess oxygen? Use $M(\text{H}_2)=2.016$ and $M(\text{H}_2\text{O})=18.015\ \text{g/mol}$.
answer: 35.7
check: 4.00 / 2.016 * 18.015
tol: 1%
unit: g
hint: Convert grams of hydrogen to moles first.
hint: The ratio of water to hydrogen is $2:2$, which is $1:1$.
solution: $4.00/2.016=1.984\ \text{mol H}_2$, which gives $1.984\ \text{mol H}_2\text{O}$, and $1.984\times18.015=35.7\ \text{g}$.

@practice num
prompt: A reactor holds $5.0\ \text{mol N}_2$ and $12.0\ \text{mol H}_2$ for $\text{N}_2+3\,\text{H}_2\rightarrow2\,\text{NH}_3$. What is the theoretical yield of $\text{NH}_3$, in moles?
answer: 8.0
check: min(5.0 * 2, 12.0 * 2 / 3)
tol: 0.02
unit: mol
hint: Calculate how much $\text{NH}_3$ each reactant could make.
hint: $\text{N}_2$ could make $10.0\ \text{mol}$. How much could $\text{H}_2$ make?
solution: $\text{N}_2$ could make $5.0\times2=10.0\ \text{mol}$; $\text{H}_2$ could make $12.0\times\tfrac{2}{3}=8.0\ \text{mol}$. Hydrogen is limiting, so the theoretical yield is $8.0\ \text{mol}$.

@practice num

answer: 79.8
check: 28.5 / 35.7 * 100
tol: 0.1
unit: %

hint: $28.5\div35.7$.
solution: $\dfrac{28.5}{35.7}\times100=79.8\%$.

@practice mc
prompt: A mixture of $10.0\ \text{g}$ Mg and $10.0\ \text{g}$ $\text{O}_2$ reacts by $2\,\text{Mg}+\text{O}_2\rightarrow2\,\text{MgO}$ ($M_\text{Mg}=24.305$, $M_{\text{O}_2}=31.998\ \text{g/mol}$). Which statement is correct?
choice*: Mg is limiting because $0.411\ \text{mol}$ Mg needs only $0.206\ \text{mol}$ $\text{O}_2$, and $0.3125\ \text{mol}$ is available.
choice: $\text{O}_2$ is limiting because the masses are equal and oxygen is heavier per mole.
choice: Neither is limiting because the masses are equal.
choice: $\text{O}_2$ is limiting because the coefficient of $\text{O}_2$ is smaller.
hint: Convert both masses to moles before comparing.
hint: Mg: $10.0/24.305$. $\text{O}_2$: $10.0/31.998$. Then apply the $2:1$ ratio.
solution: $10.0/24.305=0.411\ \text{mol Mg}$ needs $0.206\ \text{mol O}_2$. There are $10.0/31.998=0.3125\ \text{mol O}_2$, more than needed, so magnesium is limiting and oxygen is in excess.

@quiz num
prompt: Propane burns: $\text{C}_3\text{H}_8+5\,\text{O}_2\rightarrow3\,\text{CO}_2+4\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$. How many grams of $\text{CO}_2$ form from $25.0\ \text{g}$ of propane with excess oxygen? Use $M(\text{C}_3\text{H}_8)=44.097$ and $M(\text{CO}_2)=44.009\ \text{g/mol}$.
answer: 74.9
check: 25.0 / (3 * 12.011 + 8 * 1.008) * 3 * (12.011 + 2 * 15.999)
tol: 1%
unit: g
hint: Convert grams of propane to moles, apply the $3:1$ ratio, then convert to grams.
hint: $25.0/44.097=0.567\ \text{mol}$ of propane.
solution: $0.5669\ \text{mol C}_3\text{H}_8\times3=1.701\ \text{mol CO}_2$, and $1.701\times44.009=74.9\ \text{g}$.

@quiz num
prompt: Heating $50.0\ \text{g}$ of $\text{CaCO}_3$ ($M=100.09\ \text{g/mol}$) by $\text{CaCO}_3\rightarrow\text{CaO}+\text{CO}_2$ gives $24.6\ \text{g}$ of $\text{CaO}$ ($M=56.08\ \text{g/mol}$). What is the percent yield of $\text{CaO}$, to one decimal place?
answer: 87.8
check: 24.6 / (50.0 / (40.078 + 12.011 + 3 * 15.999) * (40.078 + 15.999)) * 100
tol: 0.2
unit: %
hint: First find the theoretical yield of $\text{CaO}$ in grams. The mole ratio is $1:1$.
hint: $50.0/100.09=0.4996\ \text{mol}$, so the theoretical yield is $0.4996\times56.08\ \text{g}$.
solution: Theoretical yield $=0.4996\ \text{mol}\times56.08=28.01\ \text{g}$. Percent yield $=\dfrac{24.6}{28.01}\times100=87.8\%$.
