@article
Atoms are far too small to count one by one, so chemists count them in bundles. A **mole** (mol) is the amount of substance that contains exactly $6.02214076\times10^{23}$ elementary entities. This number is **Avogadro's number**, $N_A$:

$$N_A = 6.022\times10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}.$$

A mole of atoms, a mole of molecules and a mole of eggs each contain $N_A$ items. The mole is the bridge between the atomic scale, where masses are in u, and the laboratory scale, where masses are in grams.

### Molar mass

The **molar mass** $M$ of a substance is the mass of one mole, in g/mol. It is numerically equal to the formula mass in u. Add up atomic masses for every atom in the formula:

- $\text{H}_2\text{O}$: $2(1.008)+15.999=18.015\ \text{g/mol}$.
- $\text{NaCl}$: $22.990+35.45=58.44\ \text{g/mol}$.
- $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$ (glucose): $6(12.011)+12(1.008)+6(15.999)=180.16\ \text{g/mol}$.

For an ionic compound the "formula mass" is the mass of one formula unit.

### The three-way conversion

Three quantities are linked by two conversion factors:

$$\text{grams}\ \xrightarrow{\ \div M\ }\ \text{moles}\ \xrightarrow{\ \times N_A\ }\ \text{particles}.$$

In symbols, $n = m/M$ and $N = nN_A$. Treat each as a unit factor: $\dfrac{1\ \text{mol}}{18.015\ \text{g}}$ converts grams of water to moles, and $\dfrac{6.022\times10^{23}\ \text{molecules}}{1\ \text{mol}}$ converts moles to molecules. You cannot go directly from grams to particles without passing through moles.

Keep track of what you are counting. One mole of $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ contains $N_A$ molecules, $2N_A$ hydrogen atoms and $N_A$ oxygen atoms.

### Percent composition

The **mass percent** of an element in a compound is the mass of that element in one mole divided by the molar mass, times $100$:

$$\%\ \text{element}=\frac{\text{(atoms per formula)}\times M_{\text{element}}}{M_{\text{compound}}}\times100.$$

The mass percents of all elements add to $100\%$, a quick check on your arithmetic. If you know percent composition, you can also find an **empirical formula**: assume $100$ g, convert each mass to moles, and divide by the smallest to get the simplest whole-number ratio.

### Significant figures

Use atomic masses with at least one more significant figure than the data, then round at the end. The value $N_A=6.022\times10^{23}$ is a constant and does not limit the significant figures.

@example
title: From particles to grams
problem: What is the mass, in grams, of $3.01\times10^{24}$ molecules of carbon dioxide, $\text{CO}_2$?
step: Find the molar mass: $12.011+2(15.999)=44.009\ \text{g/mol}$.
step: Convert molecules to moles with Avogadro's number: $\dfrac{3.01\times10^{24}}{6.022\times10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}}=5.00\ \text{mol}$.
step: Convert moles to grams: $5.00\ \text{mol}\times44.009\ \text{g/mol}=220\ \text{g}$.
step: The data have three significant figures, so the result is $2.20\times10^{2}\ \text{g}$.

@mistakes
- Going straight from grams to molecules without converting through moles.
- Forgetting to count the atoms inside a formula when asked about atoms rather than molecules (a mole of $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ has $2\ \text{mol}$ of H atoms).
- Using the atomic number or mass number instead of the atomic mass from the periodic table.
- Multiplying by the molar mass when the unit analysis calls for dividing.

@quiz num
prompt: How many molecules are in $5.00\ \text{g}$ of glucose, $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$? Use $\text{C}=12.011$, $\text{H}=1.008$, $\text{O}=15.999$. Give the answer in scientific notation.

hint: First compute the molar mass of glucose.

solution: $M=180.16\ \text{g/mol}$. $n=5.00/180.16=0.02775\ \text{mol}$. $N=0.02775\times6.022\times10^{23}=1.67\times10^{22}$ molecules.
