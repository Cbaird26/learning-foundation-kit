@article
A neuron in a hypothetical area $X$ fires more strongly in the second before a rat turns left. Does $X$ make the rat turn left? The data say only that firing and behavior covary. Systems neuroscience is mostly the craft of closing the gap between a covariation and a claim about causes, and this course is about the methods that narrow it: recording, imaging, tracing, perturbation and behavior.

### Four kinds of question

Marr (1982) separated levels of analysis: the **computational** level (what problem is solved and why), the **algorithmic** or representational level (what representation and procedure), and the **implementation** level (which neurons, synapses and dynamics). In practice, systems papers ask four things:

- **What** is there? Cell types, projections, tuning, response properties.
- **When** is it active? Activity relative to stimulus, choice or movement.
- **Why** does it exist? What computation or function it serves.
- **How** does it work? The mechanism: which cells, which connections, which transformation.

A claim must be matched to a method. A recording answers "when" well and "why" only through an argument; a tracing study answers "what is connected" and says nothing about whether the connection is used.

### A ladder of evidence

Think of rungs, each licensing a different sentence.

| rung | what you do | what you may say |
|---|---|---|
| observation | describe cells, anatomy, activity | the property exists |
| correlation | relate activity to a variable | activity carries information about it |
| necessity | remove or silence the population | the behavior depends on it, in this task and condition |
| sufficiency | activate it, with no other cause present | activation can produce the behavior |
| mechanism | identify cells, synapses and computation | how the behavior arises |

Information is not use. A population can carry a signal that downstream circuits ignore, so a decoder that reads out choice from $X$ shows that the information is present, not that the animal reads it. Necessity has its own caveats: silencing may fail to impair behavior because of **compensation** or incomplete silencing, and may impair it by disrupting downstream circuits (lesson 6). Sufficiency is limited by artificial patterns: forcing a population to fire can produce a behavior the population never produces naturally. A **double dissociation** (lesion $A$ impairs task 1 but not task 2, lesion $B$ the reverse) is stronger than a single dissociation, which can reflect only that one task is harder.

### Statistics belong to the logic

Many neurons are tested, few are truly task-related, and a threshold of $\alpha = 0.05$ is generous. Suppose $N$ neurons are tested, a fraction $\pi$ is truly tuned, the test has power $1-\beta$ and false-positive rate $\alpha$. Then

$$\text{TP} = N\pi(1-\beta), \qquad \text{FP} = N(1-\pi)\alpha, \qquad \text{PPV} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP}+\text{FP}} .$$

The **positive predictive value** (PPV) is the probability that a neuron flagged as tuned truly is. When $\pi$ is small it can be low even for a respectable test. Low power also exaggerates the effects that do cross the threshold (Button and colleagues, 2013), a theme of the methods lessons.

A **selectivity index** summarizes tuning: for mean rates $r_A$ and $r_B$ to two stimuli, $\text{SI} = (r_A - r_B)/(r_A + r_B)$, which runs from $-1$ to $1$. A neuron firing 40 Hz to $A$ and 10 Hz to $B$ has $\text{SI} = 30/50 = 0.6$.

### The unit of replication

Trials from one neuron are not independent, neurons from one animal are not independent, and animals are the units of a claim about a species. Treating 400 neurons from 3 animals as 400 samples is **pseudoreplication**. Hierarchical models, or analyses with the animal as the unit, are the honest alternatives.

### What is open

There is no agreed standard for when a circuit is "explained." Some researchers want mechanistic models that predict activity and behavior; others accept a causal manipulation with a clear behavioral effect. Both are defensible, and your proposal at the end of this course must say which standard it aims for and why.

@example
title: How many flagged neurons are real?
problem: A screen tests 400 neurons for task-related firing at $\alpha = 0.05$. Suppose 5% of neurons are truly task-related and the test detects 90% of those. What is the positive predictive value of a neuron flagged as significant?
step: Count the truly tuned neurons: $400 \times 0.05 = 20$. The test flags $20 \times 0.90 = 18$ of them (true positives).
step: Count the untuned neurons: $400 - 20 = 380$. At $\alpha = 0.05$ the test flags $380 \times 0.05 = 19$ of them (false positives).
step: Total flagged: $18 + 19 = 37$. The PPV is $18/37 \approx 0.49$.
step: Interpret: about half of the flagged neurons are false positives, even though each test was "significant." A claim built on the flagged list inherits that error rate, so a follow-up with an independent data set or a stricter threshold is needed.
answer: PPV $\approx 0.49$; roughly half of the flagged neurons are false positives.

@mistakes
- Treating a decoder's success as proof of use: information in a population shows the signal is present, not that downstream circuits read it.
- Reading a null result from silencing as "the region does nothing": compensation, incomplete silencing and a task that does not need the region all give the same null.
- Counting neurons as independent samples when they come from a few animals.
- Treating a single dissociation as a double dissociation: one lesion impairing one task may only mean that task is harder.

@practice num
prompt: A neuron fires at a mean of 45 Hz to stimulus A and 15 Hz to stimulus B. What is its selectivity index $(r_A - r_B)/(r_A + r_B)$?
answer: 0.5
check: (45 - 15) / (45 + 15)
hint: Subtract the two mean rates, then divide by their sum.
solution: $(45 - 15)/(45 + 15) = 30/60 = 0.5$. The index is unitless, so it is not affected by whether rates are in spikes per second or per minute.

@practice num
prompt: A laboratory tests 250 neurons for tuning at $\alpha = 0.02$. Suppose that, unknown to them, none of the neurons is truly tuned. How many neurons do they expect to flag as significant?
answer: 5
check: 250 * 0.02
hint: With no true effects, every significant result is a false positive, and each test has probability $\alpha$ of flagging.
solution: The expected number of false positives is $N\alpha = 250 \times 0.02 = 5$ neurons.

@practice mc
prompt: Silencing a cortical region with a drug during a trained task leaves performance unchanged. Which conclusion is best supported?
choice*: The region was not necessary for this task under these conditions, though incomplete silencing or compensation by other regions are possible explanations.
choice: The region carries no task information, so recordings from it must be spurious.
choice: The behavior is independent of that region in every task and training state.
choice: The region is inactive during the task.
hint: A null effect of a perturbation limits what you can claim about necessity; it says nothing about whether the region carries information.
solution: A null result shows that, in this task and with this perturbation, performance did not depend on the region enough to be detected. It does not show the region is silent or uninformative, and it cannot exclude incomplete inactivation or compensation.

@practice ms
prompt: Which of these results, taken by itself, is evidence that a neural population is sufficient to drive a behavior?
choice*: Activating the population in a resting animal, with no cue present, evokes the behavior.
choice*: Activating the population in an animal trained to report a percept makes it report that percept when no stimulus is shown.
choice: Silencing the population impairs the behavior.
choice: The population's firing rate rises before the behavior.
hint: Sufficiency asks whether activation alone produces the behavior; silencing and correlation answer different questions.
solution: Sufficiency is shown by gain of function: activation that produces the behavior without the natural cause (resting animal, or a report of a percept with no stimulus). Impairment after silencing is evidence of necessity, and a rise in firing before behavior is a correlation.

@practice self
prompt: A paper reports that neurons in area Y fire before a rodent's choice in a two-option task, and concludes that Y causes the choice. Write a critique of about 150 words that names two alternative explanations and states one experiment for each that would distinguish them from the causal claim.
hint: Ask what else could produce firing before a choice, and what perturbation or measurement would separate each alternative.
solution: Firing before a choice is a correlation, and at least two alternatives fit it. First, Y could carry a signal that is a consequence of the decision made elsewhere (for example, a corollary of motor preparation, or a feedback copy), so firing precedes the movement without driving the choice. To test this, silence Y with a perturbation fast enough to act within the trial and ask whether the choice changes; if only the movement's timing or vigor changes, Y reflects execution. Second, both Y and the choice could be driven by a third variable such as arousal, reward history or the animal's posture, which varies from trial to trial. To test this, measure the third variable and include it in the analysis, or compare trials matched on it; if the relation between firing and choice survives matching, the alternative weakens. Finally, even a positive silencing result supports necessity in this task, not sufficiency, so activating Y in a state without a choice would be the complementary test.
rubric: Names at least two distinct alternatives to the causal claim, such as a consequence of the decision or a shared third variable.
rubric: Proposes a specific experiment for each alternative and says what result would distinguish it.
rubric: Notes that necessity and sufficiency are separate claims and that the correlation alone supports neither.

@quiz mc
prompt: Lesion of region $P$ impairs a spatial-memory task but spares a visual-discrimination task. Lesion of region $Q$ impairs the visual-discrimination task but spares the spatial-memory task. What does this pattern show?
choice*: The two functions depend on at least partly separable substrates, which is stronger evidence than either lesion alone.
choice: Region $P$ is the seat of spatial memory and region $Q$ of vision, and nothing else contributes.
choice: Both tasks are equally difficult, so the lesions are equivalent.
choice: The regions have no connections with each other.
hint: Compare a single dissociation, which can be explained by a difference in task difficulty, with a double dissociation.
solution: A double dissociation rules out the simple explanation that one task is just harder, and supports separable substrates. It does not say that the regions are the only contributors or that they are unconnected.

@quiz num
prompt: A screen tests 1000 neurons at $\alpha = 0.05$. Suppose 10% of the neurons are truly tuned and the test has 80% power. What is the positive predictive value of a flagged neuron, to two decimal places?
answer: 0.64
tol: 0.01
check: (1000 * 0.1 * 0.8) / (1000 * 0.1 * 0.8 + 1000 * 0.9 * 0.05)
hint: Count true positives ($N\pi(1-\beta)$) and false positives ($N(1-\pi)\alpha$), then divide the first by their sum.
solution: There are $100$ truly tuned neurons, of which $80$ are flagged. Of the $900$ untuned, $45$ are flagged. PPV $= 80/125 = 0.64$.

@quiz self
prompt: A group claims that neurons in an area $Z$ hold a short-term memory of a cue, because their firing during a delay predicts the animal's later response. Describe a sequence of experiments, ordered by the rungs of the evidence ladder, that would support or undermine the claim, and say what each result would license you to say.
hint: Climb the ladder one rung at a time and write what each result would license you to say.
solution: Start with correlation: show that delay firing predicts the response when the cue, the delay and the movement are controlled, and that it does so on error trials in the expected way (for example, firing on error trials predicts the erroneous response). This licenses "Z carries information about the upcoming response," not use. Next test necessity: silence Z during the delay only, not during the cue or the response, and compare with control trials interleaved within the same animals, with a light-only or vehicle control; impaired responses only after delay silencing support dependence on Z during the delay, while impairment from silencing at the response alone would point to a motor role. Then test sufficiency: activate or bias Z in a controlled way and ask whether responses shift toward the value a manipulated pattern would carry. Finally, address mechanism: identify which cell types and inputs carry the delay signal and whether it persists through recurrent dynamics. Each step limits the claim: correlation shows information, necessity shows dependence under that task, sufficiency shows that activation can produce the effect, and only the last step approaches how.
rubric: Orders experiments from correlation to necessity to sufficiency to mechanism, with controls for each.
rubric: States for each result what it licenses and what it does not.
rubric: Uses epoch-specific perturbation to separate memory from cue or motor roles.
