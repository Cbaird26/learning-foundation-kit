@article
Memory and navigation are studied together because the hippocampal formation turns out to matter for both, and because the field supplies a clean example of the ladder of evidence: a lesion study showed necessity for a kind of memory, a recording study found cells with striking correlates, and perturbation studies ask whether those cells are used.

### Lesion evidence: H.M.

Scoville and Milner (1957) described the patient known as H.M., whose medial temporal lobes were removed on both sides to treat severe epilepsy. Afterward he had profound **anterograde amnesia**, an inability to form new lasting memories of events and facts, with preserved short-term retention, intelligence and some skill learning. This established that the medial temporal lobe is needed for new declarative memory and that memory is not a single system. What the case cannot say: the removal was large and included more than the hippocampus, so it cannot assign the deficit to one structure, and a single patient cannot give the statistics of an experiment.

### Recording evidence: place cells and grid cells

O'Keefe and Dostrovsky (1971) recorded hippocampal neurons in freely moving rats and found cells that fire when the animal is in a particular place, the **place field**. A population of place cells tiles an environment, and in a different environment the cells change their fields, a change called **remapping**. Hafting, Fyhn, Molden, Moser and Moser (2005) found **grid cells** in the medial entorhinal cortex, which fire at multiple locations arranged in a hexagonal lattice that tiles the environment.

These are strong **correlational** findings: firing is reliably related to position. They leave open whether the animal's behavior uses these signals, which circuit generates them, and what they contribute to memory beyond position.

### Quantifying spatial coding

Divide the arena into bins with occupancy fractions $p_i$ and mean firing rates $r_i$. The overall mean rate is $\bar r = \sum_i p_i r_i$. The **occupancy-normalized rate** in a bin is spikes divided by time spent there: 18 spikes while the animal spent 1.5 s in a bin gives 12 Hz. Without normalizing by occupancy, a bin the animal visits often would look like a place field.

A standard summary of how much a cell's spikes tell you about location is the **spatial information per spike**, in bits:

$$I = \sum_i p_i\,\frac{r_i}{\bar r}\,\log_2\frac{r_i}{\bar r},$$

with terms for $r_i = 0$ taken as zero. For two equally occupied bins with rates $8$ and $0$ Hz, $\bar r = 4$ Hz and $I = 0.5 \times 2 \times \log_2 2 = 1$ bit per spike: the spike identifies the half of the arena. This measure is **biased upward when there are few spikes or little sampling**, because a cell with a handful of spikes will look spatial by chance. The standard remedy is a **shuffle control**: shift the spike train in time relative to the position, many times, recompute $I$ each time, and ask how often the shuffled values reach the observed value.

### Plasticity and engrams

Hebb (1949) proposed that synapses strengthen when pre- and postsynaptic activity coincide. Bliss and Lømo (1973) showed long-lasting strengthening of synaptic transmission in the hippocampus after high-frequency stimulation of its inputs, **long-term potentiation** (LTP). That synapses can change in this way is established. Whether LTP is how memories are stored is supported by experiments in which interfering with plasticity impairs some forms of learning, with caveats about the specificity of the interventions.

Studies that tag neurons active during learning and later reactivate or silence them have reported behavior consistent with a memory, in some paradigms, which some researchers read as evidence that **engram** cells are part of memory expression. Others point out that driving a population can produce behavior without reproducing the original memory, that tagging captures only a subset of the active cells, and that silencing effects can have other explanations, so the interpretation is debated. How completely such cells contain a memory, and how they interact with the rest of the circuit, remain active questions.

### What is open

Whether hippocampal spatial codes are used for navigation or are a by-product of a more general function in memory; how grid and place signals interact; and how far findings in rodents carry over to people are open. A well-designed study pairs a recording with a perturbation of the same circuit and a behavior that depends on it.

@example
title: Rate and information from a small bin map
problem: A place-cell recording divides a linear track into two bins. The animal spends 1.5 s in bin 1 and 4.5 s in bin 2. The cell fires 18 spikes in bin 1 and 9 spikes in bin 2. Find the firing rate in each bin and the mean rate over the session.
step: Occupancy-normalized rates: $r_1 = 18/1.5 = 12$ Hz and $r_2 = 9/4.5 = 2$ Hz.
step: Occupancy fractions: $p_1 = 1.5/6 = 0.25$ and $p_2 = 4.5/6 = 0.75$.
step: Mean rate: $\bar r = 0.25 \times 12 + 0.75 \times 2 = 3 + 1.5 = 4.5$ Hz. Check against total spikes over total time: $27/6 = 4.5$ Hz.
step: Interpret: the cell fires at six times the rate in bin 1, although most spikes (18 of 27) also occur there, because bin 1 has a high rate in a short time.
answer: $12$ Hz in bin 1, $2$ Hz in bin 2, mean $4.5$ Hz.

@mistakes
- Using raw spike counts per bin rather than counts divided by occupancy, so the animal's visiting pattern appears as a place field.
- Reporting spatial information without a shuffle control, although it is biased upward when spikes are few.
- Concluding from H.M. that the hippocampus alone is responsible for memory, when the lesion was larger.
- Assuming that cells with spatial correlates are used for navigation without a perturbation experiment.

@practice num
prompt: A cell's rates are $10$ Hz in a bin occupied 20% of the time and $2.5$ Hz in a bin occupied 80% of the time. Its spatial information per spike is $I = \sum_i p_i (r_i/\bar r)\log_2(r_i/\bar r)$. What is $I$ in bits per spike, to two decimal places?
answer: 0.32
tol: 0.01
unit: bits/spike
check: sum(p * (r / (0.2 * 10 + 0.8 * 2.5)) * log2(r / (0.2 * 10 + 0.8 * 2.5)) for p, r in [(0.2, 10), (0.8, 2.5)])
hint: First find the mean rate $\bar r = \sum p_i r_i$, then evaluate the sum for each bin.
solution: $\bar r = 0.2 \times 10 + 0.8 \times 2.5 = 4$ Hz. Bin 1 gives $0.2 \times 2.5 \times \log_2 2.5 = 0.661$ and bin 2 gives $0.8 \times 0.625 \times \log_2 0.625 = -0.339$, so $I \approx 0.32$ bits per spike.

@practice self
prompt: A cell has 40 spikes in a recording and a spatial information of 0.9 bits per spike over 20 spatial bins. Describe in about 150 words a shuffle test to decide whether this value exceeds chance, and say what result would lead you to call the cell spatially tuned and what caveats remain.
solution: Few spikes make spatial information biased upward, so the observed value must be compared with a null distribution that has the same spike count and temporal structure but no relation to position. Shift the spike train circularly in time by a random amount, larger than a few seconds so that the temporal autocorrelation of the spikes is preserved but the relation to position is broken, then recompute the rate map and spatial information with the original positions. Repeat at least 1000 times to build the distribution. If the observed 0.9 bits per spike exceeds, say, the 99th percentile of the shuffled values, so the proportion of shuffles at least as large is below 0.01, call the cell spatially tuned at that threshold. Caveats: the threshold is arbitrary and, across many cells, needs correction for multiple testing; behavior that is stereotyped or tied to time (such as running at a fixed speed) can produce structured null differences, so it matters that the shuffle preserves the animal's path; and spatial information does not say the cell is a place cell with a single field, so field detection and cross-validated stability between halves of the session are further checks.
hint: Break the relation between spikes and position while keeping the spike train's own temporal structure, and compare the observed value with many shuffles.
rubric: Describes a time-shifted shuffle that preserves spike count and temporal structure while breaking the relation to position.
rubric: States a decision rule based on the shuffled distribution, such as a percentile or proportion.
rubric: Names a caveat such as multiple comparisons, stereotyped behavior, or the need for stability checks.

@quiz num
prompt: A cell's firing rates are $20$, $6$ and $1$ Hz in three bins, which the animal occupies for 10%, 30% and 60% of the session. What is the mean firing rate over the session, in hertz?
answer: 4.4

unit: Hz
check: 0.1 * 20 + 0.3 * 6 + 0.6 * 1

solution: $0.1 \times 20 + 0.3 \times 6 + 0.6 \times 1 = 2 + 1.8 + 0.6 = 4.4$ Hz.
