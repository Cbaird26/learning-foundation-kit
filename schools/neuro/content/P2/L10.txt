Decision circuits are where the models of lesson 7 meet the methods of lessons 2 to 6. The questions are the same: which neurons carry the relevant signals, whether the signals are used, and whether perturbation changes behavior as the model predicts.

### The drift-diffusion model

In the drift-diffusion model of two-choice decisions (Ratcliff, 1978), noisy evidence accumulates over time until it reaches one of two bounds, $+a$ or $-a$. The accumulated evidence $x$ obeys $dx = v\,dt + \sigma\,dW$, with drift rate $v$ (set by stimulus strength) and noise $\sigma$. With the start at zero, the probability of reaching the upper bound and the mean decision time are

$$P(\text{upper}) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-2va/\sigma^2}}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[T] = \frac{a}{v}\tanh\!\Big(\frac{a v}{\sigma^2}\Big).$$

With $a = 1$, $v = 1$ and $\sigma = 1$, accuracy is $1/(1+e^{-2}) = 0.88$ and the mean decision time is $\tanh(1) = 0.76$ s (plus a nondecision time for sensory and motor delays, omitted here). The model links accuracy and reaction time, and it makes a clear target for neural data: where in the brain is accumulated evidence represented?

### Correlational evidence: neural activity and choice

Recordings in monkeys performing perceptual decision tasks have found neurons in parietal and frontal areas whose firing rates ramp over time in a way that resembles accumulation, with the ramp steeper for stronger evidence. Whether single trials show smooth ramps or discrete steps, and which areas are the site of the computation, are debated.

A basic quantity is the **choice probability** of a neuron: with the stimulus held fixed, collect the neuron's spike counts on trials where the animal chose option 1 and on trials where it chose option 2, and compute the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve for discriminating the two sets. It equals the probability that a randomly drawn count from the first set exceeds one from the second, counting ties as one half. A value of $0.5$ means the counts carry no information about the choice, and values above $0.5$ mean that higher counts go with choice 1. Choice probability shows a **relation** between a neuron and choice, but not its direction: the neuron might influence the choice, the choice might feed back into the neuron, or a shared input might drive both.

### Correlational versus causal

For choice-related activity, causal tests include electrical or optical stimulation that biases choices, and inactivation that impairs them. Classic microstimulation experiments in motion-sensitive cortex (MT) of monkeys (Salzman, Britten and Newsome, 1990) biased choices toward the preferred direction of the stimulated neurons, which supports a causal contribution of that area's signals. Inactivation experiments in some decision tasks have given weaker effects than recordings would suggest, which is a reminder that necessity and information can come apart because of redundancy, compensation or task dependence.

### Context-dependent computation

Mante, Sussillo, Shenoy and Newsome (2013) recorded prefrontal cortex while monkeys judged either the motion or the color of the same stimulus depending on context. Both features were represented in the population whatever the context, but only the relevant one affected the choice. A recurrent network trained on the task reproduced this pattern, and analysis of the network suggested that the selection happens through the dynamics of the population rather than by blocking the irrelevant input at the input. This is a model-based explanation, supported by agreement between the network and the data, not a demonstration of the biological mechanism.

### Reward learning

Schultz, Dayan and Montague (1997) interpreted the phasic firing of midbrain dopamine neurons as a **reward prediction error**, matching the temporal-difference error $\delta = r + \gamma V(s') - V(s)$: firing rises for unexpected reward, is near baseline for a fully predicted reward, and dips when an expected reward is omitted. Dabney and colleagues (2020) reported that different dopamine neurons appear to encode different degrees of optimism about reward, consistent with a distributional code. Causal experiments that stimulate or silence dopamine neurons at defined times can alter learning, which supports a role of these neurons in reinforcement, while how closely the neurons implement a particular algorithm is still being tested.

### Open questions

Which signals are used rather than present, how decision variables are distributed across areas, and how well simplified models capture them remain unsettled. A strong study of a decision circuit pairs recording, model-based predictions, and a perturbation timed to the epoch the model says matters.

@example
title: Accuracy and speed from a diffusion model
problem: A drift-diffusion model has bound $a = 1.5$, drift $v = 0.8$ per second and noise $\sigma = 1$. Find the probability of choosing the upper bound and the mean decision time.
step: Accuracy: $2va/\sigma^2 = 2 \times 0.8 \times 1.5 = 2.4$, so $P = 1/(1 + e^{-2.4}) = 1/(1 + 0.0907) \approx 0.917$.
step: Mean time: $av/\sigma^2 = 1.5 \times 0.8 = 1.2$ and $\tanh(1.2) \approx 0.834$, so $\mathbb{E}[T] = (1.5/0.8) \times 0.834 \approx 1.56$ s.
step: Interpret: with these parameters the model is correct about 92% of the time and takes about 1.6 s of accumulation; raising the bound would increase accuracy and lengthen the time, a speed-accuracy trade-off.
answer: About $0.92$ probability of the upper choice, and a mean decision time of about $1.56$ s.

@mistakes
- Reading a choice probability above 0.5 as proof that the neuron causes the choice.
- Treating activity that ramps on average as evidence that every trial ramps smoothly.
- Treating agreement between a trained network and the data as demonstration of the biological mechanism.
- Concluding from a null inactivation effect that a region with strong choice-related firing carries no causal role, when redundancy or compensation could explain it.

model:
```python
def choice_probability(counts1, counts2):
    """Area under the ROC curve for counts on choice-1 versus choice-2 trials."""
    total = 0.0
    for a in counts1:
        for b in counts2:
            if a > b:
                total += 1.0
            elif a == b:
                total += 0.5
    return total / (len(counts1) * len(counts2))
```
hint: Loop over all pairs, add 1 when the first count is larger, 0.5 for a tie, and divide by the number of pairs.
solution: This is the Mann-Whitney form of the area under the ROC curve. Ties must count one half. In the last test the pairs give (2,2) one half, (2,3) and (2,4) zero, (4,2) and (4,3) one each, and (4,4) one half, so the total is 3 out of 6 pairs, or 0.5; dividing by the number of pairs, not by the length of one list, is the other point.

@practice self
prompt: Compare the correlational and the causal evidence for the claim that midbrain dopamine neurons implement a reward prediction error used for learning. In about 200 words, state what each kind of evidence shows, what it leaves open, and what single further experiment would most strengthen the claim.
solution: Correlational evidence: recordings show phasic firing that rises for unexpected reward, is near baseline for fully predicted reward and dips for omitted reward, matching the temporal-difference error, and later work reports variation in the degree of optimism across neurons. This shows that the activity carries the quantity a learning algorithm would need, and it fits quantitative predictions of the model, but it does not show that the signal is used for learning, and several other functions (salience, motivation, movement) could also produce related firing. Causal evidence: stimulating or silencing dopamine neurons at defined times can act like a reward or an omitted reward and change what animals learn, which supports a role in reinforcement. It leaves open whether the natural signal has the properties of a prediction error, because artificial stimulation is synchronous and its pattern is not the natural one, and acute manipulation may have off-target effects. A strongest further experiment would use a perturbation that mimics a graded error: for example, add or subtract a controlled amount of dopamine neuron activity at the time of an expected reward, with the size of the manipulation varied, and test whether the change in later behavior follows the error predicted by the model, with light-only and opsin-free controls and animals as the unit.
