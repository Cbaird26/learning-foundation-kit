@article
Sensory and motor systems are the places where systems neuroscience has been most successful, because the variable (a stimulus, a movement) is under the experimenter's control. They also show the ladder of evidence at work: a response property is described, a circuit model is proposed, and causal tests accept, refine or reject it.

### Orientation selectivity in visual cortex

Hubel and Wiesel (1959, 1962) recorded from single neurons in the cat's primary visual cortex and found that many respond best to a bar of light at a particular orientation, and weakly or not at all at the orthogonal orientation. They distinguished **simple cells**, with separate excitatory and inhibitory regions in the receptive field, from **complex cells**, which respond to an optimally oriented bar at a range of positions within the receptive field. Their proposal was a **feedforward** account: a cortical simple cell receives input from several geniculate neurons whose circular receptive fields are aligned along a line, so their summed input prefers an oriented bar, and a complex cell pools simple cells of the same orientation.

A tuning curve describes the result: the firing rate $r(\theta)$ to gratings at angle $\theta$. Because orientation repeats every 180°, a **vector** summary uses doubled angles:

$$\mathbf{v} = \sum_k r_k\, e^{\,i\,2\theta_k}, \qquad \theta_{\text{pref}} = \tfrac12 \arg \mathbf{v}, \qquad \text{OSI} = \frac{|\mathbf{v}|}{\sum_k r_k}.$$

The **orientation selectivity index** (OSI) is 0 for a flat tuning curve and 1 when the cell fires only at one orientation. Take rates $[12, 6, 0, 6]$ Hz at $0°, 45°, 90°, 135°$. Then $\mathbf{v} = 12 + 6\,e^{i\pi/2} + 0 + 6\,e^{i3\pi/2} = 12$, so $\theta_{\text{pref}} = 0°$ and $\text{OSI} = 12/24 = 0.5$.

The evidence: aligned geniculate inputs predict the layout of simple-cell receptive fields, and the recordings match many of those predictions. The limits: researchers have argued that inhibition and recurrent connections within the cortex also shape or sharpen tuning, and the relative contributions of feedforward input and cortical processing remain studied with intracellular recordings, silencing of cortex and models. A good summary is that a feedforward bias is part of the story, and how much is added by recurrent circuitry is debated.

### Maps

Penfield and Boldrey (1937) stimulated the surface of the human cortex during surgery and mapped the body parts that moved or felt touched, producing a **somatotopic** map in which neighboring body parts are represented by neighboring cortex, with disproportionately large areas for the hand and face. What this shows: cortical territory is organized topographically and not in proportion to body size. What it does not show: that each region has one function, or that the map is fixed; maps change with experience and injury, and stimulation evokes movements or sensations through activation of many neurons, not necessarily a natural pattern.

### Motor circuits

Damage to motor cortex or to its descending corticospinal output impairs skilled, fine movements, especially of the hand, more than gross movements, which shows that these circuits are needed for skilled movement; it does not show that motor cortex is the origin of all movement, because other structures contribute and compensation after damage is common. Recordings in motor cortex find activity related to movement before and during it. How to describe that activity is debated: some accounts relate single-neuron rates to movement parameters such as direction, and others describe the dynamics of a population. The two descriptions are not mutually exclusive, and they predict different things about how activity during preparation relates to activity during movement. As the songbird study discussed in lesson 5 showed, acute and chronic perturbations of motor circuitry can disagree, which is why convergent evidence is needed.

### Reading a perturbation study of these circuits

For any claim of the form "this region is required for this behavior," ask the questions from lessons 5 to 7 in order: was the manipulation verified, were light or ligand controls present, was the unit of replication the animal, was the behavior specific (a motor deficit versus a sensory one), and were the epoch and effector controlled?

@example
title: A preferred orientation
problem: A neuron's mean rates are $[4, 4, 16, 4]$ Hz at $0°, 45°, 90°, 135°$. What is its preferred orientation and OSI?
step: Doubled angles: $0°, 90°, 180°, 270°$. Components: $x = \sum r\cos 2\theta = 4(1) + 4(0) + 16(-1) + 4(0) = -12$.
step: $y = \sum r \sin 2\theta = 4(0) + 4(1) + 16(0) + 4(-1) = 0$.
step: The vector has length $12$ and points at $180°$, so $\theta_{\text{pref}} = 180°/2 = 90°$.
step: $\text{OSI} = 12 / (4 + 4 + 16 + 4) = 12/28 \approx 0.43$. The peak at $90°$ agrees with the vector.
answer: Preferred orientation $90°$, OSI $\approx 0.43$.

@mistakes
- Averaging orientations as ordinary angles: $10°$ and $170°$ are close neighbors, which only the doubled-angle vector respects.
- Treating a topographic map as proof that each region does one thing, or as fixed.
- Concluding from a lesion effect that motor cortex generates movement, without considering compensation and other contributors.
- Treating the feedforward account of orientation selectivity as either proven alone or refuted: both feedforward and recurrent contributions are studied.

hint: Sum $r_k \cos 2\theta_k$ and $r_k \sin 2\theta_k$ separately, find the length of the vector and divide by the total rate.

model:
```python
import math


def preferred_orientation(angles_deg, rates):
    """Preferred orientation (degrees, in [0, 180)) from the doubled-angle vector."""
    x = sum(r * math.cos(math.radians(2 * a)) for a, r in zip(angles_deg, rates))
    y = sum(r * math.sin(math.radians(2 * a)) for a, r in zip(angles_deg, rates))
    return (math.degrees(math.atan2(y, x)) / 2) % 180
```
hint: Use `math.atan2(y, x)` for the angle of the vector, halve it, convert to degrees and reduce modulo 180.
solution: Doubling the angles makes orientations 180° apart identical, so the vector sum respects the circularity of orientation; averaging raw angles would put the mean of 10° and 170° at 90°, but the doubled-angle vector gives 0° (equivalently 180°). In the test with the peak at 135°, the vector's angle is negative, which is why the result must be reduced modulo 180. For the pair at 10° and 170°, the vector points at 0°, and the tests compare orientations around the circle of 180° so that a result of 0 and a result of 180 count as the same orientation.

@practice self
prompt: A study expresses an inhibitory opsin in a population of motor cortex neurons in mice and reports: "Silencing during a lever-pull task reduced the success rate from 80% to 55% in 3 mice (240 trials each, treated as 720 independent trials, p < 0.001). We conclude that this population generates the lever pull." Light was delivered on every trial of a block, and no animals without the opsin were tested. Write a critique of about 200 words naming at least four problems and the control or analysis that addresses each.
solution: Problems and fixes. (1) No opsin-free light control: heating or visible light could reduce success, so light in animals expressing only a fluorophore is needed. (2) Light on all trials of a block: slow changes in motivation or fatigue are confounded with the manipulation, so light and no-light trials should be randomly interleaved. (3) Pseudoreplication: 720 trials from 3 mice are not independent, so the analysis should use mice as the unit, or a hierarchical model, and with 3 mice a p-value gives limited support and every animal should be shown. (4) The conclusion goes beyond the result: reduced success shows the population is needed under this manipulation, not that it generates the pull; a deficit in success could reflect impaired sensing, motivation or planning, so kinematics, epoch-specific silencing and tests of other behaviors are needed. (5) No verification: recording during light and histology of expression and fiber placement would show the extent of the manipulation. (6) Acute silencing may disturb downstream circuits, so a second manipulation with different weaknesses would help.
hint: Go through the controls from earlier lessons one at a time: light, interleaving, unit of replication, specificity of the behavior and verification.

rubric: Pairs each problem with a specific control or analysis that would address it.
