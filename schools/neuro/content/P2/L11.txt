@article
Almost every circuit result in this course comes from a species chosen for its tools: the nematode *C. elegans*, whose complete wiring diagram was reconstructed (White and colleagues, 1986), the fruit fly, larval zebrafish, mice, rats, and monkeys. The goal is usually knowledge about human brains. How far a finding travels is itself a scientific question, with an argument that must be made and tested.

### Homology and analogy

**Homologous** structures share an evolutionary origin (they descend from the same structure in a common ancestor); **analogous** structures serve a similar function but arose independently. The two have different consequences for inference. A homologous circuit with conserved connectivity and cell types gives grounds to expect similar mechanisms, though not identical function. An analogous circuit tells you about a computational solution to a problem, not about the human circuit. Some systems are strongly conserved across mammals (the basic layout of the retina, of the hippocampal formation and of the basal ganglia); others differ greatly (the expansion and subdivision of association cortex in primates, and the circuits for language, whose counterparts in other species are debated). A shared name for a region is not evidence of homology.

### Behavior across species

The same task does not guarantee the same process. Mice and humans can both succeed at a two-choice discrimination, but they may use different sensory cues, strategies and circuits, and species differ in sensory ecology (a mouse samples the world with whiskers and smell more than a primate does). A stronger comparison uses **matched tasks**, measures the same variables (accuracy, reaction time, history effects) and tests whether manipulations have predicted effects in both.

### Translation to humans

Human studies offer noninvasive imaging and electrophysiology, intracranial recordings in patients undergoing clinical monitoring, lesion cases and, in some circumstances, stimulation, but with limited resolution and no routine cell-type specificity. Animal studies provide the cell-level evidence. A **translational argument** connects them in steps:

1. The circuit is homologous, with conserved anatomy.
2. A matched behavior depends on it in the animal, with controls from earlier lessons.
3. Human evidence (lesions, imaging, recordings) is consistent with a similar role.
4. The mechanism makes a prediction that can be tested in humans.

Translation fails when the model has **face validity** (looks like the condition) but lacks **construct validity** (shares the mechanism) or **predictive validity** (predicts the response to treatment). For disorders defined by behavior and whose boundaries are debated, an animal model of one symptom may not capture the human category. A persistent difficulty in some areas of neuropsychiatric drug development has been that treatments effective in animal models have not been effective in people. Researchers disagree about how much this reflects the models, the trials or the diagnostic categories, and its causes probably differ between areas. Findings from animals are best treated as hypotheses about humans.

### The 3Rs and ethics review

Russell and Burch (1959) proposed that animal research should seek **replacement** (using non-animal methods, such as human studies or computational models, where they can answer the question), **reduction** (using the fewest animals that give reliable answers) and **refinement** (minimizing pain and distress and improving welfare). Reduction is a statistical question: underpowered experiments waste animals and give unreliable results, while excessive numbers are also wasteful. Within-animal designs, randomization, preregistered analysis and sharing of data increase the information obtained per animal.

Animal research is reviewed before it begins. In the United States, institutional animal care and use committees review protocols under the Animal Welfare Act and Public Health Service policy, and in the European Union research is regulated under Directive 2010/63/EU. Reviewers ask whether the question is important, whether alternatives were considered, whether the numbers are justified, what the humane endpoints are and how suffering is minimized. Rules and guidelines change, so check the current ones for your institution and country. Research with human participants is reviewed by ethics boards (in the United States, institutional review boards under the Common Rule), and the Declaration of Helsinki, last revised in October 2024, sets principles for medical research with people.

Whether and when animal experiments are justified is a question on which thoughtful people disagree. Some stress the value of the knowledge and its potential benefit to patients, others stress the moral weight of animal suffering. A proposal must be able to state its justification honestly, and the 3Rs are the common ground.

### Open questions

Which species and tasks best model which human functions, how to compare circuits quantitatively across species, and how to reduce the number of animals without losing reliability are all unsettled and are active methodological topics.

@example
title: How many animals per group?
problem: You want to detect an effect of standardized size $d = 1.0$ (the difference in means divided by the standard deviation) in a two-group comparison with two-sided $\alpha = 0.05$ ($z = 1.96$) and power $0.80$ ($z = 0.84$). How many animals per group, and how many in a paired design with the same $d$ computed on the paired differences?
step: Independent groups: $n = 2(z_\alpha + z_\beta)^2/d^2 = 2 \times 2.8^2 / 1 = 15.68$, rounded up to 16 per group, 32 in all.
step: Paired design: $n = (z_\alpha + z_\beta)^2/d_z^2 = 2.8^2/1 = 7.84$, rounded up to 8 animals, each tested in both conditions.
step: Compare: if $d_z$ equals $d$, the paired design needs 8 animals instead of 32, a reduction by a factor of 4, and this is a reduction in the sense of the 3Rs. In practice $d_z$ depends on the correlation between conditions and must be estimated from pilot data.
answer: 16 per group (32 in all) for independent groups, or 8 animals for the paired design.

@mistakes
- Treating a shared name or a similar appearance as evidence that two regions are homologous.
- Assuming that success at the same task means the same process in two species.
- Treating an animal model that looks like a condition as predicting how patients will respond.
- Cutting sample sizes to "reduce" animals without checking power, which wastes the animals used and yields unreliable results.

@practice mc
prompt: A laboratory redesigns an experiment so that each animal is tested with and without the manipulation, in randomized order, instead of using separate treated and control groups, and the power analysis then shows fewer animals are needed. Which of the 3Rs does this most directly serve?
choice*: Reduction, because it obtains the same information from fewer animals.
choice: Replacement, because animals are no longer used.
choice: Refinement, because the animals feel no pain in the new design.
choice: None, because the design does not change how many animals are needed.
hint: Which R is about numbers of animals?
solution: Using each animal as its own control lowers the number needed for a given power, which is reduction. It does not replace animals and does not by itself change welfare.

@practice self
prompt: A mouse study finds that silencing a projection from region $A$ to region $B$ impairs a flexible, rule-switching task. Write about 200 words arguing how far this supports a claim that the corresponding human pathway is needed for cognitive flexibility, naming the evidence you would need and one ethical consideration in the follow-up.
solution: The result supports a hypothesis, not a conclusion, about humans. First, homology: the claim needs the mouse regions to correspond to human regions with similar connectivity; the prefrontal cortex in primates is expanded and divided differently from rodent frontal regions, so naming a region the same way does not establish this, and tract tracing or comparative anatomy would need to be consulted. Second, the task: rule switching in a mouse may be solved with different strategies from human set shifting, so the human task should be matched and its dependence on the pathway tested. Third, converging human evidence: patients with damage to the putative homologous pathway, or imaging or intracranial recordings during the task, should show consistent deficits or activity; the mouse mechanism should give a prediction to test, such as a specific timing of activity during the switch. Fourth, the mouse result is itself acute silencing under one condition, so replication with a second manipulation would reduce the risk of off-target effects. Ethically, a follow-up in humans must use noninvasive, low-risk methods or clinically indicated recordings with informed consent, and further mouse work should apply the 3Rs, with the number of animals justified by a power analysis.
hint: Walk down the steps of a translational argument: homology, matched behavior, human evidence, a testable prediction.
rubric: Addresses homology and cautions that a shared name is not evidence.
rubric: Addresses matching the behavior and obtaining converging human evidence, with a testable prediction.
rubric: Raises an appropriate ethical consideration, such as consent, risk, or the 3Rs and power analysis.
