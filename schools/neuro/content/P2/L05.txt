@article
For most of the twentieth century, the main causal tools were lesions, electrical stimulation and drugs. Each acts on every cell near the site, whatever its type and projection. Optogenetics and chemogenetics changed what could be asked: perturb only the neurons of one genetic type, or only the axons that go to one target, on a chosen timescale.

### Optogenetics

**Optogenetics** makes neurons sensitive to light by expressing light-gated proteins derived from microbes. Boyden and colleagues (2005) showed that expressing channelrhodopsin-2 in neurons allowed them to be driven to fire with millisecond-scale pulses of light. Other opsins, pumps and channels, hyperpolarize neurons and reduce firing. Typical experiments pair an opsin with a way of choosing which cells express it: a promoter, a driver line that expresses a recombinase in a cell type, or a virus injected in a region. Illuminating terminals rather than cell bodies targets a **projection**, a particular pathway.

Strengths: millisecond timing, trial-by-trial control, rapid reversibility and cell-type specificity. The light is delivered through an optical fiber, so the manipulation is limited by how far light travels in tissue, which depends on scattering and absorption. Questions to ask in any paper:

- **Which cells expressed the opsin, and how many?** The extent depends on the virus and the expression, and is not the same as the extent of the light.
- **How much of the target population was changed?** Verified by recording neurons during illumination.
- **Are the evoked patterns physiological?** Light can drive many cells synchronously at rates they never reach in the animal.
- **What does the light do by itself?** Light can heat tissue, and an animal may see light leaking through the skull or eye.
- **Do inhibitory effects spread?** Silencing terminals may change activity elsewhere, and prolonged silencing may change cell state.

### Chemogenetics

**Chemogenetics** expresses engineered receptors, such as the DREADDs introduced by Armbruster and colleagues (2007), that are activated by an otherwise inert small molecule rather than their natural ligand. Depending on the receptor, activation increases or decreases the activity of the neurons that express it. The ligand is delivered by injection or in food or water, so no implanted hardware is needed, and the cells can be perturbed across a large volume.

The effect starts in minutes and lasts for hours, so it cannot be tied to a moment in a trial, and recovery is slow. An important caution: the commonly used ligand clozapine-N-oxide can be converted in the body to clozapine, which itself acts on endogenous receptors (Gomez and colleagues, 2017), so behavioral changes after the ligand may not be caused by the engineered receptor. The control is the same ligand in animals that do not express the receptor.

### Choosing

| property | optogenetics | chemogenetics |
|---|---|---|
| timescale of onset | milliseconds | minutes |
| duration of effect | the duration of light | hours |
| spatial control | limited by light spread | whole expressing volume |
| trial-by-trial control | yes | no |
| hardware in the brain | fiber or other light source | none |
| typical caveat | light and heating, non-physiological patterns | ligand metabolites, slow compensation |

The question picks the method. "Is activity during the delay needed for memory?" needs a fast, epoch-specific tool. "Does a brain-wide state change over a day?" may suit a slow, sustained manipulation.

### Acute versus chronic

Any perturbation changes the activity of regions downstream. Otchy and colleagues (2015) studied a songbird vocal circuit and reported that acute silencing of a pathway disrupted behavior in ways that a permanent lesion of the same pathway did not, which they attributed to the acute manipulation disturbing other circuitry. The lesson is general: an acute and a chronic perturbation can disagree, and neither is a clean test of "what the region does."

### Open questions

How precisely a manipulation reproduces natural activity patterns, how to calibrate the extent of effect in each experiment, and how to compare results across methods and laboratories remain active areas of method development. Researchers who report the verification of the manipulation in the same preparation provide the strongest evidence.

@example
title: Does the light drive the cell?
problem: A neuron expressing an excitatory opsin is illuminated with a train of 50 light pulses. Recording shows 45 spikes, one per pulse, at the first 30 pulses and 15 spikes at the last 20 pulses. What is the spike fidelity overall and in the second part of the train, and what does it suggest?
step: Overall fidelity: spikes per pulse is $45/50 = 0.90$, taking spike counts as given.
step: The first 30 pulses produced $45 - 15 = 30$ spikes (fidelity $30/30 = 1.0$), and the last 20 produced 15 (fidelity $15/20 = 0.75$).
step: Interpret: fidelity falls during the train, consistent with the cell failing to follow sustained fast stimulation, for example from opsin desensitization or depolarization block.
step: Consequence: the effective pattern delivered late in a train is not the pattern of the light, so the analysis should use measured spiking, not the stimulus schedule.
answer: Overall fidelity is $0.90$ (1.0 early, 0.75 late); late in the train the spiking no longer follows the light.

@mistakes
- Assuming the area illuminated is the area affected, or that every expressing cell responds: verify with recording.
- Omitting the light-only control in animals without the opsin, so that heating or visual effects are mistaken for circuit effects.
- Treating a ligand effect as receptor-specific without a control for the ligand's own pharmacology.
- Interpreting an acute silencing effect as the normal function of the region, without considering effects on downstream circuits.

choice: Give the ligand to the expressing animals twice as often.

solution: The ligand and its metabolites can act on endogenous receptors, so the key comparison holds the ligand fixed and varies receptor expression. Counting cells and repeating doses do not separate the receptor from the ligand.

@practice self
prompt: You want to test whether activity of a defined population in a frontal area is needed during a delay period for a memory-guided choice. Choose optogenetic or chemogenetic inhibition and justify the choice in about 150 words, naming one strength and one limitation of the method you chose, and one control you would include.
solution: Optogenetic inhibition is the better choice, because the claim concerns activity during the delay, and the delay lasts a second or two; only a fast, reversible manipulation can silence the population during the delay on some trials and leave the cue and response epochs intact, with control trials interleaved in the same animals. Its strength is this epoch-specific, trial-by-trial control. A limitation is that the effect depends on the extent of expression and light spread, so it is verified by recording during light, and prolonged inhibition can alter cell state or affect downstream regions. A necessary control is the light-only condition in animals expressing a fluorophore without the opsin (or light delivered at a control site), to rule out heating and visual effects. A chemogenetic manipulation would silence the population for hours, including cue and response epochs, so a deficit could not be assigned to the delay and compensation would have time to develop.
hint: The question concerns one epoch of the trial, so ask which method can act on that epoch and leave the others alone.
rubric: Chooses optogenetics and justifies it with the need for epoch-specific, trial-by-trial control.
rubric: Names a strength and a limitation of the chosen method, such as verification of extent or effects on downstream regions.
rubric: Includes an appropriate control such as light in animals without the opsin or light at a control site.

@quiz mc
prompt: Acute silencing of a pathway impairs a learned behavior, but a permanent lesion of the same pathway in other animals leaves the behavior intact. Which interpretation is most reasonable?
choice*: Either the acute manipulation disturbed other circuits, or the lesion allowed compensation; the two results do not by themselves show what the pathway normally does.
choice: The pathway is irrelevant to the behavior, because the lesion had no effect.
choice: The acute result must be right and the lesion result must be an error.
choice: The two experiments must have used different animals' behavior, so neither can be interpreted.
hint: Think about what each manipulation can do to circuits other than the target, and about what time the circuit has to adapt.
solution: Acute and chronic perturbations act differently. An acute manipulation can disrupt downstream or neighboring circuitry, while a chronic lesion allows compensation. Because each has a specific confound, the disagreement calls for further experiments rather than choosing one result.

@quiz num
prompt: An optogenetic protocol delivers 5 ms pulses at 20 Hz. What fraction of the time is the light on (the duty cycle), as a decimal?
answer: 0.1
check: 0.005 * 20
hint: Each second contains 20 pulses, each lasting 5 ms.
solution: Light is on for $20 \times 5$ ms $= 100$ ms per second, so the duty cycle is $0.1$. Duty cycle matters because average light power sets the heating.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which measurements would help verify an optogenetic inhibition experiment?
choice*: Recording neurons in the target region during illumination to measure how much firing falls.
choice*: Histology showing where the opsin was expressed and where the fiber was placed.
choice*: Testing the manipulation at several light powers.
choice: Counting how many days the animal was trained.
hint: Which measurements tell you how much of the target population was affected, and where?
solution: Recording during illumination, histology of expression and fiber placement, and dose-response tests each verify the manipulation. The number of training days does not tell you whether the manipulation worked.
