@project
title: A causal circuit experiment: a design proposal
overview: Write a ten-page proposal for an experiment that tests a causal claim about one behavior and one circuit, combining a measurement with a perturbation. It is a paper project: you propose, and you do not run, any experiment, and you use no animals and collect no new data. The proposal must include its ethics review and the 3Rs, controls for compensation and off-target effects, an analysis plan with a power calculation, and a statement of what each possible result would show.
goal: Turn a circuit hypothesis into a design in which each method is chosen for a stated reason and each alternative explanation has a control.
goal: Compute an effect estimate and a sample size from supplied pilot values, written from scratch, and justify the effect size you plan for.
goal: State in advance what each possible outcome, including a null result, would and would not show.
goal: Present the ethical review, the 3Rs and the limits of translation honestly.
step: Choose one behavior and one circuit from the literature you know, and write the causal claim as a single sentence with its rung of the evidence ladder (necessity, sufficiency or mechanism). Say which question (what, when, why or how) it answers and what would count as an explanation.
step: Write the circuit hypothesis (about one page): the cells and projections, the evidence so far at each rung (anatomy, recordings, perturbation), the species and why it is suitable, and the homology and translation argument.
step: Choose the measurement (extracellular recording, imaging or both) and the perturbation (optogenetic, chemogenetic or another), and justify each against the alternatives in terms of temporal resolution, specificity, the epoch in which the claim applies, and the limits of each method from lessons 2, 3 and 5.
step: Design the behavior and the controls (about two pages): the task, what $d'$, bias, lapse and motor measures you will report, and a table pairing each alternative explanation (light or ligand, drift, ineffective manipulation, nonspecific effect, strategy, a single animal) with its control and the verification of the manipulation.
step: Write the analysis plan with power. From the supplied pilot values, compute the per-animal effect, its standard error and the sample size for a paired design, written from scratch in plain Python or by hand (no statistics libraries); justify the smallest effect of interest, state exclusion criteria and the unit of replication, and say what you will preregister.
step: Write the ethics review and the 3Rs: replacement (what could not be replaced and why), reduction (how the design and the calculation keep numbers low) and refinement (welfare and humane endpoints), the review bodies that would apply (an animal care and use committee, or the equivalent where you work), and a note that rules change and must be checked for the institution. Include a short paragraph on what would and would not translate to humans.
step: Write the interpretation table: for at least four outcomes (for example, the expected effect with verified silencing; an effect only in the control group; no effect with verified silencing; an effect in the wrong epoch), say what each would show, what it would not show, and what you would do next. State the open questions that your design leaves open.
deliverable: A ten-page proposal with the sections above, including the control table, the interpretation table and an ethics and 3Rs section.
deliverable: The code or hand calculation for the effect estimate and the sample size, shown with its output and written without statistics libraries.
deliverable: A half-page reflection naming the weakest assumption in your design and the experiment you would run to test it.
rubric: The causal claim is stated precisely and matched to the right rung of evidence, and the methods are justified against alternatives with their limits stated.
rubric: Every alternative explanation has a specific control, the manipulation is verified, and compensation and off-target effects are addressed with reference to acute versus chronic perturbation.
rubric: The effect estimate and the sample size are computed correctly from the supplied values, the choice of the effect size is justified, and the unit of replication is the animal.
rubric: The interpretation table covers positive, negative and ambiguous outcomes honestly, and the open questions are named.
rubric: The ethics review and the 3Rs are complete and accurate, the translation argument is cautious, and the writing is clear and cites the literature accurately.
safety: This is a design exercise on paper. You do not perform any experiment, use any animals or collect any data. Any later study with animals needs approval from the relevant animal care and use committee, and any study with people needs ethics board approval and informed consent.

@review
**Weeks 1 to 2: logic of evidence and recording.** Systems claims climb a ladder: observation, correlation, necessity, sufficiency, mechanism. Information in a population is not use. Screens of many neurons produce false positives: $\text{PPV} = \text{TP}/(\text{TP}+\text{FP})$. Recordings are windows: intracellular recording, single units (biased toward large, active cells), the LFP and dense probes. Detection uses a robust noise estimate $\text{median}(|x|)/0.6745$, a threshold of $k$ times it, and a dead time; refractory violations flag contamination. Sampling above $2f_{\max}$.

**Weeks 3 to 4: imaging and anatomy.** $\Delta F/F = (F - F_0)/F_0$. Calcium is a slow, nonlinear proxy for spikes: $c_t = \gamma c_{t-1} + s_t$ with $\gamma = e^{-\Delta t/\tau}$, and deconvolution amplifies noise. Tracing has artifacts (fibers of passage, spread, tropism); fractions and densities answer different questions; a connectome gives structure, not sign, strength or use.

**Weeks 5 to 6: perturbation.** Optogenetics gives millisecond, cell-type and projection control; chemogenetics is slow and has ligand pharmacology (clozapine-N-oxide conversion). Acute and chronic perturbations disagree (compensation, off-target effects). Effect: $\hat\delta = \bar y_s - \bar y_c$ with $\text{SE} = \sqrt{s_s^2/n_s + s_c^2/n_c}$; paired designs use the differences; the animal is the unit. Sample size: $n = 2(z_\alpha + z_\beta)^2\sigma^2/\delta^2$ per group, or $(z_\alpha + z_\beta)^2/d_z^2$ paired.

**Weeks 7 to 8: behavior and sensory-motor cases.** $d' = z(H) - z(F)$ and $c = -\tfrac12[z(H) + z(F)]$; separate bias, lapse and sensitivity. Confounds: motivation, motor, sensory, strategy, history. Orientation tuning: doubled-angle vector, OSI; topographic maps are not exclusive functions.

**Weeks 9 to 10: memory, navigation, decisions.** H.M. (necessity of the medial temporal lobe for new declarative memory), place and grid cells (correlational), LTP, engram studies (suggestive). Occupancy-normalized rate; spatial information with a shuffle control. Drift-diffusion: $P = 1/(1 + e^{-2va/\sigma^2})$, $\mathbb{E}[T] = (a/v)\tanh(av/\sigma^2)$. Choice probability is a relation, not a direction. Prediction error $\delta = r + \gamma V(s') - V(s)$.

**Weeks 11 to 12: species, ethics and open problems.** Homology versus analogy; matched tasks; translation as hypothesis. The 3Rs (Russell and Burch, 1959); animal ethics review; check current rules. Participation ratio; models that fit equally well; open science.

**Self-check.** Can you (1) name the rung of evidence a result reaches, (2) pair each alternative explanation with a control, (3) compute an effect, its SE and a sample size by hand, (4) separate $d'$ from $c$, (5) say what a null result with verified silencing would show, (6) say what is still open?

**Common errors.** Treating information as use; reading a null as "does nothing"; counting trials as animals; omitting the opsin-free light control; reading calcium latency as neural latency; confusing share with density; rounding sample sizes down; treating model fit as mechanism.

prompt: In a paired perturbation experiment, eight animals' differences in accuracy (light minus no light, in percentage points) are $-3, -5, -2, -6, -4, -1, -5, -4$. What is the standard error of the mean difference, using the sample standard deviation with $n - 1$, to two decimal places?
answer: 0.59
tol: 0.01
unit: percentage points
check: np.std([-3, -5, -2, -6, -4, -1, -5, -4], ddof=1) / sqrt(8)
hint: Find the mean difference, then the sample standard deviation of the differences, and divide by the square root of the number of animals.
solution: The mean is $-30/8 = -3.75$. The squared deviations sum to $19.5$, so the sample variance is $19.5/7 = 2.786$ and the standard deviation is $1.669$. The standard error is $1.669/\sqrt 8 \approx 0.59$ percentage points, so the mean effect is about $6.4$ standard errors from zero in this invented data set.

@exam ms
prompt: A study combines two-photon imaging of a cell population with optogenetic silencing during a delay and reports that delay-period $\Delta F/F$ predicts the later choice and that silencing during the delay impairs choices. Which conclusions does the evidence, as described, support?
choice*: The population carries information about the upcoming choice during the delay (a correlation).
choice*: Under this manipulation, activity in the population during the delay is needed for correct choices in this task, subject to the controls being in place.
choice: The population alone generates the decision, and no other region contributes.
choice: The calcium signal gives the exact spike times of the neurons during the delay.
hint: Match each statement to the rung of the evidence ladder and to what the measurement can resolve.
solution: Prediction of the choice by delay activity is correlational information, and impairment after epoch-specific silencing supports necessity of delay activity in this task if the controls (light-only, verification, animals as units) are present. Neither shows that the population is the only contributor, and calcium imaging does not resolve exact spike times.

@exam self
prompt: Timed answer (about 20 minutes, 300 words). A paper reports that chemogenetic inhibition of a cortical projection in mice impairs a learned discrimination, and concludes that the projection "encodes the learned value." The ligand was given to the expressing animals, performance was compared before and after, and 120 trials per animal from 4 animals were pooled. Critique the claim and propose a revised study with controls, an analysis plan and a statement of what each result would show.
solution: Problems. (1) Ligand control: the ligand, or its metabolites such as clozapine, can act on endogenous receptors, so the same ligand must be given to non-expressing animals; without that, the effect cannot be attributed to the engineered receptor. (2) Design: a before and after comparison confounds the manipulation with time, learning, fatigue and motivation, so a randomized, counterbalanced crossover with a vehicle condition is needed. (3) Time scale: chemogenetic effects last hours and cannot isolate the epoch of the trial, so the effect could come from changes in sensation, motivation or movement, and compensation or effects on downstream circuits cannot be excluded. (4) Statistics: pooling 480 trials from 4 animals is pseudoreplication; the animal is the unit, and 4 animals have limited power. (5) The conclusion: impairment after inhibition shows that the projection is needed under this manipulation, not that it encodes value; a claim of encoding needs recordings or imaging of the projection's activity in relation to value, with value manipulated independently of stimulus and choice. Revised study: record or image the projection's terminals during learning to test coding; then use an epoch-specific optogenetic manipulation with interleaved light trials, an opsin-free light control and verified inhibition; measure $d'$, bias, lapse and motor performance; use a paired design with the number of animals set by a power calculation from the smallest effect of interest; preregister the analysis. Outcomes: if the terminal activity tracks value and epoch-specific silencing at the time of value use impairs choices without changing motor measures, the claim that the projection carries and uses value is supported; if silencing impairs choices but activity does not track value, the projection is needed but may not encode value; if there is no effect with verified silencing, it is not necessary in this task, though redundancy remains possible.
hint: Go through the ligand control, the comparison, the time scale, the unit of replication and the difference between needed and encoding.
rubric: Identifies the missing ligand control, the confounded before and after design, and pseudoreplication, with the fix for each.
rubric: Distinguishes needed under a manipulation from encoding, and proposes a recording or imaging test and an epoch-specific manipulation.
rubric: Gives an analysis plan with the animal as the unit and a power calculation, and states what at least three outcomes would show.
