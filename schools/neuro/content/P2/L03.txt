@article
Electrodes sample neurons near their tips, in whatever pattern the tissue gives. Light lets you ask a different question: record from hundreds or thousands of neurons in a field of view, identify the same cells across days, and label them by type. The price is that the signal is not voltage but fluorescence, a slow chemical proxy.

### How the signal is produced

A **genetically encoded calcium indicator** such as the GCaMP family becomes brighter when it binds calcium. When a neuron fires an action potential, calcium enters through voltage-gated channels, and indicator fluorescence rises quickly and decays over hundreds of milliseconds to seconds. Chen and colleagues (2013) described the GCaMP6 indicators, available in variants that trade speed against sensitivity (fast, medium and slow versions). Imaging is typically done with **two-photon microscopy** (Denk, Strickler and Webb, 1990): excitation occurs only where two photons arrive nearly together, which confines it to the focal point, reduces out-of-focus signal and allows imaging deeper in scattering tissue than one-photon microscopy. **Widefield** imaging records a large cortical area at low spatial resolution and mixes the signal of many cells.

### Fluorescence to a normalized signal

Raw fluorescence depends on how much indicator a cell expresses and on optics, so it is normalized by a baseline:

$$\frac{\Delta F}{F}(t) = \frac{F(t) - F_0}{F_0},$$

where $F_0$ is a baseline fluorescence, often taken as a low percentile of the trace or its mean in a quiet period. A cell whose fluorescence rises from 100 to 130 arbitrary units has $\Delta F/F = 0.30$. The signal is also contaminated by out-of-focus fluorescence (neuropil), which is commonly estimated and subtracted, with the risk that the subtraction removes or adds real signal.

### From fluorescence to events

A simple model treats the calcium-dependent fluorescence $c_t$ at sample $t$ as a decaying trace driven by non-negative events $s_t$:

$$c_t = \gamma\, c_{t-1} + s_t, \qquad \gamma = e^{-\Delta t/\tau},$$

where $\Delta t$ is the sampling interval and $\tau$ is the decay time of the indicator. For $\Delta t = 33$ ms and $\tau = 600$ ms, $\gamma = e^{-0.055} \approx 0.946$. Given the trace, the events are $s_t = c_t - \gamma c_{t-1}$, clipped at zero because events cannot be negative. This simplest deconvolution amplifies noise, because the subtraction acts like a derivative. Practical methods add noise models and sparsity constraints, and the results are estimates, not spike counts.

### Why it is not a spike train

- **Low-pass filtering.** The decay time sets how finely events can be separated in time; spikes within tens of milliseconds merge, and imaging at 30 frames per second cannot resolve spike timing finer than the frame.
- **Nonlinearity.** The relation between spikes and fluorescence is not linear: a single spike may fall below the noise, and many spikes saturate the indicator. Amplitude is therefore a poor guide to spike count outside a calibrated range.
- **Perturbation.** Indicators buffer calcium, and high expression can alter cell health and activity, so the measurement may change the system.
- **Selection.** Only neurons that express the indicator, lie in the plane and rise above the noise are analyzed, and sparse or deep neurons are under-represented.
- **Slow dynamics versus fast.** Because of the slow decay, a delay between a stimulus and a rise in fluorescence is mostly indicator kinetics, not neural latency.

Voltage indicators image the membrane potential directly and are faster, but they have lower signal-to-noise and demand very fast imaging, so the trade-off between speed, sensitivity and number of cells remains central.

### Open questions

How accurately spikes can be inferred from fluorescence in a given preparation depends on simultaneous electrical ground truth, which exists for only a small number of cells. A major open issue is therefore the generalization of calibration from a few cells to a large population in a behaving animal.

title: Recovering events by hand
problem: A calcium trace sampled at regular intervals has $\gamma = 0.8$ and values $c = [0,\ 2.0,\ 1.6,\ 1.28,\ 3.024]$. Estimate the event sizes $s_t = c_t - \gamma c_{t-1}$, with $c_{-1} = 0$ and negative values clipped to zero.
step: Start: $s_0 = 0 - 0.8 \times 0 = 0$.
step: At $t = 1$: $s_1 = 2.0 - 0.8 \times 0 = 2.0$, a jump of 2.0 units.
step: At $t = 2$ and $t = 3$: $s_2 = 1.6 - 0.8 \times 2.0 = 0$ and $s_3 = 1.28 - 0.8 \times 1.6 = 0$, so the trace is decaying exactly at the indicator rate and there are no new events.
step: At $t = 4$: $s_4 = 3.024 - 0.8 \times 1.28 = 3.024 - 1.024 = 2.0$, a second event of the same size.
answer: $s = [0,\ 2.0,\ 0,\ 0,\ 2.0]$: two events of size 2.0.

@mistakes
- Reading the delay between stimulus onset and the rise of fluorescence as neural latency, when indicator kinetics and frame rate account for most of it.
- Treating $\Delta F/F$ amplitude as a spike count, which is valid only in a calibrated, roughly linear range.
- Comparing $\Delta F/F$ between cells expressing different amounts of indicator without considering that nonlinearity and baseline differ.
- Forgetting that deconvolution amplifies noise, so small positive events may be noise.

model:
```python
def deconvolve(c, gamma):
    """Non-negative event estimates from a first-order calcium trace."""
    events = []
    previous = 0.0
    for value in c:
        events.append(max(0.0, value - gamma * previous))
        previous = value
    return events
```
hint: Keep the previous value of the trace, starting at zero, and clip each difference at zero.
solution: The model $c_t = \gamma c_{t-1} + s_t$ is inverted sample by sample. Using the previous raw value (not the previous event) is the point, and the clip at zero removes negative values that come from noise or an over-estimated decay. The first sample is compared with zero, and an empty trace gives an empty list.

```python
def delta_f_over_f(f, n_base):
    """Normalized fluorescence relative to the mean of the first n_base samples."""
    f0 = sum(f[:n_base]) / n_base
    return [(x - f0) / f0 for x in f]
```
