@article
Every claim about neural activity rests on a measurement, and each measurement has a window through which some activity is visible and the rest is not. This lesson builds the habit of asking, before reading any result, what the recording could resolve.

### Three windows

**Intracellular recording** (sharp electrodes, and the patch clamp of Neher and Sakmann, 1976) measures the membrane potential of one neuron: subthreshold inputs, spikes and currents. It resolves synaptic events, but a single neuron at a time is usually accessible, and the neuron is not always behaving as it would unperturbed.

**Extracellular single-unit recording** places an electrode near neurons and records the voltage outside them. A spike appears as a brief, mostly negative deflection lasting about a millisecond. Spikes from one neuron look alike, spikes from different neurons differ in shape and amplitude, and **spike sorting** assigns detected events to putative neurons. This is the workhorse for behavior-related activity, but it sees spikes only and is biased toward neurons that are large, close to the electrode and active.

**Local field potentials** (LFP) are the low-frequency part of the extracellular voltage and reflect summed synaptic and other currents from a population around the electrode. They resolve rhythms and population input but not single-cell spiking, and they are shaped by volume conduction.

### Dense probes

Silicon probes carry many recording sites along a shank. Jun and colleagues (2017) described a probe with hundreds of sites that records from many neurons along a column of tissue at once. Dense sites let the same spike be seen on neighboring sites, which improves sorting and localizes the source. Counts have grown rapidly, but the principle is unchanged: more channels increase the number of neurons sampled, not what any recording can see about their inputs or molecular identity.

### From voltage to spikes

**Sampling.** To represent content up to a frequency $f_{\max}$ without aliasing, sample at more than $2f_{\max}$ (the Nyquist criterion). Spike waveforms contain energy up to several kilohertz, which is why extracellular recordings are typically sampled at tens of kilohertz.

**Threshold detection.** A common procedure estimates the noise level robustly from the median of the absolute voltage, $\hat\sigma = \text{median}(|x|)/0.6745$, which is insensitive to spikes because they are rare, and flags a negative-going crossing of $-k\hat\sigma$ with $k$ typically between 4 and 5. A **dead time** after each detection prevents one spike from being counted twice.

Detection is a signal detection problem: a low threshold misses fewer spikes but admits more noise events, a high threshold the reverse, and small neurons fall below any threshold that excludes noise.

**Sorting and its failures.** Two neurons with similar waveforms can be merged, one neuron whose waveform drifts or changes with bursting can be split, and spikes that overlap in time can be missed. A common check uses the refractory period: spikes from one neuron cannot occur closer than about 1 to 2 ms, so a unit with many interspike intervals shorter than that is contaminated by other neurons.

### What a recording can resolve

Suppose a probe yields $n$ well-isolated units from a layer, and a fraction $p$ of the layer's neurons belongs to a subpopulation of interest. The number of subpopulation members among the sample is approximately binomial. The probability of seeing none is $(1-p)^n$. A subpopulation of 2% among 200 units has an expected count of 4 and a probability of none of $0.98^{200} \approx 0.018$. The sample would also give a noisy estimate of the fraction itself, because the units recorded are not a random sample: large, active neurons are over-represented, and some cell classes (small, sparsely active) may be nearly invisible.

### Open questions

How completely spike sorting recovers the neurons present is hard to know without ground truth, such as simultaneous intracellular and extracellular recording of the same cell. Whether sorted populations differ systematically from the full population in ways that bias conclusions about coding is a continuing methodological concern, so papers should report isolation quality and how units were selected.

@example
title: A threshold from the noise
problem: A recording has a median absolute voltage of 9.0 µV. Using $\hat\sigma = \text{median}(|x|)/0.6745$ and a threshold of $-4.5\hat\sigma$, what voltage must a negative-going spike reach to be detected? Why use the median rather than the standard deviation?
step: Estimate the noise: $\hat\sigma = 9.0/0.6745 \approx 13.34$ µV.
step: Multiply by the factor: $4.5 \times 13.34 \approx 60.0$ µV, so the threshold is $-60.0$ µV.
step: Why the median: spikes are large and sparse, so they inflate the standard deviation much more than the median of the absolute values; the median reflects the background noise that the threshold must clear.
answer: About $-60.0$ µV; the median is robust to the spikes themselves.

@mistakes
- Treating a sorted unit as one neuron: clusters can merge neurons or split one, and the refractory-period check only detects contamination, not splitting.
- Reading absence of a cell class in recordings as absence in the tissue: small or sparsely firing neurons are under-sampled.
- Estimating the noise with the standard deviation of a trace full of spikes, which inflates the threshold and misses small spikes.
- Treating the LFP as a sum of spikes: it reflects synaptic and other currents and is shaped by the geometry of the tissue.

model:
```python
import statistics


def detect_spikes(trace, k, dead):
    """Indices of negative-going threshold crossings with a dead time."""
    sigma = statistics.median(abs(x) for x in trace) / 0.6745
    threshold = -k * sigma
    spikes = []
    for i in range(1, len(trace)):
        if trace[i] <= threshold and trace[i - 1] > threshold:
            if not spikes or i >= spikes[-1] + dead:
                spikes.append(i)
    return spikes
```
hint: Loop from index 1, test the crossing with the previous sample, and compare the new index with the last accepted index plus the dead time.
solution: The robust noise estimate avoids inflation by spikes. A crossing requires the previous sample to be above threshold, so a spike that stays below threshold for several samples is counted once, and the dead time suppresses a second crossing shortly after a detection. In the third test a dead time of 3 rejects the crossing two samples later, while a dead time of 2 accepts it; the last test shows that a spike at index 0 has no previous sample and is never detected.

solution: With $p = 0.03$ and $n = 150$ units, the expected number of subpopulation members is $np = 4.5$. The probability of recording none is $0.97^{150} \approx 0.010$, and the probability of fewer than 2 members is $0.97^{150} + 150(0.03)(0.97^{149}) \approx 0.010 + 0.047 = 0.057$, so a single session will usually include a few members but the count will fluctuate widely (standard deviation about $\sqrt{np(1-p)} \approx 2.1$), too few for a precise estimate of their response properties. Two reasons the numbers could be wrong: extracellular sampling is biased toward large, active neurons, so if the subpopulation is small or sparsely active the true expected count is lower and the 3% estimate itself comes from a biased method; and sorting errors could merge members with neighbors or split them, changing counts. model:
```python
def isi_violation_fraction(times_ms, refractory_ms):
    """Fraction of interspike intervals shorter than the refractory period."""
    if len(times_ms) < 2:
        return 0.0
    isis = [b - a for a, b in zip(times_ms, times_ms[1:])]
    return sum(1 for d in isis if d < refractory_ms) / len(isis)
```
hint: Build the list of consecutive differences, count those below the cutoff, and divide by how many intervals there are.
solution: There are one fewer intervals than spikes, so divide by `len(times_ms) - 1`, not by the number of spikes. The fourth test has intervals exactly equal to the cutoff, which are not violations because the comparison is strict.
