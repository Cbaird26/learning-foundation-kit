@article
A spiking network of thousands of neurons is too big to analyze, but what a cortical area computes often depends only on how many spikes each group of neurons produces per second. Replacing spikes by **firing rates** turns the network into a small set of differential equations, and the tools of the phase plane (lesson 4) apply to it directly.

### The rate model

Let $r_i$ be the firing rate of unit $i$ in hertz (Hz) and $\tau$ a time constant in milliseconds (here $\tau = 10$ ms, standing for the time over which a unit's rate follows its input). The **firing-rate model** is

$$\tau \frac{dr_i}{dt} = -r_i + F\Big(\sum_j W_{ij} r_j + h_i\Big), \qquad F(x) = [x]_+ = \max(0, x),$$

with a **weight matrix** $\mathbf{W}$ (dimensionless: Hz of input per Hz of output), an external input $h_i$ expressed in Hz, and the **threshold-linear** transfer function $[x]_+$: zero below threshold and linear above it. The model is a good approximation when synaptic time constants are short compared with $\tau$ and neurons fire asynchronously, so that the population rate is smooth. Wilson and Cowan (1972) introduced equations of this form for excitatory and inhibitory populations.

### One unit

A single unit with self-excitation $w$ and input $h > 0$ has $\tau\,\dot r = -r + [w r + h]_+$. While active, the equation is linear: $\tau\,\dot r = -(1 - w)r + h$, with fixed point $r^* = h/(1 - w)$ and time constant $\tau/(1 - w)$. For $w < 1$ the fixed point is stable and feedback **amplifies** the input by $1/(1 - w)$ and slows the dynamics: with $w = 0.5$ and $h = 10$ Hz the unit settles at 20 Hz with time constant 20 ms. For $w > 1$ the rate grows without bound, because $[x]_+$ has no saturation; real populations are stopped by saturation, adaptation or inhibition.

### Networks and stability

For $N$ units that are all active, a fixed point satisfies $(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{W})\mathbf{r}^* = \mathbf{h}$. Linearizing around it, the Jacobian is $\mathbf{J} = (\mathbf{W} - \mathbf{I})/\tau$, so its eigenvalues are $(\lambda_W - 1)/\tau$ where $\lambda_W$ are the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{W}$. The fixed point is stable when the real part of every $\lambda_W$ is below 1. For a $2\times 2$ matrix the eigenvalues follow from the trace $t$ and determinant $d$ of $\mathbf{W} - \mathbf{I}$: $\lambda = \tfrac12(t \pm \sqrt{t^2 - 4d})$.

Units whose input is negative are silent, which changes the equations. A fixed point is **consistent** if every active unit has $r_i > 0$ satisfying the linear equation, and every silent unit has non-positive input. A network can have several consistent fixed points.

### Competition: winner-take-all

Two units inhibit each other with $\mathbf{W} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -2 \\ -2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and receive inputs $\mathbf{h} = (20, 18)$ Hz. There are three consistent fixed points:

| fixed point | $(r_1, r_2)$ (Hz) | silent unit's input | stable? |
|---|---|---|---|
| both active | (5.33, 7.33) | — | no (eigenvalues $-0.3$, $+0.1$ per ms) |
| unit 1 wins | (20, 0) | $18 - 2\times 20 = -22$ | yes |
| unit 2 wins | (0, 18) | $20 - 2 \times 18 = -16$ | yes |

The coexistence state is a saddle: the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{W}$ are $\pm 2$, giving $(2-1)/10 = +0.1$ and $(-2-1)/10 = -0.3$ per ms. Any difference between the units grows until the stronger one silences the other. Starting from rest, the simulation below reaches $(7.7, 4.6)$ Hz at 10 ms and ends at $(20, 0)$ Hz. This is **winner-take-all** competition, a model of selection in decision and attention circuits; it is a consequence of mutual inhibition stronger than 1, and the unit with the larger input wins.

### In code

Euler steps with $\Delta t = 1$ ms ($\Delta t/\tau = 0.1$) are accurate enough here; every unit must be updated from the old rates:

```python
def simulate_rates(W, h, r0, tau, dt, n_steps):
    """Euler trajectory of tau dr/dt = -r + [W r + h]_+ ; returns all rates."""
    r, out, n = list(r0), [list(r0)], len(r0)
    for _ in range(n_steps):
        drive = [sum(W[i][j] * r[j] for j in range(n)) + h[i] for i in range(n)]
        r = [r[i] + dt / tau * (-r[i] + max(0.0, drive[i])) for i in range(n)]
        out.append(list(r))
    return out
```

### Limits

Rate models average over spike timing, so they miss synchrony and the effect of fluctuations, and the threshold-linear function is a simplification of the smooth input–output curves seen in noisy neurons. They are the language of the next lessons: inhibition-stabilized networks, attractors and working memory.

@example
title: Fixed point and speed of a two-unit network
problem: Two excitatory units have $\mathbf{W} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & 0.2 \\ 0.2 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix}$, inputs $\mathbf{h} = (6, 6)$ Hz and $\tau = 10$ ms. Find the fixed point and the slowest time constant.
step: By symmetry $r_1 = r_2 = r$ and $r = 0.5 r + 0.2 r + 6$, so $r(1 - 0.7) = 6$ and $r^* = 20$ Hz, positive, so both are active.
step: The eigenvalues of $\mathbf{W}$ are $0.5 + 0.2 = 0.7$ (for the pattern $(1, 1)$) and $0.5 - 0.2 = 0.3$ (for $(1, -1)$).
step: The eigenvalues of $\mathbf{J}$ are $(0.7 - 1)/10 = -0.03$ and $(0.3 - 1)/10 = -0.07$ per ms, both negative, so the fixed point is stable.
step: The slowest decay has time constant $1/0.03 = 33.3$ ms, three times the single-unit $\tau$: recurrent excitation slows the common (sum) mode.
answer: The fixed point is $(20, 20)$ Hz, stable, with a slowest time constant of $33.3$ ms.

@mistakes
- Forgetting the rectification when solving $(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{W})\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{h}$: the solution may have a negative rate, which is not a fixed point of the threshold-linear model; silence that unit and solve again.
- Judging stability from the entries of $\mathbf{W}$ rather than its eigenvalues: a matrix with all entries below 1 can still have an eigenvalue above 1 (two units with $W_{12} = W_{21} = 0.8$ and $W_{11} = W_{22} = 0.5$ have $\lambda_W = 1.3$).
- Forgetting that stability needs $\text{Re}(\lambda_W) < 1$, not $< 0$: the $-\mathbf{I}$ comes from the leak term $-r_i$.
- Updating the rates one at a time with the already-updated values: use the old values of all units, as in the code above.

model:
```python
import math


def max_real_eig(W, tau):
    """Largest real part of the eigenvalues of (W - I) / tau for 2 units."""
    a, b = W[0][0] - 1.0, W[0][1]
    c, d = W[1][0], W[1][1] - 1.0
    tr, det = a + d, a * d - b * c
    disc = tr * tr - 4.0 * det
    if disc >= 0:
        return (tr + math.sqrt(disc)) / 2.0 / tau
    return tr / 2.0 / tau
```
hint: Subtract 1 from the diagonal of $\mathbf{W}$ first, then use the trace and determinant of that matrix; the complex case has real part $t/2$.
solution: The eigenvalues of $\mathbf{W} - \mathbf{I}$ are those of $\mathbf{W}$ minus 1, so the model is stable exactly when this number is negative. The complex-eigenvalue cases (a rotation-like $\mathbf{W}$, as in the fourth test) have a real part equal to half the trace, and the first test shows that the symmetric excitatory pair is stable with the slowest mode at $-0.03$ per ms.

```python
def fixed_points_2d(W, h):
    """All consistent fixed points of a two-unit threshold-linear network."""
    eps = 1e-9
    found = []
    for active in [(0, 1), (0,), (1,), ()]:
        r = [0.0, 0.0]
        if active == (0, 1):
            a, b = 1.0 - W[0][0], -W[0][1]
            c, d = -W[1][0], 1.0 - W[1][1]
            det = a * d - b * c
            if abs(det) < 1e-12:
                continue
            r = [(d * h[0] - b * h[1]) / det, (-c * h[0] + a * h[1]) / det]
        elif len(active) == 1:
            i = active[0]
            if W[i][i] == 1:
                continue
            r[i] = h[i] / (1.0 - W[i][i])
        ok = all(r[i] > eps for i in active)
        for i in range(2):
            if i not in active:
                ok = ok and W[i][0] * r[0] + W[i][1] * r[1] + h[i] <= eps
        if ok:
            found.append(r)
    return sorted(found)
```
hint: For each active set, solve the linear equations, then check two conditions: active rates positive, and silent units with non-positive input. Sort the survivors.
solution: The winner-take-all network has three consistent fixed points (the coexistence point and two winners), and the code finds each one by assuming its active set and checking self-consistency. The fifth test has all inputs zero: the all-active solution is the zero vector, which fails the positivity requirement, and the silent solution passes, so the origin is counted once.

```python
def is_fixed_point(W, h, r, tol):
    """True if r satisfies r_i = max(0, sum_j W_ij r_j + h_i) within tol."""
    for i in range(len(r)):
        drive = sum(W[i][j] * r[j] for j in range(len(r))) + h[i]
        if abs(r[i] - max(0.0, drive)) >= tol:
            return False
    return True
```
hint: For each unit compute its drive, rectify it, and compare with the given rate; all units must agree.
solution: Rectifying the drive is what lets a silent unit with negative input count as consistent: in the second test unit 1 has drive $20 - 36 = -16$ and rate 0. The origin fails because unit 1 would be driven to 20 Hz.

@quiz self
prompt: A colleague models a cortical area as an excitatory unit E and an inhibitory unit I with $\mathbf{W} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & -0.8 \\ 0.6 & -0.3 \end{pmatrix}$ (row 1 is the input to E) and finds a stable fixed point. Another colleague says that raising the excitatory self-connection to $W_{EE} = 1.2$ must make the network run away, because self-excitation above 1 means growth without bound. Check this claim with the eigenvalues, say what is right and wrong in it, find the value of $W_{EE}$ at which the fixed point actually loses stability, and say what a real population adds that stops runaway.
hint: Find the trace and the determinant of $\mathbf{W} - \mathbf{I}$ as functions of $W_{EE}$.
solution: With $a = W_{EE} - 1$, the matrix $\mathbf{W} - \mathbf{I} = \begin{pmatrix} a & -0.8 \\ 0.6 & -1.3 \end{pmatrix}$ has trace $a - 1.3$ and determinant $-1.3a + 0.48$. At $W_{EE} = 0.5$ ($a = -0.5$) the trace is $-1.8$ and the determinant $1.13$: the discriminant is negative, so the eigenvalues are complex with real part $-0.9$ (in units of $1/\tau$), a stable spiral. At $W_{EE} = 1.2$ ($a = 0.2$) the trace is $-1.1$ and the determinant $0.22 > 0$, so both eigenvalues have negative real parts ($-0.26$ and $-0.84$): the network is still stable, because the inhibitory unit's feedback is strong enough to hold the excitatory unit down (an inhibition-stabilized regime). The colleague's claim is wrong because the stability of a recurrent network depends on all of $\mathbf{W}$, not on one entry. The determinant becomes negative, giving a real positive eigenvalue and runaway growth, when $-1.3a + 0.48 < 0$, that is $W_{EE} > 1 + 0.48/1.3 = 1.37$. In a real cortex, runaway activity is limited by saturation of firing rates, spike-frequency adaptation and synaptic depression, which a threshold-linear model omits.
