@article
Two neurons that fire regularly at slightly different rates drift in and out of step like two clocks. Couple them with a synapse and the result depends on the sign of the coupling: excitation pulls the spikes together, inhibition pushes them apart. Cortex has thousands of neurons per cubic millimeter, and the first step to understanding it is the smallest network, built from the neuron of lesson 2 and the synapse of lesson 5.

### The network model

Take $N$ integrate-and-fire neurons, $i = 1, \dots, N$, each with membrane time constant $\tau_m = 20$ ms, input resistance $R = 10$ MΩ, rest $E_L = -70$ mV, threshold $V_{\text{th}} = -50$ mV and reset $V_{\text{reset}} = -65$ mV. Each neuron receives an external current $I_i^{\text{ext}}$ (nA) and synaptic input from the others:

$$\tau_m \frac{dV_i}{dt} = -(V_i - E_L) + R\Big(I_i^{\text{ext}} + \sum_j W_{ij}\, s_j\Big), \qquad \tau_s \frac{ds_j}{dt} = -s_j .$$

The **weight matrix** $\mathbf{W}$ has entry $W_{ij}$ (nA) for the connection from neuron $j$ onto neuron $i$, so row $i$ lists the inputs to neuron $i$. Positive entries are excitatory, negative inhibitory. The variable $s_j$ is the synaptic trace of neuron $j$: it jumps by 1 when neuron $j$ spikes and decays with $\tau_s$ (ms). Each spike therefore delivers a pulse of current $W_{ij}\,e^{-t/\tau_s}$ with total charge $W_{ij}\tau_s$ (in pC, for nA and ms). The rheobase of an isolated neuron is $(V_{\text{th}} - E_L)/R = 2$ nA, so a current of $2.5$ nA gives a spike every $20\ln\big(20/5\big) = 27.7$ ms.

### Two rules of thumb

For a fast synapse ($\tau_s \ll \tau_m$) a presynaptic spike gives the membrane a charge $Q = W\tau_s$ on a capacitance $C = \tau_m/R$ (here 2 nF), so the voltage jumps by $\Delta V \approx R\,W\tau_s/\tau_m$. For a presynaptic neuron firing at a steady rate $f$, the trace averages $\bar s = f\tau_s$ (rate in spikes per ms), so the mean synaptic current is $W f \tau_s$ and the mean depolarization is $R W f \tau_s$. If the spikes arrive from many neurons, the means add. Mean-field estimates like these are the starting point for the rate models of the next lessons.

### Simulating the network

Because every neuron reads the synaptic traces from the start of the step, the order of updates matters and must be fixed: compute all voltages from the old traces, record which neurons fired, then decay all traces, then add the jumps. A time step of $\Delta t = 0.1$ ms is far below $\tau_s$.

```python
def simulate_network(W, I_ext, n_steps, dt, tau_s):
    """Spike times (ms) of each neuron in a current-based LIF network."""
    N = len(W)
    tau, R, E_L, V_th, V_reset = 20.0, 10.0, -70.0, -50.0, -65.0
    V, s = [E_L] * N, [0.0] * N
    spikes = [[] for _ in range(N)]
    for k in range(n_steps):
        fired = []
        for i in range(N):
            I = I_ext[i] + sum(W[i][j] * s[j] for j in range(N))
            V[i] += dt / tau * (-(V[i] - E_L) + R * I)
            if V[i] >= V_th:
                fired.append(i)
                V[i] = V_reset
        for j in range(N):
            s[j] += dt * (-s[j] / tau_s)
        for j in fired:
            s[j] += 1.0
            spikes[j].append(round((k + 1) * dt, 6))
    return spikes
```

With $I^{\text{ext}} = (2.5, 2.3)$ nA, $\tau_s = 5$ ms and 300 ms of simulation, a pair of neurons gives (spike times in ms):

| connection | neuron 1, first four spikes | neuron 2, first four spikes |
|---|---|---|
| none | 32.2, 59.9, 87.6, 115.3 | 40.7, 76.5, 112.3, 148.1 |
| mutual excitation $W = 0.6$ nA | 32.2, 57.4, 82.1, 106.3 | 36.3, 65.7, 96.0, 127.7 |

Excitation shortens the intervals of both neurons: neuron 1 fires 11 times in 300 ms instead of 10, neuron 2 nine times instead of eight, and the lag between their first spikes falls from 8.5 ms to 4.1 ms. With mutual inhibition $W = -1.5$ nA and currents $(2.6, 2.5)$ nA both neurons slow to about 8 spikes in 300 ms instead of 11 and 10 uncoupled, and their spikes interleave.

### Measuring activity

A **raster** lists the spike times of each neuron. The **population rate** counts spikes from all neurons in each time bin and divides by the bin width and the number of neurons, giving a rate in hertz per neuron. A **coincidence** count tallies how many spikes of one neuron have a spike of another within a window; it measures synchrony but depends on the rates, since two fast neurons coincide often by chance.

Real cortical synapses have delays of a millisecond or more because of conduction and synaptic transmission. A delay can be added by scheduling each spike's jump a fixed number of steps later. Delays matter for oscillations and synchrony, which we treat in lesson 12.

### Limits

This network uses current-based synapses, so the postsynaptic current does not depend on the membrane potential, and identical neurons with no noise. Conductance-based synapses (lesson 5), heterogeneous neurons and noisy drive change the results quantitatively, and thousands of neurons require specialized simulators. But the small network already shows the main idea: coupling turns separate oscillators into a system with its own timing.

title: Will a presynaptic partner make a silent neuron fire?
problem: A neuron with $I^{\text{ext}} = 1.8$ nA ($R = 10$ MΩ, $E_L = -70$ mV, $V_{\text{th}} = -50$ mV) receives an excitatory synapse with $W = 0.6$ nA and $\tau_s = 5$ ms from a neuron that fires steadily at $36$ Hz. Estimate the mean membrane potential and say whether the neuron fires.
step: The presynaptic rate is $0.036$ per ms, so the mean trace is $\bar s = 0.036 \times 5 = 0.18$.
step: The mean synaptic current is $0.6 \times 0.18 = 0.108$ nA, and the mean total current is $1.8 + 0.108 = 1.908$ nA.
step: The mean steady-state voltage is $V_\infty = -70 + 10 \times 1.908 = -50.92$ mV.
step: This is below the threshold $-50$ mV, so the neuron stays silent on average. Fluctuations around this mean could still cause occasional spikes, but a deterministic simulation gives none.
answer: The mean potential is about $-50.9$ mV, below threshold, so the neuron does not fire in the deterministic model.

@mistakes
- Reading $W_{ij}$ as the connection from $i$ to $j$: in this lesson row $i$ is the input to neuron $i$, and swapping the indices turns a feed-forward chain into a chain running backward.
- Updating the voltages and the traces in one loop over neurons in an order that lets a neuron see a trace jump from the same step: compute all inputs from the old traces first.
- Concluding that excitation always synchronizes: it pulls spike times together in this model, but with slow or strong coupling, or with delays, excitatory networks can also oscillate or burst.
- Counting a spike on a bin edge twice, or dividing a population count by the bin width in milliseconds without converting to seconds: a rate in hertz needs the bin width in seconds.

```python
def population_rate(spike_lists, t_max, bin_ms):
    """Population rate (Hz per neuron) in consecutive bins of width bin_ms."""
    n_bins = int(t_max // bin_ms)
    counts = [0] * n_bins
    for spikes in spike_lists:
        for t in spikes:
            k = int(t // bin_ms)
            if 0 <= k < n_bins:
                counts[k] += 1
    return [c / len(spike_lists) / (bin_ms / 1000.0) for c in counts]
```
hint: Count the spikes of all neurons per bin first, then convert each count to hertz per neuron. Ignore spikes whose bin index is outside the range.
solution: A spike exactly at 10.0 ms belongs to the bin that starts at 10 ms, so edges are counted once. Dividing by the bin width in seconds (`bin_ms / 1000`) gives hertz; dividing by the number of neurons gives a rate per neuron even when one neuron is silent.

```python
def simulate_synapse_delays(W, D, I_ext, n_steps, dt, tau_s):
    """Spike times of a current-based LIF network with per-synapse delays."""
    N = len(W)
    tau, R, E_L, V_th, V_reset = 20.0, 10.0, -70.0, -50.0, -65.0
    V = [E_L] * N
    s = [[0.0] * N for _ in range(N)]
    spikes = [[] for _ in range(N)]
    due = {}
    for k in range(n_steps):
        fired = []
        for i in range(N):
            I = I_ext[i] + sum(W[i][j] * s[i][j] for j in range(N))
            V[i] += dt / tau * (-(V[i] - E_L) + R * I)
            if V[i] >= V_th:
                fired.append(i)
                V[i] = V_reset
        for i in range(N):
            for j in range(N):
                s[i][j] += dt * (-s[i][j] / tau_s)
        for j in fired:
            spikes[j].append((k + 1) * dt)
            for i in range(N):
                due.setdefault(k + D[i][j], []).append((i, j))
        for i, j in due.pop(k, []):
            s[i][j] += 1.0
    return spikes
```
hint: Keep one trace per connection, as a table `s[i][j]`. When neuron `j` fires, schedule a jump for each `(i, j)` pair at step `k + D[i][j]`, and apply the jumps due now at the end of the step.
solution: With a delay of zero everywhere the code reproduces the instantaneous network (first test). Giving each connection its own trace is what lets two targets of the same presynaptic neuron receive its spikes at different times: in the last test neuron 2 hears neuron 0 ten milliseconds later than neuron 1 does, so it fires about 9 ms later in the first cycle. A single trace per presynaptic neuron could not represent this.

```python
def coincidences(spikes_a, spikes_b, window):
    """Number of spikes in spikes_a with a partner in spikes_b within window."""
    return sum(1 for t in spikes_a if any(abs(t - u) <= window for u in spikes_b))
```

solution: The simulation shows what this model does with these parameters, and the result depends on features that were fixed. The synaptic time constant and delay matter: slow inhibition can synchronize neurons by resetting them together after a shared inhibitory pulse, while fast excitation with a delay can produce alternation or bursting. The shape of the neuron's phase response (how a pulse shifts its next spike) decides whether excitation advances or delays the partner, and neurons with adaptation or resonance behave differently from integrate-and-fire neurons. Coupling strength matters, since strong coupling can overwhelm the intrinsic currents. A cortical network also has many neurons, heterogeneity and noise, which blur pairwise results.
