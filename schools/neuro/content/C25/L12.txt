@article
Record the local field potential in visual cortex while an animal views a grating and you often see a rhythm near 40 Hz (gamma) that waxes and wanes. Neurons at different sites tend to fire at consistent phases of the rhythm when they are engaged by the same stimulus. Rhythms and synchrony are among the most measured features of brain activity, and also among the most debated in function. Models let you ask what produces a rhythm, when two rhythms lock, and how to quantify the result.

### Phase oscillators

A regularly firing neuron is described by its **phase** $\theta$ (radians): $\dot\theta = \omega$, with $\omega = 2\pi f$, so a 40 Hz oscillator has $\omega = 2\pi \times 0.04 = 0.2513$ rad/ms. Two oscillators with a symmetric coupling of strength $K$ (rad/ms) obey

$$\dot\theta_1 = \omega_1 + K\sin(\theta_2 - \theta_1), \qquad \dot\theta_2 = \omega_2 + K\sin(\theta_1 - \theta_2).$$

The phase difference $\varphi = \theta_1 - \theta_2$ follows $\dot\varphi = \Delta\omega - 2K\sin\varphi$ with $\Delta\omega = \omega_1 - \omega_2$. A **locked** state has $\dot\varphi = 0$, which requires $|\Delta\omega| \le 2K$, with a stable lag $\varphi^* = \arcsin\big(\Delta\omega/(2K)\big)$ (the root with $\cos\varphi^* > 0$; the other root is unstable). If $|\Delta\omega| > 2K$ the phases **slip**: $\varphi$ keeps growing at the mean rate $\sqrt{\Delta\omega^2 - 4K^2}$.

For $f_1 = 40$ Hz and $f_2 = 38$ Hz, $\Delta\omega = 2\pi \times 0.002 = 0.012566$ rad/ms, so locking needs $K \ge 0.006283$ rad/ms. With $K = 0.01$ the lag is $\varphi^* = \arcsin(0.6283) = 0.679$ rad, about $39°$: the faster oscillator leads. With $K = 0.005$ the pair slips at $\sqrt{0.012566^2 - 0.01^2} = 0.00761$ rad/ms, one slip about every $0.83$ s ($1.21$ Hz). An Euler step of 1 ms is much smaller than the locking time scale (about 60 ms) and is accurate for $\varphi$.

### Many oscillators and measures of synchrony

For $N$ oscillators with all-to-all coupling, the **Kuramoto model** is

$$\dot\theta_i = \omega_i + \frac{K}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sin(\theta_j - \theta_i),$$

and synchrony is measured by the **order parameter** $R = \big|\frac1N\sum_j e^{i\theta_j}\big|$, which is 1 when all phases are equal and near 0 when they are spread evenly around the circle. With a spread of frequencies, $R$ stays low for weak coupling and rises toward 1 as $K$ grows: with $\omega = (0.01, 0.012, 0.008, 0.011)$ rad/ms and starting phases $(0, 1.5, 3.0, 4.5)$, the value after 500 ms is $R = 0.138$ for $K = 0$ and $R = 0.9996$ for $K = 0.05$ rad/ms.

For two recorded signals, such as the phase of a field potential and the phase of spiking, the **phase-locking value** (PLV) is $\big|\frac1n\sum_k e^{i(\phi_k - \psi_k)}\big|$ over samples or trials. It is 1 if the phase difference is constant, and near 0 if it is random. A PLV of 1 means a constant lag, not zero lag, and it says nothing about which signal drives which.

```python
import math


def order_parameter(phases):
    """Kuramoto order parameter R of a list of phases (radians)."""
    n = len(phases)
    x = sum(math.cos(p) for p in phases) / n
    y = sum(math.sin(p) for p in phases) / n
    return math.hypot(x, y)
```

### How a loop makes a rhythm

An excitatory population that excites an inhibitory one, which inhibits it back, has a natural rhythm. In the rate model of lesson 8 the Jacobian has complex eigenvalues $\lambda = \sigma \pm i\omega$ when its discriminant is negative, and the linearized oscillation has frequency $f = \omega/(2\pi)$ with $\omega = \tfrac12\sqrt{4\Delta - \tau^2}$ (trace $\tau$ and determinant $\Delta$). For $w_{EE} = 1.5$, $w_{EI} = 1.2$, $w_{IE} = 2.0$, $w_{II} = 0.5$, the imaginary part is $0.118$ rad/ms for time constants of 10 ms, a frequency of $18.8$ Hz, and it scales as $1/\tau$: with time constants of 5 ms it is $37.7$ Hz, in the gamma range. This interplay of fast excitation and delayed inhibition is the basis of the pyramidal–interneuron gamma (PING) mechanism in spiking models. Whether the oscillation is sustained depends on the real part $\sigma$: negative means damped, and noise can then sustain an irregular rhythm.

### What a rhythm shows

A rhythm and synchrony are correlates, and the claims built on them differ in strength. Models show how a rhythm can arise, so its presence is consistent with a mechanism, not a proof of one. The hypothesis that coherence between areas gates communication (Fries, 2005) is debated, as is whether gamma carries information or is a by-product of E–I interactions. Testing function needs manipulations, for example driving the rhythm at a chosen phase and frequency and measuring behavior, and not only recordings.

@example
title: The locked phase lag of two oscillators
problem: Two oscillators fire at $f_1 = 42$ Hz and $f_2 = 39$ Hz. (a) What is the smallest coupling $K$ (rad/ms) that locks them? (b) For $K = 0.012$ rad/ms, what is the locked lag?
step: Convert the frequency difference: $\Delta\omega = 2\pi \times 3/1000 = 0.018850$ rad/ms.
step: Locking needs $|\Delta\omega| \le 2K$, so $K_{\min} = \Delta\omega/2 = 0.009425$ rad/ms.
step: For $K = 0.012$, $\Delta\omega/(2K) = 0.018850/0.024 = 0.7854$, so $\varphi^* = \arcsin(0.7854) = 0.9033$ rad.
step: In degrees this is $51.8°$, with oscillator 1 (the faster) leading oscillator 2.
answer: (a) $K \ge 0.00942$ rad/ms. (b) $\varphi^* \approx 0.903$ rad ($51.8°$).

@mistakes
- Mixing units: a frequency in hertz must be converted with $\omega = 2\pi f$ and the time in milliseconds to give rad/ms, or $K$ and $\Delta\omega$ differ by a factor 1000.
- Using $\Delta\omega \le K$ for locking: for the symmetric coupling above the condition is $|\Delta\omega| \le 2K$, because both oscillators adjust.
- Reading a PLV of 1 as zero phase lag or as causation: it means only a constant phase difference between the two signals.
- Computing the order parameter by averaging the phases: average the unit vectors $e^{i\theta}$ and take the length of the result, because phases wrap around the circle.

```python
import math


def plv(phases_a, phases_b):
    """Phase-locking value of two lists of phases (radians)."""
    n = len(phases_a)
    c = sum(math.cos(a - b) for a, b in zip(phases_a, phases_b)) / n
    s = sum(math.sin(a - b) for a, b in zip(phases_a, phases_b)) / n
    return math.hypot(c, s)
```
hint: Average the cosines and sines of the phase differences separately, then take the length of the resulting vector.
solution: A constant lag, whatever its size (first test), gives 1; uniformly spread differences cancel to 0 (second and fifth tests); and a single sample is always perfectly "locked". Averaging the angles themselves would fail on wrap-around, which is why the unit vectors are averaged.

model:
```python
import math


def kuramoto_final_R(omegas, K, theta0, dt, n_steps):
    """Order parameter after Euler integration of the Kuramoto model."""
    theta = list(theta0)
    n = len(theta)
    for _ in range(n_steps):
        theta = [theta[i] + dt * (omegas[i] + K / n * sum(
            math.sin(theta[j] - theta[i]) for j in range(n))) for i in range(n)]
    x = sum(math.cos(t) for t in theta) / n
    y = sum(math.sin(t) for t in theta) / n
    return math.hypot(x, y)
```
hint: Build the new list of phases from the old one in a single comprehension, so that no phase sees an updated neighbor within a step.
solution: With no coupling and equal frequencies the phases rotate together, so $R$ stays at its starting value $0.0518$ (first and last tests). With unequal frequencies and no coupling $R$ changes only slowly here, while coupling $K = 0.05$ pulls the phases together and $R$ reaches $0.9996$; when the frequency spread is large relative to $K$ (the last simulation) the oscillators remain mostly unsynchronized, $R = 0.43$.

model:
```python
import math


def oscillation_frequency(wEE, wEI, wIE, wII, tau_E, tau_I):
    """Frequency (Hz) of the linearized E-I oscillation, 0.0 if overdamped."""
    a = (wEE - 1.0) / tau_E
    b = -wEI / tau_E
    c = wIE / tau_I
    d = -(1.0 + wII) / tau_I
    tr = a + d
    det = a * d - b * c
    disc = tr * tr - 4.0 * det
    if disc >= 0:
        return 0.0
    return math.sqrt(-disc) / 2.0 * 1000.0 / (2.0 * math.pi)
```
hint: Build the Jacobian, then its trace and determinant; the frequency exists only when the discriminant is negative.
solution: Halving both time constants doubles the frequency (first two tests), since the Jacobian scales as $1/\tau$. When the inhibitory time constant is much shorter, the discriminant becomes positive and the response is a non-oscillatory decay (fourth test). The fifth network has a positive trace, so its fixed point is unstable and the oscillation grows until nonlinearities limit it; the formula gives the frequency of the linearized motion only.

@quiz self
prompt: A lab finds that gamma-band coherence between two visual areas increases when an animal attends to a stimulus, and proposes that the coherence causes the improved communication between the areas. Explain what the data show and what they do not, offer one alternative explanation, and describe an experiment, with a model-based prediction, that would test the causal claim.
hint: Think of what else changes with attention and how you could manipulate the rhythm directly.
solution: The data show that coherence and attention are associated: when the animal attends, the two areas' gamma phases are more consistent. They do not show that coherence is the cause of better communication, because attention changes many things at once, including firing rates, the strength of inputs and the balance of excitation and inhibition, and an increase in gamma coherence could be a by-product of stronger, more correlated drive. An alternative explanation is that a shared input (for example from a third area or the thalamus) increases both coherence and the response, so the two areas are coherent because they share a driver, without coherence doing any work. A causal test would manipulate the rhythm, for example by driving one area with optogenetic pulses at 40 Hz and varying the phase relative to the ongoing rhythm in the other area, while keeping the mean drive fixed, and measuring the downstream response and the animal's behavior. The coherence-gating model predicts that the response should depend on the phase, with a larger response at the phase that aligns spikes with the excitable part of the receiving area's cycle, and a control that matches the mean drive with random timing should show no such dependence. The model-based prediction can be quantified with the oscillator model of this lesson: a coupled-oscillator pair predicts a locked lag $\varphi^*$ that depends on the frequency mismatch and coupling, so one can test whether the optimal phase matches it.
