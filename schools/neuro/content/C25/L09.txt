@article
Show a monkey a spot of light, hide it, and ask it to look where the light was a second later. During the delay, neurons in prefrontal cortex keep firing at a rate that depends on the remembered location, although nothing in the world drives them. The information is held in the activity of the network itself. How can a network of leaky neurons hold a state for a thousand times longer than any single time constant? The standard model is an **attractor**: a network whose dynamics has stable states that the activity falls into and stays in.

### A bistable memory unit

Take the rate model of lesson 7 with a saturating transfer function, $F(x) = r_{\max}/\big(1 + e^{-(x - \theta)/s}\big)$, with $r_{\max} = 100$ Hz, $\theta = 50$ Hz and $s = 10$ Hz. A population with self-excitation $w$, tonic input $h$ and time constant $\tau = 20$ ms obeys

$$\tau\,\frac{dr}{dt} = -r + F(w\,r + h).$$

Fixed points satisfy $r = F(wr + h)$, and a fixed point is stable when $w\,F'(wr + h) < 1$, with $F'(x) = F(x)\big(1 - F(x)/r_{\max}\big)/s$. For $w = 1$ and $h = -10$ Hz there are three fixed points:

| $r^*$ (Hz) | $w F'$ | status |
|---|---|---|
| 0.254 | 0.025 | stable (low, "off") |
| 67.15 | 2.206 | unstable (threshold) |
| 97.76 | 0.219 | stable (high, "on") |

The unit is **bistable**: starting below the threshold at 67 Hz it falls to the off state, above it rises to the on state. A transient extra input of $+40$ Hz lasting 50 ms (with Euler steps of 1 ms from rest) pushes the rate across the threshold and leaves it at 97.76 Hz after the input ends, whereas $+30$ Hz for the same time peaks at only 7.4 Hz and decays. A memory is a state that persists without input, and an input of the opposite sign (here a strong negative pulse) can erase it. Persistence needs recurrent excitation strong enough to create the high state: for $w = 0.6$ and $h = 0$ the unit has a single fixed point near 0.7 Hz, and no pulse is remembered.

### The Hopfield network

To store many patterns, consider $N$ binary units $s_i \in \{-1, +1\}$ and $P$ stored patterns $\xi^\mu$. The **Hebbian rule** (Hebb, 1949) sets the weights, as in Hopfield's 1982 model,

$$W_{ij} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P} \xi_i^\mu \xi_j^\mu \quad (i \ne j), \qquad W_{ii} = 0 .$$

Units are updated one at a time, in turn: $s_i \leftarrow \text{sign}\big(\sum_j W_{ij} s_j\big)$, keeping $s_i$ unchanged if the sum, the **local field**, is exactly zero. A full pass over all units is a **sweep**. The **overlap** with pattern $\mu$ is $m^\mu = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \xi_i^\mu s_i$, equal to 1 for a perfect match and 0 for an unrelated state. The **energy** $E = -\tfrac12 \sum_{i,j} W_{ij} s_i s_j$ never increases under symmetric asynchronous updates, so the state slides downhill into a minimum, usually a stored pattern: this is **pattern completion**.

With $N = 8$ and two orthogonal patterns $\xi^1 = (1, 1, 1, 1, -1, -1, -1, -1)$ and $\xi^2 = (1, -1, 1, -1, 1, -1, 1, -1)$, every weight is $0$ or $\pm 0.25$, and each stored pattern has energy $-3.0$. Corrupt $\xi^1$ in two places (units 1 and 5) to get the cue $(1, -1, 1, 1, -1, 1, -1, -1)$, with energy $0$ and overlap $m^1 = 0.5$. One sweep restores $\xi^1$ exactly.

```python
def energy(W, s):
    """Hopfield energy -1/2 sum_ij W_ij s_i s_j."""
    n = len(s)
    return -0.5 * sum(W[i][j] * s[i] * s[j] for i in range(n) for j in range(n))
```

### Capacity and spurious states

A Hopfield network with $N$ units retrieves random patterns reliably only up to about $P \approx 0.138\,N$ (Amit, Gutfreund and Sompolinsky, 1985). Beyond that, retrieval degrades and the network falls into **spurious states** such as mixtures of stored patterns. Correlated patterns reduce capacity further, and learning rules other than Hebb's (such as the covariance rule or the pseudo-inverse rule) raise it.

### Continuous attractors

Some memories are continuous, such as a head direction or a remembered location on a circle. In a **ring attractor**, units are arranged by preferred angle with local excitation and broader inhibition, so the network has a continuous family of stable bump states, one for each position of the bump on the ring. Input moves the bump, and when it is gone the bump stays. This also reveals a weakness: with noise or imperfect connectivity the bump **drifts** along the ring, which is one explanation for the gradual loss of precision in spatial working memory.

### Limits

Discrete attractor models predict that delay activity is stable and all-or-none, whereas many recorded neurons show time-varying, heterogeneous delay activity, and some memories may be held in synaptic changes without persistent firing. The attractor model is one hypothesis, not a settled account of working memory.

@example
title: Correcting one error in the Hopfield network
problem: Using the two-pattern network of the article ($N = 8$), the cue is $(1, -1, 1, 1, -1, 1, -1, -1)$. Compute the local field of unit 1 (counting from 0) and say what the update does.
step: Row 1 of the weights has entries $W_{1,3} = \tfrac18(1\cdot 1 + (-1)(-1)) = 0.25$, $W_{1,4} = \tfrac18((1)(-1) + (-1)(1)) = -0.25$, $W_{1,6} = \tfrac18((1)(-1) + (-1)(1)) = -0.25$, and zeros elsewhere.
step: The local field is $0.25\,s_3 - 0.25\,s_4 - 0.25\,s_6 = 0.25(1) - 0.25(-1) - 0.25(-1) = 0.75$.
step: The field is positive, so $s_1$ flips from $-1$ to $+1$, which is the value in $\xi^1$. The energy falls from $0$ to $-1.5$.
step: Continuing the sweep corrects unit 5 in the same way, giving $\xi^1$ with energy $-3.0$.
answer: The local field is $+0.75$, so unit 1 flips to $+1$, matching the stored pattern.

@mistakes
- Updating all units at once from the old state (synchronous update): this can oscillate between two states; the energy argument needs one unit at a time.
- Leaving a nonzero self-connection $W_{ii}$: it favors keeping the current state and distorts the energy and the basins of attraction.
- Counting the overlap as the number of matches instead of $(\text{matches} - \text{mismatches})/N$: the overlap runs from $-1$ to $+1$, and $-1$ is the mirror image, which is also a stored memory.
- Believing that a stable high state proves a memory mechanism: a bistable unit also needs a threshold input to switch it on and a reset to switch it off, and these determine what is remembered.

model:
```python
def hebb_weights(patterns):
    """Hebbian weight matrix (1/N) sum_mu xi_i xi_j with a zero diagonal."""
    n = len(patterns[0])
    return [[0.0 if i == j else sum(p[i] * p[j] for p in patterns) / n
             for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)]
```
hint: For each pair of units, sum the product of their values over the patterns and divide by $N$; put zero on the diagonal.
solution: The matrix is symmetric because the product $\xi_i\xi_j$ is. Dividing by the number of units $N$ (not by the number of patterns) is the Hopfield convention, which keeps the local field of a stored pattern close to the pattern itself. The diagonal is set to zero explicitly.

model:
```python
def recall(W, s, n_sweeps):
    """Asynchronous Hopfield dynamics; returns the final state."""
    s = list(s)
    n = len(s)
    for _ in range(n_sweeps):
        for i in range(n):
            field = sum(W[i][j] * s[j] for j in range(n))
            if field > 0:
                s[i] = 1
            elif field < 0:
                s[i] = -1
    return s
```
hint: Copy the state first. Inside a sweep, update each unit in place so that later units see the new values, and leave units with zero field alone.
solution: The copy protects the caller's list, which the second test checks. Updating in place makes the dynamics asynchronous, so the energy never increases; the zero-field rule keeps a unit unchanged, as the last test shows with an all-zero weight matrix.

model:
```python
import math


def memory_final(w, h, amp, pulse_steps, n_steps, dt, tau):
    """Final rate (Hz) of a bistable unit after a brief input pulse."""
    r = 0.0
    for k in range(n_steps):
        u = amp if k < pulse_steps else 0.0
        x = w * r + h + u
        r += dt / tau * (-r + 100.0 / (1.0 + math.exp(-(x - 50.0) / 10.0)))
    return r
```
hint: Add the pulse to the input only for the first `pulse_steps` steps, and compute the input from the old rate before updating.
solution: With $w = 1$ and $h = -10$ the unit is bistable: a $40$ Hz pulse for 50 ms crosses the unstable threshold near 67 Hz and ends in the high state, while a $30$ Hz pulse or a $100$ Hz pulse of only 20 ms does not. With $w = 0.6$ there is only one fixed point, so even a strong pulse is forgotten, which shows that persistence needs strong recurrent excitation.

@quiz self
prompt: A theorist proposes that a bistable population of prefrontal neurons holds one bit of information through a one-second delay, and that a ring of such populations holds a remembered angle. An experimentalist reports that the recorded neurons' rates during the delay drift upward and downward over time, and that the remembered angle becomes less precise as the delay gets longer. Explain how the ring-attractor model accounts for the loss of precision, what the model predicts about the distribution of errors, and what the drifting rates might mean for the claim that the memory is held by a stable attractor.
hint: Consider what happens to a bump on a ring when noise or heterogeneity acts along the direction in which the bump can move without any restoring force.
solution: In a continuous ring attractor, the bump can sit at any angle with the same energy, so there is no restoring force along the ring. Noise, and imperfections in the connectivity, push the bump along the ring, so its position undergoes a random walk (drift) whose variance grows with the delay, and the remembered angle becomes less precise as the delay lengthens. The model predicts that the error distribution is centered on the true angle with a spread that grows roughly with the square root of the delay for noise-driven diffusion, plus systematic drift toward a few preferred angles when the connectivity has imperfections. The drifting rates of single neurons are compatible with this: a moving bump changes the rate of each neuron as it passes its preferred angle, and the rate of any one unit need not be constant even when the population state is a stable bump. But strong time-varying activity that carries the information in its dynamics, rather than a stable bump, would count against a fixed-point attractor and point to dynamic coding or to memory held partly in short-term synaptic changes. To separate these, decode the angle from the population and ask whether it drifts diffusively while the bump's shape is preserved.
