@article
A neuron has a few bits per spike to work with, and the world presents far more variation than that. How should a neuron use its limited response range? Barlow (1961) proposed that sensory neurons should code in a way that makes the most of their capacity, removing redundancy. **Information theory**, developed by Shannon, turns "making the most of capacity" into a quantity you can compute.

### Entropy and mutual information

For a discrete variable $X$ with probabilities $p_x$, the **entropy** in bits is

$$H(X) = -\sum_x p_x \log_2 p_x ,$$

with $0\log_2 0 = 0$. It is the average number of yes-or-no questions needed to identify $X$; it is largest, $\log_2 K$ for $K$ outcomes, when all outcomes are equally likely, and zero when one outcome is certain. A fair coin has $H = 1$ bit, and the distribution $(0.5, 0.3, 0.2)$ has $H = 1.485$ bits.

The **mutual information** between a stimulus $S$ and a response $R$ is how much knowing $R$ reduces the uncertainty about $S$ (and vice versa):

$$I(S;R) = \sum_{s,r} p(s,r)\,\log_2\frac{p(s,r)}{p(s)\,p(r)} = H(R) - H(R \mid S), \qquad H(R \mid S) = \sum_s p(s)\,H(R \mid S = s).$$

It is zero when the response is independent of the stimulus and cannot exceed $H(S)$ or $H(R)$. Consider two equally likely stimuli and a spike count with values 0, 1, 2, with counts of trials

| | $R = 0$ | $R = 1$ | $R = 2$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| stimulus A | 40 | 8 | 2 |
| stimulus B | 5 | 15 | 30 |

The response distribution is $(0.45, 0.23, 0.32)$ with $H(R) = 1.532$ bits. Given A the response distribution is $(0.8, 0.16, 0.04)$ with entropy $0.866$ bits, and given B it is $(0.1, 0.3, 0.6)$ with $1.295$ bits, so $H(R\mid S) = 1.081$ bits and $I(S;R) = 1.532 - 1.081 = 0.451$ bits, less than the 1 bit needed to tell the two stimuli apart perfectly.

```python
import math


def entropy(probs):
    """Entropy in bits of a list of probabilities."""
    return -sum(p * math.log2(p) for p in probs if p > 0)
```

### Estimation bias and Gaussian channels

Estimating $I$ from a finite number of trials by plugging in the observed frequencies is **biased upward**: with few trials the table looks more structured than the truth, and shuffling the stimulus labels of a limited data set gives a positive information. The bias grows with the number of response bins and shrinks with the number of trials, and must be corrected or at least checked.

For a continuous response corrupted by Gaussian noise, a signal with variance $\sigma_s^2$ and noise variance $\sigma_n^2$ carries, per independent sample, at most

$$I = \tfrac12\log_2\Big(1 + \frac{\sigma_s^2}{\sigma_n^2}\Big) \text{ bits},$$

when the signal is Gaussian. A signal-to-noise ratio of 4 gives $\tfrac12\log_2 5 = 1.161$ bits per sample, and doubling the number of independent samples doubles the information, while doubling the SNR from 4 to 8 adds only $\tfrac12\log_2(9/5) = 0.42$ bits.

### Efficient coding

Suppose a neuron has $L$ distinguishable response levels and a stimulus (say, contrast) with distribution $p(c)$. The output entropy is largest when all levels are used equally often. The rule is to place the thresholds at the **quantiles** of the stimulus distribution, which is the same as making the response function the cumulative distribution of the stimulus. Laughlin (1981) showed that the response of fly visual neurons to contrast closely follows the cumulative distribution of natural contrasts. The prediction is that the neuron's sensitivity is highest for the most common stimuli.

Take 12 skewed contrast values $0.1, 0.15, 0.2, 0.3, 0.35, 0.5, 0.9, 1.4, 2.0, 2.8, 3.5, 4.0$ and three response levels. Thresholds at the quantiles $0.35$ and $2.0$ send four samples to each level, so the output entropy is $\log_2 3 = 1.585$ bits. Thresholds spaced evenly over the range $0$ to $4$ (at $1.33$ and $2.67$) send $7, 2, 3$ samples to the three levels, an entropy of only $1.384$ bits. Efficient coding extends to populations: sparse coding (Olshausen and Field, 1996) finds basis functions for natural images that give a code with few active units, resembling the receptive fields of simple cells.

### Limits

Efficient coding assumes that the goal is to transmit as much information as possible about the stimulus and that noise and cost are as modeled. Real systems may prioritize information that is relevant for behavior rather than all information, and metabolic cost, not channel capacity, may be the binding constraint. Information measures how much, not what is represented or whether it is used.

title: Mutual information from a count table
problem: In 100 trials a neuron responds with 0 or 1 spikes to two equally likely stimuli, with counts: stimulus A has 8 trials with 0 spikes and 42 with 1 spike; stimulus B has 38 trials with 0 spikes and 12 with 1 spike. Find $I(S;R)$ in bits.
step: The response probabilities are $P(R=0) = 46/100 = 0.46$ and $P(R=1) = 0.54$, so $H(R) = -0.46\log_2 0.46 - 0.54\log_2 0.54 = 0.5153 + 0.4800 = 0.9954$ bits.
step: Given A, $P = (0.16, 0.84)$, so $H = 0.16 \times 2.644 + 0.84 \times 0.2515 = 0.4230 + 0.2113 = 0.6343$ bits. Given B, $P = (0.76, 0.24)$, so $H = 0.76 \times 0.3959 + 0.24 \times 2.059 = 0.3009 + 0.4941 = 0.7950$ bits.
step: Average over the stimuli: $H(R\mid S) = 0.5(0.6343 + 0.7950) = 0.7147$ bits.
step: Subtract: $I(S;R) = 0.9954 - 0.7147 = 0.281$ bits.
answer: About $0.28$ bits.

@mistakes
- Using natural logarithms and calling the result bits: with $\ln$ the unit is nats, and 1 nat $= 1.443$ bits.
- Forgetting the $p = 0$ cells: $0\log_2 0$ is defined as 0, but code that calls `log2(0)` raises an error.
- Taking a larger information as proof of a better code for the animal: information about the stimulus matters only if downstream circuits can use it.
- Trusting the plug-in estimate from few trials: it is biased upward, so check by shuffling labels or by estimating on subsets of the data.

model:
```python
import math


def mutual_information(joint):
    """Mutual information (bits) of a table of counts (rows: stimulus)."""
    total = float(sum(sum(row) for row in joint))
    p_s = [sum(row) / total for row in joint]
    p_r = [sum(row[j] for row in joint) / total for j in range(len(joint[0]))]
    info = 0.0
    for i, row in enumerate(joint):
        for j, count in enumerate(row):
            if count > 0:
                p = count / total
                info += p * math.log2(p / (p_s[i] * p_r[j]))
    return info
```
hint: Compute the row and column marginals first, then add the contribution of each non-empty cell.
solution: Independent variables (the second test) give zero, perfectly correlated binary variables give one bit (the first), and the empty cells in the fifth test must be skipped so that `log2(0)` is never evaluated. The information cannot exceed the entropy of either marginal.

model:
```python
def equalized_levels(samples, n_levels):
    """Response levels from quantile thresholds of the samples."""
    ordered = sorted(samples)
    n = len(ordered)
    thresholds = [ordered[(k * n) // n_levels] for k in range(1, n_levels)]
    return [sum(1 for t in thresholds if t <= x) for x in samples]
```
hint: Build the thresholds from the sorted samples first; then count, for each sample, how many thresholds do not exceed it.
solution: The thresholds sit at the empirical quantiles, so with 12 samples and 3 levels each level gets 4 samples and the output entropy is $\log_2 3$. The single-sample test shows that with $n = 1$ every threshold equals the one sample, so it reaches the top level; the last test checks that the original order is kept.

model:
```python
import math


def conditional_entropy(joint):
    """H(R | S) in bits for a table of counts with rows = stimuli."""
    total = float(sum(sum(row) for row in joint))
    result = 0.0
    for row in joint:
        row_total = sum(row)
        if row_total == 0:
            continue
        h = -sum(c / row_total * math.log2(c / row_total) for c in row if c > 0)
        result += row_total / total * h
    return result
```
hint: For each non-empty row compute the entropy of its normalized counts and weight it by the row's share of the trials.
solution: When the response is determined by the stimulus (first test) the conditional entropy is zero; when it is independent of the stimulus (fourth test) it equals $H(R) = 1$ bit. Together with $H(R)$ it gives $I = H(R) - H(R\mid S)$. The empty first row of the last test must be skipped.

@quiz self
prompt: A student claims that because the efficient-coding principle predicts a response function equal to the cumulative distribution of the stimulus, any neuron whose tuning curve does not follow the stimulus statistics is poorly designed. Evaluate the claim: state what the principle assumes about the neuron's goal and constraints, give two reasons a real neuron might deviate from it, and describe an experiment that would test the principle in a sensory system.
hint: Consider noise, cost and what the information is for.
solution: The principle assumes that the goal is to maximize the information the output carries about the stimulus, with a fixed number of distinguishable response levels, noise at the output only, and a stimulus distribution that the neuron has adapted to. Real neurons may deviate for good reasons. Their goal may be to carry information that is useful for behavior (for example, to discriminate near a decision boundary) rather than the most information about all stimuli; energy cost may favor sparse, low-rate responses over equal use of all levels; noise may also be present in the input and depend on the stimulus; and the neuron may be adapting to a different stimulus ensemble than the one the experimenter used. An experiment would measure the distribution of a natural stimulus feature (such as contrast in natural scenes), measure the neuron's input–output function with the same feature, and compare it with the cumulative distribution; a stronger test changes the stimulus statistics and checks that the response function adapts to the new distribution within a predicted time. Agreement supports the principle for that neuron under those conditions, and disagreement points to a different objective or a constraint, not necessarily to a poorly designed neuron.
