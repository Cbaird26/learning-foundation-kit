@article
Present the same oriented bar to a neuron in visual cortex twenty times. The counts of spikes in a half second might be 3, 5, 4, 6, 2 and so on: not the same twice. Yet an animal can judge the orientation of the bar to within a degree or two. How can reliable behavior come from unreliable neurons? The answer has two parts, the **variability** of single neurons and the **population** of many of them, and both can be modeled with a few lines of arithmetic.

### Tuning curves and Poisson counts

The **tuning curve** $f_i(\theta)$ is the mean firing rate of neuron $i$ (Hz) as a function of a stimulus value $\theta$. A common model is a Gaussian bump on a baseline,

$$f_i(\theta) = r_0 + r_{\max}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{(\theta - \theta_i)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big),$$

where $\theta_i$ is the preferred stimulus. The simplest model of variability says the spike count $n_i$ in a window of $T$ seconds is a **Poisson** variable with mean $T f_i(\theta)$: $P(n_i \mid \theta) = e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{n_i}/n_i!$ with $\lambda = T f_i(\theta)$, and the neurons are independent given $\theta$. For a Poisson count the variance equals the mean, so the **Fano factor** (variance divided by mean) is 1. Recorded cortical neurons often have Fano factors near 1, sometimes above; values clearly below 1 appear with very regular firing. For counts $3, 5, 4, 6, 2$ the mean is 4, the sample variance is 2.5 and the Fano factor is $0.625$.

### Decoding

**Decoding** asks the inverse question: given the counts, what was the stimulus? Two standard decoders:

- The **population vector** (used for arm direction in motor cortex by Georgopoulos and colleagues in the 1980s) adds up each neuron's preferred direction vector weighted by its count: $\hat\theta = \text{atan2}\big(\sum_i n_i \sin\theta_i,\ \sum_i n_i\cos\theta_i\big)$. It is simple and works well for broad, evenly spaced cosine tuning.
- **Maximum likelihood** picks the $\theta$ that makes the observed counts most probable. For independent Poisson neurons the log-likelihood is $\ln P(\mathbf{n}\mid\theta) = \sum_i \big[n_i \ln f_i(\theta) - T f_i(\theta)\big] + \text{const}$, and the decoder is the $\theta$ on a grid that maximizes it.

### How much does a population know? Fisher information

The **Fisher information** measures how sharply the likelihood depends on $\theta$. For independent Poisson neurons,

$$I_F(\theta) = T\sum_i \frac{f_i'(\theta)^2}{f_i(\theta)}, \qquad f_i'(\theta) = -\frac{r_{\max}(\theta - \theta_i)}{\sigma^2}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{(\theta - \theta_i)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big).$$

The **Cramér–Rao bound** says that any unbiased estimate has variance at least $1/I_F$, so the best achievable standard deviation is $1/\sqrt{I_F}$. Two consequences follow. First, **information is in the slope, not the peak**: a neuron at its peak has $f' = 0$ and carries no Fisher information about small changes, and the most informative neurons are those whose tuning curves are steepest at the stimulus, relative to $\sqrt{f}$. Second, information from independent neurons adds.

Take five neurons with $r_0 = 5$ Hz, $r_{\max} = 40$ Hz, $\sigma = 30°$, preferred values $-60, -30, 0, 30, 60$ degrees, and $T = 0.5$ s. The expected counts for a stimulus at $10°$ are $3.8, 10.7, 21.4, 18.5, 7.5$.

| stimulus $\theta$ | $I_F$ (deg$^{-2}$) | best SD $1/\sqrt{I_F}$ (deg) |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0.0349 | 5.36 |
| 10 | 0.0344 | 5.40 |
| 45 | 0.0219 | 6.76 |

The population is most informative in the middle of its range and less near its edge.

```python
import math


def poisson_loglik(counts, rates, T):
    """Log-likelihood (up to a constant) of counts for rates in Hz, window T s."""
    return sum(n * math.log(r) - T * r for n, r in zip(counts, rates))
```

### Timing and correlations

A **rate code** carries information in the count in a window. A **temporal code** carries it in precise spike times: the latency of the first spike, the phase relative to an oscillation, or the order in which neurons fire. Both exist: retinal and auditory neurons fire with millisecond precision, and the question is which features of the spike train the brain downstream actually uses. Noise that is shared between neurons (noise correlations) changes the Fisher information, and can lower or raise it depending on its structure; independent Poisson neurons are a baseline, not a fact. Information theory (next lesson) gives a model-free way to ask how much a response says about a stimulus.

@example
title: Fisher information of a single neuron
problem: A neuron has $r_0 = 5$ Hz, $r_{\max} = 40$ Hz, $\sigma = 30°$ and preferred stimulus $0°$. For a stimulus at $30°$ and a window $T = 0.5$ s, find its Fisher information and the best standard deviation of a decoder using only this neuron.
step: At $30°$ the exponent is $-(30)^2/(2 \times 30^2) = -0.5$, so $e^{-0.5} = 0.6065$.
step: The rate is $f = 5 + 40 \times 0.6065 = 29.26$ Hz. The slope is $f' = -40 \times 30/900 \times 0.6065 = -0.8087$ Hz per degree.
step: $I_F = T f'^2/f = 0.5 \times 0.654/29.26 = 0.01118$ deg$^{-2}$.
step: The best standard deviation is $1/\sqrt{0.01118} = 9.46°$. At the neuron's peak ($0°$) the slope is zero, so $I_F = 0$ there.
answer: $I_F \approx 0.0112$ deg$^{-2}$, a best SD of about $9.5°$; at the peak the information is zero.

@example
title: A population vector
problem: Four neurons have preferred directions $0°, 90°, 180°, 270°$ and spike counts $10, 2, 1, 6$. What direction does the population vector decode?
step: The horizontal component is $\sum n_i\cos\theta_i = 10(1) + 2(0) + 1(-1) + 6(0) = 9$.
step: The vertical component is $\sum n_i\sin\theta_i = 10(0) + 2(1) + 1(0) + 6(-1) = -4$.
step: The angle is $\text{atan2}(-4, 9) = -23.96°$, which is $336.04°$ on a $0°$ to $360°$ scale.
answer: About $336°$.

@mistakes
- Treating the peak of the tuning curve as the most informative point: a neuron is most informative where its tuning curve is steep, and at the peak the slope is zero.
- Computing the Fano factor from the count rate or from a single trial: it needs the variance and mean of counts over repeated trials of the same stimulus and window.
- Adding the Fisher information of neurons whose noise is correlated: independence is an assumption, and shared noise can change the total.
- Decoding with the population vector when the preferred directions are not evenly spread: the sum is then biased toward the directions that are over-represented.

model:
```python
import math


def fisher_info(prefs, theta, T, r0, r_max, sigma):
    """Fisher information of independent Poisson neurons, Gaussian tuning."""
    total = 0.0
    for pref in prefs:
        bump = math.exp(-(theta - pref) ** 2 / (2.0 * sigma ** 2))
        rate = r0 + r_max * bump
        slope = -r_max * (theta - pref) / sigma ** 2 * bump
        total += slope ** 2 / rate
    return T * total
```
hint: For each neuron compute the Gaussian bump, then the rate and the slope; add the squared slope divided by the rate, and multiply the sum by $T$.
solution: A neuron at $\theta = \theta_i$ has zero slope and contributes zero, as the fifth test shows. The population value is the sum of the single-neuron values because the neurons are independent, and the last test adds two neurons that sit on opposite flanks of the stimulus.

model:
```python
import math


def ml_decode(counts, prefs, grid, T, r0, r_max, sigma):
    """Grid maximum-likelihood estimate for Poisson neurons, Gaussian tuning."""
    best, best_ll = grid[0], None
    for theta in grid:
        ll = 0.0
        for n, pref in zip(counts, prefs):
            rate = r0 + r_max * math.exp(-(theta - pref) ** 2 / (2.0 * sigma ** 2))
            ll += n * math.log(rate) - T * rate
        if best_ll is None or ll > best_ll:
            best, best_ll = theta, ll
    return best
```
hint: Loop over the grid, sum $n\ln f - Tf$ over the neurons for each candidate, and keep the best. Update only on a strictly larger value so the first of a tie wins.
solution: The $-T f$ term matters: it penalizes candidate stimuli that predict many more spikes than were observed, and dropping it would bias the estimate toward stimuli that make the neurons' predicted rates large. The estimate is limited to the grid, so the finer the grid the closer it gets to the true maximum.

```python
import math


def population_vector(counts, prefs_deg):
    """Decoded direction (degrees, 0 to 360) from the population vector."""
    x = sum(n * math.cos(math.radians(p)) for n, p in zip(counts, prefs_deg))
    y = sum(n * math.sin(math.radians(p)) for n, p in zip(counts, prefs_deg))
    return math.degrees(math.atan2(y, x)) % 360.0
```
hint: Convert each preferred direction to radians, accumulate the two components, then use `atan2` and the modulo to land in $[0, 360)$.
solution: Using `atan2` rather than `atan(y / x)` keeps the correct quadrant (the first test lies in the second quadrant and the fifth case at 315 degrees), and the modulo turns negative angles into the $[0, 360)$ range.

@quiz self
prompt: A lab records from neurons in an auditory area and finds that the number of spikes in the first 100 ms after a tone predicts the tone frequency about as well as the exact time of the first spike does. A colleague concludes that the brain uses a rate code and that spike timing is irrelevant. Explain what the experiment shows and does not show, how you would test whether downstream circuits use spike timing, and how noise correlations between neurons could change the conclusion about how much the population knows.
hint: Separate what an experimenter can decode from what a downstream neuron can use.
solution: The recording shows that the count and the first-spike time carry similar information about the frequency in this data set; it does not show which one the brain uses, because an experimenter's decoder is not a downstream neuron. Information that is present may not be read out, and information that the experimenter ignores may be read out. To test the use of timing, one could add or remove timing information while keeping the counts fixed (for example by jittering spike times by a few milliseconds in the stimulus or by electrical or optogenetic stimulation with controlled timing) and ask whether the behavior or a downstream neuron's response changes, and compare decoders that use counts only, timing only and both on held-out trials. The finding also depends on the time window: a 100 ms count averages away timing finer than that. Noise correlations matter because if the variability is shared among neurons, averaging over many neurons does not remove it, so the Fisher information of the population can saturate or even fall relative to the independent sum; if the shared noise is not aligned with the signal direction it can instead help. The conclusion about the population therefore needs measured pairwise correlations, not the independence assumption.
