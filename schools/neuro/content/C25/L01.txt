@article
After a neuron fires, calcium floods into its terminal and then drains away over tens of milliseconds. A fluorescent calcium indicator makes this visible, and the glow rises at each spike and decays between spikes. Can a few lines of mathematics turn that glow back into spike counts? That is the first job of a **model**: a precise, runnable statement of what you think is going on, simple enough to test.

### Why build models

A verbal theory such as "calcium builds up when the neuron is busy" cannot be wrong in any interesting way. A model commits you to numbers: how fast the signal decays, how much each spike adds, how the pieces combine. Those commitments produce **predictions** you can check and parameters you can estimate. Models also expose hidden assumptions and tell you which measurement would distinguish two ideas.

Marr (1982) separated three **levels of analysis**. The **computational** level asks what problem the system solves and why (estimate firing from a fluorescence signal). The **algorithmic** level asks what representation and procedure it uses (a running sum with exponential forgetting). The **implementational** level asks how neurons, channels and molecules realize it (calcium pumps and buffers). A model can live at any level, and a good one says which. Models of single ion channels, of a neuron, of a small circuit and of a whole brain area differ in how much detail they keep, and the right amount is the least that still answers your question.

### A first model: exponential decay with jumps

Let $c(t)$ be the intracellular calcium concentration in micromolar (µM), with time in milliseconds (ms). Between spikes it decays at a rate proportional to its value, and each spike adds a fixed amount $A$ (in µM):

$$\frac{dc}{dt} = -\frac{c}{\tau_{\text{Ca}}} + A \sum_k \delta(t - t_k).$$

Without spikes the solution is $c(t) = c_0 e^{-t/\tau_{\text{Ca}}}$, where $\tau_{\text{Ca}}$ is the **time constant** (ms): after $\tau_{\text{Ca}}$ the concentration has fallen to $e^{-1} \approx 37\%$. Taking logarithms gives a straight line, $\ln c = \ln c_0 - t/\tau_{\text{Ca}}$, which is how time constants are usually estimated.

Most models in this course have no closed-form solution, so we simulate them. The **Euler method** replaces the derivative by a finite step of size $\Delta t$:

$$c_{k+1} = c_k + \Delta t\,\Big(-\frac{c_k}{\tau_{\text{Ca}}}\Big) = c_k\Big(1 - \frac{\Delta t}{\tau_{\text{Ca}}}\Big).$$

```python
def euler_decay(c0, tau, dt, n_steps):
    """Euler trace of dc/dt = -c/tau (c in uM, tau and dt in ms)."""
    c, out = c0, [c0]
    for _ in range(n_steps):
        c += dt * (-c / tau)
        out.append(c)
    return out
```

For $\tau_{\text{Ca}} = 50$ ms and $c_0 = 1$ µM, the exact value at $t = 50$ ms is $0.3679$ µM. Euler with $\Delta t = 10$ ms gives $0.8^5 = 0.3277$, an error of about 11%; with $\Delta t = 1$ ms it gives $0.98^{50} = 0.3642$, an error of about 1%. The error shrinks in proportion to $\Delta t$, so a rule of thumb is to keep $\Delta t$ well below the fastest time constant in the model, and to halve it and check that nothing important changes.

| $\Delta t$ (ms) | Euler $c(50\text{ ms})$ (µM) | exact (µM) |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | 0.3277 | 0.3679 |
| 5 | 0.3487 | 0.3679 |
| 1 | 0.3642 | 0.3679 |

### Judging a model

A model with enough free parameters can fit any data set: six parameters will pass through six points exactly and predict nothing. Prefer models that (1) fit with few parameters, (2) predict **held-out** data that were not used in fitting, and (3) fail visibly when they are wrong. The sum of squared errors, $\text{SSE} = \sum_i (y_i - \hat y_i)^2$, measures fit, but a smaller SSE on the data you fitted is not evidence of a better model by itself.

Fitting the decay model by least squares on the log scale, with $c_0$ known, minimizes $\sum_i (\ln c_i - \ln c_0 + t_i/\tau)^2$ over the rate $1/\tau$. Setting the derivative to zero gives $1/\tau = -\sum_i t_i \ln(c_i/c_0) \big/ \sum_i t_i^2$. The log transform weights small concentrations strongly, so it is only appropriate when measurement noise is proportional to the signal. Different noise needs a different fit: part of modeling is knowing what the data-generating process is.

Throughout the course the loop is the same. Choose a level, write the equations with units, simulate or solve them, compare with data or with a simpler model, and ask what the model predicts that you have not yet measured.

@example
title: Euler steps and a time constant from two points
problem: A calcium trace starts at $c_0 = 2.0$ µM and decays with $\tau_{\text{Ca}} = 40$ ms. (a) Take two Euler steps of $\Delta t = 10$ ms and compare with the exact value at 20 ms. (b) A different trace reads $1.6$ µM at $t = 0$ and $0.6$ µM at $t = 50$ ms; estimate its time constant.
step: Each Euler step multiplies $c$ by $1 - \Delta t/\tau = 1 - 10/40 = 0.75$. After one step $c = 1.5$ µM, after two $c = 1.125$ µM.
step: The exact value is $2.0\,e^{-20/40} = 2.0 \times 0.6065 = 1.2131$ µM. Euler undershoots by $1.2131 - 1.125 = 0.088$ µM, about 7%, because it uses the slope at the start of each step, which is steeper than the average slope over the step.
step: For (b), take logarithms: $\ln(1.6/0.6) = t/\tau$ with $t = 50$ ms, so $\tau = 50/\ln(2.667) = 50/0.9808$.
step: This gives $\tau \approx 50.98$ ms. With only two points the estimate has no check; more points let you test whether the decay is a single exponential.
answer: (a) $1.125$ µM against an exact $1.213$ µM. (b) $\tau \approx 51.0$ ms.

@mistakes
- Treating a good fit as proof: a model with many parameters fits its own data almost perfectly, so test it on data it has not seen.
- Using a time step that is not small compared with the time constant: Euler error grows with $\Delta t$, and for $\Delta t > 2\tau$ the decay equation gives a simulation that oscillates and diverges.
- Mixing units: a time constant in milliseconds with a time step in seconds gives results that are wrong by a factor of 1000.
- Confusing the levels: showing that an algorithm works (the computational and algorithmic levels) does not show that neurons use it (the implementational level).

model:
```python
def calcium_trace(spike_steps, n_steps, tau, dt, jump):
    """Calcium (uM) after each Euler step, with a jump at each spike step."""
    c, out = 0.0, []
    for k in range(n_steps):
        c += dt * (-c / tau)
        if k in spike_steps:
            c += jump
        out.append(c)
    return out
```
hint: Keep one running value `c`. Decay first, then add the jump if this step is a spike step, then append.
solution: The order matters: a spike in step `k` is added after that step's decay, so it shows at full size in the recorded value and starts to decay in the next step. Starting at zero means a trace with no spikes stays at zero.

model:
```python
import math


def fit_tau(times, conc, c0):
    """Least-squares time constant of an exponential decay from c0."""
    num = sum(t * t for t in times)
    den = sum(t * math.log(c / c0) for t, c in zip(times, conc))
    return -num / den
```
hint: Compute the sum of $t^2$ and the sum of $t\ln(c/c_0)$ separately, then divide. The second sum is negative for decaying data.
solution: Minimizing the squared error of $\ln c_i$ against $\ln c_0 - t_i/\tau$ with respect to $1/\tau$ gives the formula; with a single exact point it reduces to $\tau = -t/\ln(c/c_0)$, which the last test checks. A sign error returns a negative time constant.

model:
```python
import math


def max_euler_error(c0, tau, dt, n_steps):
    """Largest |Euler - exact| over the first n_steps steps."""
    c, worst = c0, 0.0
    for k in range(1, n_steps + 1):
        c += dt * (-c / tau)
        worst = max(worst, abs(c - c0 * math.exp(-k * dt / tau)))
    return worst
```
hint: Update the Euler value step by step and compare it with the exact exponential at the same time, $k\,\Delta t$, keeping the maximum.
solution: The error is zero at step 0 and, for a decaying exponential, grows and then shrinks as both curves approach zero, so the maximum is taken over all steps rather than read at the last one.

@quiz self
prompt: A colleague says that a model of calcium dynamics at the algorithmic level (a running sum with exponential forgetting) is useless unless it also models pumps and buffers. Argue for or against, naming what each level of description can and cannot tell you, and say what evidence would link the two levels.
hint: Say what question each level answers.
solution: The algorithmic model answers what computation the signal supports: an exponentially weighted spike count, with $\tau_{\text{Ca}}$ setting how far back in time it looks, so it predicts how fluorescence should respond to bursts and what sampling is needed to recover spikes. It cannot say how the cell achieves the decay, and several implementations (pumps, buffering, diffusion) give the same exponential. A pump-and-buffer model answers how, and predicts effects the algorithmic model cannot, such as saturation at high rates, but it carries many parameters that must be measured. Useful models match the question: the simple one is enough to decode spikes, the detailed one to predict the effect of a drug on the pump. Evidence linking them includes showing that the detailed model reduces to the exponential-with-jumps form over the physiological range, and that perturbing a pump changes $\tau_{\text{Ca}}$ as predicted.

rubric: Recognizes that one algorithm can have many implementations, so the simple model need not be wrong.
rubric: Proposes evidence that links the levels, such as a reduction or a perturbation experiment.
