@article
Record from a cortical neuron while you step the injected current up and down. At a modest step the neuron fires fast at first, then slows, settling to a lower rate within a few hundred milliseconds. At a large step it fires faster, but never faster than some ceiling set by its refractory period. A model that captures this needs three things beyond a leaky membrane: a **reset**, a **refractory period** and an **adaptation current**.

### The model, in conductance form

Write the model with a capacitance in picofarads (pF), a leak conductance in nanosiemens (nS), currents in picoamperes (pA), voltages in millivolts (mV) and time in milliseconds (ms). Since 1 pA divided by 1 nS is 1 mV, and 1 pA divided by 1 pF is 1 mV/ms, the units close:

$$C \frac{dV}{dt} = -g_L (V - E_L) - w + I, \qquad \tau_w \frac{dw}{dt} = -w .$$

Here $w$ is an **adaptation current** (pA). When $V$ reaches the threshold $V_{\text{th}}$ a spike is recorded, $V$ is set to the reset $V_{\text{reset}}$, $w$ jumps up by $b$ (pA) and $V$ is held at reset for the refractory period $t_{\text{ref}}$. In this lesson $C = 200$ pF, $g_L = 10$ nS, so $\tau_m = C/g_L = 20$ ms; $E_L = -70$ mV, $V_{\text{th}} = -50$ mV, $V_{\text{reset}} = -60$ mV (reset above rest) and $t_{\text{ref}} = 3$ ms.

### The f–I curve with a refractory period

Without adaptation ($w = 0$) the voltage relaxes toward $V_\infty = E_L + I/g_L$. Starting from reset, the time to reach threshold is

$$T(I) = \tau_m \ln \frac{V_\infty - V_{\text{reset}}}{V_\infty - V_{\text{th}}} \quad (V_\infty > V_{\text{th}}),$$

and the interspike interval is $t_{\text{ref}} + T(I)$, so $f = 1000/(t_{\text{ref}} + T)$ Hz. As $I \to \infty$, $T \to 0$ and $f \to 1000/t_{\text{ref}} = 333$ Hz, the ceiling set by refractoriness.

| $I$ (pA) | $V_\infty$ (mV) | $T$ (ms) | $f$ (Hz) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 250 | −45 | 21.97 | 40.0 |
| 300 | −40 | 13.86 | 59.3 |
| 400 | −30 | 8.11 | 90.0 |
| 600 | −10 | 4.46 | 134.0 |

Real $f$–$I$ curves rise less steeply than this model's at high currents, and noise rounds the corner at the lowest currents: with fluctuating input, the neuron fires occasionally even when the mean current is below the deterministic threshold.

### Adaptation

After each spike $w$ is increased by $b$ and decays with time constant $\tau_w$. Because $w$ subtracts from the input, each spike makes the next one a little later. At a steady rate $f$ (in spikes per ms), $w$ gains $b$ per spike and loses $w/\tau_w$ per ms, so its average is roughly $\bar w \approx b\,\tau_w f$, and the neuron behaves as if driven by $I - \bar w$. This **spike-frequency adaptation** is a standard feature of excitatory cortical neurons. It is also a slow negative feedback: the output history changes the input drive.

```python
def simulate_adaptive(I, n_steps, dt, tau_w, b, t_ref=3.0):
    """Spike times (ms) of the adaptive LIF neuron for a constant I (pA)."""
    C, gL, E_L, V_th, V_reset = 200.0, 10.0, -70.0, -50.0, -60.0
    V, w, hold, spikes = E_L, 0.0, 0, []
    for k in range(n_steps):
        w += dt * (-w / tau_w)
        if hold > 0:
            hold -= 1
            continue
        V += dt / C * (-gL * (V - E_L) - w + I)
        if V >= V_th:
            spikes.append(round((k + 1) * dt, 6))
            V, hold = V_reset, int(round(t_ref / dt))
            w += b
    return spikes
```

With $I = 400$ pA, $\tau_w = 100$ ms, $b = 40$ pA and $\Delta t = 0.1$ ms, the first ten spikes fall at $13.9, 26.5, 40.8, 57.2, 75.8, 96.4, 118.8, 142.4, 166.8, 191.7$ ms. The intervals grow from $12.6$ ms to $24.9$ ms (80 Hz down to 40 Hz). With $b = 0$ every interval is $11.1$ ms, the $3 + 8.11$ ms of the table.

### Noise and variability

Real neurons receive fluctuating input. Adding a noise term to $I$ at each step, supplied as a list of values in pA, makes the interspike intervals vary. The **coefficient of variation** $\text{CV} = \text{SD}/\text{mean}$ of the intervals is 0 for a perfectly regular neuron and about 1 for a Poisson process. Cortical neurons usually fall between those extremes.

### Limits

This model has no spike shape, no sodium or potassium channels, and one compartment. It cannot produce bursting or resonance; adding an exponential spike-initiation term or a second variable (the next lessons) restores some of these. Its strength is that its few parameters can be fitted directly to a measured $f$–$I$ curve, as in the exercises below.

@example
title: A frozen-adaptation estimate of the rate
problem: The neuron in the article ($g_L = 10$ nS, $E_L = -70$ mV, $V_{\text{th}} = -50$ mV, $V_{\text{reset}} = -60$ mV, $\tau_m = 20$ ms, $t_{\text{ref}} = 3$ ms) receives $I = 400$ pA while its adaptation current is $w = 80$ pA. Treating $w$ as constant over one interval, what is the firing rate?
step: The net drive is $I - w = 320$ pA, so $V_\infty = -70 + 320/10 = -38$ mV, above threshold.
step: Distances from the steady state: to reset $-38 - (-60) = 22$ mV, to threshold $-38 - (-50) = 12$ mV.
step: $T = 20 \ln(22/12) = 20 \times 0.6061 = 12.12$ ms.
step: The interval is $t_{\text{ref}} + T = 15.12$ ms, so $f = 1000/15.12 \approx 66.1$ Hz, below the 90.0 Hz that 400 pA gives without adaptation.
answer: About $66.1$ Hz, compared with $90.0$ Hz with no adaptation current.

@mistakes
- Dropping the refractory period from the interval: the rate is $1000/(t_{\text{ref}} + T)$, not $1000/T$, and the first matters most at high currents.
- Treating the adaptation current as depolarizing: $w$ is subtracted from the input, so it lowers $V_\infty$ and lengthens intervals.
- Mixing nS, pA and mV: pA divided by nS is mV, and a time step in ms with a capacitance in pF keeps $dt/C$ in the right units.
- Expecting the first interval after a step to equal the last: with adaptation the first interval is the shortest, and intervals lengthen toward a steady value.

model:
```python
import math


def fit_tref(currents, rates):
    """Mean refractory period (ms) implied by the measured rates."""
    values = []
    for current, rate in zip(currents, rates):
        if rate > 0:
            v_inf = -70.0 + current / 10.0
            t_free = 20.0 * math.log((v_inf + 60.0) / (v_inf + 50.0))
            values.append(1000.0 / rate - t_free)
    if not values:
        return None
    return sum(values) / len(values)
```
hint: The interval is $1000/f$ ms, which equals $t_{\text{ref}} + T(I)$, so each pair gives one estimate of $t_{\text{ref}}$. Average the estimates and skip zero rates.
solution: The model is linear in $t_{\text{ref}}$ on the interval scale, so least squares for $t_{\text{ref}}$ is the mean of the per-pair differences. Dividing by a zero rate fails, so those pairs must be skipped before the division, and an empty list needs its own return value.

model:
```python
def adaptive_isis(i_list, dt, tau_w, b):
    """Interspike intervals (ms) of the adaptive LIF neuron."""
    C, gL, E_L, V_th, V_reset = 200.0, 10.0, -70.0, -50.0, -60.0
    V, w, hold, spikes = E_L, 0.0, 0, []
    for k, I in enumerate(i_list):
        w += dt * (-w / tau_w)
        if hold > 0:
            hold -= 1
            continue
        V += dt / C * (-gL * (V - E_L) - w + I)
        if V >= V_th:
            spikes.append((k + 1) * dt)
            V, hold = V_reset, int(round(3.0 / dt))
            w += b
    return [y - x for x, y in zip(spikes, spikes[1:])]
```
hint: Keep the step loop of a constant-current simulation but read the current from the list, store spike times, and take differences at the end.
solution: The noise enters only through `i_list[k]`, so the same loop handles constant and fluctuating input. The test with $b = 0$ shows that the intervals are then all equal ($3 + 8.11$ ms, rounded by the time step), while with adaptation each interval is longer than the last; the noisy case checks that the intervals now vary.

model:
```python
import math


def cv_isi(spike_times):
    """Coefficient of variation of the interspike intervals."""
    isis = [b - a for a, b in zip(spike_times, spike_times[1:])]
    if len(isis) < 2:
        return None
    mean = sum(isis) / len(isis)
    var = sum((x - mean) ** 2 for x in isis) / len(isis)
    return math.sqrt(var) / mean
```
hint: Take differences of consecutive spike times, then the mean, then the population variance, then the square root over the mean.
solution: The three spikes give intervals 5 and 20 ms, with mean 12.5 and population variance $56.25$, so the SD is 7.5 and the CV $0.6$. With one interval no variability exists, so the function returns `None`; dividing by $n - 1$ instead of $n$ would give a different value.

prompt: A student records a neuron at 200 pA, 300 pA and 400 pA and reports that its rate saturates near 100 Hz. hint: Think about which mechanism depends on the history of firing.
solution: A refractory period adds a fixed dead time to every interval, so the rate cannot exceed $1000/t_{\text{ref}}$, and the ceiling is the same in the first interval as in the last. To tell them apart, compare the initial rate (first interval) with the steady rate (late intervals) at each current, and plot the interval against spike number. A refractory ceiling shows intervals that approach a floor from above with no trend over the step, while adaptation shows steadily lengthening intervals. In the first 50 ms the refractory ceiling would show clearly, while adaptation would still be small because $w$ has had few spikes to accumulate.
