@article
A synapse is not a fixed number. Stimulate a presynaptic axon at 20 Hz and the postsynaptic responses can shrink with each spike at one synapse and grow at another, even though the neurons and the stimulus are the same. Modeling a network requires modeling the synapse in two parts: the **time course** of a single response and the **history dependence** of its size.

### The exponential synapse

When a presynaptic spike arrives at time $t_s$, the synaptic conductance $g_s$ (in nS) jumps up by a weight $\bar g$ and then decays with time constant $\tau_s$ (ms):

$$\tau_s \frac{dg_s}{dt} = -g_s, \qquad g_s \to g_s + \bar g \text{ at each spike}, \qquad g_s(t) = \bar g \sum_{t_s \le t} e^{-(t - t_s)/\tau_s}.$$

Because the equation is linear, the contributions of separate spikes simply add. The current it passes depends on the driving force: for a membrane at $V$ (mV) and a synapse with reversal potential $E_{\text{syn}}$ (mV), the current into the cell is

$$I_{\text{syn}} = g_s\,(E_{\text{syn}} - V) \quad (\text{pA, with } g_s \text{ in nS and voltages in mV}).$$

An excitatory (AMPA-like) synapse has $E_{\text{syn}} \approx 0$ mV and $\tau_s \approx$ 2–5 ms; an inhibitory (GABA$_A$-like) synapse has $E_{\text{syn}} \approx -80$ mV. An inhibitory synapse hyperpolarizes a cell resting at $-70$ mV, but if $E_{\text{syn}}$ equals the resting potential it produces no voltage change, only a larger conductance, called **shunting inhibition**.

```python
import math


def conductance(spike_times, t, g_jump, tau_s):
    """Synaptic conductance (nS) at time t (ms) for spikes at spike_times."""
    return sum(g_jump * math.exp(-(t - ts) / tau_s)
               for ts in spike_times if ts <= t)
```

Placed on the leaky membrane of lesson 2 ($C = 200$ pF, $g_L = 10$ nS, $E_L = -70$ mV, so $\tau_m = 20$ ms), a single spike with $\bar g = 2$ nS, $\tau_s = 5$ ms and $E_{\text{syn}} = 0$ mV produces a peak depolarization of about $2.17$ mV, reached about $9$ ms after the spike. The peak is smaller than $\bar g \tau_s \cdot 70/C = 3.5$ mV, the value for a membrane with no leak: the leak and the decay of the conductance both limit it.

### Short-term plasticity

The Tsodyks–Markram model describes how the strength of a synapse depends on its recent use. Two variables lie between 0 and 1: the fraction $x$ of **available resources** (vesicles ready to release) and the **utilization** $u$, the fraction of those that a spike releases. Between spikes:

$$\frac{dx}{dt} = \frac{1 - x}{\tau_D}, \qquad \frac{du}{dt} = -\frac{u}{\tau_F},$$

where $\tau_D$ is the recovery time of depression and $\tau_F$ the decay of facilitation (both in ms). At each spike, first $u \leftarrow u + U(1 - u)$, with $U$ the baseline release probability, then the **efficacy** (the relative size of the postsynaptic response) is $u\,x$, and finally $x \leftarrow x - u\,x$. Starting from $x = 1$ and $u = 0$, the first efficacy is $U$. Between spikes separated by $\Delta$, the equations solve exactly: $x \to 1 - (1 - x)e^{-\Delta/\tau_D}$ and $u \to u\,e^{-\Delta/\tau_F}$.

| spike | depressing ($U = 0.5$, $\tau_D = 200$, $\tau_F = 20$) | facilitating ($U = 0.15$, $\tau_D = 100$, $\tau_F = 500$) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 (0 ms) | 0.500 | 0.150 |
| 2 (50 ms) | 0.318 | 0.241 |
| 3 (100 ms) | 0.234 | 0.283 |
| 4 (150 ms) | 0.203 | 0.298 |
| 5 (200 ms) | 0.191 | 0.304 |

A **depressing** synapse (large $U$, slow recovery) loses efficacy as resources are used up faster than they recover, and it settles near a steady value; for a train of interval $T$ with $u$ fixed at $U$, the resources before a spike satisfy $x^* = (1 - e^{-T/\tau_D})/\big(1 - (1 - U)e^{-T/\tau_D}\big)$. A **facilitating** synapse (small $U$, long $\tau_F$) accumulates $u$ and grows. Depression makes a synapse respond most to the onset of a train and to changes in rate, a high-pass-like behavior, while facilitation emphasizes later spikes of a burst. Real synapses mix both, and cortical synapse types differ systematically in which dominates.

### Limits

The model is phenomenological: it captures average efficacy, not the stochastic release of single vesicles (quantal release; Fatt and Katz 1952), and it has no postsynaptic plasticity. It also ignores effects such as receptor desensitization and saturation. Even so, its two time constants reproduce the main features of many recordings and explain why the same spike train can carry different information through different synapses.

@example
title: Summing two exponential conductances
problem: A synapse with $\bar g = 2$ nS and $\tau_s = 5$ ms receives spikes at $t = 0$ and $t = 10$ ms. At $t = 12$ ms, with the membrane at $V = -60$ mV and $E_{\text{syn}} = 0$ mV, what are the conductance and the synaptic current?
step: The first spike is 12 ms old: $2\,e^{-12/5} = 2 \times 0.0907 = 0.1814$ nS.
step: The second spike is 2 ms old: $2\,e^{-2/5} = 2 \times 0.6703 = 1.3406$ nS.
step: Add the contributions: $g_s = 0.1814 + 1.3406 = 1.522$ nS.
step: Apply the driving force: $I_{\text{syn}} = 1.522 \times (0 - (-60)) = 91.3$ pA, inward and depolarizing.
answer: $g_s \approx 1.52$ nS and $I_{\text{syn}} \approx 91.3$ pA.

@example
title: The second efficacy of a depressing synapse
problem: A Tsodyks–Markram synapse has $U = 0.5$, $\tau_D = 200$ ms and $\tau_F = 20$ ms. Spikes arrive at 0 and 50 ms. What is the efficacy of the second spike?
step: First spike: $u = 0 + 0.5(1 - 0) = 0.5$ and efficacy $0.5 \times 1 = 0.5$; then $x = 1 - 0.5 = 0.5$.
step: Recover for 50 ms: $x = 1 - 0.5\,e^{-50/200} = 1 - 0.5 \times 0.7788 = 0.6106$, and $u = 0.5\,e^{-50/20} = 0.5 \times 0.0821 = 0.0410$.
step: Second spike: $u = 0.0410 + 0.5(1 - 0.0410) = 0.5205$.
step: Efficacy $= u\,x = 0.5205 \times 0.6106 = 0.318$, a drop of 36% from the first.
answer: About $0.318$.

@mistakes
- Using $x$ before the recovery step or $u$ after the release: the order is recover over the interval, update $u$, record $u\,x$, then deplete $x$.
- Forgetting the driving force: a synaptic conductance does nothing to a membrane sitting at $E_{\text{syn}}$, and an inhibitory synapse can depolarize a cell whose potential is below $E_{\text{syn}}$.
- Calling every shrinking response "depression" from the first two spikes alone: a facilitating synapse can still depress for particular intervals, so check the whole train and the parameters.
- Mixing units: a conductance in nS times a voltage in mV is a current in pA, and times must be in the same unit (ms) as the time constants.

model:
```python
import math


def tm_efficacies(spike_times, U, tau_D, tau_F):
    """Efficacy u*x of each spike in the Tsodyks-Markram model."""
    u, x, last, out = 0.0, 1.0, None, []
    for t in spike_times:
        if last is not None:
            gap = t - last
            x = 1.0 - (1.0 - x) * math.exp(-gap / tau_D)
            u = u * math.exp(-gap / tau_F)
        u = u + U * (1.0 - u)
        out.append(u * x)
        x = x - u * x
        last = t
    return out
```
hint: Keep `u`, `x` and the previous spike time. Recover over the gap, then update $u$, record, and deplete $x$.
solution: With a very short $\tau_F$ the utilization returns to zero between spikes, so $u = U$ at every spike and only depression remains; the last test checks this limit, where the second efficacy is $U x$ with $x = 1 - (1 - U)e^{-200/100} = 0.9188$, that is $0.6 \times 0.9188 = 0.551$. The recorded value uses $u$ after its jump and $x$ before depletion.

```python
def epsp_peak(g_jump, n_steps, dt, tau_s, E_syn):
    """Largest signed deviation of V from rest after one synaptic event."""
    C, g_L, E_L = 200.0, 10.0, -70.0
    V, g, best = E_L, g_jump, 0.0
    for _ in range(n_steps):
        V += dt / C * (-g_L * (V - E_L) + g * (E_syn - V))
        g += dt * (-g / tau_s)
        if abs(V - E_L) > abs(best):
            best = V - E_L
    return best
```
hint: Use the old `V` in the synaptic term, update `V` before decaying `g`, and keep the deviation of largest magnitude.
solution: The test with $E_{\text{syn}} = E_L$ gives zero deviation: this is shunting inhibition, which changes the conductance but not the voltage at rest. Inhibition with $E_{\text{syn}} = -80$ mV hyperpolarizes by only $0.31$ mV because the driving force is 10 mV, compared with 70 mV for the excitatory synapse.

```python
import math


def dep_efficacies(spike_times, U, tau_D):
    """Efficacies U*x of a depressing synapse with fixed utilization."""
    x, last, out = 1.0, None, []
    for t in spike_times:
        if last is not None:
            x = 1.0 - (1.0 - x) * math.exp(-(t - last) / tau_D)
        out.append(U * x)
        x -= U * x
        last = t
    return out
```
hint: Track $x$ and the last spike time; recover, record $U x$, then deplete.
solution: Depression alone is the Tsodyks–Markram model with $u$ held at $U$. The efficacy falls from $U$ toward the steady state $U x^*$, and with a long interval relative to $\tau_D$ it recovers almost fully, so the four-spike train at 100 ms intervals settles near $0.219$.

@quiz self
prompt: Two synapses onto the same neuron both produce a first postsynaptic potential of 0.5 mV. Synapse A is depressing and synapse B is facilitating. A presynaptic neuron fires a burst of five spikes at 50 Hz and then pauses for a second. Describe how each synapse's responses evolve through the burst and after the pause, what each tells the postsynaptic neuron about the presynaptic firing, and which Tsodyks–Markram parameters you would change to turn A into B.
hint: Use the two recovery time constants and think of the pause as a reset.
solution: Synapse A has a large release probability $U$ and a slow recovery of resources: each spike uses a large fraction of the available vesicles, so the later responses fall, for instance from 0.5 mV to under 0.2 mV by the fifth spike, and the synapse reports the first spike or a change in rate more than the sustained rate. After a one-second pause the resources $x$ recover (with $\tau_D$ of a few hundred milliseconds), so the next burst begins with a full-size response again. Synapse B has a small $U$ and a long facilitation time constant $\tau_F$: each spike leaves residual $u$, so the responses grow through the burst, and the synapse emphasizes the later spikes of a high-frequency burst, acting as a detector of bursts rather than isolated spikes. After the pause, $u$ has decayed over $\tau_F$ (about half a second), so the response returns toward its small starting value. Turning A into B means lowering $U$ and lengthening $\tau_F$, with a modest $\tau_D$ so that resources do not limit the later responses.
