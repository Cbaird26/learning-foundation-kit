@article
When we cannot ask, how can we know? An infant cannot report, an octopus has a nervous system built on a different plan from ours, and a computer program can produce fluent sentences about its inner life whatever is or is not happening inside it. As of 2026 there is no scientific or philosophical consensus on which animals are conscious, on whether infants are conscious in the way adults are, or on whether any artificial system is or could be conscious, nor on what would settle these questions. This lesson sets out the arguments and the evidence, and leaves the verdict open.

### The shape of the problem

Every attribution of consciousness is an inference from something observable, and its strength depends on the evidence and on one's theory. Three kinds of evidence recur: **behavior** (flexible learning, trade-offs between avoiding harm and obtaining reward, responses to injury that go beyond reflex), **neural similarity** (homologous structures and signatures such as those calibrated in adults), and **theory-based indicators** (does the system have the architecture a given theory requires?). The three can come apart, and the weight of each depends on which theory is assumed.

### Infants

The case for early experience points to the continuity of brain development, to behavior such as preferences and distress, and to neural markers. Kouider and colleagues (2013) found that in infants of 5 months and older, faces shown long enough to be visible produced a late slow-wave EEG response with a nonlinear dependence on duration, resembling the adult late response but slower, and they interpreted it as a marker of conscious access. The case for caution points to the immaturity of long-range connectivity and of prefrontal cortex, to the fact that the marker was calibrated on adult reports and its link to infant experience is an assumption, and to theories that require capacities that develop later, as some higher-order views do. Theories also differ: a recurrent processing view locates experience in sensory cortex and so allows early emergence, whereas views tying consciousness to broadcast or to higher-order representation depend on how and when those mechanisms mature. The argument is thus partly empirical and partly a matter of which theory, and which version of the access-phenomenal distinction (week 1), is assumed.

### Animals

The 2012 Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness stated that non-human animals possess the neurological substrates that generate consciousness. On 19 April 2024 the New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness, signed by many researchers, stated that there is strong scientific support for conscious experience in mammals and birds and at least a realistic possibility of it in all vertebrates and many invertebrates, such as cephalopods, decapod crustaceans and insects. It is a statement of expert opinion and not a consensus of all researchers, and it recommends taking the possibility into account in decisions about animals. The strongest arguments for broad attribution: homologous brain systems and shared evolutionary history; behavioral evidence of flexible, goal-directed responses to pain; and effects of anesthetics and analgesics in many species. The strongest arguments for caution: reliance on analogy from humans can mislead; behavior can be produced by simple mechanisms; some theories tie consciousness to structures, such as a thalamocortical system or a specific type of recurrence, that other taxa lack or implement differently; and tests such as mirror self-recognition (Gallup, 1970) measure a specific capacity that depends on vision and manipulation of one's body, and not experience as such. Birch, Schnell and Clayton (2020) proposed treating consciousness as having several dimensions, for example perceptual richness, unity and self-awareness, so that the question becomes how a species varies on each and not whether it has "it" or not.

### Machines

Positions differ on what could in principle matter. **Computational functionalism** holds that the right organization suffices, whatever the substrate, so that a system implementing the relevant computations, for example a workspace or higher-order monitoring, could be conscious. **Biological views** hold that features of living systems, such as metabolism, a body regulating itself, or specific physical dynamics, may be necessary, and Searle's Chinese room argument (1980) maintains that running a program, however fluent, does not by itself yield understanding. **Integrated information theory** denies that computation matters and holds that conventional digital hardware has little integrated causal structure whatever it runs (week 8). Butlin, Long and colleagues (2023) proposed assessing artificial systems for indicator properties drawn from several theories, under the working assumption of computational functionalism, which is itself contested.

A distinctive problem is the **gaming problem**: a system trained on human writing will produce human-like statements about experience whether or not it has any, so such statements weigh little as evidence, in either direction. The probabilistic point is general. A behavior's evidential weight is the likelihood ratio between its probability if the system is conscious and if it is not; the more easily non-conscious explanations produce it, the closer the ratio is to 1.

### A balanced analysis

To analyze one system, state which kinds of evidence exist for it, what each theory implies about it, what the strongest counterarguments are, and what further evidence would change the assessment. A balanced analysis does not end in a verdict but in a map of what the question depends on.

@example
title: How much does a behavior move credence?
problem: Before considering a behavior, you give a credence of 0.4 that a system is conscious. The behavior has probability 0.9 if the system is conscious and 0.3 if it is not (for example because simple mechanisms could produce it). What is the posterior credence?
step: Prior odds are $0.4/0.6 = 2/3$.
step: The likelihood ratio is $0.9/0.3 = 3$.
step: Posterior odds are $(2/3)\times 3 = 2$, so the posterior credence is $2/3 = 0.667$.
step: If the behavior could be produced almost as easily without consciousness (probability 0.8), the ratio would be $0.9/0.8 = 1.125$ and the credence would barely move, from 0.4 to about 0.43.
answer: Posterior about 0.667; with an easy non-conscious explanation the same behavior would move credence only to about 0.43.

@mistakes
- Treating the New York Declaration as the consensus of all researchers. It is a statement of expert opinion signed by many researchers.
- Using fluent self-reports from a system trained on human text as strong evidence for or against experience. Such reports are expected either way.
- Taking failure of a test such as mirror self-recognition as evidence against consciousness. It tests a specific capacity that depends on the senses and the body.

@practice self
prompt: Write a balanced analysis (about 300 words) of consciousness in one non-human system of your choice (an animal group, an infant, or an artificial system). Include the kinds of evidence, what at least two theories imply, the strongest arguments each way, and what further evidence would change the assessment. Do not give a verdict.
hint: Organize by evidence type, then theory, then objections, and end with what would change the assessment.
solution: System: octopuses (cephalopods). Evidence: behavior includes flexible learning, problem solving and wound-directed responses that may go beyond reflex; the New York Declaration (2024) states that there is at least a realistic possibility of experience in cephalopods. Neural evidence is of a different kind: a large, complex nervous system with many neurons distributed in the arms, no cortex or thalamus, and reported effects of anesthetics on behavior and on electrical activity. Theory-based implications differ. A workspace theory asks whether a global broadcast architecture exists; the vertical lobe and the interaction of central and arm systems might serve, but the question depends on a functional criterion that has not been settled for a decentralized system. A recurrent processing view would look for feedback in visual and learning circuits, which are known to be recurrent. Integrated information theory would ask about the physical causal structure, and the answer would depend on detailed connectivity that is not known. Higher-order views would ask for monitoring of one's own states, for which evidence is limited. Arguments for: behavioral flexibility, convergent evolution of complex cognition, and the precautionary reasoning that errors of denying experience are costly. Arguments against: flexibility can be produced by mechanisms without experience, human-based analogies are weak for a distant lineage, and the arm-centered organization makes the unity of a subject unclear. Evidence that would change the assessment: a theory-neutral marker validated in other species, evidence of integrated processing across the arms and brain, or demonstrations of metacognitive behavior. The assessment stays open: the question depends on theory, and the evidence is compatible with both views.

@quiz self
prompt: In about 250 words, compare how a recurrent processing theory and a higher-order theory would approach the question of when infant consciousness begins, what evidence each would look for, and what would count as evidence against each view.
hint: Recurrent processing locates experience in sensory feedback; higher-order theories require a representation of one's own states.
solution: A recurrent processing theory locates phenomenal consciousness in local recurrent activity in sensory cortex, so it would look for evidence of feedback-dependent processing, such as figure-ground modulation or context effects, in the infant visual system, probably early in development as cortical circuits mature. On this view experience may precede the ability to report or to use information flexibly, which would be dissociated from access. It would be counted against if recurrent signatures were absent when experience is otherwise strongly suggested, or if recurrence were found where experience is clearly missing. A higher-order theory requires that the infant represent its own sensory states in some way, even minimally, so it would look for evidence of monitoring: confidence-like behavior, sensitivity to the reliability of one's own perception, or developing prefrontal and related circuits supporting reality monitoring. It predicts that experience emerges when these mechanisms do, which may be later than the sensory circuits. It would be counted against by clear signs of experience, as the theory's critics would define them, in infants lacking any plausible monitoring, though the proponent can respond that the required representation may be minimal. Both views face the difficulty that the independent evidence of experience in infants is behavioral or neural and interpreted through the theories themselves, so a result that looks decisive can be reinterpreted. Progress would come from markers validated in adults with no-report designs and tested for the same properties in infants, together with predictions fixed in advance.

rubric: Notes that evidence for infant experience is interpreted through theories and that the question remains open.
