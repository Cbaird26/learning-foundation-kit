@article
Your brain processes many things at once: the sound of a fan, the position of your hand, the words you are reading. Yet only some of this is in consciousness at any moment, and what is conscious can be reported, remembered and used to plan. **Global workspace theory** explains the difference by an architecture: consciousness is what happens when information is made globally available.

### The theory in its strongest form

Baars (1988) proposed a cognitive architecture of many specialized, unconscious processors that work in parallel and compete for access to a limited-capacity **global workspace**. Information that wins is **broadcast** to all processors, including those for memory, evaluation, language and motor control. Conscious contents are those in the workspace. Three features give the theory its explanatory force: limited capacity explains why consciousness seems serial and narrow; broadcast explains why conscious content can be reported, remembered and used flexibly; and competition explains the role of attention.

Dehaene, Kerszberg and Changeux (1998) gave a neuronal version, later developed by Dehaene and Naccache (2001) and others, the **global neuronal workspace** (GNW). Sensory areas process stimuli in modular ways. A subset of neurons with long axons, concentrated in prefrontal, parietal and cingulate cortex, connect distant areas and form the workspace. A stimulus becomes conscious when its representation triggers **ignition**: a nonlinear, self-sustaining, reverberating activation of this network, typically beginning around 250 to 300 ms after the stimulus, that amplifies the representation and makes it available to many systems. Ignition is all-or-none: below a threshold the activity dies away, above it the whole network is recruited. The theory identifies consciousness with this conscious access, and it holds that the question of a phenomenal remainder beyond access is not needed to explain the data.

### Evidence

- **Attentional blink.** When a second target appears within roughly 200 to 500 ms of a first target in a rapid stream, it is often missed. Sergent, Baillet and Dehaene (2005) found that when the second target was missed, early sensory responses were preserved, but the later, widespread, frontoparietal activity and the late positivity were absent, and that the late activity was all-or-none, large when the target was seen and absent when it was not.
- **Masking.** Del Cul, Baillet and Dehaene (2007) varied the delay between a target and a mask and found a nonlinear transition in reported visibility and a late signal that changed abruptly, in a pattern consistent with a threshold.
- **Unconscious processing.** Masked stimuli can be processed in sensory and even semantic areas without ignition, which fits the claim that ignition separates the conscious from the unconscious.

### Objections

1. **The report confound.** Many signals attributed to ignition, the late positivity and frontal activity, are reduced when report is not required (week 2). Defenders reply that no-report conditions reduce attention and the activity may be weakened, not absent.
2. **Overflow.** If partial-report experiments show that phenomenology exceeds access (week 1), a theory that equates consciousness with access misses some experience. Defenders reply that the evidence for overflow is not decisive.
3. **All-or-none versus graded.** Some studies find that awareness is graded, with many participants reporting partial visibility (Overgaard and colleagues, 2006). Defenders reply that the all-or-none claim applies to the neural access of a given representation, and graded reports reflect representations that are unstable or mixed.
4. **The frontal lobes.** Extensive frontal lesions do not obviously abolish experience, and a theory that gives prefrontal cortex a central role must say what is lost.
5. **The explanatory gap.** Broadcast explains access and report, which are easy problems; critics say it leaves open why broadcast is felt. Proponents reply that the theory addresses what is measurable and that the remaining question may dissolve (week 1).
6. **Scope.** Which animals and systems have a global workspace? The theory says it depends on architecture, not on species, and that is what makes its extension to animals and machines both possible and contested.

In the adversarial collaboration of week 10, the GNW prediction that content is represented in prefrontal cortex and that ignition occurs at both stimulus onset and offset was tested; as week 10 reports, the results challenged key predictions of this theory, and what that shows for it is still debated.

@example
title: Conditional accuracy and blink depth
problem: In an attentional blink task, the first target is correct on 80% of trials, and both targets are correct on 44% of trials at a short lag. At a long lag, the second target is correct on 88% of trials given a correct first target. What is the conditional accuracy at the short lag, and how deep is the blink?
step: Conditional accuracy is $P(T2\mid T1) = P(\text{both})/P(T1) = 0.44/0.80 = 0.55$.
step: Blink depth is the long-lag accuracy minus the short-lag accuracy, both conditional on a correct first target: $0.88 - 0.55 = 0.33$.
step: Interpret: for one in three trials in which the first target was seen, the second target, presented briefly afterwards, was lost, which is the dip the workspace account attributes to the limited capacity of access.
answer: Conditional accuracy 0.55 and a blink depth of 0.33.

@mistakes
- Equating the global workspace with prefrontal cortex alone. The neuronal version recruits a distributed frontoparietal network with long-range connections.
- Taking ignition to mean that every conscious state involves a single event at the same latency. The timing is estimated from averages across trials and conditions.
- Stating that the theory has been confirmed or refuted by the adversarial collaboration. The results challenged key predictions, and their implications for the theory remain debated.

@practice self
prompt: In about 250 words, give the strongest case for the global workspace theory and the strongest objection, and say what the theory would need to show to answer it.
hint: Use the attentional-blink and masking evidence for the case, and the report confound or overflow for the objection.
solution: The strongest case is that the theory ties a precise architecture to a specific neural event and explains why consciousness is limited, unified-seeming and reportable. Attentional blink and masking studies show that when a stimulus is not seen, sensory processing is retained while a late, widespread, all-or-none frontoparietal response is missing, and that the transition has a nonlinear, threshold-like profile, which is what ignition predicts; unconscious processing of masked stimuli shows that much can be done without the workspace. The strongest objection is the report confound. Late positivity and frontal activity are largely seen when participants must report or when stimuli are task relevant, and in several no-report designs they shrink or vanish while earlier posterior signals persist, so ignition may reflect post-perceptual access and report, not conscious experience. A related objection is that if experience overflows access, a theory of access cannot account for it. To answer, the theory would need to show ignition-like signatures in no-report designs with matched attention and relevance, or give an independent argument for why a no-report reduction is expected on its own terms; it would also need a way to distinguish ignition-without-report from report preparation, for example content-specific decoding in frontoparietal cortex when participants are not reporting. Its defenders can also note that the theory is about conscious access and that phenomenal consciousness without any access is not a quantity the data could reveal.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain in about 250 words how a defender of the global neuronal workspace should answer the claim that awareness is graded, and what the critic should say in return.
hint: Distinguish the graded reports from the all-or-none neural access of a given representation.
solution: The critic's evidence is that, with scales that allow intermediate ratings, participants often report partial or vague visibility, and that these ratings vary continuously with stimulus strength, which seems to contradict all-or-none ignition (Overgaard and colleagues, 2006). The defender can reply in three ways. First, the all-or-none claim is about the neural state of a particular representation on a particular trial: ignition either occurs or does not, and graded behavior can arise from averaging across trials or across neurons and features. Second, a stimulus may be partly accessed, with ignition of some features or a unstable one, so that a graded report reflects mixtures of accessed and non-accessed information. Third, the reports themselves are influenced by criteria and by the number of scale categories offered, and dichotomous results have been found in designs, such as the attentional blink, where the stimulus and the task are held constant. The critic's reply is that these moves make the theory harder to test: if graded reports can be explained by trial mixtures, partial ignition and criterion effects, what pattern of reports would count against it? The critic would also point out that trial-by-trial neural data are noisy, so the claim that ignition is binary on single trials requires decoding that is difficult to achieve. The disagreement therefore turns on whether the theory commits to a specific, measurable single-trial signature, and both sides would gain from preregistered predictions about the distribution of single-trial signals.
