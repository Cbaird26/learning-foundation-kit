@article
A person's eyes are open and they follow no one with their gaze. Another person's eyes are closed and they cannot be roused. A third cannot move or speak but is wide awake inside. Clinicians must tell these cases apart, and for the science of consciousness they are natural experiments on the *level* of consciousness (week 1). This lesson describes them at the level of a textbook; it contains no clinical or treatment guidance, and decisions about real patients belong to clinicians and families.

### The disorders

- **Coma**: eyes closed, no sleep-wake cycles, no purposeful behavior. It is usually temporary, evolving over weeks into other states.
- **Unresponsive wakefulness syndrome** (formerly called the vegetative state): cycles of eye opening and closing, but only reflexive behavior and no signs of awareness of self or environment.
- **Minimally conscious state**: inconsistent but reproducible signs of awareness, such as following a simple command or tracking an object. Subcategories are based on whether the behaviors involve language (Bruno and colleagues, 2011).
- **Locked-in syndrome**: awareness and cognition are preserved, but nearly all voluntary movement is lost, often leaving only vertical eye movements. It is not a disorder of consciousness, but it illustrates that behavior can fail to reveal it.
- **Death determined by neurologic criteria** (brain death): the irreversible cessation of all functions of the brain, including the brainstem. It is accepted in most countries, with procedures set by national guidelines and law; in the United States a 2023 consensus guideline from several medical societies covers adults and children.

Bedside assessment uses scales. The Glasgow Coma Scale (Teasdale and Jennett, 1974) scores eye opening (1 to 4), verbal response (1 to 5) and motor response (1 to 6), for a total between 3 and 15, and the Coma Recovery Scale–Revised (Giacino and colleagues, 2004) scores finer-grained behavior for distinguishing unresponsive wakefulness from the minimally conscious state.

### Covert awareness

Behavior is an unreliable guide because awareness may be present when behavior is absent. Owen and colleagues (2006) asked a patient diagnosed as being in a vegetative state to imagine playing tennis or walking through her house while she was in an fMRI scanner. Her brain activity resembled that of healthy volunteers: motor-planning regions for tennis and spatial-navigation regions for the house. A later study (Monti and colleagues, 2010) found that some of a larger group of patients could modulate such activity on command, and one answered questions this way. This pattern is now called **cognitive motor dissociation**. Studies with EEG have since reported covert command-following in a substantial minority of behaviorally unresponsive patients.

The inference has limits. A positive result is strong evidence of willful, task-following activity; a negative result is uninterpretable, since patients may fail because of fatigue, hearing or language deficits or fluctuating arousal. A skeptic can also ask whether the response could be automatic, though the specificity of the activity to the instruction makes this hard to maintain.

### Perturbational markers

The **perturbational complexity index** (Casali and colleagues, 2013) is computed from the EEG response to a pulse of transcranial magnetic stimulation: a response that is both widespread (integrated) and varied over time and place (differentiated) has higher compressed complexity. In a benchmark set it separated wakefulness and dreaming from deep non-REM sleep and anesthesia, and values in minimally conscious patients exceeded those in patients in unresponsive wakefulness, with the reported cut-off chosen empirically. The measure needs no behavior and no task, which is its attraction. Whether it measures consciousness or something correlated with it is a theoretical question: theories that emphasize integration and differentiation draw support from it, and other theories note that it is also consistent with their accounts.

### Anesthesia and sleep

General anesthetics are a family of agents with different molecular targets, producing loss of responsiveness together with characteristic EEG changes, for example slow and frontal rhythms, and a loss of effective long-range and feedback communication in cortex. Sleep has structure: in deep non-REM sleep, cortical neurons alternate between active and silent periods, and the response to stimulation is local and brief; REM sleep has a wake-like EEG and vivid dreams. A key lesson is that **unresponsiveness is not unconsciousness** (Sanders, Tononi, Laureys and Sleigh, 2012): people awakened from non-REM sleep often report dream experience, and sedated or anesthetized people may have experiences without behavior, so one distinguishes consciousness that is *connected* to the environment from consciousness that is *disconnected*. Siclari and colleagues (2017) found that a local decrease in slow-wave activity in a posterior cortical region predicted dream reports on awakening, in both REM and non-REM sleep, a result discussed in the debate over a posterior substrate (week 3).

Every theory in weeks 5 to 9 gives some account of these states, whether as loss of ignition, loss of feedback, loss of integration or a change in precision, and the data so far fit more than one.

@example
title: Sensitivity, specificity and predictive value
problem: A marker is tested in 40 patients. By an independent standard, 24 are conscious and 16 are not. The marker is positive in 21 of the conscious patients and in 3 of the unconscious ones. Compute sensitivity, specificity and positive predictive value.
step: Sensitivity is the proportion of conscious patients with a positive marker: $21/24 = 0.875$.
step: Specificity is the proportion of unconscious patients with a negative marker: $(16 - 3)/16 = 13/16 = 0.8125$.
step: Positive predictive value is the proportion of positives that are conscious: $21/(21 + 3) = 0.875$.
step: Caution: these values depend on the standard used and on the mix of patients; if the independent standard itself misses covert awareness, some "false positives" may be true positives.
answer: Sensitivity 0.875, specificity 0.8125, positive predictive value 0.875.

@mistakes
- Calling locked-in syndrome a disorder of consciousness. Awareness is preserved; what is lost is movement.
- Reading a negative result in a command-following task as showing the patient is unconscious. It is uninterpretable.
- Treating the unresponsive state as unconscious in sleep and anesthesia. Dreaming and connected or disconnected experience occur in both.

@practice self
prompt: In about 250 words, assess the inference from "this patient's brain activity differs between imagining tennis and imagining navigating, on command" to "this patient is conscious." Give the strongest case, the strongest objections, and what a negative result would and would not show.
hint: Consider specificity to the instruction, alternative explanations such as automatic responses, and false negatives.
solution: The strongest case is that the pattern matches the one in healthy volunteers, depends on the content of the instruction, and is sustained for the length of the instruction, which requires understanding the command, selecting a mental task, and maintaining it; it is difficult to produce without some form of awareness, since reflexes or priming should not differentiate two imagery tasks on cue and keep the pattern for 30 seconds. It thus counts as evidence of willful, task-following activity, which is the criterion clinicians use for awareness at the bedside. Objections are that the activity could in principle be an automatic response to the words, with semantic processing of "tennis" activating motor regions, that group-level statistics may overstate single-patient reliability unless the analysis is validated, that willful activity might not imply phenomenal experience for those who separate them, and that a positive result in one session says little about the patient's usual state, which may fluctuate. Defenders answer with controls: testing that activity stays when instructions are paired with other tasks, that it is time-locked to instruction onset and offset, and that it can be used to answer questions. A negative result would show very little, since the patient could fail from fatigue, hearing or language deficits, fluctuating arousal, or the difficulty of the task, so it cannot be interpreted as absence of awareness. This asymmetry has ethical consequences for how results are reported to families, which should be done by clinicians with these limits explained.

@quiz self
prompt: Choose two theories from weeks 5 to 9 and explain in about 250 words how each would describe the loss of consciousness in deep non-REM sleep or general anesthesia, and what finding about reports of dreaming in non-REM sleep would pose a challenge or an opportunity for each.
hint: Link each theory's key quantity (ignition, recurrence, integration and so on) to the physiology of the state, and address the dream reports.
solution: A global workspace account describes deep non-REM sleep and anesthesia as states in which ignition and long-range communication fail, so sensory input is processed locally but not broadcast; it predicts that experience in these states, if any, should be associated with some preserved or restored global activity. Dream reports after non-REM awakenings challenge it unless they arise from occasional episodes of broadcast or from memory reconstruction at waking; they are an opportunity to test which neural events predict reports. An integrated information account describes the cortex in deep non-REM sleep as bistable, with periods of silence that reduce the system's capacity for differentiated, integrated causal structure, so its structure collapses; it predicts that dreaming in non-REM should accompany local restoration of integrated activity in the posterior cortex. The finding of Siclari and colleagues that local reductions of slow waves in posterior regions predict dream reports fits this, though it is also consistent with accounts that locate experience in the posterior cortex without invoking integration. Each theory can accommodate the dream reports by adding assumptions about where and when the relevant state occurs, so the finding constrains the theories only if the assumptions are stated in advance; a further complication is that reports of dreaming are retrospective, so they could reflect memory formed at awakening and not experience during sleep.
