@article
Helmholtz suggested in the nineteenth century that perception is a kind of unconscious inference: the brain infers the likely causes of its sensory input. Modern **predictive processing** makes the idea quantitative. The brain, on this view, is a hierarchical generative model that constantly predicts its input, and what it sends upward is not the input but the **prediction error**, the part of the input not explained by the prediction.

### Predictive coding and the free-energy principle

Rao and Ballard (1999) built a hierarchical model in which higher levels send predictions of lower-level activity downward and lower levels return residual errors upward, and showed that it reproduced some extra-classical receptive field effects in visual cortex. Friston (2010) generalized the idea to the **free-energy principle**: any system that maintains itself in a changing world must keep the states it visits within a limited range, which can be described as minimizing a quantity called variational free energy, an upper bound on the surprise of its sensory states. Minimizing it can be done by changing beliefs to fit the input (perception) or by acting to change the input (**active inference**). Clark (2013) offered an accessible synthesis for cognitive science.

In the simplest Gaussian case the computation is precision-weighted averaging. Let the prior prediction have mean $\mu_p$ and variance $\sigma_p^2$, and the sensory estimate have mean $x$ and variance $\sigma_s^2$. Define **precision** as inverse variance, $\pi = 1/\sigma^2$. The posterior mean is

$$\mu = \frac{\pi_p\,\mu_p + \pi_s\,x}{\pi_p + \pi_s}, \qquad \pi_{\text{post}} = \pi_p + \pi_s ,$$

so the more precise source gets more weight. In an iterative scheme, the estimate moves by a fraction of the prediction error $\varepsilon = x - \mu$ at each step, $\mu \leftarrow \mu + k\,\varepsilon$, where the gain $k$ reflects the relative precision of the error. Attention has been modeled as an increase of the precision assigned to prediction errors from attended sources (Feldman and Friston, 2010), which links the framework to week 5's questions about access.

### Predictive processing and consciousness

Several authors have asked whether the framework can explain consciousness. Hohwy, Roepstorff and Friston (2008) proposed that binocular rivalry arises because neither percept explains all the input: the dominant percept leaves unexplained prediction error from the suppressed eye, and this drives a switch. Seth and colleagues emphasize **interoceptive inference**, the idea that experiences of emotion and of being a self arise from predictions about the body's internal state (Seth, 2013), and describe perception as a "controlled hallucination" constrained by sensory data. Seth and Hohwy (2021) argued that predictive processing can serve as an empirical program for consciousness science, in which one explains specific properties of experience by mechanisms (the "real problem") without claiming to solve the hard problem. The conscious content is, on many versions, the winning hypothesis, or the posterior, and not the error signal.

### Evidence and objections

Supporting findings include effects of expectations on perception, reduced neural responses to expected stimuli (expectation suppression) and mismatch responses to violations. These are compatible with the framework, but other theories predict them as well, and evidence for enhancement as well as suppression by expectation shows that results are mixed.

1. **Framework versus theory.** Predictive processing is a general account of brain function, and it is not specific about which predictions or inferences are conscious. Without a further criterion, such as broadcast to a workspace or a depth of temporal modeling, it does not distinguish conscious from unconscious inference, and Seth and Hohwy themselves present it as complementary to other theories.
2. **Flexibility.** The framework can absorb many results, since a mismatch can be attributed to a change in precision. A critic asks what pattern of data would count against it.
3. **Neural implementation.** The cortical microcircuit implementation of prediction and error units is debated, and evidence for distinct error-carrying populations is mixed.
4. **The dark-room problem.** If organisms minimize surprise, why do they not sit in a dark, quiet room? The standard reply is that organisms have strong prior preferences for certain states, and that active inference minimizes expected free energy, which includes exploration; critics ask whether this makes the principle empty.
5. **The hard problem.** Like other theories, it explains functions; the question of why inference is felt remains. Defenders reply that explaining properties of experience is the realistic goal.

@example
title: A precision-weighted estimate
problem: A prior predicts a size of 20 units with variance 16. A sensory estimate gives 28 units with variance 4. Find the posterior mean and variance.
step: Convert variances to precisions: $\pi_p = 1/16 = 0.0625$ and $\pi_s = 1/4 = 0.25$.
step: Add precisions for the posterior: $\pi_{\text{post}} = 0.3125$, so the variance is $1/0.3125 = 3.2$.
step: Weighted average: $\mu = (0.0625\times20 + 0.25\times28)/0.3125 = (1.25 + 7)/0.3125 = 26.4$.
step: Check: the estimate lies between the prior (20) and the data (28), and nearer the more precise source, the sensory estimate; the posterior variance 3.2 is smaller than either input variance.
answer: Posterior mean 26.4 with variance 3.2.

@mistakes
- Saying that the brain sends predictions up and errors down. In predictive coding, predictions descend and prediction errors ascend.
- Treating the free-energy principle as a testable claim about a specific neural mechanism. It is a general principle, and specific process theories are what make predictions.
- Assuming predictive processing is a theory of consciousness by itself. Proponents themselves usually pair it with further claims about which inferences are conscious.

@practice self
prompt: In about 250 words, evaluate this claim: "Predictive processing is a theory of consciousness." Say in what sense it is and is not, and what additional commitment would make it a theory.
hint: Separate a general account of brain function from a criterion for what is conscious.
solution: Predictive processing is a theory of how the brain does perception and action, by generating predictions and minimizing prediction error, with the free-energy principle as its general statement. It is a theory of consciousness only in the sense that it offers a way to explain properties of experience: for example, why perception is a unified, constructive best guess, why rivalry alternates, why the experience of the body and the self may be grounded in predictions about internal states, and why expectations shape what we see (Seth and Hohwy, 2021). In that sense it supports a research program for explaining properties of experience by mechanism. It is not a theory of consciousness in the sense of specifying which states are conscious, because the machinery runs in unconscious perception, in sleeping brains and perhaps in many systems. To become a theory it needs an additional commitment, such as that the content of the highest level's winning hypothesis is the content of experience, or that experience requires hypotheses with counterfactual or temporal depth, or that the hypothesis must be globally broadcast, which would combine it with workspace theory. Each such commitment makes testable predictions: that experience should follow the posterior and not the prediction error, or should require broadcast. Until one is adopted, an observation such as an effect of expectation on awareness can be accommodated by almost any account of awareness, so the framework's explanatory reach exceeds its discriminating power.

@quiz self
prompt: In about 250 words, present the predictive processing account of binocular rivalry as strongly as you can, and give one alternative account and one way that data could separate them.
hint: Consider unexplained prediction error from the suppressed eye, and alternatives based on mutual inhibition and adaptation.
solution: On the predictive account (Hohwy, Roepstorff and Friston, 2008), the brain tries to find a hypothesis that explains the input from both eyes. Each percept, face or house, explains the input from one eye well but leaves the other eye's input as unexplained prediction error, so neither hypothesis is stable. The system settles for the hypothesis with the lower error, which then suppresses the other. As the dominant hypothesis stays in place, the unexplained error grows relative to the alternative, which makes the alternative more likely to win, and the percept switches. The account explains why rivalry occurs when inputs cannot be fused, and it suggests that priors and precision should influence dominance, as they seem to when expectations or attention bias rivalry. An alternative account is based on mutual inhibition between populations that represent the two percepts, together with slow adaptation of the dominant population, which produces alternation without a generative model. Data could separate them if the accounts predict different effects of manipulations that change expectations but leave low-level adaptation unchanged: for example, a strong prior that favors one percept, if it changes the proportion of time but not the rate of switching in the way the predictive account says; or neural data showing that signals attributed to prediction error from the suppressed eye grow before a switch. A difficulty is that both accounts have free parameters and can usually be adjusted to fit, so the comparison requires specific quantitative predictions made in advance.

rubric: Gives a genuine alternative account (mutual inhibition and adaptation).
