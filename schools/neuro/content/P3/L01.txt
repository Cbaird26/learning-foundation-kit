@article
A photodiode responds to red light. A camera sorts red objects from green ones. A visual system can be described in full: wavelength-tuned cones, opponent channels, cortical areas, a verbal report "that apple is red." Suppose every one of these mechanisms were explained to the last synapse. Many researchers feel that one question would still be open: why is any of it accompanied by the experience of redness? Others feel that this feeling is the thing to be explained away. This course starts from that disagreement, and as of 2026 there is no scientific or philosophical consensus on how consciousness arises or on what would settle the question.

### Senses of the word

Three uses must be kept apart. **Creature consciousness** is being awake and responsive, as opposed to being in a coma or in dreamless sleep. **State** or **level** consciousness varies along a scale from deep anesthesia through sleep to alert wakefulness. **Content** consciousness concerns what a creature is conscious *of*: the red apple, a pain, a thought. Research on levels (anesthesia, disorders of consciousness) and on contents (masking, rivalry) uses different methods and, as later weeks show, different theories emphasize one or the other.

A second distinction, due to Block (1995), separates **phenomenal consciousness**, the experiential character of a state (what it is like, in Nagel's 1974 phrase for the bat), from **access consciousness**, a state's availability for reasoning, report and the control of action. Whether these can come apart is itself disputed.

### The partial-report experiment as a test case

In Sperling's 1960 experiments a grid of letters flashes for a fraction of a second. If observers report as many letters as they can, they name about four of twelve. If a tone just after the flash cues one row, they report most of that row, whichever row it is. Suppose they get three of the four cued letters right. If the cue was random, then about $\tfrac{3}{4}$ of the letters in *every* row must have been available when the cue arrived, an estimate of $\tfrac{3}{4}\times 12 = 9$ letters, far above the four reported in the whole-report condition.

Block argues that this is evidence that phenomenology overflows access: observers had a rich experience of the whole grid but could access only a few letters. Critics reply that the cue may itself create the availability, that observers may hold only unspecific information such as "letters" for the uncued rows, and that "rich experience" is just what cannot be checked without a report. The case shows the structure of the whole field: the data are agreed, and the interpretation turns on how phenomenal and access consciousness are related.

### Easy problems and the hard problem

Chalmers (1995) divided the questions. The **easy problems** are those that are, in principle, answerable by specifying a mechanism that performs a function: discriminating and categorizing stimuli, integrating information, reporting mental states, focusing attention, controlling behavior deliberately, the difference between wakefulness and sleep. They are "easy" only in that we know what an answer looks like; they are hard in practice. The **hard problem** is why the performance of these functions is accompanied by experience at all. Levine (1983) called the corresponding difficulty the **explanatory gap**: unlike the identity of water with H$_2$O, which makes the liquid's properties intelligible, a physical account of a brain state does not seem to make intelligible why that state feels as it does.

### Five responses, in their strongest forms

1. **Deflationary functionalism.** Once all functions, including those of introspection and report, are explained, nothing remains. The feeling that something is left over is itself a product of the cognitive system. Objection: the explanation of functions seems silent on why they are felt, and the position seems to redefine the explanandum.
2. **Illusionism.** Phenomenal properties as philosophers conceive them do not exist; the brain represents itself as having them. The task becomes the *meta-problem* (Chalmers 2018): why do we judge that there is a hard problem? Objection: an illusion of experience seems to presuppose experience.
3. **A posteriori physicalism.** Experiences are identical to brain states, and the gap is in our concepts, not in nature, as with water and H$_2$O. Objection: the identity, unlike those in chemistry, is said to lack the explanatory payoff that made the others acceptable.
4. **Non-reductive views.** Experience is fundamental, as in property dualism, or pervasive, as in panpsychism, or the intrinsic nature behind physical structure. Objection: such views risk being unfalsifiable and face a combination problem for how micro-experiences form a unified mind.
5. **Methodological pragmatism.** Whatever the metaphysics, one can find the mechanisms that correlate with, and then explain, specific properties of experience; this is the strategy behind most of the experiments in this course.

You need not choose among these to do the science, but you should know which of them an experiment or theory presupposes.

@example
title: What partial report implies
problem: A grid has 3 rows of 4 letters. In whole report an observer names 4.2 letters on average. When a cue after the flash points to one randomly chosen row, the observer names 2.6 of its 4 letters correctly on average. How many letters does the partial-report logic say were available, and how does this compare with whole report?
step: Compute the fraction available per row: $2.6/4 = 0.65$.
step: Because the cue was random, every row had this availability, so the estimate for the grid is $0.65 \times 12 = 7.8$ letters.
step: Compare: $7.8$ is well above the $4.2$ of whole report. The data say that more was available at cue time than was reported without a cue.
step: State what this does not show: that the extra letters were *experienced* in detail. It shows availability to a later cue; the overflow reading and the reading in terms of limited gist or cue-created availability both predict these numbers.
answer: 7.8 letters available, against 4.2 reported; the numbers fit both the overflow and the limited-access interpretations.

@mistakes
- Treating "the hard problem" as the claim that consciousness is non-physical. It is a question about explanation; many physicalists and many dualists agree that the question is posed, and disagree about its answer (others deny that it is a distinct problem).
- Calling the easy problems trivial. They are easy only in the sense that a mechanistic answer is in principle available; many are unsolved.
- Concluding from Sperling-type data that phenomenology overflows access. The result shows that more information was available to a cue than was reported without it; whether that information was experienced is the open question.

choice*: A complete physical account of a brain state does not make intelligible why that state is the experience it is, unlike an account of water as H$_2$O that makes its properties intelligible.

@practice self
prompt: In about 250 words, state the hard problem and the strongest reply from a functionalist or illusionist who denies that it is a distinct problem. Then give the strongest remaining objection and say what kind of evidence, if any, could bear on the dispute.
hint: Distinguish the functions to be explained from the question of why they are felt, and put the reply in its best form before objecting.
solution: The hard problem asks why the performance of cognitive functions, such as discrimination, integration and report, is accompanied by experience, when mechanisms explaining those functions seem to leave this question open (Chalmers 1995). The strongest functionalist or illusionist reply is that the intuition of a residue is itself a cognitive phenomenon that needs explaining. Our introspective systems represent our states as having ineffable, intrinsic qualities; this representation drives our judgments that there is something more to explain, and a complete theory of the mechanisms behind those judgments (the meta-problem) leaves nothing further. The analogy is to the historical dissolution of the question about what makes living things alive once the functions of life were explained. The strongest objection is that the reply seems to deny the datum: if it seems to me that I have an experience of red, the seeming is itself an experience, so an illusion of experience presupposes what it denies. The illusionist answers that the seeming can be a representation without phenomenal character, but this makes the position depend on a contested reading of what the datum is. Evidence bears on the dispute only indirectly. Work on the meta-problem could show whether the judgments about ineffability are produced by identifiable mechanisms, and the success or failure of functional theories at predicting which states are reported as experienced would test whether functional explanation leaves a systematic remainder; but neither result forces the choice, because each side can reinterpret the same data.

@quiz self
prompt: A colleague argues: "Explaining all the easy problems will dissolve the hard problem, as explaining the functions of life dissolved vitalism." Give the best argument for the analogy and the best argument that it fails, in about 250 words, and say which empirical developments would strengthen each side.
hint: Ask what was left to explain in the case of life and whether the same can be said of experience.
solution: The best case for the analogy is historical: vitalists believed that no mechanism could explain growth, metabolism and reproduction, and once those functions were explained the question of an extra vital essence lost its grip. Proponents say that the intuition of an explanatory gap for experience is of the same kind, an artifact of how little we understood the mechanisms, and that it will weaken as accounts of attention, report and introspection mature; the meta-problem makes this concrete by treating the intuition as a datum to be explained. The best case against the analogy is that life was defined functionally from the start, so explaining the functions explained life, whereas the explanandum in consciousness is, on the opposing view, not a function but the felt character of experience, so a functional explanation seems to change the subject. The disanalogy is therefore about what the target is. Developments that would strengthen the analogy include successful mechanistic accounts that predict, in detail, the judgments people make about their own experience, including which stimuli seem ineffable, with intuitions about a gap weakening in those who know the theory. Developments that would strengthen the disanalogy include theories that explain every functional dissociation yet continue to leave principled disagreement about whether the explained systems are conscious, as in debates about animals and machines. Neither development settles the matter, but each shifts the burden of argument.
rubric: Gives the vitalism analogy in its strongest form and identifies what was explained in that case.
rubric: Identifies the disanalogy about the nature of the explanandum.
rubric: Names empirical developments that would favor each side, without declaring a winner.
