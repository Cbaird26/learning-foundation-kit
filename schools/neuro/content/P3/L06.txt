@article
A visual system can respond to a red patch without you being aware of it, as in blindsight (on the usual description) or after a masked flash. What makes the difference between a visual state of which you are aware and one of which you are not? **Higher-order theories** give a striking answer: a mental state is conscious when the subject has a suitable higher-order representation of being in that state. The consciousness of the state is not an intrinsic property of the state, or a matter of its broadcast, but a matter of how the mind represents itself.

### The core claim and its variants

On Rosenthal's **higher-order thought** (HOT) theory, a state is conscious when one has a thought, roughly non-inferential and assertoric, that one is in it. Variants differ on the nature of the higher-order state. **Higher-order perception** or inner-sense theories treat it as a quasi-perceptual scanning of one's own states. **Dispositional** versions (Carruthers) require only the disposition to form such a thought. **Self-representational** theories (Kriegel) place the higher-order content within the state itself. In the neuroscientific literature, Lau and Rosenthal (2011) argued that the theory is supported by data on metacognition and awareness, Brown, Lau and LeDoux (2019) set out the higher-order approach in cognitive neuroscience, and Fleming (2020) proposed that awareness is an inference, in a higher-order state space, about whether a first-order representation reflects the world. Lau's **perceptual reality monitoring** view holds that a first-order representation is experienced when a higher-order system judges it to be a reliable representation of the current external world.

Higher-order theories predict that awareness depends on a metarepresentational system, usually associated with prefrontal and related cortex, and that awareness can dissociate from first-order sensitivity.

### Evidence in its strongest form

The most cited evidence is the performance-matched dissociation: if two conditions produce equal first-order performance, but reported awareness differs, awareness cannot simply be first-order sensitivity. Lau and Passingham (2006) matched discrimination across masking delays and found that visibility reports differed and were accompanied by differing dorsolateral prefrontal activity (week 4). Defenders also cite the dependence of subjective ratings on metacognitive mechanisms, evidence that confidence and visibility depend on prefrontal circuits and, as in Rosenthal's account, the explanation of why we can have rich unconscious processing: first-order states without higher-order representation.

### Objections

1. **Misrepresentation and "empty" higher-order states.** If a higher-order thought represents oneself as seeing red when there is no first-order state, what is experienced: the red, or nothing? Rosenthal accepts that what it is like is determined by the higher-order content, even in the absence of a target; critics find this implausible. Others dispute whether this is a problem.
2. **Over-intellectualization: animals and infants.** If awareness requires thoughts about one's own states, creatures without the relevant concepts or prefrontal development might lack experience, which many find implausible. Defenders reply that the required representations are minimal and nonconceptual, or that the theory may have counterintuitive implications; proponents of perceptual reality monitoring say it needs only simple systems of reliability assessment.
3. **Frontal lesions.** Extensive prefrontal damage does not obviously remove experience, though defenders reply that the relevant frontal circuitry may be spared or that the tests are insensitive.
4. **Confidence is not awareness.** Dissociations in confidence and visibility could reflect response criteria, not awareness itself, so the performance-matched evidence needs controls for criterion.
5. **Why does representing a state as experienced make it experienced?** Like the workspace, the theory is said to leave the hard problem open; defenders say it explains the difference between conscious and unconscious states, which is the tractable part.

### Separating higher-order from workspace theories

Both theories predict prefrontal involvement in awareness, so a prefrontal effect alone cannot separate them (their likelihood ratio is close to one). Distinguishing predictions come from what each treats as essential. A workspace theory holds that being globally broadcast to many consumer systems is essential; a higher-order theory holds that being represented as one's own current state is essential, whether or not it is broadcast. Candidate separating experiments therefore manipulate one without the other: for example, interfering with metacognitive monitoring of a first-order representation while leaving its broadcast to action and report systems intact, or the converse.

@example
title: Can a shared prediction separate two theories?
problem: Both theory A and theory B predict that prefrontal activity increases with awareness. Suppose the probability of observing a prefrontal increase is 0.9 under A and 0.8 under B. What is the likelihood ratio, and how much does the observation favor A?
step: The likelihood ratio is the probability of the observation under A divided by that under B: $0.9/0.8 = 1.125$.
step: With equal prior odds, posterior odds equal the likelihood ratio, so the posterior probability of A is $1.125/(1+1.125) = 0.529$.
step: Interpret: an observation that both theories expect moves belief from 0.5 to only 0.53; to separate theories one needs a prediction on which they disagree strongly, such as probabilities of 0.9 and 0.1, giving a ratio of 9.
answer: Likelihood ratio 1.125, posterior about 0.53: a shared prediction barely discriminates.

@mistakes
- Saying that higher-order theories require explicit, conceptual, language-like thoughts. Several variants require only minimal or nonconceptual higher-order representation.
- Treating a prefrontal correlate of awareness as evidence for higher-order theories over workspace theories. Both predict it.
- Reading a dissociation between confidence and accuracy as showing that awareness is metacognition without checking criterion effects.

@practice self
prompt: State two predictions that separate a higher-order theory from a global workspace theory, each as an experiment with a manipulation, a measurement and the result each theory expects. About 250 words.
hint: Manipulate metacognitive monitoring without changing broadcast, or the reverse, and say what each theory expects in each case.
solution: Prediction one: disrupt higher-order monitoring while leaving access intact. Present a stimulus at a contrast that yields matched discrimination and apply a manipulation that lowers the quality of the monitoring system's assessment of the first-order signal, for example an interfering noise component that changes the reliability of the signal without changing its content, or transient perturbation of the prefrontal regions thought to support monitoring. Measure forced-choice accuracy, visibility ratings and the availability of the content for report and action. A higher-order theory expects visibility to fall with matched accuracy, since the content is no longer represented as reliable. A workspace theory expects visibility to follow whether the content is broadcast, and, if accuracy and use of the content remain intact, expects visibility to be unchanged. Prediction two: produce broadcast without self-representation. Use a stimulus that is made task relevant and drives action, so that it ignites the workspace and is used flexibly, under conditions, such as high noise in the monitoring signal or a prior that the stimulus is a hallucination, in which the subject should not judge it to represent the current world. The workspace theory expects the content to be conscious; the higher-order theory expects it to be unreported as seen or experienced differently. For each, the investigators must fix beforehand what outcome counts against each theory, and must show with criterion-free measures that the manipulation does not simply change response bias.
rubric: Gives two distinct experiments, each with a manipulation, measure and expected result for each theory.
rubric: The predictions genuinely differ between the two theories rather than both predicting a prefrontal effect.
rubric: Notes controls for response bias and the need to fix predictions in advance.

@quiz self
prompt: In about 250 words, state the "empty higher-order state" objection as strongly as you can, give the best reply, and say whether any evidence could bear on the disagreement.
hint: Consider what Rosenthal's view says about the experienced content when there is no target.
solution: The objection is that if experience is determined by the higher-order representation, then a higher-order thought to the effect that I am seeing red, occurring when there is no red first-order state, would produce an experience of red even though nothing visual occurred. Thus the theory seems to detach experience from the visual state it is supposed to be about, and it makes consciousness depend on a state that may be a mere thought. The best reply is to accept the consequence and to argue that it is not absurd: what it is like for me is how things seem to me, so a mismatch between a first-order state and a higher-order content is a case of misrepresentation, as in hallucination or illusion, and the theory says the experienced character follows the higher-order content. The reply adds that such cases are rare and that evidence from hallucinations and from confabulated experience is at least consistent with it. Critics respond that this makes the first-order state irrelevant to experience, so that nothing explains why we experience the world as we do, and that the account of hallucination is not evidence for the claim that all experience is fixed by the higher-order content. Evidence could bear indirectly: cases where higher-order content and first-order content come apart, such as manipulations of confidence or of perceptual reality judgments, could show whether experienced content follows the higher-order or the first-order state; but because the result depends on how the experienced content is measured, which is by report, the dispute is difficult to settle empirically.
rubric: States the objection accurately and in its strongest form.
rubric: Gives the best reply of the theory, not a caricature.
rubric: Explains what evidence could and could not decide the dispute.
