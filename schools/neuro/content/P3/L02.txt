@article
Every experiment on conscious perception depends on a measurement, and the measurements are indirect. Show a faint grating and ask "did you see it?" and the answer mixes what the participant experienced, how willing they were to say yes, and what they thought the experimenter wanted. Consciousness science is, to a large extent, the art of making these inferences safe.

### Objective and subjective measures

An **objective measure** scores performance on a task about the stimulus: a forced choice of orientation, scored right or wrong. A **subjective measure** asks about the experience: a yes/no visibility report, a rating on a scale such as the Perceptual Awareness Scale (Ramsøy and Overgaard, 2004), or a bet on the answer (post-decision wagering). The **objective threshold** is where discrimination falls to chance ($d' = 0$); the **subjective threshold** is where the participant says they see nothing. Perception between the two, with above-chance performance and reports of no awareness, is what claims of unconscious perception rely on (week 4).

Signal detection theory (Green and Swets, 1966) separates **sensitivity** $d' = z(H) - z(F)$ from the **criterion** $c = -\tfrac12\,[z(H) + z(F)]$, where $H$ and $F$ are hit and false-alarm rates and $z$ is the inverse of the standard normal cumulative distribution. A participant who becomes more conservative in saying "seen" changes $c$ with no change in $d'$. A visibility report is a type-1 judgment about the stimulus, so a conservative criterion alone can produce reports of "unseen" on trials with real sensitivity.

### Confidence and metacognition

Asking, after each choice, "how confident are you?" yields a **type-2** task: discriminating one's own correct from incorrect responses. **Metacognitive sensitivity** is how well confidence tracks accuracy. A simple, nonparametric measure is the **type-2 area under the ROC curve**, $\mathrm{AUROC}_2$: the probability that a randomly chosen correct trial received higher confidence than a randomly chosen incorrect one, with ties counted half. It is 0.5 when confidence carries no information about accuracy and 1.0 when it separates them perfectly.

Take four confidence levels with these counts:

| confidence | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| correct trials | 5 | 15 | 25 | 55 |
| incorrect trials | 10 | 10 | 8 | 2 |

There are $100 \times 30 = 3000$ (correct, incorrect) pairs. A pair is won when the correct trial has higher confidence: level 2 correct beats level 1 incorrect, giving $15\times10$; level 3 beats levels 1 and 2, giving $25\times(10+10)$; level 4 beats levels 1 to 3, giving $55\times(10+10+8)$. Wins: $150 + 500 + 1540 = 2190$. Ties: $5\times10 + 15\times10 + 25\times8 + 55\times2 = 510$, counted half, 255. So $\mathrm{AUROC}_2 = (2190+255)/3000 = 0.815$.

The trouble is that $\mathrm{AUROC}_2$ depends on type-1 performance: an observer with high $d'$ makes few errors, and there is less to discriminate. Maniscalco and Lau (2012) proposed **meta-$d'$**: the type-1 $d'$ that an ideal observer would need to produce the observed confidence data. The ratio $M = \text{meta-}d'/d'$ (the **M-ratio**) measures metacognitive efficiency: 1 means confidence uses all the information the choice used. It assumes a signal-detection model and needs enough trials per condition.

### The report confound and no-report paradigms

If a brain signal is larger when a stimulus is reported than when it is not, it may reflect experience, or the act of reporting, deciding and remembering. Aru and colleagues (2012) called the processes that precede or follow experience without being part of it **prerequisites** and **consequences**; report is full of them.

**No-report paradigms** (Tsuchiya and colleagues, 2015) infer the content of experience from involuntary or incidental indicators, such as optokinetic nystagmus or pupil size tracking which image dominates in binocular rivalry, or from a physiological marker first calibrated against reports. Their promise is to remove frontal and motor activity tied to reporting. Their costs are real: the indicator is validated by report in the first place; participants may still think about the stimulus; and removing report removes the only direct access to experience we have, so the signal that disappears may be a correlate, a prerequisite, or a consequence. Mixed designs that compare report and no-report conditions are more informative than either alone.

@example
title: Type-2 AUROC from three confidence levels
problem: In a discrimination task with three confidence levels (low, medium, high), the numbers of correct trials are 4, 8 and 12 and the numbers of incorrect trials are 6, 5 and 3. Compute $\mathrm{AUROC}_2$ to three decimal places.
step: Count the pairs: there are $24$ correct and $14$ incorrect trials, so $24 \times 14 = 336$ pairs.
step: Count wins, where the correct trial has the higher confidence. Medium correct beats low incorrect: $8\times 6 = 48$. High correct beats low and medium incorrect: $12\times(6+5) = 132$. Total wins: $180$.
step: Count ties: $4\times6 + 8\times5 + 12\times3 = 24 + 40 + 36 = 100$, counted half: $50$.
step: Combine: $(180 + 50)/336 = 0.6845$, so $\mathrm{AUROC}_2 \approx 0.685$.
answer: 0.685

@mistakes
- Treating "unseen" reports as proof of no experience. A conservative criterion produces them without any change in sensitivity.
- Interpreting $\mathrm{AUROC}_2$ across conditions without regard to type-1 performance. Observers with higher $d'$ have fewer errors to discriminate, so compare M-ratios or match performance.
- Assuming a no-report paradigm has removed all report-related processing. The indicator was validated by reports, and participants may still think about the stimulus.

@practice self
prompt: A study finds that a frontal event-related potential is large when a faint stimulus is reported and absent when the same stimulus is not reported. Write a paragraph (about 200 words) that explains why this does not yet show that the frontal signal is a neural correlate of experience, and design a minimal control that would help.
hint: Think of what differs between reported and unreported trials besides experience.
solution: Reported and unreported trials differ in more than the experience of the stimulus: on reported trials the participant has also decided to respond, held the stimulus in working memory, and prepared a motor act or a verbal answer, and these prerequisites and consequences (Aru and colleagues, 2012) could generate the frontal potential. The comparison also confounds trials with a liberal and a conservative criterion, because a participant who reports on a trial may have used a weaker internal signal. A minimal control is a no-report condition in which the same stimuli appear but the task is changed so that no response is needed, with a behavioral or pupillary indicator, validated against report in other blocks, that shows when the stimulus was seen. If the frontal potential falls sharply there while the indicator shows the stimulus is still seen, the potential reflects report-related processing; if it persists, it is a better candidate. Matching task relevance across conditions, and checking that the participant had no hidden incentive to attend, strengthens the control. Even then, the persistence of a signal is consistent with it being a consequence of awareness rather than a constituent, so the control narrows, but does not close, the interpretive gap.
rubric: Names specific confounds, such as decision, memory, motor preparation or criterion, not just "report."
rubric: Proposes a concrete no-report or matched-relevance control with a validated indicator.
rubric: Acknowledges what the control still cannot show.

answer: 0.731

solution: There are $30\times20 = 600$ pairs. Wins: $9\times7 + 19\times15 = 63 + 285 = 348$. Ties: $14 + 72 + 95 = 181$, worth $90.5$. So $(348 + 90.5)/600 = 0.7308$, about $0.731$.

prompt: In signal detection terms, what changes when a participant becomes more reluctant to say "I saw it," with no change in the stimulus-related information they have?

@quiz self
prompt: Compare post-decision wagering and a verbal visibility scale as measures of awareness in about 250 words: what each is meant to capture, one way each could mislead, and a design that would reveal the difference.
hint: Think about risk attitudes for wagering and criterion placement for verbal scales.
solution: A visibility scale asks participants to describe their experience directly, so it targets what the participant takes themselves to have seen and is cheap and quick; it can mislead because the scale labels are interpreted differently by different participants and the response depends on a criterion that varies with instructions and mood. Post-decision wagering asks participants to bet on their own choice and is meant to make the report implicit, since earning money rewards using whatever information they have without requiring them to classify an experience; it can mislead because attitudes to risk, loss aversion and rules of thumb about the payoff structure shift bets without changing awareness, and participants may bet well from information they would not describe as experienced, which then counts as awareness only if one accepts that betting well implies it. A design that reveals the difference would give the same stimuli with both measures in separate blocks and a manipulation of the payoff matrix, for example making losses larger, and of the instructions about the verbal scale. If the criterion for wagering shifts with payoffs while the verbal scale does not, or the reverse, the measures are sensitive to different nuisance factors. Comparing both with type-1 accuracy and with metacognitive sensitivity measured by $\mathrm{AUROC}_2$ would show whether they track the same information. Agreement across measures supports a common signal but does not prove it is experience.
rubric: Describes what each measure is intended to capture and gives a distinct, plausible failure for each.
rubric: Proposes a design that manipulates a nuisance factor (payoffs or instructions) and states what pattern would distinguish the measures.
rubric: Does not claim that agreement between measures proves experience.
