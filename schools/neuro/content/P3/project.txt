@project
title: Seminar paper: one theory of consciousness against the evidence, its rival, and a decisive experiment
overview: Write a 5,000-word seminar paper that evaluates one theory of consciousness against the strongest evidence and objections, presents its main rival fairly, and proposes one experiment whose result would count against the theory you find more plausible. The paper is judged on the quality of its argument and its fairness to both views, not on which theory you favor.
goal: Present a theory of consciousness in its strongest form, with the evidence for it and the best objections, using accurate attributions.
goal: Present the main rival theory fairly, in a form its proponents would accept, and identify exactly where the two theories make different predictions.
goal: Design one preregistrable experiment whose outcome would count against the theory you find more plausible, stating the auxiliary assumptions in advance.
step: Choose a pair of theories from weeks 5 to 9 (for example a global workspace theory and recurrent processing theory, a higher-order theory and a workspace theory, or integrated information theory and predictive processing) and write a one-paragraph statement of what each claims, in terms its proponents would accept.
step: Build an evidence ledger for each theory: five to eight findings or arguments, each with the method, what it shows, what it does not show, and the strongest alternative explanation, including the report, attention and task-relevance confounds from weeks 2 and 3.
step: Fill in the prediction grid (see the material): for at least five situations, state what each theory predicts, and mark each prediction as shared or diagnostic. Compute or estimate a likelihood ratio for at least two predictions, as in weeks 6 and 10, using stated probabilities.
step: Draft the main sections: introduction and the question (about 600 words), theory one with its evidence and strongest objections (about 1,400 words), the rival theory treated with equal care (about 1,200 words), where they differ (about 600 words), and the proposed experiment (about 900 words).
step: Design the experiment: participants and sample, stimuli and manipulations, how report, attention and task relevance are controlled or matched, the measures, the predicted result for each theory, the result that would count against the theory you find more plausible, an equivalence test or power analysis for a predicted null, and the auxiliary assumptions that link each theory to the measure.
step: Write the last section (about 300 words) saying what the experiment could and could not settle, including the part of the problem it leaves open, such as the hard problem or the problem of other minds.
step: Have a classmate who prefers the other theory read the draft, ask them whether each theory is stated in a way its proponents would accept, and revise. Check every attribution, and paraphrase sources rather than quoting them.

material: **Experiment checklist.** The design should state: (1) the two theories and the manipulation on which they disagree; (2) what is held constant; (3) report, attention and task relevance; (4) the primary measure and its link to each theory; (5) preregistered predictions and the result that would count against each theory; (6) sample size or power, and an equivalence test if the prediction is a null; (7) auxiliary assumptions; (8) a participant-safe protocol if you plan human data (low-risk tasks, volunteers who agree, no deception, no stimulation or devices, anonymous data). The paper proposes the experiment; it need not be run.
deliverable: The seminar paper (5,000 words, plus references) with the completed prediction grid as an appendix.
deliverable: A one-page experiment pre-registration summary listing hypotheses, predictions, the decisive result, power or equivalence criteria and auxiliary assumptions.
rubric: Each theory is stated accurately and in its strongest form, with attributions that are correct and paraphrased, and the rival is treated as carefully as the favored theory.
rubric: The evidence is analyzed critically: each finding is paired with what it does not show, with the report, attention and task-relevance confounds addressed explicitly.
rubric: The prediction grid distinguishes shared from diagnostic predictions, and the likelihood ratios are justified.
rubric: The experiment is feasible and diagnostic: the theories genuinely disagree, the result that would count against the favored theory is stated in advance, and the auxiliary assumptions and the role of null results are handled correctly.
rubric: The paper is honest about what remains open and does not claim that the experiment or the field has settled how consciousness arises.
safety: This project is a paper and a design. If you decide to collect data, use only low-risk tasks (reaction times, perception, memory, questionnaires without sensitive questions) with volunteers who agree, no deception, no stimulation or devices, anonymous data, and approval from your institution's review board where required. The project never involves animals.
tutor: Here is my statement of the rival theory. Would a proponent accept it, and what have I left out or misdescribed?
tutor: I think this prediction separates my two theories. Can you play a proponent of each and argue that the other theory could also predict it?
tutor: Which auxiliary assumptions does my experiment rely on, and how could I make a null result informative?

@review
**Week 1: what needs explaining.** Creature, state and content consciousness; phenomenal and access consciousness; the easy problems and the hard problem; the explanatory gap; five responses and the strongest objection to each. Partial report: available letters = (fraction of the cued row correct) × (letters in the display); the overflow reading and its rivals.

**Week 2: measuring.** Objective and subjective measures; sensitivity $d' = z(H) - z(F)$ and criterion $c = -\tfrac12[z(H)+z(F)]$; type-2 tasks and confidence; $\mathrm{AUROC}_2$ = P(correct trial has higher confidence than incorrect trial) with ties half; meta-$d'$ and the M-ratio; the report confound, prerequisites and consequences, and no-report paradigms with their limits.

**Week 3: contrastive methods.** The NCC as a minimal sufficient set; near-threshold sorting, masking, blink, rivalry; visual awareness negativity and P3b; confounds of attention, task relevance, stimulus and criterion; the posterior and prefrontal debate.

**Week 4: unconscious processing.** Blindsight, neglect and extinction, masked priming; direct and indirect measures; criterion, exhaustiveness, regression to the mean and nulls; relative blindsight.

**Weeks 5 to 9: theories.** Workspace (broadcast and ignition); higher-order (representation of one's own states, misrepresentation, animals and infants); recurrent processing (four stages, local recurrence and the methodological puzzle); integrated information (axioms, identity claim, $\Phi$, toy integration as whole minus parts, the 2023 letter); predictive processing (precision weighting $\mu = (\pi_p\mu_p + \pi_s x)/(\pi_p+\pi_s)$, framework versus theory).

**Week 10: testing.** Adversarial collaboration, preregistration, likelihood ratios and Bayes factors, auxiliary assumptions; the 2025 Cogitate results challenged key predictions of both integrated information and global workspace theories, and their meaning is debated.

**Weeks 11 and 12: states and systems.** Disorders of consciousness, covert awareness, sensitivity and specificity, the perturbational complexity index, unresponsiveness is not unconsciousness; infants, animals (the 2024 New York Declaration is expert opinion, not consensus) and machines (the gaming problem), with no verdict.

**Self-check.** Can you state each theory in a form its proponents accept? Name one finding for and one against each? Say which predictions are shared and which diagnostic? Compute $\mathrm{AUROC}_2$, a precision-weighted mean, a toy integration and a posterior probability by hand?

**Common errors.** Treating unseen reports as no sensitivity; treating a correlate as a constituent; reading a null as absence; calling a framework a theory; reporting the open letter, the Declaration or the collaboration as consensus or proof; giving a verdict on infants, animals or machines.

@exam num
prompt: A task has three confidence levels (low, medium, high). Correct trials: 1, 4 and 15. Incorrect trials: 6, 3 and 2. Compute $\mathrm{AUROC}_2$ to three decimal places, counting ties as half.
answer: 0.832
tol: 0.001
check: (4 * 6 + 15 * (6 + 3) + 0.5 * (1 * 6 + 4 * 3 + 15 * 2)) / (20 * 11)
hint: There are $20\times11 = 220$ pairs; count wins, then ties at half.
solution: Wins: $4\times6 + 15\times(6+3) = 24 + 135 = 159$. Ties: $6 + 12 + 30 = 48$, worth $24$. So $(159+24)/220 = 0.832$.

@exam mc
prompt: Two experiments are proposed to separate theory A from theory B. In experiment 1 the probability of the predicted result is 0.9 under A and 0.85 under B. In experiment 2 it is 0.9 under A and 0.1 under B. Which statement is correct?
choice*: Experiment 2 is far more diagnostic, since its likelihood ratio is 9 while that of experiment 1 is about 1.06.
choice: Experiment 1 is more diagnostic because both probabilities are high.
choice: The two are equally diagnostic because A's probability is the same in both.
choice: Neither can be informative unless the probability under B is exactly zero.
hint: Compare the likelihood ratios.
solution: The ratios are $0.9/0.85 = 1.06$ and $0.9/0.1 = 9$. Diagnostic power depends on how differently the theories predict the result, not on how likely it is under one theory.

@exam self
prompt: Timed essay (about 40 minutes, 600 to 800 words). A stimulus is shown at threshold. On trials when it is reported, a late frontoparietal response is large; when the same kind of stimulus is shown but the participant is performing an unrelated task and reports nothing, a posterior response persists but the frontoparietal response is small, and a validated eye-movement indicator shows that the stimulus was perceived. Interpret these data from the standpoint of a global workspace theory and of recurrent processing theory, say what each would do to answer the other's reading, state two further results that would discriminate between them, and say what the data cannot show whichever theory is preferred.
hint: Separate what the data show from the interpretations, and list the auxiliary assumptions each theory needs.
solution: The data show a posterior response that does not depend on report and a frontoparietal response that does. A recurrent processing theorist reads the persistence of the posterior response, with the indicator showing the stimulus was perceived, as evidence that experience occurred without frontoparietal ignition, so the late response is a consequence of report; the stimulus was phenomenally conscious but not accessed. A workspace theorist replies that the small frontoparietal response does not mean none occurred: in the unattended condition ignition may be weaker, briefer or below the sensitivity of the recording, and the indicator, validated on reports, may reflect attention to or processing of the stimulus without consciousness. The workspace view would then predict that decoding of the stimulus in frontoparietal cortex should still be possible with sensitive methods, and that the indicator should fail when attention is fully absorbed. The recurrent theorist can reply that decoding would not show ignition and that indicators validated against report are the best available. Two results would discriminate: first, content-specific decoding in frontoparietal cortex in the no-report condition, preregistered with an equivalence test for its absence, which would favor the workspace reading if present and the recurrent reading if absent under sufficient power; second, a manipulation that disrupts posterior feedback, for example masking timed to remove recurrence, which should remove the indicator under the recurrent reading and should leave it unchanged under the workspace reading only if ignition survives. Auxiliary assumptions include the validity of the indicator, matched attention and task relevance, and the sensitivity of the measures. The data cannot show whether the unreported stimulus was experienced, because the only evidence of experience is access in some form, and they cannot settle which theory is correct; they test the theories only together with these assumptions.
rubric: Interprets the data accurately from each theory's standpoint and states each side's reply to the other.
rubric: Proposes two discriminating results, each with the result each theory predicts and a sensitivity or equivalence consideration.
rubric: States the auxiliary assumptions and the limit that experience cannot be directly verified without access, without declaring one theory correct.
