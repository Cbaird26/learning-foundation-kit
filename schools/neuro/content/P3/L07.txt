@article
When a picture flashes in front of you, the first wave of neural activity runs up the visual hierarchy in a few tens of milliseconds, from the retina to the thalamus to primary visual cortex (V1) and on to higher visual areas. Neurons in later areas can already signal a face or a word. Yet, on the view developed here, this "feedforward sweep" leaves a stimulus unconscious. **Recurrent processing theory** (RPT) says what is missing: feedback.

### The theory in its strongest form

Lamme and Roelfsema (2000) distinguished two modes of visual processing. In the **feedforward sweep**, activity travels forward from lower to higher areas; it can support rapid categorization and some behavior, but its receptive fields are limited in what they integrate. In **recurrent processing**, higher areas send signals back to lower areas and to each other, and lateral connections link neurons within an area. This allows figure to be segregated from ground and local features to be bound with global context. Lamme (2006) proposed that recurrent processing is the neural basis of **phenomenal consciousness**, and he described four stages:

| stage | processing | status |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | superficial feedforward, from V1 to nearby areas | unconscious |
| 2 | deep feedforward, reaching widespread areas including frontal cortex | unconscious |
| 3 | superficial recurrent, local feedback within visual cortex | phenomenally conscious, not accessible |
| 4 | widespread recurrent, including frontoparietal cortex | phenomenally conscious and accessible, reportable |

The distinctive claim is that stage 3 suffices for experience: the global access emphasized by workspace theories is a further stage, needed to report and use what is already experienced. RPT therefore predicts that phenomenology can overflow access (week 1), and it is a prominent local, sensory-based theory that disagrees with workspace and higher-order theories on where consciousness is located.

### Evidence

- **Figure-ground modulation in V1.** Neurons in V1 respond with an initial burst that depends on local features and, tens of milliseconds later, with a modulation that depends on whether their receptive field lies on a figure or on the background. In monkeys, Supèr, Spekreijse and Lamme (2001) found that this later modulation was present when the animal detected the figure and weaker or absent when it did not, while the early response was similar.
- **Masking.** Fahrenfort, Scholte and Lamme (2007) found with EEG in humans that a backward mask abolished a signal for texture-defined figure-ground segregation that depends on recurrent interactions, while leaving the feedforward response intact, in parallel with the loss of perception.
- **Brain stimulation.** Transcranial magnetic stimulation over V1 at a delay at which feedback arrives disrupts perception of a stimulus, and the experience of phosphenes evoked by stimulating a motion area is reported to depend on activity in V1 at a later time (Pascual-Leone and Walsh, 2001).
- **Unconscious states.** Reports that anesthetic agents and some sleep states reduce feedback more than feedforward activity are cited as support, though the picture is not uniform across agents and states.

### Objections

1. **The methodological puzzle.** The theory claims phenomenal experience without access, but all evidence of experience comes from report, which is access. How could the claim of experience without access be tested? Defenders propose inferring experience from the best theory of the report cases and extending it to cases without report (Block's discussion of this puzzle is the standard reference); critics say this is circular.
2. **Recurrence is not enough.** Recurrent processing is found in many systems and states, and some studies report feedback-dependent processes, such as certain grouping and contextual effects, without awareness. The theory must say which recurrence matters, and why.
3. **Confounds.** Recurrent processing in a masking paradigm co-varies with attention, decision and report, so the correlate may be a consequence of awareness, not the basis.
4. **Scope.** The theory was developed for vision; extensions to other senses and to non-human species need an account of what counts as the relevant recurrence.
5. **Stage 3 is hard to isolate.** The theory predicts a condition with local recurrence but no global access; locating it, and showing that participants have experience in it, is the same problem as objection 1.

@example
title: A figure-ground modulation index
problem: A V1 neuron fires at 30 spikes/s in the late window when its receptive field lies on a figure and 20 spikes/s when it lies on the background. Compute the modulation index $(R_{\text{figure}} - R_{\text{ground}})/(R_{\text{figure}} + R_{\text{ground}})$ and say what a value of zero would mean.
step: The difference is $30 - 20 = 10$ spikes/s.
step: The sum is $30 + 20 = 50$ spikes/s.
step: The index is $10/50 = 0.2$.
step: A value of zero means no difference between figure and ground responses, as would be expected from a purely feedforward response that depends only on local features.
answer: 0.2; zero would mean no figure-ground modulation.

@mistakes
- Saying that recurrent processing theory denies the role of frontal cortex in report. It assigns frontal involvement to access, a later stage, not to phenomenal experience.
- Equating recurrence with consciousness in general. The theory requires a specific kind of recurrence, in sensory cortex, strong enough to support integrated feature binding and segmentation.
- Treating the methodological puzzle as a minor technicality. It affects how any claim of experience without access could be established.

@practice self
prompt: In about 250 words, explain how recurrent processing theory and global workspace theory would interpret the same observation, that a stimulus produces strong feedback in visual cortex but no frontoparietal ignition and cannot be reported.
hint: One theory counts the state as phenomenally conscious and unreported; the other counts it as unconscious.
solution: Recurrent processing theory would interpret the state as stage 3: local feedback has made the stimulus a segregated, bound figure, so it is phenomenally experienced, and it cannot be reported because the later stage of widespread recurrence to frontoparietal cortex, which supports access, did not occur. The failure to report is a failure of access, not of experience. Global workspace theory would interpret the same observation as unconscious processing: the representation is strong and locally amplified but not broadcast, so it is not conscious, and the inability to report is exactly what is expected of an unconscious state. The observation by itself therefore does not discriminate, because each theory has a story for it, and the dispute concerns what experience is rather than what happened. To discriminate, one needs evidence about experience that does not rely on report, which is the methodological puzzle: for example, indirect behavioral traces that would be hard to explain without experience, or a theory-neutral argument that an independent signature of experience exists in the absence of access. Both sides would also need to show that attention and task relevance are matched. Because each interpretation is internally coherent, the result is a test of the background assumptions about how to infer experience, not only of the theories.

rubric: Recognizes that the observation alone does not discriminate between them.
rubric: Names a way forward and its difficulty.

@quiz self
prompt: In about 250 words, state the strongest reply a defender of recurrent processing theory can give to the charge that recurrence is also found in unconscious processing, and the strongest rejoinder.
hint: Think about what kind of recurrence the theory requires and whether this can be specified in advance.
solution: The defender can reply that the theory never claimed that any feedback is sufficient: the relevant recurrence is the kind that segments and binds features within visual cortex at a particular stage, as in stage 3, and the studies reporting feedback-dependent effects without awareness either concern simpler forms of contextual modulation, probe the wrong stage, or rely on awareness measures that are not exhaustive, so that weak experience may have been present. The defender can add that the evidence from masking and V1 modulation shows that the kind of recurrence that tracks perception is lost when perception is lost, which is what the theory needs. The rejoinder is that this reply makes the theory hard to refute: if every case of recurrence without awareness is reclassified as the wrong kind of recurrence or as unmeasured weak experience, the theory risks accommodating any result. A critic would ask for an independent, advance specification of which recurrent interactions suffice, in terms of, for example, laminar profile, timing and spatial extent, and for evidence that these predict awareness without being defined by it. The defender could accept the challenge and propose such a specification as a research program. The disagreement reveals a general problem with theories of consciousness: the more flexibly the core notion is specified, the less each experiment can count against it.
