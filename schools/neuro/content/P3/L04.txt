@article
A person with damage to the primary visual cortex on one side reports seeing nothing in the opposite half of the visual field. Asked nevertheless to guess whether a bar there is vertical or horizontal, or to point to a light, they perform far above chance. This is **blindsight** (Weiskrantz and colleagues, 1974). Does it show perception without experience, or does it show that a weak, hard-to-describe experience goes unreported? The answer depends on measurement, and this week is a case study in how to evaluate a claim of unconscious perception.

### Three phenomena

**Blindsight.** After lesions of the striate cortex, patients show above-chance forced-choice discrimination, localization and sometimes emotion or motion processing in the blind field, while denying any visual experience. Weiskrantz distinguished a form where patients report no awareness at all (type 1) from one where they report a feeling that something happened without seeing it (type 2). Residual abilities are usually attributed to pathways that bypass the primary visual cortex, though the details remain studied.

**Neglect and extinction.** After damage that most often involves the right parietal cortex, patients may fail to attend to or report the left side of space and of objects. In a classic demonstration (Marshall and Halligan, 1988), a patient shown two drawings of a house, one with flames at the left, said the two were identical but chose the one without the flames when forced to choose which house she would prefer to live in. Related is **extinction**, in which a stimulus on the affected side is reported when it is alone but not when a rival stimulus appears on the other side. These cases show that processing of the ignored stimulus continues, and they raise the question of what is missing: awareness, or access to report.

**Masked priming.** A word or digit is flashed briefly and masked so that observers cannot discriminate it, yet it speeds or slows the response to a following target that is related to it. Dehaene and colleagues (1998) showed with fMRI that masked words activate cortical regions linked to reading even though participants denied seeing them.

### Evaluating a claim

A claim of unconscious perception needs two measures: a **direct** measure showing the stimulus was not consciously perceived, and an **indirect** measure showing that it nevertheless had an effect (Reingold and Merikle, 1988). Each can fail.

1. **Criterion.** A report of "I see nothing" depends on how much evidence the observer needs before saying yes. Blindsight patients may adopt a very conservative criterion for what counts as seeing; forced-choice performance would then show sensitivity to weak experience. Lau and Passingham (2006) addressed this by equating forced-choice performance across conditions and varying only the delay in a metacontrast mask, and found that reported visibility differed even though discrimination was matched, with different activity in dorsolateral prefrontal cortex. Their result is called *relative blindsight*.
2. **Exhaustiveness.** The direct measure should capture all the relevant conscious information. If the awareness question is a single yes/no, it may miss partial awareness, and a $d' = 0$ test of a stimulus's identity may miss awareness of some feature.
3. **Statistics.** If participants or trials are selected because their awareness score is near chance, noise in that score guarantees that the selected group's true awareness is above chance on average: regression to the mean. A null result for awareness is also not a demonstration of absence unless it comes with enough power or an equivalence test.
4. **Relevance.** The indirect effect should depend on the same stimulus feature as the awareness test.

Defenders of unconscious perception answer that it is established by converging methods across paradigms, including cases where the effect of an unseen stimulus has a different time course or neural signature from that of a seen one. Skeptics answer that each paradigm can be explained by weak awareness, and the field's agreed standard is shifting toward preregistered designs with Bayesian or equivalence tests for the null.

@example
title: Is forced-choice performance above chance?
problem: A blindsight patient makes 40 forced-choice discriminations in the blind field and is correct on 29. Is this above chance (probability 0.5 per trial) under the normal approximation to the binomial?
step: Under chance, the expected number correct is $40\times0.5 = 20$ and the standard deviation is $\sqrt{40\times0.5\times0.5} = \sqrt{10}\approx3.16$.
step: The observed excess is $29 - 20 = 9$ trials.
step: Divide: $z = 9/3.16 \approx 2.85$.
step: A one-sided $z$ of $2.85$ corresponds to $p\approx0.002$, so performance is above chance. Whether this reflects unconscious perception depends on the awareness measures, not on this test.
answer: $z\approx2.85$, above chance, which says nothing yet about awareness.

@mistakes
- Treating above-chance forced-choice performance as proof of unconscious perception. It shows sensitivity; the claim also needs a valid, exhaustive measure of absence of awareness.
- Selecting participants or trials with chance-level awareness scores and then testing them. Noise in the selection measure makes the selected group look better on a repeated test than it truly is.
- Concluding from a null result that awareness is absent without asking about power or using an equivalence test.

@practice self
prompt: A report claims unconscious perception because participants with $d' = 0$ on a first visibility test showed a 20 ms priming effect on a second test. In about 250 words, evaluate the claim: what the study needs to show, which objections apply, and what additional analysis or design would strengthen it.
hint: Check how the group was selected, whether the awareness measure is exhaustive and whether the null is established.
solution: The claim needs two things: evidence that the primes were not consciously perceived, and evidence that they nevertheless influenced behavior, with both measures tapping the same stimulus information. The 20 ms effect satisfies the second if it is reliable. The first is weaker. Selecting participants for $d' = 0$ on one test and measuring them again invites regression to the mean: some of those participants had a true $d'$ above zero and happened to score low, so their group-level awareness on the second test is positive. A single test of $d'$ with limited trials cannot show that sensitivity is exactly zero; absence of significance is not evidence of absence, so a power analysis, a confidence interval around $d'$ or a Bayes factor or equivalence test is needed. The awareness measure should also be exhaustive: if it asks only whether participants could identify the prime, it might miss awareness of a feature relevant to the priming, and a subjective scale would show whether participants felt anything. I would strengthen the study by measuring awareness on the same trials as priming and not in a separate block, avoiding selection by using all participants and testing whether priming correlates with awareness measures, with an intercept at zero awareness as the key estimate, preregistering the analysis, and including a condition where the prime is clearly visible so that the effect sizes can be compared. If priming remains with an equivalence test supporting no sensitivity, the claim is stronger, though a conservative-criterion objection can never be wholly excluded.

prompt: In the Marshall and Halligan demonstration of neglect, a patient said two drawings of a house were the same yet preferred the house without flames on the neglected side. What does this show?
choice*: Information from the neglected side was processed enough to influence a choice, even though the patient did not report any difference.

solution: The preference implies that the flames were processed and affected the choice while the patient denied a difference. @quiz self
prompt: Present, in about 250 words, the strongest case that blindsight is perception without any visual experience and the strongest case that it is a degraded, low-confidence experience combined with a conservative reporting criterion. Say which kind of data would distinguish them.
hint: Consider the criterion explanation and what confidence ratings or relative blindsight add.
solution: The strongest case for perception without experience is that patients consistently deny any visual experience in the field, even when urged to report any feeling, yet discriminate and localize well above chance, and that the discrimination depends on pathways that bypass the damaged cortex; the patients' conduct, such as having to be forced to guess and treating their answers as guesses, fits a lack of experience. The strongest case for degraded experience is that blindsight performance is evaluated against a report criterion that may be extremely conservative for a damaged field, so that experience too weak or unfamiliar to count as seeing is called "nothing"; studies in healthy participants that matched discrimination and still found differences in visibility show that experience and discrimination can be partly separated, but also that reports follow criterion shifts, and that blindsight discrimination may be accompanied by confidence ratings that carry information. Data that could distinguish the accounts include measures that do not depend on a binary yes/no criterion, such as the type-2 relationship between confidence and accuracy in the blind field compared with matched healthy-observer performance, bets or wagers whose payoffs are manipulated to shift the criterion, and neural signatures that mark awareness in healthy observers, checked against the patient. If confidence carries no information about accuracy at all and shifts in payoffs do not reveal any hidden awareness, the first account gains; if confidence tracks accuracy, the second does. Neither result eliminates the other interpretation entirely, which is why the question remains open.
