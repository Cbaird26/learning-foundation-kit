@article
Theories of consciousness are numerous and often difficult to compare, since each emphasizes different data and defines its terms differently. A researcher who finds support for one theory may be unmoved by another's supporters. An **adversarial collaboration** tries to break this impasse: proponents of rival theories agree in advance, with a neutral party, on an experiment, on the predictions each theory makes about it, and on how the data will be analyzed, so that the result cannot easily be reinterpreted afterward. The approach was proposed for psychology by Mellers, Hertwig and Kahneman (2001).

### What makes a test diagnostic

A prediction separates two theories only if they disagree about it. If theory A and theory B both predict prefrontal activation for conscious content, finding it supports neither more than the other. The useful quantity is the **likelihood ratio** (or Bayes factor): the probability of the observed result under one theory divided by its probability under the other. A ratio near 1 means the result is uninformative; a ratio of 10 or more is strongly diagnostic. Four requirements follow.

1. **Prediction agreed in advance.** Both camps sign off on what each theory predicts, and on what would count against it.
2. **Preregistration.** The design, sample size and analysis are fixed before the data are seen.
3. **Measures that can fail.** The planned signals (a decoding result, a synchronization effect, a response at stimulus offset) must be able to come out against the theory.
4. **Control of confounds.** Report, attention and task relevance are manipulated or matched, as in weeks 2 and 3.

### The first large test: integrated information theory and global neuronal workspace theory

The Cogitate Consortium, within an initiative funded by the Templeton World Charity Foundation to accelerate research on consciousness, carried out the first large adversarial collaboration between two theories. On 30 April 2025 it published in *Nature* the results of a study of 256 participants who viewed stimuli of several categories while their brains were recorded with fMRI, MEG and intracranial EEG. It tested preregistered predictions of integrated information theory (IIT) and global neuronal workspace theory (GNWT). The results challenged key predictions of both theories:

- IIT predicted sustained synchronization within posterior cortex while a stimulus was experienced; this was not found.
- GNWT predicted an ignition-like response at the offset of a stimulus as well as at its onset, which was not found, and it predicted that the content of experience would be represented in prefrontal cortex, where only part of the conscious content could be decoded.

What this shows for each theory is still debated. Proponents have argued that some tested predictions were not central or depended on auxiliary assumptions about measurement, such as the sensitivity of fMRI or the choice of a signal as a proxy; critics have answered that the predictions were agreed in advance, and that a theory that disowns agreed predictions after they fail is hard to test. This is a version of the old point that a failed prediction refutes a conjunction of a theory and its auxiliary assumptions (the Duhem–Quine problem), and the lesson of the collaboration was in part a lesson in writing down the auxiliary assumptions in advance.

### Reading a result of this kind

Several habits help. Ask which predictions each theory committed to, and whether the data refuted all or only some. Ask whether the null result was established with power or an equivalence test, since failing to find a signal differs from showing it to be absent. Ask which measure stood in for the theory's concept, and what other results the same measure would also have produced. And ask what the result changes in the theories' remaining predictions: after the 2025 study the discussion was about which parts of each theory are core and which are revisable, and about the next experiments that could test them, including designs that vary task demands and use no-report methods. A single study, however large, does not settle which theory is correct, and as of 2026 there is no consensus on how consciousness arises.

@example
title: How precisely does a sample pin down an effect?
problem: A preregistered test uses 256 participants, and the effect of interest is a standardized difference with standard deviation 1 across participants. What is the standard error, and the half-width of the 95% confidence interval?
step: The standard error is $\text{sd}/\sqrt{n} = 1/\sqrt{256} = 1/16 = 0.0625$.
step: The half-width of a 95% interval is about $1.96\times0.0625 = 0.1225$.
step: Interpret: effects larger than about $0.12$ standard deviations could be detected as differing from zero, and a null result excludes effects larger than that, which is what makes the test informative about the absence of a predicted large effect.
answer: Standard error 0.0625 and half-width about 0.123.

@mistakes
- Saying that the 2025 study proved one theory right or disproved both. It challenged key predictions of both, and what it shows for each is debated.
- Treating a failure to find a predicted signal as proof it is absent. Power, measurement sensitivity and equivalence testing matter.
- Ignoring auxiliary assumptions. A test is of a theory together with the assumptions that link it to the measure.

solution: I choose global workspace theory and recurrent processing theory. They disagree about stimuli that produce local recurrence in visual cortex but no frontoparietal ignition: recurrent processing theory says such stimuli are phenomenally experienced and workspace theory says they are not. The experiment presents a figure-ground stimulus at several contrasts and durations under two task conditions, one in which the stimulus is task relevant and reported and one in which attention is drawn to another task, with the figure detectable only through texture segregation, so that figure-ground segregation requires recurrence. The measures are an EEG or MEG signature of figure-ground segregation, a frontoparietal signature of ignition (the late response and decodable content in frontoparietal sensors), and, in the second condition, a no-report indicator that is validated against report in separate blocks. Recurrent processing theory predicts that the segregation signal tracks the no-report indicator of experience even when frontoparietal ignition is absent; workspace theory predicts that experience requires ignition, so that the indicator should not show experience when ignition is absent. Evidence against recurrent processing theory would be the segregation signal present, ignition absent and the validated indicator showing no experience; evidence against workspace theory would be the indicator showing experience reliably when ignition is absent. Auxiliary assumptions include the validity of the indicator, the sensitivity of the sensors to ignition, the equivalence of attention across conditions and the assumption that the indicator reflects experience in the unattended condition and not residual attention. These must be agreed beforehand, with an equivalence test for the absence of ignition and a power analysis. The study could not show which theory is right, only which of these predictions fails given the assumptions.

@quiz self
prompt: In about 250 words, explain the Duhem-Quine problem in the context of testing a theory of consciousness, and describe two practices that reduce its force.
hint: A test is of a theory together with auxiliary assumptions linking it to the measure.
solution: A theory of consciousness does not predict a particular voltage or voxel pattern by itself: it predicts such data only together with assumptions about which neural signals reflect the theory's key quantities, how conscious content is measured, how attention and task relevance are controlled, and how sensitive the recording method is. When a prediction fails, logic shows only that the theory or one of these assumptions is false, and the theory's defenders can retain the theory by rejecting an assumption, while critics can reject the theory. For example, if prefrontal decoding of content fails in fMRI, a defender may say that fMRI lacks the sensitivity to detect it, and a critic may say that the prediction was explicit. Two practices reduce this force. First, adversarial collaboration with preregistration requires proponents to state the auxiliary assumptions and the predictions in advance, so that a failure cannot be attributed afterward to an assumption that they had not written down. Second, convergent evidence across methods and tests of the assumptions themselves, for example checking in separate data that the measure is sensitive to a known signal of the right size or using equivalence tests to show that an absent signal is smaller than a predicted effect, narrows the escape routes. These practices do not eliminate the problem, since new assumptions can always be proposed, but they make revisions visible and costly, so that the theories' core can be distinguished from their flexible periphery.
