@article
Where in the brain is a particular experience? The standard answer to such questions is to find a **neural correlate of consciousness** (NCC). A widely used definition describes the NCC for a conscious content as the minimal set of neural events jointly sufficient for that content to be experienced, given the right background conditions (a level of consciousness that is already present). The definition is more demanding than "whatever lights up": it asks for sufficiency, and it asks for minimality.

### The contrastive method

The logic goes back to what Baars (1988) called contrastive analysis: compare two situations that differ in whether a content is conscious while other things are as similar as possible. The art is in holding everything else constant.

- **Near-threshold stimuli.** The same physical stimulus is sometimes reported and sometimes not, owing to trial-to-trial fluctuation, and trials are sorted after the fact by report. The stimulus is identical; the contrast is clean in that respect.
- **Masking.** A brief target followed by a mask is reported when the delay between them is long and not when it is short. Here the stimulus differs across conditions, and the contrast is between delays.
- **Attentional blink and inattentional blindness.** A second target is missed when it follows the first within a few hundred milliseconds, or an unexpected object is missed when attention is engaged elsewhere.
- **Binocular rivalry.** Different images are shown to the two eyes, and perception alternates between them every few seconds under constant stimulation. Tong and colleagues (1998) showed with fMRI that activity in a face-selective region and a place-selective region swapped as the reported percept alternated between a face and a house. Recordings in monkeys found that the proportion of neurons whose firing followed the percept increased from early visual areas to inferotemporal cortex.

### Electrical signatures

In EEG studies of visual awareness two components recur. A posterior negativity at roughly 200 ms after stimulus onset, the **visual awareness negativity**, differs between seen and unseen stimuli. A later positivity over central and parietal electrodes, the **P3b**, at roughly 300 to 500 ms, also differs. Which of these is a correlate of experience and which a consequence of reporting is contested. Studies that kept the stimulus the same and changed whether it was task relevant found that the late positivity could shrink or vanish when no report was required, while the earlier posterior effect persisted, which has been read as evidence that the P3b reflects post-perceptual processing. Defenders of late-onset theories reply that task-irrelevant stimuli may be weakly attended and that the absence of a signal in no-report conditions is not evidence of absence.

### Confounds

Every contrast must be examined for factors that correlate with awareness without being it.

1. **Prerequisites** are processes needed for a content to be experienced but not part of the experience, such as arousal or attention that must be present beforehand. **Consequences** are processes that follow, such as memory encoding, decision and report (Aru and colleagues, 2012).
2. **Attention.** Attended stimuli are more often reported. Is the NCC of the content, or of the attention that favored it?
3. **Task relevance and report.** Seen items that matter to the task are processed further.
4. **Stimulus differences.** Contrasting a visible stimulus with its absence confounds awareness with all the neural effects of the stimulus.
5. **Criterion.** Trials sorted by a yes/no report mix real differences in experience with differences in response bias.

### The back and the front

A prominent proposal is that the NCC for contents of experience lies in a posterior cortical "hot zone" that includes sensory areas in parts of occipital, temporal and parietal cortex (Koch, Massimini, Boly and Tononi, 2016); a later study of dreaming linked activity in a similar posterior region to reports of dream experience (Siclari and colleagues, 2017). Others hold that prefrontal cortex is essential, citing frontal activity in awareness contrasts and the role of frontal regions in metacognition. Arguments for the posterior view note that extensive frontal lesions in patients have not been shown to abolish experience and that frontal differences shrink in no-report designs. Odegaard, Knight and Lau (2017) replied that these null findings are weak evidence against frontal involvement: the lesions and the signal removals may leave the relevant frontal circuitry intact, and no-report designs may reduce rather than eliminate it. The debate is unresolved, and it is a good example of an empirical question whose answer depends on a theory of what the report confound is.

@example
title: How much of a contrast is due to report?
problem: A study measures the amplitude of a late EEG component for seen minus unseen stimuli. When participants report the stimuli, the seen-minus-unseen difference is $4.0\ \mu$V. When a no-report indicator is used instead, the difference is $1.0\ \mu$V. What share of the report-condition effect is not present without report, and what does it suggest?
step: Subtract the effect that remains without report from the full effect: $4.0 - 1.0 = 3.0\ \mu$V.
step: Express it as a share of the report-condition effect: $3.0/4.0 = 0.75$, or 75%.
step: Interpret cautiously: three quarters of the effect depends on the report requirement, so it behaves like a prerequisite or consequence of report; the remaining quarter may reflect awareness, or weaker attention and task relevance in the no-report condition.
answer: 75%; most of this component's effect depends on report, but the remainder is itself ambiguous.

@mistakes
- Calling any signal that differs between seen and unseen trials an NCC. It may be a prerequisite, a consequence, or an effect of attention, report or criterion.
- Contrasting a visible stimulus with a blank screen. The difference then includes all of the stimulus's neural effects, awareness or not.
- Concluding from a signal that vanishes in a no-report condition that it was only a consequence of report. Weaker attention in the no-report condition is an alternative.

@practice self
prompt: In about 250 words, design a contrast for the NCC of a faint visual grating that tries to separate awareness from attention and from report. Say what you would hold constant, what you would manipulate, and what result would suggest that a signal is a correlate of awareness and not of attention.
hint: Use a design where attention is manipulated independently of whether the grating is seen, and include a condition without report.
solution: The aim is a signal that tracks whether the grating is seen after attention, task relevance and report have been varied independently. I would present a grating at a contrast set near the individual's threshold, so that it is seen on roughly half the trials with the same physical stimulus each time. Attention would be manipulated by a spatial cue that is valid on most trials (attended) or points to the opposite side (unattended), crossed with the report requirement: in some blocks participants report whether they saw the grating, and in others they perform an unrelated task and visibility is inferred from an involuntary indicator validated against report in separate blocks, such as pupil responses or fixational eye movements. Trials would be sorted by seen versus unseen within each combination of attention and report. A signal that differs between seen and unseen trials in the attended and the unattended conditions and persists in the no-report blocks would be a candidate correlate of awareness, since it is not explained by a change in attention or by the report requirement. A signal that appears only when attention is on the stimulus is an attention effect; one that appears only with report is a consequence or a prerequisite of report. Remaining limits should be stated: the indicator is itself inferred, task relevance may differ, and a persisting signal could still be a consequence of experience rather than part of its minimal basis.

prompt: In a near-threshold task, 40% of 50 trials are reported as seen with a mean amplitude of $8.0\ \mu$V, and the remaining trials are reported as unseen with a mean amplitude of $5.0\ \mu$V. What is the mean amplitude over all 50 trials, in microvolts?
answer: 6.2
tol: 0.01
unit: µV
check: 0.4 * 8.0 + 0.6 * 5.0
hint: Weight each condition's mean by its share of trials.
solution: The grand mean is $0.4\times 8.0 + 0.6\times 5.0 = 3.2 + 3.0 = 6.2\ \mu$V. This is why averaging all trials, without sorting by report, blurs the seen-unseen difference.

@quiz self
prompt: In about 250 words, present the strongest argument that prefrontal cortex is part of the neural basis of conscious perception, the strongest argument that it is not, and the kind of evidence that could separate them.
hint: Consider evidence from lesions, from imaging, from no-report designs, and the reply that null findings are weak.
solution: The case for prefrontal involvement is that frontal activity increases in many contrasts between seen and unseen stimuli, that prefrontal regions support the metacognitive and evaluative processes by which we know what we see, that stimulation and lesion effects on confidence and subjective visibility have been reported, and that a null result in a no-report design could mean that the experiments reduced rather than abolished the relevant activity, since frontal circuits may still operate without overt report (Odegaard, Knight and Lau, 2017). The case against is that frontal activity in awareness contrasts is largely explained by report, task relevance and decision, because it shrinks in several no-report designs, that patients with large frontal lesions are not reported to lose perceptual experience, and that recordings from posterior sensory cortex track the contents of perception closely. The posterior proposal (Koch and colleagues, 2016) takes this view. Evidence that could separate them would include no-report designs in which attention and relevance are matched, so that frontal differences are tested without the confounds; intracranial recordings that distinguish content-specific frontal signals from report signals; reversible inactivation of frontal regions during perception with careful measures of both sensitivity and awareness; and tests of whether frontal content representations exist when participants are not reporting. Each result remains open to reinterpretation unless the theory fixes beforehand what pattern it requires.
rubric: States the best case on each side, including the reply that null findings in no-report designs are weak evidence.
rubric: Proposes distinct kinds of evidence that bear on the question.
rubric: Does not simply endorse one position.
