## Week-by-week key ideas

- **Week 1, describing data.** Mean, median, sample standard deviation ($n-1$), quartiles and the IQR rule; random assignment, control conditions, blinding and confounds.
- **Week 2, sampling distributions.** $\text{SE} = s/\sqrt{n}$; the 95% interval $\bar{x} \pm t^* s/\sqrt{n}$ and what the 95% means; Wald interval for a proportion; sample size from a target half-width.
- **Week 3, t tests.** One-sample, paired (a one-sample test on differences), Welch (default) and pooled; what a p-value is.
- **Week 4, effect sizes and power.** Cohen's $d$ with the pooled SD, Hedges' $g$; $n = 2\big((z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})/d\big)^2$ per group; plan from the smallest effect of interest.
- **Week 5, ANOVA.** $SS_{\text{total}} = SS_{\text{between}} + SS_{\text{within}}$, $F = MS_B/MS_W$; the balanced 2 by 2 design with main effects and the interaction contrast $m_{11} - m_{12} - m_{21} + m_{22}$.
- **Week 6, correlation and regression.** $r = S_{xy}/\sqrt{S_{xx}S_{yy}}$, $b = S_{xy}/S_{xx}$, residuals, the slope $t$ test and Fisher's interval; outliers, restricted range, confounds.
- **Week 7, multiple regression.** Normal equations, partial slopes, adjusted $R^2$, the variance inflation factor.
- **Week 8, repeated measures.** Subject variance removed from error; $F = t^2$ for two conditions; the ICC and design effect; random intercepts and shrinkage.
- **Week 9, permutation tests and the bootstrap.** Exact enumeration of splits and sign flips, Mann-Whitney $U$, percentile intervals from resamples.
- **Week 10, multiple comparisons.** $1-(1-\alpha)^m$; Bonferroni, Holm, Benjamini-Hochberg; FWER versus FDR.
- **Week 11, research practices.** p-hacking, optional stopping, positive predictive value, preregistration, replication.
- **Week 12, reporting.** Raw effect, interval, standardized effect, exact $p$; wording that matches the design.

## Key formulas

$\bar{x}\pm t^*\dfrac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$; Welch $t = \dfrac{\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2}{\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1+s_2^2/n_2}}$; $d = \dfrac{\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2}{s_p}$; $\text{power} \approx \Phi(d\sqrt{n/2} - z_{1-\alpha/2})$; $R^2_{\text{adj}} = 1-(1-R^2)\dfrac{n-1}{n-k-1}$; $\text{PPV} = \dfrac{\pi\,\text{power}}{\pi\,\text{power}+(1-\pi)\alpha}$.

model:
```python
import math


def analyze_two_groups(a, b):
    """Difference, Welch t, Welch df and Cohen's d (pooled SD)."""
    na, nb = len(a), len(b)
    ma, mb = sum(a) / na, sum(b) / nb
    ssa = sum((v - ma) ** 2 for v in a)
    ssb = sum((v - mb) ** 2 for v in b)
    va, vb = ssa / (na - 1) / na, ssb / (nb - 1) / nb
    diff = ma - mb
    t = diff / math.sqrt(va + vb)
    df = (va + vb) ** 2 / (va ** 2 / (na - 1) + vb ** 2 / (nb - 1))
    sp = math.sqrt((ssa + ssb) / (na + nb - 2))
    return diff, t, df, diff / sp
```
hint: The pooled variance is the total of the two sums of squared deviations divided by $n_a + n_b - 2$; the Welch quantities use each group's $s^2/n$.
solution: Welch's statistic and degrees of freedom use each group's own variance, while Cohen's $d$ standardizes by the pooled standard deviation, so the same data give a Welch $t$ and a pooled-SD $d$. With identical groups the difference, $t$ and $d$ are zero and the Welch degrees of freedom equal $n_a + n_b - 2$ when the group sizes and variances match.

@exam self
prompt: A lab tests whether a drug speeds responses. Volunteers choose whether to take the drug or a sugar pill; the lab records 12 RT-based outcomes, tests each at $\alpha = 0.05$ with $n = 10$ per group, finds one significant outcome ($p = 0.03$) in a post hoc subgroup of women, and writes: "The drug speeds up responses in women." Critique the claim, covering assignment, multiplicity, subgroup analysis, power and the wording, and describe a study that would give a more trustworthy answer.
hint: Go through the design from assignment to wording and ask what each step could do to the false-positive rate.
solution: Volunteers who choose their condition may differ in age, motivation or expectations, so the comparison is confounded, and no causal statement is licensed without random assignment and blinding with a placebo. The lab ran 12 tests; with independent tests at $\alpha = 0.05$ the chance of at least one false positive under no effect is $1 - 0.95^{12} = 0.46$, and one result with $p = 0.03$ is far above any corrected threshold (Bonferroni gives $0.05/12 = 0.0042$, and Holm and Benjamini-Hochberg would not reject it either). The subgroup was chosen after seeing the data, which is a further forking path and HARKing: the hypothesis was formed from the result. With 10 per group and a plausible small or medium effect, power is low, so a significant estimate is probably inflated and the positive predictive value is poor. The claim should instead read: no outcome survived correction; one exploratory subgroup pattern ($p = 0.03$ uncorrected) needs a new sample. A better study preregisters one primary outcome, the hypothesis (including any subgroup), the exclusion rules and a sample size computed from the smallest effect of interest (for example about 64 per group for $d = 0.5$ at 80% power), randomly assigns participants to drug or an identical placebo with double blinding, corrects any secondary outcomes (Holm or Benjamini-Hochberg), and reports the effect with an interval.
rubric: Identifies self-selection as a confound and calls for random assignment with a blinded placebo.
rubric: Explains the multiplicity problem across 12 outcomes with the numerical risk of a false positive or a corrected threshold.
rubric: Names the post hoc subgroup analysis as exploratory or HARKing and notes low power and inflated estimates.
rubric: Proposes a preregistered design with a computed sample size, a primary outcome and an effect size with an interval.
