@article
A neuron's firing rate tends to rise with the contrast of a visual stimulus, but not perfectly: trial-to-trial noise scatters the points. How strong is the relationship, and how many extra spikes per second does one more percent of contrast buy? **Correlation** summarizes the strength of a linear relationship; **regression** gives a line for prediction.

Suppose six stimuli of contrast $x$ (percent) evoked mean firing rates $y$ (Hz):

| contrast $x$ (%) | 5 | 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 | 60 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| rate $y$ (Hz) | 12 | 9 | 22 | 18 | 34 | 31 |

### Pearson's correlation

Define the sums of squares and cross-products about the means $\bar{x} = 27.5$ and $\bar{y} = 21$:

$$S_{xx} = \sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2, \quad S_{yy} = \sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2, \quad S_{xy} = \sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y}).$$

**Pearson's $r$** is $r = S_{xy}/\sqrt{S_{xx}S_{yy}}$. It lies between $-1$ and $1$, has the sign of the relationship, is unchanged by changing units or adding constants to either variable, and equals $\pm 1$ only when the points lie exactly on a line. Here $S_{xx} = 2087.5$ and $S_{xy} = 885$, giving $r = 0.863$.

### The least-squares line

The **regression line** $\hat{y} = a + bx$ chooses $a$ and $b$ to minimize the sum of squared **residuals** $e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$. Setting the derivatives of $\sum e_i^2$ with respect to $a$ and $b$ to zero gives

$$b = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}} = r\,\frac{s_y}{s_x}, \qquad a = \bar{y} - b\,\bar{x}.$$

The line passes through $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$, and the residuals sum to zero. For the table, $b = 885/2087.5 = 0.424$ Hz per percent contrast and $a = 21 - 0.424 \times 27.5 = 9.34$ Hz. The slope is the predicted change in $y$ for one unit of $x$; the intercept is the prediction at $x = 0$, which may be meaningless if $x = 0$ is far from the data. The square $r^2 = 0.744$ is the **proportion of the variance of $y$ explained** by the line.

```python
import math


def pearson_r(x, y):
    mx, my = sum(x) / len(x), sum(y) / len(y)
    sxy = sum((a - mx) * (b - my) for a, b in zip(x, y))
    sxx = sum((a - mx) ** 2 for a in x)
    syy = sum((b - my) ** 2 for b in y)
    return sxy / math.sqrt(sxx * syy)


def fit_line(x, y):
    """Least-squares slope and intercept."""
    mx, my = sum(x) / len(x), sum(y) / len(y)
    sxy = sum((a - mx) * (b - my) for a, b in zip(x, y))
    sxx = sum((a - mx) ** 2 for a in x)
    slope = sxy / sxx
    return slope, my - slope * mx


contrast = [5, 10, 20, 30, 40, 60]
rate = [12, 9, 22, 18, 34, 31]
slope, intercept = fit_line(contrast, rate)
print(round(pearson_r(contrast, rate), 3), round(slope, 3), round(intercept, 2))
```

It prints `0.863 0.424 9.34`. To judge whether a slope could be due to chance, test $H_0\!: \beta = 0$ with $t = b/\text{SE}_b$, where $\text{SE}_b = \sqrt{\dfrac{SS_{\text{res}}/(n-2)}{S_{xx}}}$ and $SS_{\text{res}} = \sum e_i^2$, with $n - 2$ degrees of freedom (two parameters were fitted). For $r$ itself, a confidence interval uses the **Fisher transformation**: $z' = \operatorname{arctanh}(r)$ is approximately normal with standard error $1/\sqrt{n-3}$, so the interval is $\tanh\big(z' \pm 1.96/\sqrt{n-3}\big)$ for 95%.

### What a correlation does not tell you

- **Outliers and leverage.** One extreme point can create or destroy a correlation: the pairs $(1,1), (2,2), (3,3), (4,40)$ have $r = 0.80$, while the first three alone lie exactly on a line. Plot the data, and report results with and without influential points.
- **Restricted range.** If you sample only a narrow slice of $x$ (say, only good students), $r$ shrinks even if the underlying relationship is strong.
- **Nonlinearity.** $r$ measures linear association; a perfect U-shaped relationship can have $r = 0$.
- **Confounding and direction.** A correlation between hippocampal volume and memory score does not show that volume causes memory (or the reverse); age or a third variable may drive both. Only manipulation, as in Week 1, supports a causal reading.
- **Prediction outside the data.** A line fitted on contrasts 5 to 60% says nothing reliable about 100%, where firing rates saturate.

Regression also produces **regression toward the mean**: when two measures are imperfectly correlated, a participant extreme on the first is, on average, less extreme on the second, with no causal explanation needed.

@example
title: Fitting a line by hand
problem: Five participants slept 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9 hours, and their mean RTs the next morning were 560, 540, 515, 500 and 485 ms. Find the least-squares line, $r$, and the predicted RT after 6.5 hours of sleep.
step: Means: $\bar{x} = 7$ hours and $\bar{y} = 520$ ms. Deviations of $x$: $-2, -1, 0, 1, 2$; deviations of $y$: $40, 20, -5, -20, -35$.
step: $S_{xx} = 4 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 4 = 10$ and $S_{xy} = (-2)(40) + (-1)(20) + 0 + (1)(-20) + (2)(-35) = -190$. Also $S_{yy} = 1600 + 400 + 25 + 400 + 1225 = 3650$.
step: The slope is $b = -190/10 = -19$ ms per hour and the intercept is $a = 520 - (-19)(7) = 653$ ms, so $\hat{y} = 653 - 19x$.
step: The correlation is $r = -190/\sqrt{10 \times 3650} = -190/191.05 = -0.995$, so $r^2 = 0.989$.
step: At $x = 6.5$ hours, $\hat{y} = 653 - 19 \times 6.5 = 529.5$ ms. This is a prediction inside the observed range of 5 to 9 hours. Because the participants were not randomly assigned sleep durations, the slope describes an association, not what extra sleep would cause.
answer: $\hat{y} = 653 - 19x$, $r = -0.995$, predicted RT $529.5$ ms at 6.5 hours.

@mistakes
- Interpreting $r$ as the slope or as the "percent explained": $r^2$ is the proportion of variance explained, and the slope depends on the units.
- Concluding causation from a correlation: confounds and reverse causation produce the same scatter.
- Extrapolating the line beyond the range of the data.
- Computing $r$ on a range that was selected on the outcome or on $x$ (restricted range), then comparing it with correlations from full samples.

```python
def residuals(x, y):
    """Residuals of the least-squares line of y on x."""
    n = len(x)
    mx, my = sum(x) / n, sum(y) / n
    sxy = sum((a - mx) * (b - my) for a, b in zip(x, y))
    sxx = sum((a - mx) ** 2 for a in x)
    slope = sxy / sxx
    intercept = my - slope * mx
    return [b - (intercept + slope * a) for a, b in zip(x, y)]
```
hint: Fit the slope and intercept first, then subtract each prediction from the matching observed value.
solution: The residuals of a least-squares line have two properties that the tests check: they sum to zero, and they are uncorrelated with $x$, so $\sum e_i x_i = 0$. Points exactly on a line have all residuals zero.

model:
```python
import math


def fisher_ci(r, n, z=1.96):
    """Fisher-transformation confidence interval for a correlation."""
    center = math.atanh(r)
    half = z / math.sqrt(n - 3)
    return math.tanh(center - half), math.tanh(center + half)
```
hint: Work on the transformed scale, where the standard error is $1/\sqrt{n-3}$, and transform back at the end.
solution: The transformation $\operatorname{arctanh}(r)$ makes the sampling distribution of $r$ roughly normal with a standard error that does not depend on the true correlation. The back-transformed interval is asymmetric around $r$ and stays inside $(-1, 1)$; it narrows as $n$ grows, as the last test shows.

model:
```python
import math


def slope_t(x, y):
    """t statistic and df for the slope of the least-squares line."""
    n = len(x)
    mx, my = sum(x) / n, sum(y) / n
    sxx = sum((a - mx) ** 2 for a in x)
    sxy = sum((a - mx) * (b - my) for a, b in zip(x, y))
    slope = sxy / sxx
    intercept = my - slope * mx
    ss_res = sum((b - intercept - slope * a) ** 2 for a, b in zip(x, y))
    se = math.sqrt(ss_res / (n - 2) / sxx)
    return slope / se, n - 2
```
hint: Residual variance is $SS_{\text{res}}/(n-2)$ because two parameters were fitted; divide it by $S_{xx}$ before the square root.
solution: The slope's standard error is the residual standard deviation divided by $\sqrt{S_{xx}}$. Spreading $x$ more widely (a larger $S_{xx}$) or having less scatter around the line makes $t$ larger. Flipping the sign of $y$ flips the sign of $t$ and leaves its size unchanged.
