@article
You test RTs under three drug doses (none, low, high) with four participants each. Group means are 487.5, 458.8 and 435.0 ms. You could run three t tests, but each carries a 5% false-alarm rate, and the chance of at least one false alarm across three independent tests at $\alpha = 0.05$ is $1 - 0.95^3 = 14.3\%$. **Analysis of variance** (ANOVA) asks one question first: are the group means more different than chance variation among people would produce?

### Partitioning the variability

Let there be $k$ groups, $N$ observations in total, group means $\bar{x}_j$ and the grand mean $\bar{x}$. Total variability splits exactly into two parts:

$$\underbrace{\sum (x_{ij} - \bar{x})^2}_{SS_{\text{total}}} = \underbrace{\sum_j n_j(\bar{x}_j - \bar{x})^2}_{SS_{\text{between}}} + \underbrace{\sum_j\sum_i (x_{ij} - \bar{x}_j)^2}_{SS_{\text{within}}}.$$

Each sum of squares is divided by its degrees of freedom to give a **mean square**: $MS_{\text{between}} = SS_{\text{between}}/(k-1)$ and $MS_{\text{within}} = SS_{\text{within}}/(N-k)$. If $H_0$ (all population means equal) is true, both mean squares estimate the same noise variance, and their ratio

$$F = \frac{MS_{\text{between}}}{MS_{\text{within}}}$$

is near 1; it follows an $F$ distribution with $(k-1, N-k)$ degrees of freedom. A large $F$ means the group means differ more than the within-group scatter predicts. The test is one-sided in $F$ because only large values contradict $H_0$. For two groups, $F = t^2$ for the pooled $t$ test.

```python
def one_way_anova(groups):
    """F statistic, df between, df within for a list of lists."""
    values = [v for g in groups for v in g]
    n_total, k = len(values), len(groups)
    grand = sum(values) / n_total
    ss_between = ss_within = 0.0
    for g in groups:
        m = sum(g) / len(g)
        ss_between += len(g) * (m - grand) ** 2
        ss_within += sum((v - m) ** 2 for v in g)
    df_b, df_w = k - 1, n_total - k
    return (ss_between / df_b) / (ss_within / df_w), df_b, df_w


doses = [[480, 495, 470, 505], [455, 470, 462, 448], [430, 445, 425, 440]]
print(one_way_anova(doses))
```

The output has $F = 20.04$ with $(2, 9)$ degrees of freedom; here $SS_{\text{between}} = 5529.2$ and $SS_{\text{within}} = 1241.8$. The 5% critical value for $F(2, 9)$ is $4.256$, so the means differ by more than chance predicts ($p \approx 0.0005$). The **effect size** $\eta^2 = SS_{\text{between}}/SS_{\text{total}} = 0.82$ is the proportion of variability explained by group.

A significant $F$ says that not all means are equal; it does not say which differ. Follow-up comparisons between pairs need a correction for multiplicity (Week 10). The test assumes independent observations, roughly normal residuals and similar variances in the groups; it is fairly robust to mild departures when group sizes are equal, and a design with the same people in every condition needs the repeated-measures analysis of Week 8.

### Factorial designs and interactions

A **factorial design** crosses two or more factors so that every combination of levels occurs. Take a 2 by 2 experiment on RTs: sleep (rested, deprived) crossed with trial type (congruent, incongruent). It answers three questions at once: the **main effect** of sleep (averaging over trial type), the main effect of trial type, and the **interaction**: does the effect of one factor depend on the level of the other? With cell means $m_{11}, m_{12}, m_{21}, m_{22}$ (first index sleep, second index trial type), the interaction contrast is

$$c = (m_{11} - m_{12}) - (m_{21} - m_{22}) = m_{11} - m_{12} - m_{21} + m_{22},$$

the difference between the two effects of trial type. With $n$ observations per cell (a **balanced** design), $SS_{AB} = n\,c^2/4$, each effect has $1$ degree of freedom, and the error term is the pooled within-cell scatter, $MS_{\text{within}} = SS_{\text{within}}/(4(n-1))$. Each effect's $F$ is its sum of squares divided by $MS_{\text{within}}$. For the main effects, $SS_A = n\,(\bar{m}_{1\cdot} - \bar{m}_{2\cdot})^2$, where $\bar{m}_{1\cdot}$ is the average of the two cell means in row 1 (and $SS_B$ is defined the same way for columns).

Interactions deserve interpretation first. If the interaction is large, a main effect averaged over the other factor can mislead: "incongruent trials are slower" may be true only for deprived participants. Always look at the cell means (or a table of simple effects) before reading the main effects. A "crossover" interaction in which an effect reverses direction between levels is the clearest case.

@example
title: The interaction in a balanced 2 by 2 design
problem: Sleep (rested or deprived) is crossed with trial type (congruent or incongruent), with $n = 3$ participants per cell. Cell data in ms are rested-congruent $500, 520, 510$; rested-incongruent $560, 590, 575$; deprived-congruent $530, 545, 540$; deprived-incongruent $650, 630, 640$. Is the interaction significant at $\alpha = 0.05$, given the critical value $F(1, 8) = 5.318$?
step: Compute the cell means: $510$, $575$, $538.33$ and $640$ ms (in the order given).
step: Find the simple effects of trial type: $575 - 510 = 65$ ms for rested participants and $640 - 538.33 = 101.67$ ms for deprived participants. The interaction contrast is their difference: $c = 101.67 - 65 = 36.67$ ms.
step: Compute $SS_{AB} = n c^2/4 = 3 \times 36.67^2/4 = 1008.3$, with $1$ degree of freedom.
step: Find the within-cell sum of squares: the cells contribute $200$, $450$, $116.67$ and $200$, totaling $966.67$. With $4(n-1) = 8$ degrees of freedom, $MS_{\text{within}} = 966.67/8 = 120.83$.
step: Divide: $F(1, 8) = 1008.3/120.83 = 8.34 > 5.318$, so the interaction is significant. The congruency effect is larger when participants are sleep deprived (about 102 ms) than when rested (65 ms), and the main effects should be read with that in mind.
answer: $F(1, 8) = 8.34$, significant; the incongruent-trial cost is about 37 ms larger for deprived than for rested participants.

@mistakes
- Running all pairwise t tests instead of one overall test or a corrected follow-up: the familywise false-alarm rate grows with every extra test.
- Reading a significant $F$ as "every group differs from every other": it says only that the means are not all equal.
- Interpreting main effects first when there is a strong interaction: the average effect may describe neither level.
- Mixing up the two degrees of freedom: between is $k - 1$ (the numerator), within is $N - k$ (the denominator).

```python
def eta_squared(groups):
    """Proportion of total variability explained by group membership."""
    values = [v for g in groups for v in g]
    grand = sum(values) / len(values)
    ss_total = sum((v - grand) ** 2 for v in values)
    ss_between = sum(
        len(g) * (sum(g) / len(g) - grand) ** 2 for g in groups
    )
    return ss_between / ss_total
```
hint: Compute the grand mean of all observations first; $SS_{\text{total}}$ uses every observation, $SS_{\text{between}}$ uses the group means weighted by group size.
solution: Because $SS_{\text{total}} = SS_{\text{between}} + SS_{\text{within}}$, the ratio is the explained share. Identical groups give 0 and groups with no internal scatter give 1. If every observation is identical, $SS_{\text{total}}$ is 0 and the ratio is undefined, so a real analysis must handle that case.

```python
def two_by_two_f(cells):
    """F statistics for A, B and the A x B interaction, balanced design."""
    n = len(cells[0])
    means = [sum(c) / n for c in cells]
    m11, m12, m21, m22 = means
    ss_a = n * ((m11 + m12) / 2 - (m21 + m22) / 2) ** 2
    ss_b = n * ((m11 + m21) / 2 - (m12 + m22) / 2) ** 2
    ss_ab = n * (m11 - m12 - m21 + m22) ** 2 / 4
    ss_within = sum(
        sum((v - m) ** 2 for v in c) for c, m in zip(cells, means)
    )
    ms_within = ss_within / (4 * (n - 1))
    return ss_a / ms_within, ss_b / ms_within, ss_ab / ms_within
```
hint: Get the four cell means, build the three sums of squares from the formulas in the prompt, and divide each by the same error mean square.
solution: With one degree of freedom per effect, each mean square equals its sum of squares, and all three effects share the pooled within-cell error. In the additive test the interaction contrast $10 - 20 - 14 + 24$ is exactly 0, so $F_{AB} = 0$ even though both main effects are large.

```python
def omega_squared(groups):
    """Omega squared effect size for a one-way design."""
    values = [v for g in groups for v in g]
    n_total, k = len(values), len(groups)
    grand = sum(values) / n_total
    ss_total = sum((v - grand) ** 2 for v in values)
    ss_within = 0.0
    for g in groups:
        m = sum(g) / len(g)
        ss_within += sum((v - m) ** 2 for v in g)
    ss_between = ss_total - ss_within
    ms_within = ss_within / (n_total - k)
    return (ss_between - (k - 1) * ms_within) / (ss_total + ms_within)
```
hint: Get $SS_{\text{total}}$ and $SS_{\text{within}}$; $SS_{\text{between}}$ is their difference.
solution: $\omega^2$ subtracts the amount of between-group variability expected from noise alone, $(k-1)MS_{\text{within}}$, so it is smaller than $\eta^2$, noticeably so in small samples. In the last test, $\eta^2 = 24/28 = 0.857$ but $\omega^2 = 23/29 = 0.793$.
