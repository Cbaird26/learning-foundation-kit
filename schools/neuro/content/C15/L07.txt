@article
Eight adults do a reaction-time task. The more hours per week they play video games, the faster they are: the simple regression slope of RT on gaming hours is $-8.1$ ms per hour, with $r = -0.91$. Tempting headline: gaming speeds you up. But the more they game, the younger they are, and young adults are quick anyway. **Multiple regression** puts both predictors in one model so each coefficient measures a predictor's association with the outcome while the others are held fixed.

| age (years) | 20 | 25 | 30 | 35 | 40 | 45 | 50 | 55 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| gaming (h/week) | 14 | 12 | 13 | 8 | 9 | 5 | 6 | 2 |
| RT (ms) | 352 | 371 | 390 | 396 | 419 | 431 | 452 | 455 |

### The model and its fit

The model is $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots + \beta_k x_k + \varepsilon$. With the data matrix $\mathbf{X}$ (a first column of ones for the intercept, then one column per predictor) and the response vector $\mathbf{y}$, the least-squares coefficients $\mathbf{b}$ minimize $\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{b}\|^2$ and satisfy the **normal equations**

$$(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})\,\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}.$$

This is a small linear system, solvable by Gaussian elimination: no library is needed.

```python
def solve(A, b):
    """Solve A x = b by Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting."""
    n = len(b)
    M = [row[:] + [b[i]] for i, row in enumerate(A)]
    for c in range(n):
        p = max(range(c, n), key=lambda r: abs(M[r][c]))
        M[c], M[p] = M[p], M[c]
        for r in range(c + 1, n):
            f = M[r][c] / M[c][c]
            M[r] = [a - f * b2 for a, b2 in zip(M[r], M[c])]
    x = [0.0] * n
    for r in range(n - 1, -1, -1):
        x[r] = (M[r][n] - sum(M[r][j] * x[j] for j in range(r + 1, n))) / M[r][r]
    return x


def ols(rows, y):
    """Least-squares coefficients [b0, b1, ...]; rows hold the predictors."""
    X = [[1.0] + list(r) for r in rows]
    k = len(X[0])
    XtX = [[sum(r[i] * r[j] for r in X) for j in range(k)] for i in range(k)]
    Xty = [sum(r[i] * yy for r, yy in zip(X, y)) for i in range(k)]
    return solve(XtX, Xty)


age = [20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50, 55]
gaming = [14, 12, 13, 8, 9, 5, 6, 2]
rt = [352, 371, 390, 396, 419, 431, 452, 455]
b = ols(list(zip(gaming, age)), rt)
print([round(v, 2) for v in b])
```

The output is `[236.27, 2.8, 3.94]`: with age fixed, each additional gaming hour per week goes with $+2.8$ ms, and each extra year of age with $+3.94$ ms. The gaming slope **changed sign** once age entered the model. A coefficient's value depends on which other variables are in the model.

### Interpreting and judging the model

A partial slope $b_j$ is the predicted change in $y$ for a one-unit change in $x_j$ **among people who have the same values of the other predictors**. Equivalently (the Frisch–Waugh result), $b_j$ is the simple slope of $y$ on the part of $x_j$ left over after regressing it on the other predictors. It supports a causal reading only if no important variable is left out and the direction is known; unmeasured confounds still bias it.

$R^2 = 1 - SS_{\text{res}}/SS_{\text{total}}$ never decreases when a predictor is added, even a useless one, so it rewards overfitting. **Adjusted $R^2$** charges for each predictor:

$$R^2_{\text{adj}} = 1 - (1 - R^2)\,\frac{n - 1}{n - k - 1},$$

with $n$ observations and $k$ predictors. Here $R^2 = 0.9958$ and $R^2_{\text{adj}} = 0.9941$. A model with many predictors and few observations fits the noise: it predicts the sample well and new data badly, so predictors should be chosen by theory or checked on held-out data, never selected after looking at which ones reach $p < 0.05$.

### Collinearity

When predictors are strongly correlated, the data cannot separate their effects. The **variance inflation factor** for predictor $j$ is $\text{VIF}_j = 1/(1 - R_j^2)$, where $R_j^2$ is the $R^2$ from regressing $x_j$ on the other predictors; with two predictors $R_j^2 = r_{12}^2$. The standard error of $b_j$ is multiplied by $\sqrt{\text{VIF}_j}$. Here $r_{12} = -0.95$ between age and gaming, so $\text{VIF} = 10.2$, and the standard errors are about three times as large as they would be with uncorrelated predictors: the sign flip of the gaming slope should not be over-read either. Collinearity does not bias the coefficients or the predictions; it makes individual coefficients unstable. Remedies are more data that vary the predictors independently, dropping or combining redundant predictors, or an experiment that sets them independently.

@example
title: Two predictors from sums of squares
problem: Five trials have predictors $x_1 = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5$ and $x_2 = 2, 1, 4, 3, 5$ and outcome $y = 5, 6, 11, 10, 16$. Fit $\hat{y} = b_0 + b_1 x_1 + b_2 x_2$ using centered sums.
step: Center each variable (means 3, 3 and 9.6), then compute $S_{11} = \sum (x_1 - \bar{x}_1)^2 = 10$, $S_{22} = 10$, $S_{12} = \sum (x_1 - \bar{x}_1)(x_2 - \bar{x}_2) = 8$, $S_{1y} = 26$ and $S_{2y} = 26$.
step: For two predictors the normal equations reduce to $S_{11}b_1 + S_{12}b_2 = S_{1y}$ and $S_{12}b_1 + S_{22}b_2 = S_{2y}$, so $b_1 = \dfrac{S_{1y}S_{22} - S_{2y}S_{12}}{S_{11}S_{22} - S_{12}^2} = \dfrac{260 - 208}{100 - 64} = \dfrac{52}{36} = 1.444$.
step: By the symmetry of these numbers, $b_2 = (S_{2y}S_{11} - S_{1y}S_{12})/36 = 52/36 = 1.444$ as well.
step: The intercept makes the plane pass through the means: $b_0 = 9.6 - 1.444 \times 3 - 1.444 \times 3 = 0.933$.
step: Compare with the simple regression of $y$ on $x_1$ alone: its slope is $S_{1y}/S_{11} = 2.6$, larger than the partial slope $1.444$, because $x_2$ is correlated with $x_1$ ($r_{12} = 0.8$) and carries part of the effect.
answer: $\hat{y} = 0.933 + 1.444\,x_1 + 1.444\,x_2$; the simple slope of $x_1$ (2.6) overstates its partial effect.

@mistakes
- Reading a partial slope as the simple slope: the two answer different questions and can even differ in sign.
- Judging a model by $R^2$ alone: it cannot fall when predictors are added, so use adjusted $R^2$, held-out data or theory.
- Dropping predictors one at a time until everything is significant: the surviving p-values are no longer valid.
- Claiming that a coefficient is "the effect of $x_j$" when important confounds were not measured.

model:
```python
def r_squared(y, yhat):
    """Proportion of the variance of y accounted for by the fitted values."""
    my = sum(y) / len(y)
    ss_res = sum((a - b) ** 2 for a, b in zip(y, yhat))
    ss_tot = sum((a - my) ** 2 for a in y)
    return 1 - ss_res / ss_tot
```
hint: Compare the squared errors of the model with the squared errors of simply predicting the mean of `y`.
solution: Predicting the mean gives $R^2 = 0$, and perfect predictions give 1. For fitted values that are not from a least-squares fit with an intercept, $R^2$ can be negative, as in the last test, where the predictions are worse than the mean.

model:
```python
def residualize(x, z):
    """Residuals of x after regressing x on z."""
    n = len(x)
    mx, mz = sum(x) / n, sum(z) / n
    szz = sum((b - mz) ** 2 for b in z)
    sxz = sum((a - mx) * (b - mz) for a, b in zip(x, z))
    slope = sxz / szz
    intercept = mx - slope * mz
    return [a - (intercept + slope * b) for a, b in zip(x, z)]
```
hint: Regress `x` on `z` as in simple regression, then subtract each fitted value from the observed `x`.
solution: The residuals have mean zero and are uncorrelated with `z`, so they contain only the variation in `x` that `z` cannot account for. The slope of `y` on them, $1.745$ here, equals the coefficient of `x` from the full two-predictor regression (the Frisch–Waugh result), which is why a partial slope means "with `z` held fixed".

```python
import math


def vif_two(x1, x2):
    """Variance inflation factor for a two-predictor model."""
    n = len(x1)
    m1, m2 = sum(x1) / n, sum(x2) / n
    s12 = sum((a - m1) * (b - m2) for a, b in zip(x1, x2))
    s11 = sum((a - m1) ** 2 for a in x1)
    s22 = sum((b - m2) ** 2 for b in x2)
    r2 = s12 ** 2 / (s11 * s22)
    return 1 / (1 - r2)
```
hint: Compute $r^2 = S_{12}^2/(S_{11}S_{22})$ from the centered sums, then invert $1 - r^2$.
solution: The VIF depends only on the squared correlation between the predictors, so a negative correlation inflates variance as much as a positive one. Uncorrelated predictors give 1, and the value grows without bound as $|r| \to 1$ (it is undefined for perfectly collinear predictors).
