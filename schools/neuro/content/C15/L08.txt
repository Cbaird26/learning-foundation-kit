@article
Five participants each perform a color-naming task in three conditions: congruent, neutral and incongruent. Their mean RTs (ms) are:

| participant | congruent | neutral | incongruent |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 480 | 505 | 540 |
| 2 | 520 | 550 | 570 |
| 3 | 450 | 465 | 505 |
| 4 | 560 | 580 | 630 |
| 5 | 500 | 520 | 555 |

Everyone is slower on incongruent trials than on congruent ones, yet participants differ by more than 100 ms in overall speed. A one-way ANOVA that treats the 15 numbers as three independent groups would let that large between-person variability swamp the condition effect. Because each participant appears in every condition, we can remove it.

### Repeated-measures ANOVA

With $n$ participants and $k$ conditions, total variability splits into three parts:

$$SS_{\text{total}} = SS_{\text{cond}} + SS_{\text{subj}} + SS_{\text{error}}.$$

$SS_{\text{cond}} = n\sum_j(\bar{x}_{\cdot j} - \bar{x})^2$ measures differences between condition means; $SS_{\text{subj}} = k\sum_i(\bar{x}_{i\cdot} - \bar{x})^2$ measures stable differences between participants; the remainder, $SS_{\text{error}}$, is how much participants' condition effects vary around the average effect. Then

$$F = \frac{SS_{\text{cond}}/(k-1)}{SS_{\text{error}}/\big((k-1)(n-1)\big)}, \qquad \text{df} = \big(k-1,\ (k-1)(n-1)\big).$$

The subject sum of squares is simply taken out of the error term. With two conditions, $F$ equals the square of the paired $t$ statistic.

```python
def rm_anova(data):
    """Repeated-measures F; data[i][j] is subject i in condition j."""
    n, k = len(data), len(data[0])
    grand = sum(sum(r) for r in data) / (n * k)
    cond = [sum(r[j] for r in data) / n for j in range(k)]
    subj = [sum(r) / k for r in data]
    ss_cond = n * sum((m - grand) ** 2 for m in cond)
    ss_subj = k * sum((m - grand) ** 2 for m in subj)
    ss_total = sum((v - grand) ** 2 for r in data for v in r)
    ss_error = ss_total - ss_cond - ss_subj
    df1, df2 = k - 1, (k - 1) * (n - 1)
    return (ss_cond / df1) / (ss_error / df2), df1, df2


data = [[480, 505, 540], [520, 550, 570], [450, 465, 505],
        [560, 580, 630], [500, 520, 555]]
F, df1, df2 = rm_anova(data)
print(round(F, 2), df1, df2)
```

The result is $F(2, 8) = 123.95$ ($SS_{\text{cond}} = 8573.3$, $SS_{\text{subj}} = 22723.3$, $SS_{\text{error}} = 276.7$), far above the 5% critical value $4.459$. The same data in a between-subject ANOVA give only $F(2, 12) = 2.24$ (critical value $3.885$), not significant, because the subject variance sits in the error term. That is the power of a within-subject design. Its cost: order and practice effects, handled by counterbalancing the order of conditions. The test also assumes **sphericity** (equal variances of the differences between all pairs of conditions) when $k \ge 3$; violations inflate $F$, and common corrections (Greenhouse–Geisser) shrink the degrees of freedom.

### Why trials are not independent

Participants differ in speed, so two RTs from the same person are more alike than two RTs from different people. The **intraclass correlation** (ICC) is the share of variance due to the person, $\text{ICC} = \sigma_u^2/(\sigma_u^2 + \sigma_e^2)$. From the ANOVA mean squares, $\hat{\sigma}_e^2 = MS_{\text{error}}$ and $\hat{\sigma}_u^2 = (MS_{\text{subj}} - MS_{\text{error}})/k$, floored at zero. Here $\hat{\sigma}_u^2 = 1882$ and $\hat{\sigma}_e^2 = 34.6$, so the ICC is $0.98$. Analyzing 200 trials from each of 10 people as if they were 2000 independent observations is **pseudoreplication**: the effective sample size is closer to $N/(1 + (m-1)\,\text{ICC})$ for $m$ trials per person (the design effect), and p-values become far too small.

### Mixed-effects models in outline

A **linear mixed-effects model** states the structure directly. For participant $i$ in condition $j$,

$$y_{ij} = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{cond}_j + u_i + e_{ij}, \qquad u_i \sim N(0, \sigma_u^2),\ e_{ij} \sim N(0, \sigma_e^2).$$

The $\beta$'s are **fixed effects** (the effects of the conditions we care about); $u_i$ is a **random intercept**: each participant's own baseline, treated as drawn from a population. A **random slope** lets the condition effect itself vary across participants; **crossed random effects** let stimuli (items) vary as well as participants. Mixed models handle unequal numbers of trials and missing cells that break the classical ANOVA, and they use trial-level data without pseudoreplication. They estimate each participant's baseline by **partial pooling**: the estimated offset is the raw offset shrunk toward the grand mean by a factor $\lambda = \sigma_u^2/(\sigma_u^2 + \sigma_e^2/m)$, where $m$ is the number of observations behind that participant's mean. Noisy means are pulled in more. Researchers fit these models with `lme4` in R or `MixedLM` in statsmodels; the model structure (which effects are random) should follow the design and be stated in advance.

```text
# statsmodels (not run in the browser)
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf
fit = smf.mixedlm("rt ~ cond", df, groups=df["subject"]).fit()
```

@example
title: The repeated-measures F equals the paired t squared
problem: Four participants have mean RTs in two conditions: (12, 16), (14, 17), (10, 15), (16, 18) in units of 10 ms. Compute the repeated-measures $F$ and confirm that it equals $t^2$ from a paired test.
step: Means: grand mean $14.75$; condition means $13$ and $16.5$; subject means $14, 15.5, 12.5, 17$.
step: $SS_{\text{cond}} = 4\big((13 - 14.75)^2 + (16.5 - 14.75)^2\big) = 24.5$ and $SS_{\text{subj}} = 2(0.75^2 + 0.75^2 + 2.25^2 + 2.25^2) = 22.5$.
step: The deviations of all eight values from the grand mean square and sum to $SS_{\text{total}} = 49.5$, so $SS_{\text{error}} = 49.5 - 24.5 - 22.5 = 2.5$ with $(k-1)(n-1) = 3$ degrees of freedom: $MS_{\text{error}} = 0.833$.
step: $F(1, 3) = 24.5/0.833 = 29.4$ (the 5% critical value is 10.13, so it is significant).
step: Paired $t$: the differences are $4, 3, 5, 2$ with mean $3.5$ and $s_d = 1.291$, so $t = 3.5/(1.291/2) = 5.42$ and $t^2 = 29.4$, matching $F$.
answer: $F(1, 3) = 29.4 = 5.42^2$.

@mistakes
- Analyzing within-subject data with an independent-groups test: it wastes the power gained by removing stable differences between people.
- Treating every trial as an independent observation: trials from one participant are correlated, so p-values come out too small.
- Fitting a mixed model with random effects chosen after seeing which fit gives the best p-value: the random-effects structure follows the design.
- Forgetting to counterbalance the order of conditions in a within-subject design: order effects then pose as condition effects.

model:
```python
def icc_rm(data):
    """Intraclass correlation from repeated-measures variance components."""
    n, k = len(data), len(data[0])
    grand = sum(sum(r) for r in data) / (n * k)
    cond = [sum(r[j] for r in data) / n for j in range(k)]
    subj = [sum(r) / k for r in data]
    ss_cond = n * sum((m - grand) ** 2 for m in cond)
    ss_subj = k * sum((m - grand) ** 2 for m in subj)
    ss_total = sum((v - grand) ** 2 for r in data for v in r)
    ss_error = ss_total - ss_cond - ss_subj
    ms_subj = ss_subj / (n - 1)
    ms_error = ss_error / ((k - 1) * (n - 1))
    var_u = max(0.0, (ms_subj - ms_error) / k)
    return var_u / (var_u + ms_error)
```
hint: Compute the three sums of squares as in the lesson, convert two of them to mean squares, and floor the subject variance at zero.
solution: The ICC is the share of variance due to stable differences between people. In the last test every participant has the same mean, so the subject mean square is 0, less than the error mean square; the raw estimate of $\sigma_u^2$ would be negative, and flooring at zero gives an ICC of exactly 0.

model:
```python
def shrunken_means(means, m, var_u, var_e):
    """Partial-pooling estimates: shrink each mean toward the grand mean."""
    grand = sum(means) / len(means)
    lam = var_u / (var_u + var_e / m)
    return [grand + lam * (x - grand) for x in means]
```
hint: Compute the shrinkage factor once; each estimate is the grand mean plus that fraction of the participant's deviation.
solution: When between-person variance dominates (`var_e` tiny), `lam` is near 1 and nothing changes; when noise dominates, `lam` is near 0 and every participant is pulled to the grand mean. Shrinkage preserves the average of the estimates, as the last test shows, and it pulls extreme, noisy means in the most.

model:
```python
def normalize_within(data):
    """Subtract each participant's mean and add back the grand mean."""
    n, k = len(data), len(data[0])
    grand = sum(sum(r) for r in data) / (n * k)
    return [[v - sum(r) / k + grand for v in r] for r in data]
```
hint: Use a list comprehension per row, and compute the grand mean once before the loop.
solution: After the adjustment every participant has the same mean (the grand mean), so only within-person variation remains, while the condition means stay as they were: the subject offset averages to zero across participants. Standard errors computed on the normalized values reflect the precision of within-subject differences.
