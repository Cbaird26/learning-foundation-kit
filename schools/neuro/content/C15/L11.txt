@article
A lab measures four outcomes in a study of a training program, and a participant either does or does not respond to each of two different exclusion rules. If the program has no effect, how likely is it that some combination reaches $p < 0.05$? Far more often than 5%. The Week 10 formula already says why: with $k$ independent looks at pure noise, the chance of at least one hit is $1 - 0.95^k$, which is $0.185$ for $k = 4$ and $0.265$ for $k = 6$. The analyses are not truly independent, so the real inflation is somewhat smaller, but it is large.

### Questionable research practices

Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn (2011) showed by simulation that ordinary-looking flexibility in data collection and analysis can push the false-positive rate far above the nominal 5%. The main practices are:

- **p-hacking**: trying several outcomes, covariates, exclusion rules or subgroups and reporting the one that gave $p < 0.05$.
- **Optional stopping**: testing after every few participants and stopping as soon as the result is significant. With enough looks, a true null will cross the line sooner or later.
- **HARKing** (hypothesizing after the results are known): presenting an exploratory finding as if it had been predicted.
- **Selective reporting**: leaving out the conditions or measures that did not work (the file-drawer problem for a whole study is **publication bias**).

None requires bad intent. The remedy is to make the analysis plan unable to depend on the data.

### Counting the chances

How fast does the risk grow with the number of chances? For $k$ independent tests of true nulls it is $1 - (1-\alpha)^k$:

```python
def chance_of_false_positive(k, alpha=0.05):
    """P(at least one p < alpha among k independent true-null tests)."""
    return 1 - (1 - alpha) ** k


for k in (1, 2, 4, 6, 10):
    print(k, round(chance_of_false_positive(k), 3))
```

It prints the pairs `1 0.05`, `2 0.098`, `4 0.185`, `6 0.265` and `10 0.401`. Repeated looks at accumulating data are not independent (each look reuses the earlier participants), so for optional stopping the inflation is smaller than these numbers, but it has no upper limit short of 100%: the more often you look, the more likely noise crosses the line at some look. The worked example below shows the mechanism with a procedure run by hand. The honest options are a **fixed sample size** set in advance, or a **sequential design** whose thresholds are adjusted for the number of looks.

### Preregistration and registered reports

A **preregistration** is a time-stamped, public record, made before the data are analyzed (ideally before they are collected), of the hypotheses, design, planned sample size and stopping rule, the exclusion criteria, the primary outcome and the planned analysis, including the tests and any correction for multiple comparisons. It does not forbid exploring the data; it separates **confirmatory** analyses (those in the plan) from **exploratory** ones (reported as such, as hypotheses for the next study). Deviations are allowed if disclosed with reasons. In a **registered report**, a journal reviews the question and the methods before the results exist and agrees to publish regardless of the outcome, which also removes publication bias.

### Low power makes significant results unreliable

Suppose a fraction $\pi$ of the hypotheses a field tests are true. Among all tests, a fraction $\pi \cdot \text{power}$ are true positives and $(1 - \pi)\alpha$ are false positives, so the **positive predictive value** (PPV) of a significant result is

$$\text{PPV} = \frac{\pi \cdot \text{power}}{\pi \cdot \text{power} + (1 - \pi)\,\alpha}.$$

With $\pi = 0.1$, power $= 0.2$ and $\alpha = 0.05$: $0.02/(0.02 + 0.045) = 0.31$, so fewer than a third of the significant results are real. Button and colleagues (2013) argued, from published meta-analyses, that typical power in neuroscience is low, which lowers the PPV and also inflates the estimated effects that pass the threshold (the winner's curse).

```python
def ppv(prior, power, alpha=0.05):
    """Probability that a significant result reflects a true effect."""
    hits = prior * power
    return hits / (hits + (1 - prior) * alpha)


print(round(ppv(0.1, 0.2), 3), round(ppv(0.5, 0.8), 3))
```

It prints `0.308 0.941`. With a plausible hypothesis and good power, a significant result is trustworthy; with a long-shot hypothesis and low power, it is not.

### Replication

The Open Science Collaboration (2015) repeated 100 published psychology studies with new samples, mostly larger than the originals. About 97 of the originals had reported significant results; about 36 of the replications did, and the replication effects averaged about half the original sizes. Researchers still debate what this shows: how much is due to false positives, to inflated original estimates, to differences between the original and replication conditions, or to what counts as a successful replication. Both sides agree that a single study, especially a small one, is weak evidence and that a **direct replication** (same procedure, new sample) is informative. A failed replication does not by itself prove the original false, and a single successful one does not prove it true; the cumulative picture across studies and larger samples is what matters. Neuroscience has the added problems of small samples, many analysis choices in imaging and electrophysiology pipelines, and costly data collection, so practices such as pre-planned sample sizes, shared data and code, and registered reports apply directly.

@example
title: Optional stopping by hand
problem: A lab tests whether a mean RT difference is nonzero, looking at the data after 6 and after 8 participants and stopping at the first look with $|t| > t^*$, where $t^* = 2.571$ for $n = 6$ and $2.365$ for $n = 8$. The differences (ms) are $14, -6, 22, 9, -3, 17, 25, 12$. Run the procedure and say what is wrong with it if no correction is made.
step: First look, $n = 6$: the differences $14, -6, 22, 9, -3, 17$ have mean $8.833$ and sample standard deviation $11.197$, so $t = 8.833/(11.197/\sqrt{6}) = 1.93$. This is below $2.571$, so continue.
step: Second look, $n = 8$: add $25$ and $12$. The mean is $11.25$, $s = 11.029$ and $t = 11.25/(11.029/\sqrt{8}) = 2.885$, which exceeds $2.365$.
step: The procedure stops at $n = 8$ and reports "significant at the 5% level".
step: The flaw is that each look used a threshold calibrated for one test. Two looks give two chances for chance to cross the line, so the real false-positive rate under $H_0$ exceeds 5%; with many looks it can approach 100%. The fix is to fix $n$ in advance, or to use thresholds designed for several looks.
answer: The procedure stops at $n = 8$ with $t = 2.885$, but the unadjusted repeated testing makes the 5% claim invalid.

@mistakes
- Treating preregistration as a ban on exploration: exploratory analyses are valuable when labeled as exploratory.
- Equating a failed replication with proof that the original effect is false: low power, differences in procedure and chance all matter.
- Believing that p-hacking needs bad intent: unplanned flexibility inflates false positives even when each choice seems reasonable.
- Trusting a significant result from a small study as if its estimated effect were accurate: significant estimates from low-power studies are inflated.

```python
import math


def replication_power(d_obs, n_rep, shrink=0.5, zcrit=1.96):
    """Approximate power of a replication that expects a shrunken effect."""
    z = shrink * d_obs * math.sqrt(n_rep / 2) - zcrit
    return 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(z / math.sqrt(2)))
```
hint: The planning effect is `shrink * d_obs`; use it in the expected test statistic $d\sqrt{n/2}$.
solution: Planning a replication on the original study's observed effect assumes no inflation. Shrinking the effect to half, as here, shows that a replication with the same size as the original often has far less power than the original's significant result suggests: 30 per group gives only 34% power for an original $d = 0.8$.

model:
```python
import math


def sequential_test(diffs, looks, tcrit):
    """First look with a significant one-sample t test, as (n, t), or None."""
    for n in looks:
        x = diffs[:n]
        m = sum(x) / n
        s = math.sqrt(sum((v - m) ** 2 for v in x) / (n - 1))
        t = m / (s / math.sqrt(n))
        if abs(t) > tcrit[n]:
            return n, t
    return None
```
hint: For each look, use only the first `n` values, compute $t$, and compare its absolute value with `tcrit[n]`; return as soon as one passes.
solution: This is the optional-stopping procedure of the lesson, applied to different data. On `d3` the first look is not significant ($t = 2.39 < 2.776$) but the second is ($t = 3.17 > 2.447$), so the procedure stops at 7. Testing only at the final look would also pass, but a procedure with several looks at a fixed threshold rejects true nulls more often than 5% of the time.

model:
```python
def mean_significant(estimates, ses, zcrit=1.96):
    """Mean of the estimates that reach significance (positive direction)."""
    kept = [e for e, s in zip(estimates, ses) if e / s >= zcrit]
    if not kept:
        return None
    return sum(kept) / len(kept)
```
hint: Filter the estimates with a list comprehension over `zip(estimates, ses)`, then average what is left, taking care with an empty list.
solution: When all studies are noisy, only the unusually large estimates reach the threshold, so the average of the significant ones (27.5) is more than double the average of all (13.29): this is the winner's curse. The last two tests check that each estimate is judged against its own standard error.
