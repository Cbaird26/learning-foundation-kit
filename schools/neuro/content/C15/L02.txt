@article
Ten participants each give one mean RT, and the group mean is 510.5 ms. Run the same experiment tomorrow with ten new participants and the mean will differ, say 502 or 521. A single experiment gives one sample mean, yet we want to say something about the population mean $\mu$ that the next experiment would also be estimating. How far could 510.5 plausibly be from $\mu$?

### The sampling distribution

Take a tiny population of four values, $2, 4, 6, 8$ (population mean 5, population standard deviation $\sigma = \sqrt{5} \approx 2.236$ when dividing by $N$). Draw samples of size $n = 2$ with replacement: there are 16 equally likely ordered samples. Their means are

| sample mean | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| number of samples | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 3 | 2 | 1 |

The mean of the 16 sample means is exactly 5, the population mean: the sample mean is an **unbiased** estimator of $\mu$. Their standard deviation (dividing by 16) is $1.581 = \sigma/\sqrt{2}$. In general, for independent observations, the **sampling distribution** of $\bar{x}$ has mean $\mu$ and standard deviation

$$\text{SE} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}},$$

the **standard error**. Quadrupling the sample size halves it. The **central limit theorem** adds that for moderately large $n$ the sampling distribution is close to normal even when individual values are skewed, as RTs are; how large "moderately" is depends on how skewed the data are.

### From $\sigma$ to $s$: the $t$ distribution

In practice $\sigma$ is unknown and we estimate it by the sample standard deviation $s$ (with $n - 1$), giving $\widehat{\text{SE}} = s/\sqrt{n}$. Replacing $\sigma$ by $s$ adds extra uncertainty, so the standardized mean $(\bar{x} - \mu)/\widehat{\text{SE}}$ follows a **Student $t$ distribution** with $\text{df} = n - 1$ degrees of freedom, which has heavier tails than the normal; it approaches the normal as $n$ grows. A **95% confidence interval** for the mean is

$$\bar{x} \pm t^{*}\,\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}},$$

where $t^*$ cuts off the central 95% of the $t$ distribution. Some values for the central 95%: $t^* = 2.262$ for df $= 9$, $2.131$ for df $= 15$, $2.064$ for df $= 24$ and $1.960$ for the normal ($n \to \infty$). The browser has no statistics library, so this course supplies $t^*$ whenever a problem needs it.

```python
import math


def mean(x):
    return sum(x) / len(x)


def sem(x):
    """Standard error of the mean, using the sample SD (n - 1)."""
    m = mean(x)
    s = math.sqrt(sum((v - m) ** 2 for v in x) / (len(x) - 1))
    return s / math.sqrt(len(x))


def ci_mean(x, tcrit):
    """Confidence interval for the mean, given the t critical value."""
    half = tcrit * sem(x)
    return mean(x) - half, mean(x) + half


rts = [520, 480, 545, 505, 498, 530, 512, 490, 525, 500]
lo, hi = ci_mean(rts, 2.262)
print(round(lo, 2), round(hi, 2))
```

It prints `496.35 524.65`: $\bar{x} = 510.5$, $s = 19.78$, $\widehat{\text{SE}} = 6.25$ ms and half-width $2.262 \times 6.254 = 14.15$ ms.

### What a confidence interval means

The 95% describes the **procedure**: if you repeated the whole experiment many times and built an interval each time by this recipe, about 95% of the intervals would contain $\mu$ (assuming the model's conditions hold). It is not the probability that $\mu$ lies in this particular interval: $\mu$ is a fixed number, and this interval either contains it or does not. A useful reading is the range of population means that are reasonably compatible with the data. The interval's width shows precision; a p-value does not.

For a **proportion** such as accuracy, with $k$ correct out of $n$ and $\hat{p} = k/n$, the **Wald interval** is $\hat{p} \pm z^*\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}$ with $z^* = 1.96$ for 95%. It works poorly for small $n$ or for $\hat{p}$ near 0 or 1 (it can leave $[0, 1]$), where better intervals exist.

To plan a precise study, solve for $n$. If the target half-width is $h$ and a pilot suggests $\sigma$, then $z^*\sigma/\sqrt{n} \le h$ gives $n \ge (z^*\sigma/h)^2$, rounded up. Halving the half-width needs four times the data.

@example
title: A confidence interval from summary numbers
problem: In a go/no-go task, 25 participants have a mean RT of 342 ms with sample standard deviation $s = 48$ ms. Find the 95% confidence interval for the population mean, using $t^* = 2.064$ for df $= 24$.
step: Compute the standard error: $\widehat{\text{SE}} = s/\sqrt{n} = 48/\sqrt{25} = 48/5 = 9.6$ ms.
step: Multiply by the critical value: the half-width is $2.064 \times 9.6 = 19.81$ ms.
step: Add and subtract: $342 - 19.81 = 322.19$ and $342 + 19.81 = 361.81$.
step: Interpret: the data are compatible with population means from about 322 to 362 ms. A theory predicting a mean of 340 ms is not contradicted; one predicting 300 ms is hard to reconcile with this sample.
answer: The 95% interval is about $[322.2, 361.8]$ ms.

@mistakes
- Saying "there is a 95% probability that the true mean lies in this interval": the 95% refers to the long-run behavior of the method, not to this one interval.
- Using $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ language with $s$ but a normal critical value of 1.96 for a small sample: with $n = 10$, $t^* = 2.262$ and the interval would be too narrow.
- Mixing up the standard deviation (spread of individual trials) with the standard error (uncertainty of the mean); error bars should say which they show.
- Expecting overlapping 95% intervals of two group means to mean "no difference": the proper comparison is an interval for the difference (Week 3).

model:
```python
import math


def geo_ci(x, tcrit):
    """Confidence interval for the geometric mean, computed on log RTs."""
    y = [math.log(v) for v in x]
    n = len(y)
    m = sum(y) / n
    s = math.sqrt(sum((v - m) ** 2 for v in y) / (n - 1))
    half = tcrit * s / math.sqrt(n)
    return math.exp(m - half), math.exp(m + half)
```
hint: Work entirely with the logged values for the mean, the SD and the half-width, and apply `math.exp` only at the end.
solution: The interval is symmetric around the mean of the logs, so on the RT scale it is not symmetric around the geometric mean: it is longer on the slow side, matching the skew. The last test checks this: the geometric mean of the two limits is the geometric mean of the data, $\sqrt{100 \times 400} = 200$.

model:
```python
import itertools


def sampling_means(pop, n):
    """Sorted means of all ordered samples of size n with replacement."""
    return sorted(sum(s) / n for s in itertools.product(pop, repeat=n))
```
hint: `itertools.product(pop, repeat=n)` yields every ordered sample as a tuple; take each tuple's mean and sort.
solution: Enumerating all $N^n$ samples gives the exact sampling distribution. The tests confirm the two facts of the lesson on this tiny population: the mean of the sample means equals the population mean 3, and their standard deviation (dividing by the number of samples) equals $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ with $\sigma = \sqrt{8/3}$.

model:
```python
import math


def wald_ci(k, n, z):
    """Wald interval for a proportion, clipped to [0, 1]."""
    p = k / n
    half = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n)
    return max(0.0, p - half), min(1.0, p + half)
```
hint: Compute $\hat{p}$ and the half-width first, then clip each limit separately with `max` and `min`.
solution: The interval is $\hat{p}$ plus or minus $z$ standard errors, with standard error $\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}$. With 29 of 30 correct the unclipped upper limit is about 1.031, which is impossible for a probability, so it is cut to 1.0; this is a sign that the Wald interval is unreliable near the boundary.
