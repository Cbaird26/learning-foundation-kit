@article
Energy tells you about speeds at two instants; **momentum** tells you what happens when objects interact for a short time, as in collisions, explosions and recoil.

### Momentum and impulse

The **momentum** of an object is a vector in the direction of its velocity:

$$\vec p = m\vec v \qquad (\text{units: kg}\cdot\text{m/s}).$$

Newton's second law can be written as $\vec F_{net} = \Delta\vec p/\Delta t$. Multiplying by $\Delta t$ gives the **impulse-momentum theorem**:

$$\vec J = \vec F_{avg}\,\Delta t = \Delta \vec p = m\vec v_f - m\vec v_i.$$

**Impulse** $J$ has the same units as momentum ($\text{N}\cdot\text{s}$). The same change in momentum can come from a large force over a short time or a small force over a long time. That is why airbags, crumple zones and bending your knees on landing reduce injury: they lengthen $\Delta t$ and so lower the average force. On a force-time graph, the area under the curve is the impulse.

Be careful with signs. If a ball moving $+10\ \text{m/s}$ rebounds at $6\ \text{m/s}$, then $v_f = -6\ \text{m/s}$ and $\Delta v = -16\ \text{m/s}$, not $-4$.

### Conservation of momentum

If the net external force on a system is zero, its total momentum is constant:

$$m_1v_{1i} + m_2v_{2i} = m_1v_{1f} + m_2v_{2f}.$$

Internal forces come in third-law pairs, so they change the momenta of the parts by equal and opposite amounts and cancel in the total. This holds in every collision, whether or not energy is conserved, as long as external forces (friction, say) are negligible during the brief interaction.

### Types of collisions

- **Elastic:** kinetic energy is conserved as well as momentum (nearly true for hard steel balls).
- **Inelastic:** momentum is conserved, kinetic energy decreases, with the loss going to heat, sound and deformation.
- **Perfectly inelastic:** the objects stick together and move with a common velocity. The kinetic energy loss is as large as it can be, but it is not necessarily all the kinetic energy.

For a perfectly inelastic collision, $v_f = \dfrac{m_1v_{1i} + m_2v_{2i}}{m_1 + m_2}$.

### Explosions and recoil

Starting from rest, the total momentum is zero, so the pieces move in opposite directions with $m_1v_1 = -m_2v_2$: the lighter piece moves faster. A gun recoils, and a person throwing a ball from a frictionless surface slides backward, for the same reason.

### Method

Define a positive direction, list each object's mass and signed velocity before and after, write total momentum before equal to total after, and then check kinetic energy to classify the collision.

@example
title: Force on a bat
problem: A $0.15\ \text{kg}$ ball arrives at the bat at $40\ \text{m/s}$ and leaves in the opposite direction at $50\ \text{m/s}$. The contact lasts $1.0\ \text{ms}$. Find the impulse and the average force on the ball.
step: Take the incoming direction as positive: $v_i = +40\ \text{m/s}$ and $v_f = -50\ \text{m/s}$.
step: Change in momentum: $\Delta p = 0.15(-50) - 0.15(40) = -7.5 - 6.0 = -13.5\ \text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$.
step: The impulse has magnitude $13.5\ \text{N}\cdot\text{s}$, directed opposite to the incoming motion.
step: $F_{avg} = J/\Delta t = 13.5/0.0010 = 13\,500\ \text{N}$, about 9000 times the ball's weight of $1.5\ \text{N}$. Dividing by milliseconds, not seconds, was the key conversion.

@mistakes
- Dropping the sign of velocity, so a rebound is treated like a stop.
- Assuming kinetic energy is conserved in every collision; only momentum is (when external forces are negligible).
- Using conservation of momentum for a system with a large external force acting during the interaction.
- Forgetting to convert milliseconds to seconds in the impulse-momentum theorem.

@practice num
prompt: A $0.06\ \text{kg}$ tennis ball travels at $30\ \text{m/s}$. What is the magnitude of its momentum in $\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$?
answer: 1.8
check: 0.06 * 30
tol: 0.01
hint: Momentum is mass times velocity.
hint: Multiply $0.06 \times 30$.
solution: $p = mv = (0.06)(30) = 1.8\ \text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$.
unit: kg*m/s

@practice num
prompt: A $0.5\ \text{kg}$ ball strikes a wall at $10\ \text{m/s}$ and bounces straight back at $6\ \text{m/s}$. The contact lasts $0.02\ \text{s}$. What is the magnitude of the average force of the wall on the ball, in newtons?
answer: 400
check: 0.5 * (10 + 6) / 0.02
tol: 0.5
hint: The velocity reverses, so the change in velocity is the sum of the two speeds.
hint: $\Delta p = 0.5(10 + 6) = 8\ \text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$, then $F = \Delta p/\Delta t$.
solution: $\Delta p = 0.5(6) - 0.5(-10) = 8\ \text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$ in magnitude. $F_{avg} = 8/0.02 = 400\ \text{N}$.
unit: N

@practice num
prompt: A $2\ \text{kg}$ cart moving at $6\ \text{m/s}$ collides with a stationary $4\ \text{kg}$ cart, and the two carts stick together. What is their common speed after the collision, in m/s?
answer: 2
check: 2 * 6 / (2 + 4)
hint: Momentum is conserved in a collision with negligible external force.
hint: $2(6) + 4(0) = (2 + 4)v$.
solution: $12 = 6v$, so $v = 2\ \text{m/s}$.
unit: m/s

@practice mc
prompt: Two ice skaters collide on frictionless ice and bounce apart, and their total kinetic energy afterward is less than before. Which statement is correct?
choice: Momentum was not conserved, because kinetic energy decreased.
choice*: Momentum was conserved, and the collision was inelastic.
choice: Both momentum and kinetic energy were conserved.
choice: The collision was perfectly inelastic, because the skaters moved apart.
hint: With no net external force, momentum is conserved in every collision.
solution: Momentum is conserved whenever external forces are negligible. A decrease in kinetic energy makes the collision inelastic. It would be perfectly inelastic only if the skaters stuck together.

@practice num
prompt: A $60\ \text{kg}$ skater stands at rest on frictionless ice and throws a $3\ \text{kg}$ ball horizontally at $8\ \text{m/s}$. What is the skater's recoil speed, in m/s?
answer: 0.4
check: 3 * 8 / 60
tol: 0.01
hint: The total momentum is zero before the throw, so it is zero afterward.
hint: $60\,v_s = 3(8)$ in magnitude.
solution: $0 = 3(8) + 60 v_s$, so $|v_s| = 24/60 = 0.4\ \text{m/s}$, in the direction opposite the ball.
unit: m/s

@quiz num
prompt: A $1.5\ \text{kg}$ cart moving right at $4\ \text{m/s}$ collides head-on with a $0.5\ \text{kg}$ cart moving left at $2\ \text{m/s}$. They stick together. What is their velocity after the collision in m/s, taking right as positive?
answer: 2.5
check: (1.5 * 4 + 0.5 * (-2)) / (1.5 + 0.5)
tol: 0.01
hint: Give the left-moving cart a negative velocity.
hint: $1.5(4) + 0.5(-2) = (1.5 + 0.5)v$.
solution: $6 - 1 = 2v$, so $v = 2.5\ \text{m/s}$ to the right.
unit: m/s

@quiz num
prompt: A $3\ \text{kg}$ block sliding at $5\ \text{m/s}$ on a frictionless surface hits a stationary $2\ \text{kg}$ block and the two stick together. How much kinetic energy is converted to other forms in the collision, in joules?
answer: 15
check: 0.5 * 3 * 5**2 - 0.5 * (3 + 2) * (3 * 5 / (3 + 2))**2
tol: 0.01
hint: First find the common speed from conservation of momentum.
hint: $v = 15/5 = 3\ \text{m/s}$. Compare kinetic energy before and after.
solution: $v = 3(5)/5 = 3\ \text{m/s}$. $KE_i = \tfrac12(3)(25) = 37.5\ \text{J}$ and $KE_f = \tfrac12(5)(9) = 22.5\ \text{J}$, so $15\ \text{J}$ is lost.
unit: J
