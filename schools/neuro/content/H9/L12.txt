@article
Atoms are mostly empty space: a tiny, dense **nucleus** of protons and neutrons surrounded by electrons. The nucleus is about $10^{-15}\ \text{m}$ across, about $10^5$ times smaller than the atom.

### Nuclear notation

A nuclide is written ${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$, where $Z$ is the **atomic number** (protons), $A$ is the **mass number** (protons plus neutrons) and the number of neutrons is $N = A - Z$. **Isotopes** have the same $Z$ and different $A$, such as carbon-12 and carbon-14. Protons and neutrons are bound against electric repulsion by the **strong nuclear force**, which is short-ranged.

### Radioactive decay

Unstable nuclei decay spontaneously. In every decay, total $A$ and total $Z$ (charge) are conserved.

- **Alpha ($\alpha$) decay:** emits a helium nucleus ${}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$. The nucleus loses $4$ in $A$ and $2$ in $Z$.
- **Beta-minus ($\beta^-$) decay:** a neutron becomes a proton, emitting an electron and an antineutrino. $Z$ increases by $1$; $A$ is unchanged.
- **Beta-plus ($\beta^+$) decay:** a proton becomes a neutron, emitting a positron and a neutrino. $Z$ decreases by $1$.
- **Gamma ($\gamma$) decay:** a nucleus drops to a lower energy state emitting a high-energy photon; $A$ and $Z$ do not change.

Penetration differs: alpha particles are stopped by paper or skin, beta particles by a few millimeters of aluminum, and gamma rays need thick lead or concrete. Alpha emitters are most hazardous when taken into the body; gamma sources are hazardous from outside.

### Half-life

Decay is random for each nucleus, but a large sample decays predictably. The **half-life** $T_{1/2}$ is the time for half the radioactive nuclei to decay. After a time $t$, the number of nuclei $N$, and equally the **activity** $A$ (decays per second, in becquerels, Bq), is

$$N = N_0\left(\tfrac12\right)^{t/T_{1/2}}, \qquad A = A_0\left(\tfrac12\right)^{t/T_{1/2}}.$$

After $n$ half-lives, the fraction left is $(1/2)^n$: $1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16, \dots$ Count half-lives first, then halve that many times. After $10$ half-lives only about $0.1\%$ remains. The half-life depends on the nuclide, not on the amount, temperature or chemical form. Radioactive dating and medical imaging use known half-lives.

### Energy scales

A photon of frequency $f$ or wavelength $\lambda$ has energy

$$E = hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda}, \qquad h = 6.63\times 10^{-34}\ \text{J}\cdot\text{s}.$$

Atomic transitions emit photons in the eV range ($1\ \text{eV} = 1.6\times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$); nuclear transitions release MeV, a million times more. Einstein's relation $E = mc^2$ links mass and energy. The mass of a nucleus is slightly less than the sum of its separated nucleons; this **mass defect** $\Delta m$ corresponds to the **binding energy** $E_b = \Delta m\,c^2$, and $1\ \text{u}$ of mass is equivalent to $931.5\ \text{MeV}$.

### Method

For decay equations, balance $A$ at the top and $Z$ at the bottom. For half-life, divide elapsed time by $T_{1/2}$ before doing anything else.

@example
title: Fraction remaining after several half-lives
problem: A sample of carbon-14 (half-life $5730$ years) has $25\%$ of its original carbon-14 activity. How old is it, and what would the activity be after a third half-life if the original activity was $400\ \text{Bq}$?
step: Quarter remaining means $(1/2)^n = 1/4$, so $n = 2$ half-lives.
step: Age $= 2 \times 5730 = 11\,460$ years.
step: After a third half-life the fraction is $(1/2)^3 = 1/8$.
step: Activity $= 400/8 = 50\ \text{Bq}$. Check: $400 \to 200 \to 100 \to 50$, three halvings.

@mistakes
- Thinking that after two half-lives everything has decayed; half remains after one, a quarter after two.
- Forgetting to divide the elapsed time by the half-life to get the number of half-lives.
- Changing $A$ in beta decay; only $Z$ changes.
- Believing that heating, cooling or chemical changes alter a nuclide's half-life.

@practice num
prompt: An iron-56 nucleus has atomic number $26$. How many neutrons does it contain?
answer: 30
check: 56 - 26
hint: Mass number is protons plus neutrons.
hint: $N = A - Z$.
solution: $N = 56 - 26 = 30$ neutrons.

@practice num
prompt: Radium-226 (atomic number $88$) undergoes alpha decay. What is the mass number of the daughter nucleus?
answer: 222
check: 226 - 4
hint: An alpha particle is a helium-4 nucleus.
hint: The mass number decreases by $4$.
solution: ${}^{226}_{88}\text{Ra} \to {}^{222}_{86}\text{Rn} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$, so the daughter has $A = 222$.

@practice num
prompt: A radioactive sample has an activity of $80\ \text{mCi}$ and a half-life of $6\ \text{h}$. What is its activity after $24\ \text{h}$, in mCi?
answer: 5
check: 80 * 0.5**(24 / 6)
tol: 0.01
hint: First find how many half-lives have passed.
hint: $24/6 = 4$ half-lives, so the activity is halved four times.
solution: $80 \to 40 \to 20 \to 10 \to 5\ \text{mCi}$, that is $80/2^4 = 5\ \text{mCi}$.
unit: mCi

@practice mc
prompt: Which type of radiation is the most penetrating and requires thick lead or concrete for effective shielding?
choice: Alpha particles
choice: Beta particles
choice*: Gamma rays
choice: All three are stopped by a sheet of paper.
hint: Compare charge and mass: heavily charged particles lose energy quickly in matter.
solution: Gamma rays are uncharged high-energy photons and penetrate deeply. Alpha particles are stopped by paper or skin and beta particles by a few millimeters of aluminum.

@practice num
prompt: Green light has a wavelength of $500\ \text{nm}$. Using $h = 6.63\times 10^{-34}\ \text{J}\cdot\text{s}$ and $c = 3.00\times 10^8\ \text{m/s}$, what is the energy of one photon in joules?
answer: 3.98e-19
check: 6.63e-34 * 3.00e8 / 500e-9
tol: 1%
hint: Use $E = hc/\lambda$ and convert nanometers to meters.
hint: $\lambda = 5.00\times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}$.
solution: $E = (6.63\times 10^{-34})(3.00\times 10^8)/(5.00\times 10^{-7}) \approx 3.98\times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$ (about $2.5\ \text{eV}$).
unit: J

@quiz num
prompt: A sample of a radioactive isotope with a half-life of $3\ \text{days}$ has an initial activity of $640\ \text{Bq}$. What is its activity after $15\ \text{days}$, in becquerels?
answer: 20
check: 640 * 0.5**(15 / 3)
tol: 0.01
hint: Find the number of half-lives first.
hint: $15/3 = 5$, so halve $640$ five times.
solution: $640 \to 320 \to 160 \to 80 \to 40 \to 20$, so $A = 640/2^5 = 20\ \text{Bq}$.
unit: Bq

@quiz num
prompt: The mass defect of a helium-4 nucleus is $0.0304\ \text{u}$. Using $1\ \text{u} = 931.5\ \text{MeV}/c^2$, what is its binding energy in MeV, to the nearest $0.1\ \text{MeV}$?
answer: 28.3
check: 0.0304 * 931.5
tol: 1%
hint: Binding energy is the energy equivalent of the mass defect.
hint: Multiply the mass defect in u by $931.5\ \text{MeV/u}$.
solution: $E_b = (0.0304)(931.5) \approx 28.3\ \text{MeV}$.
unit: MeV
