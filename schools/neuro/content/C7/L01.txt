@article
Genetics starts with a simple observation: traits are carried by discrete units, **genes**, that come in versions called **alleles**. A person has two copies of each autosomal gene, one from each parent. The pair of alleles is the **genotype**; the observable result is the **phenotype**. A person with two identical alleles is **homozygous**; with two different alleles, **heterozygous**.

### Mendel's two laws

The **law of segregation**: the two alleles of a gene separate during gamete formation, so each gamete carries exactly one. An $Aa$ parent makes $A$ and $a$ gametes in equal numbers. The **law of independent assortment**: alleles of genes on different chromosomes (or far apart on one chromosome) sort into gametes independently. Because of independence, probabilities multiply. For an $AaBb \times AaBb$ cross, the chance of an offspring showing both dominant traits is $\frac{3}{4}\times\frac{3}{4}=\frac{9}{16}$, which is the familiar 9:3:3:1 ratio.

Two further rules make pedigree arithmetic easy. The **product rule** says that independent events multiply. The **sum rule** says that mutually exclusive ways of reaching an outcome add. If two carriers of a recessive allele ($Aa\times Aa$) have three children, the chance that exactly one is affected is $3\times\frac{1}{4}\times\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^2$, where the factor $3$ counts which child is affected.

### Reading a pedigree

In a pedigree, squares are males, circles are females, filled symbols are affected, and a horizontal line joins partners. Work through the patterns:

| Pattern | Typical clues |
|---|---|
| Autosomal dominant | Every affected person has an affected parent; vertical transmission; males and females equal; an affected heterozygote passes it to about half of children |
| Autosomal recessive | Affected children of unaffected parents; skipped generations; males and females equal; consanguinity raises the chance |
| X-linked recessive | Mostly affected males; no male-to-male transmission; carrier mothers pass it to about half of sons; affected fathers give it to all daughters (who become carriers) |
| X-linked dominant | Affected fathers have all daughters affected and no affected sons; affected mothers pass it to half of all children |
| Mitochondrial | Passed by the mother to all children; affected fathers pass it to none |

**Penetrance** is the fraction of people with a disease genotype who show the phenotype. **Expressivity** is how severe the phenotype is in those who show it. Incomplete penetrance can make a dominant disease appear to skip a generation.

### Conditional probability for carriers

Suppose a woman is unaffected, but her sibling has an autosomal recessive disease. Her parents must both be carriers. Among her four equally likely genotype outcomes ($AA$, $Aa$, $aA$, $aa$), the $aa$ outcome is excluded because she is unaffected, leaving three, of which two are carriers. Her carrier probability is $\frac{2}{3}$, not $\frac{1}{2}$. This is the most tested trap in pedigree questions.

To find a child's risk, multiply the chance that each parent is a carrier by $\frac{1}{4}$ (for two carriers).

### Sex-linked genes

Males are hemizygous for X-linked genes, so one recessive allele is enough to cause disease. A carrier mother ($X^A X^a$) and unaffected father give each son a $\frac{1}{2}$ chance of being affected and each daughter a $\frac{1}{2}$ chance of being a carrier.

@example
title: Risk for a sister of an affected patient
problem: A woman is unaffected, but her brother has an autosomal recessive disease. Her partner is a known carrier. What is the probability that their first child is affected?
step: Her brother is affected, so both of her parents are heterozygous carriers.
step: She is unaffected, so the $aa$ outcome is excluded. Of the three remaining outcomes, two are carriers: $P(\text{carrier})=\frac{2}{3}$.
step: Her partner is a carrier with probability $1$. A child is affected only if both parents transmit $a$: $\frac{1}{2}\times\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{4}$ given both are carriers.
step: Multiply: $\frac{2}{3}\times 1\times\frac{1}{4}=\frac{1}{6}$.

@example
title: Exactly one affected child in a family of three
problem: Two carriers of a recessive allele plan three children. What is the probability that exactly one child is affected?
step: Each child is affected with probability $\frac{1}{4}$ and unaffected with probability $\frac{3}{4}$, independently.
step: One particular order, such as affected-unaffected-unaffected, has probability $\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{3}{4}\cdot\frac{3}{4}=\frac{9}{64}$.
step: There are $3$ orders (the affected child can be first, second or third). Add: $3\times\frac{9}{64}=\frac{27}{64}$.

@mistakes
- Using $\frac{1}{2}$ instead of $\frac{2}{3}$ as the carrier risk of an unaffected sibling of a patient with a recessive disease.
- Calling a pedigree with unaffected parents and affected children "dominant"; dominant disease needs an affected parent unless penetrance is incomplete or the mutation is new.
- Forgetting that a father passes his X chromosome to all daughters and none to sons.
- Adding probabilities of independent events that should be multiplied.

@practice self
prompt: A pedigree shows a rare disease in several males across three generations, passing through unaffected women. Explain which features you would look for to decide between X-linked recessive and autosomal recessive inheritance, and how each feature supports one pattern.
solution: Look for male-to-male transmission: it is impossible in X-linked inheritance because fathers give sons their Y chromosome, so any affected father with an affected son argues for an autosomal pattern. Look at the sex ratio: X-linked recessive disease affects mostly males, while autosomal recessive disease affects both sexes equally. Look at the route: in X-linked recessive disease an affected man's daughters are obligate carriers and the disease passes through unaffected carrier women to roughly half their sons. Consanguinity favors autosomal recessive inheritance. Finally, rare X-linked disease in a woman is unusual and needs an affected father and carrier mother.
