@article
A **mutation** is a heritable change in DNA sequence. Mutations arise from replication errors, spontaneous chemical change, and damage from radiation or chemicals. Most are harmless or neutral; a few change protein function.

### Types of mutation

Point mutations substitute one base. A **transition** swaps purine for purine or pyrimidine for pyrimidine ($A\leftrightarrow G$, $C\leftrightarrow T$); a **transversion** swaps a purine for a pyrimidine. Their effect on the protein depends on where they fall.

| Class | Effect |
|---|---|
| Silent | Codon changes but the amino acid does not |
| Missense | A different amino acid (for example the HbS mutation, Glu6Val in $\beta$-globin) |
| Nonsense | A codon changes to a stop codon, truncating the protein |
| Frameshift | An insertion or deletion whose length is not a multiple of $3$ shifts the reading frame downstream |
| In-frame indel | A multiple of $3$ nucleotides is gained or lost, adding or removing amino acids |
| Splice-site | Alters a splice signal (see the next topic) |

**Trinucleotide repeat expansions** (Huntington disease, fragile X syndrome) grow across generations, a phenomenon called **anticipation**. Mutations can be **germline** (heritable) or **somatic** (confined to the individual's cells).

### Sources of damage

Spontaneous **deamination** converts cytosine to uracil, and 5-methylcytosine to thymine, making CpG sites mutation hotspots. **Depurination** leaves abasic sites. Oxidation produces 8-oxoguanine. Ultraviolet light fuses adjacent pyrimidines into **pyrimidine dimers**. Ionizing radiation causes double-strand breaks. Replication errors leave mismatches and small loops.

### Repair pathways

| Damage | Pathway | Key idea |
|---|---|---|
| Deaminated, oxidized or alkylated single base | **Base excision repair (BER)** | A glycosylase removes the base, an endonuclease cuts, polymerase fills in |
| Bulky lesions, pyrimidine dimers | **Nucleotide excision repair (NER)** | A stretch of about $25$ to $30$ nucleotides is excised and replaced |
| Replication mismatches and small loops | **Mismatch repair (MMR)** | MutS-like proteins (MSH2, MSH6) recognize the error; MLH1 and PMS2 coordinate excision of the new strand |
| Double-strand break | **Homologous recombination (HR)** | Error-free; uses the sister chromatid as template; needs BRCA1 and BRCA2 |
| Double-strand break | **Non-homologous end joining (NHEJ)** | Error-prone; joins ends, often with small deletions |

### Repair defects and cancer

- **Xeroderma pigmentosum**: autosomal recessive defect in NER (or in translesion polymerase eta). Patients develop skin cancers and sunburn severely in childhood.
- **Lynch syndrome** (hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer): autosomal dominant mutation in an MMR gene (*MLH1*, *MSH2*, *MSH6*, *PMS2*). Tumors show **microsatellite instability**. Besides colorectal cancer, there is increased risk of endometrial cancer.
- **Hereditary breast and ovarian cancer**: autosomal dominant *BRCA1* or *BRCA2* mutations disable homologous recombination. Cells rely on error-prone repair, and tumors are sensitive to PARP inhibitors (synthetic lethality).
- **Ataxia telangiectasia** (*ATM*) and **Li-Fraumeni syndrome** (*TP53*) involve damage-response defects.

Knudson's **two-hit hypothesis** explains why dominant cancer syndromes are recessive at the cell level: a person inherits one defective allele in every cell, and a single somatic mutation of the second allele in any cell removes the remaining function. This is why an inherited mutation yields earlier, multifocal, often bilateral tumors.

@example
title: Classifying a mutation
problem: The codon $\text{CAG}$ (Gln) in a coding region is changed to $\text{TAG}$. Another mutation deletes one nucleotide in a different exon. Classify both.
step: $\text{CAG}\to\text{TAG}$ changes mRNA codon $\text{CAG}$ to $\text{UAG}$, a stop codon. The result is a nonsense mutation that truncates the protein.
step: A one-nucleotide deletion changes the reading frame because $1$ is not a multiple of $3$, so it is a frameshift. Downstream codons are read incorrectly and a premature stop often appears.

@example
title: Linking a repair defect to cancer risk
problem: A 38-year-old woman has colon cancer; her tumor shows loss of MSH2 staining and microsatellite instability. Explain how the defect raises cancer risk.
step: MSH2 is a mismatch recognition protein, so the tumor cells cannot repair replication mismatches and small loops in repetitive DNA.
step: Errors accumulate in microsatellites and in the coding regions of growth control genes, producing the hypermutable phenotype and microsatellite instability.
step: Germline MMR mutations are dominant at the organism level: one inherited defective allele, plus a somatic loss of the other allele (second hit), disables repair in a colonic cell. The result is Lynch syndrome with early-onset colorectal and endometrial cancer.

@mistakes
- Equating transitions with transversions; transitions are purine-to-purine or pyrimidine-to-pyrimidine.
- Saying every deletion causes a frameshift; deletions of $3$ or a multiple of $3$ nucleotides keep the frame.
- Assigning pyrimidine dimers to mismatch repair; they are fixed by nucleotide excision repair.
- Describing BRCA-related cancer as autosomal recessive at the level of the family; it is dominant, though the tumor has lost both copies.

@practice self
prompt: A 34-year-old woman has a germline BRCA1 mutation. Explain, in terms of DNA repair and Knudson's two-hit hypothesis, why she has a high lifetime risk of breast and ovarian cancer and why her tumors are sensitive to PARP inhibitors.
solution: BRCA1 is needed for homologous recombination, the error-free repair of double-strand breaks. She inherits one mutant allele in every cell, so every cell is one hit down. A somatic mutation or loss of the remaining normal allele in a breast or ovarian cell is the second hit and abolishes homologous recombination. Such a cell must repair breaks by error-prone pathways such as NHEJ, causing genomic instability and accumulation of cancer-promoting mutations. Because the tumor cells depend on other repair routes, inhibiting PARP (which supports single-strand break repair) leaves unrepaired lesions that become double-strand breaks that the tumor cannot repair; normal cells with one working BRCA1 allele survive. This is synthetic lethality.
rubric: States that BRCA1 supports homologous recombination.
rubric: Explains the inherited first hit and a somatic second hit.
rubric: Explains PARP inhibitor sensitivity as synthetic lethality.
hint: Name the repair pathway BRCA1 supports, then count how many functional alleles are left in a tumor cell.

@quiz num
prompt: A segment of coding DNA has a one-nucleotide insertion followed, 7 nucleotides downstream, by a one-nucleotide deletion. What is the net change in length of the segment, in nucleotides?
answer: 0
check: 1 - 1
hint: Add the length gained and subtract the length lost.
solution: The net change is $+1-1=0$ nucleotides, so beyond the second change the original frame is restored. Codons between the two changes are misread.
