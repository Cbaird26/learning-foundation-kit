@article
Most common diseases (type 2 diabetes, coronary disease, schizophrenia, height) do not follow Mendelian ratios. They are **complex** (**multifactorial**) traits: many variants of small effect plus environmental factors.

### Heritability

**Heritability** is the proportion of variation in a trait **in a population** that is due to genetic differences. It is not the fraction of one person's trait that is "genetic," and it is not fixed: it changes with the environment and the population. **Broad-sense heritability** is $H^2=\frac{V_G}{V_P}$, where $V_G$ is genetic variance and $V_P$ is total phenotypic variance. **Narrow-sense heritability** counts only additive genetic variance.

Twin studies estimate it: identical (monozygotic, MZ) twins share essentially all variants; fraternal (dizygotic, DZ) twins share about half of segregating variants. A classic estimate is **Falconer's formula**

$$H^2\approx 2\,(r_{MZ}-r_{DZ}),$$

where $r$ is the correlation of the trait between twins. A trait with MZ concordance well below $100\%$ (for example type 1 diabetes at about $40$ to $50\%$) must have a major non-genetic contribution. Adoption studies help to separate genes from shared environment.

The **recurrence risk** to relatives of an affected person is expressed by $\lambda_R=\frac{\text{risk to relatives}}{\text{population risk}}$.

### Genome-wide association studies

A **GWAS** compares frequencies of hundreds of thousands to millions of SNPs between cases and controls, using linkage disequilibrium so that typed SNPs tag nearby causal variants. Because millions of tests are done, significance requires $p<5\times10^{-8}$ (a Bonferroni-type correction). Each associated variant usually has a small **odds ratio** (often $1.05$ to $1.3$), and most lie in noncoding regulatory regions. Hits point to loci, not necessarily genes, and need functional follow-up. Confounding by **population stratification** (ancestry differences between cases and controls) can create false associations.

### Polygenic risk scores

A **polygenic risk score (PRS)** adds up the effect sizes from GWAS across many variants:

$$\text{PRS}=\sum_i \beta_i\,x_i,$$

where $x_i\in\{0,1,2\}$ is the number of risk alleles at variant $i$ and $\beta_i$ the log odds ratio. A PRS gives a relative risk ranking, not a diagnosis. Compared with a **single-gene (monogenic) test**:

| Feature | Single-gene test | Polygenic risk score |
|---|---|---|
| Variants | One, rare, high effect (e.g. *BRCA1*) | Thousands to millions, common, small effects |
| Effect | Large; may justify surgery or surveillance | Modest; shifts risk percentile |
| Output | Yes or no (carrier or not) | A continuous score relative to a population |
| Transferability | Fairly portable across ancestries | Weaker in groups poorly represented in GWAS |
| Use | Diagnosis, family testing | Risk stratification, e.g. for coronary disease or breast cancer screening |

### Cancer genetics

Cancer is a genetic disease of somatic cells: accumulated mutations drive clonal expansion.

- **Proto-oncogenes** become **oncogenes** by gain-of-function mutation, amplification or translocation. Only one allele needs to be affected (dominant at the cell level). Examples: *RAS* point mutations, *MYC* translocation in Burkitt lymphoma, *BCR-ABL1* in chronic myeloid leukemia (treated with the tyrosine kinase inhibitor imatinib), *HER2* amplification.
- **Tumor suppressor genes** restrain growth; loss of function in **both** alleles is required (Knudson's two-hit hypothesis). Examples: *TP53*, *RB1*, *APC*, *BRCA1*/*BRCA2*, *PTEN*. Inherited syndromes (Li-Fraumeni, hereditary retinoblastoma, familial adenomatous polyposis) give one germline hit.
- **DNA repair genes** (previous topics) raise the mutation rate.

Cancer arises by multiple steps. In colorectal cancer, loss of *APC* is followed by *KRAS* activation, then *TP53* loss. Tumors evolve, and drugs select for resistant subclones.

A monogenic test (such as *BRCA1/2* sequencing) and a PRS can be combined: a carrier of a high-risk variant can still have a risk modified by her polygenic background.

@example
title: Heritability from twins
problem: The correlation of a quantitative trait is 0.80 in MZ twins and 0.50 in DZ twins. Estimate the heritability with Falconer's formula, and say what the rest represents.
step: Apply $H^2\approx2(r_{MZ}-r_{DZ})=2(0.80-0.50)=0.60$.
step: About $60\%$ of the variance in this population is attributed to genetic differences. The rest, about $40\%$, reflects environment and measurement error (and interactions not captured by the formula).

@example
title: Comparing a PRS with a single-gene test
problem: A 50-year-old woman has no family history. A PRS places her in the top 5% for breast cancer, but a *BRCA1/2* test is negative. A second woman is a *BRCA1* carrier. How should the two results be interpreted?
step: The *BRCA1* carrier has a single variant of high effect: lifetime risk of about $55$ to $72\%$ for breast cancer (about $45$ to $69\%$ for *BRCA2*; estimates vary by study), which justifies enhanced surveillance and risk-reducing options.
step: The first woman's score places her in a higher relative-risk group, but the effect is modest compared with a pathogenic variant; the absolute risk depends on age and other factors. She may be offered earlier or more intensive screening, not surgery.
step: Neither test replaces clinical assessment; the PRS is a risk estimate, and its accuracy depends on how well her ancestry is represented in the GWAS used to build it.

@mistakes
- Interpreting a heritability of $0.6$ as meaning that $60\%$ of one person's trait is caused by genes.
- Using the usual $p<0.05$ threshold for a GWAS instead of $5\times10^{-8}$.
- Treating a PRS as a diagnosis, or assuming it works equally well in all ancestries.
- Assuming that oncogene mutations are recessive; usually a single activating allele suffices.

@practice self
prompt: A 45-year-old woman asks whether she should have a polygenic risk score for breast cancer or *BRCA1/2* sequencing. Compare what each test can tell her, and explain how you would counsel her about the limits of each.
solution: BRCA1/2 sequencing detects rare pathogenic variants of large effect; a positive result implies a high lifetime risk of breast and ovarian cancer, supports risk-reducing options, and enables cascade testing of relatives. A negative result in a family without a known variant does not exclude other causes. A PRS sums many common variants of small effect into a relative risk percentile. It shifts risk modestly, is continuous rather than yes/no, and is less accurate in people whose ancestry is underrepresented in the data used to build it. Counseling should explain that a high PRS is not a diagnosis, absolute risk depends on age and other factors such as family history, and screening decisions follow clinical guidelines. Both tests need informed consent about possible findings.
rubric: Describes a monogenic result as high-effect and actionable, including cascade testing.
rubric: Describes a PRS as a modest, continuous relative risk with ancestry limits.
rubric: Counsels that neither replaces clinical risk assessment.
hint: Compare the size of effect and the number of variants for each test.
