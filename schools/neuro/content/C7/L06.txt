@article
**Translation** converts the nucleotide sequence of an mRNA into the amino acid sequence of a polypeptide. Ribosomes read the message in non-overlapping groups of three nucleotides, called **codons**. There are $4^3=64$ codons: $61$ specify amino acids and $3$ are stop codons ($\text{UAA}$, $\text{UAG}$, $\text{UGA}$). The start codon is $\text{AUG}$, which codes for methionine (formylmethionine in bacteria).

### Properties of the code

The code is **nearly universal** across life, **non-overlapping**, and **degenerate** (redundant): most amino acids have more than one codon, with variation usually in the third base ("wobble"). It is **unambiguous**: each codon specifies only one amino acid. Because of degeneracy, many third-position changes are silent.

Reading frames matter. A sequence has three possible frames, and only one is the **open reading frame (ORF)**, which begins at AUG and continues without a stop codon.

### The machinery

- **tRNA** carries an amino acid at its $3'$ CCA end and contains an **anticodon** that pairs antiparallel with the codon.
- **Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases** attach the correct amino acid to its tRNA using ATP. They are the true "interpreters" of the code and have proofreading activity.
- The **ribosome** has a small subunit (decoding; $30$S in bacteria, $40$S in eukaryotes) and a large subunit (peptidyl transferase, a ribozyme; $50$S and $60$S). The whole ribosomes are $70$S and $80$S. The ribosome has **A** (aminoacyl), **P** (peptidyl) and **E** (exit) sites.

### The steps

1. **Initiation**: the small subunit binds the mRNA (via the Shine-Dalgarno sequence in bacteria, the $5'$ cap in eukaryotes), the initiator tRNA pairs with AUG, and the large subunit joins.
2. **Elongation**: a charged tRNA enters the A site (helped by EF-Tu, which uses GTP); the ribosome forms a peptide bond; translocation (EF-G) moves the ribosome by one codon.
3. **Termination**: a stop codon in the A site is recognized by release factors, the polypeptide is released, and the ribosome dissociates.

Each peptide bond costs about $4$ high-energy phosphate bonds (two ATP equivalents to charge the tRNA, plus GTP for delivery and for translocation).

### Antibiotics

Because bacterial ribosomes differ from eukaryotic ones, many antibiotics are selectively toxic. Aminoglycosides (such as gentamicin) and tetracyclines act on the $30$S subunit; macrolides (such as azithromycin) and clindamycin act on the $50$S subunit; chloramphenicol inhibits peptidyl transferase.

### Mutations and the reading frame

- **Missense**: one amino acid is replaced.
- **Nonsense**: a sense codon becomes a stop, truncating the protein. Because premature stop codons more than about $50$ to $55$ nucleotides upstream of the last exon-exon junction trigger **nonsense-mediated decay (NMD)**, the mRNA is often destroyed and little truncated protein is made.
- **Frameshift**: insertion or deletion of a number of nucleotides not divisible by $3$.
- **Start-loss**: a mutation in AUG prevents initiation at the normal site.

Duchenne muscular dystrophy illustrates the importance of the frame: deletions that disrupt the reading frame of the dystrophin gene cause severe disease, while in-frame deletions leave a partly functional protein and cause the milder Becker form.

@example
title: Translating an mRNA
problem: Translate the mRNA $5'\text{-AUGGCUUAUUGAC-}3'$ beginning at the first AUG, using AUG = Met, GCU = Ala, UAU = Tyr, UGA = stop.
step: Find the start codon: the message begins with AUG, so group into triplets: AUG | GCU | UAU | UGA | C.
step: Translate each codon: Met, Ala, Tyr, then UGA is a stop codon.
step: The peptide is Met-Ala-Tyr, three residues long. The trailing C is never translated.

@example
title: Nonsense mutation and NMD
problem: A patient has a nonsense mutation in an early exon of a gene with 12 exons. Predict the consequence for the protein.
step: A premature termination codon far upstream of the last exon-exon junction triggers nonsense-mediated decay.
step: Most of the mRNA is degraded, so little or no truncated protein is made. The effect is closer to a null allele (loss of function), unless the nonsense mutation is in the last exon, where NMD does not occur and a truncated protein may be produced.

@mistakes
- Reading the mRNA in the wrong direction; codons are read $5'\to3'$ and the anticodon pairs antiparallel.
- Thinking that every point mutation changes the amino acid; silent changes are common because of degeneracy.
- Treating all deletions as frameshifts rather than checking whether the length is a multiple of $3$.
- Confusing the bacterial $30$S/$50$S subunits with the eukaryotic $40$S/$60$S subunits.

@practice self
prompt: A boy has Duchenne muscular dystrophy caused by a deletion in the dystrophin gene that removes a number of coding nucleotides that is not a multiple of 3. A second patient has a deletion whose length is a multiple of 3 and a mild Becker phenotype. Explain the difference and how a nonsense mutation would compare.
solution: The dystrophin gene is large, and the effect of a deletion depends on the reading frame. A deletion whose length is not divisible by 3 shifts the frame, so downstream codons are misread and a premature stop codon is reached; the truncated mRNA is usually degraded by nonsense-mediated decay and the protein is absent, causing severe Duchenne disease. A deletion that is a multiple of 3 preserves the frame, so a shortened but partly functional dystrophin with intact ends is made, causing the milder Becker phenotype. A nonsense mutation introduces a premature stop codon directly and, like the frameshift, usually triggers NMD and loss of protein, so it behaves like the Duchenne case.
rubric: Links the frame-disrupting deletion to a premature stop and loss of protein.
rubric: Explains that an in-frame deletion yields a shortened functional protein.
rubric: States that a nonsense mutation also tends to cause loss of function through NMD.
hint: Check whether each deletion length divides evenly by three.
