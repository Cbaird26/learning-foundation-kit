@article
Mendel's law of independent assortment holds for genes on different chromosomes. Genes on the same chromosome travel together, so they are **linked**. Linkage is not absolute, because during prophase I of meiosis, homologous chromosomes undergo **crossing over**, exchanging segments. A gamete whose allele combination differs from both of the parent's chromosomes is **recombinant**.

### Parental and recombinant classes

Consider an individual heterozygous at two linked genes, with alleles arranged $AB/ab$ (the **cis** or coupling arrangement: both dominant alleles on one chromosome). Without crossing over, only $AB$ and $ab$ gametes form; these are **parental** types. A crossover between the genes produces $Ab$ and $aB$ gametes, the **recombinant** types.

A **testcross** (crossing the heterozygote to a homozygous recessive $ab/ab$) reveals the gametes directly, because each offspring's phenotype reflects the heterozygote's gamete. The **recombination frequency** is

$$RF=\frac{\text{number of recombinant offspring}}{\text{total offspring}}.$$

Parental classes are always the most common. If $RF=0.5$, the genes assort independently: either they are on different chromosomes or so far apart that at least one crossover almost always occurs. RF can never exceed $0.5$ in a simple cross, because a double crossover or more restores parental arrangements about half the time.

### Map units

One **map unit**, or **centimorgan (cM)**, equals $1\%$ recombination. So $RF=0.12$ means $12$ cM. Map distances are approximately additive over short intervals, but over long ones double crossovers hide recombination and RF underestimates the true distance.

### Three-point crosses

Crossing a triple heterozygote with a triple recessive gives eight phenotypic classes. The two **most frequent** classes are parental; the two **least frequent** are **double crossovers**. Comparing a double crossover with a parental class shows which gene has switched: that is the **middle gene**. Then, for each interval, count all recombinants (single crossovers in that interval plus double crossovers, which recombine both intervals).

**Interference** measures how one crossover discourages another nearby. Compute the **coefficient of coincidence**

$$\text{CoC}=\frac{\text{observed double crossovers}}{\text{expected double crossovers}},\qquad \text{expected}=RF_1\times RF_2\times N,$$

and $I=1-\text{CoC}$. Positive interference ($I>0$) means fewer double crossovers than expected.

### LOD scores in human families

Human families are small and matings cannot be designed, so geneticists compare two hypotheses: linkage at recombination fraction $\theta$ versus no linkage ($\theta=0.5$). The **LOD score** is

$$\text{LOD}=\log_{10}\frac{\theta^{R}(1-\theta)^{NR}}{(0.5)^{R+NR}},$$

where $R$ and $NR$ are the numbers of recombinant and non-recombinant meioses. A LOD of $3$ or more (odds of $1000:1$) is the conventional threshold for declaring linkage in a single-gene study; a LOD of $-2$ or less excludes linkage at that $\theta$. Linkage analysis in families localized genes such as the cystic fibrosis gene long before sequencing.

@example
title: Ordering genes in a three-point cross
problem: A cross of a triple heterozygote with a triple recessive $abc/abc$ gives 1000 progeny. The parental classes are $+++$ (380) and $abc$ (370). Single crossovers: $a++$ (60) and $+bc$ (70); $++c$ (55) and $ab+$ (45). Double crossovers: $a+c$ (10) and $+b+$ (10). Find the gene order and the map distances.
step: The two largest classes, $+++$ and $abc$, are parental. The two smallest, $a+c$ and $+b+$, are double crossovers.
step: Compare $a+c$ with the parental arrangement $abc$: only $b$ has changed. So $b$ is the middle gene, and the order is $a$–$b$–$c$.
step: The interval $a$–$b$ is recombined in the $a++$, $+bc$ and both double-crossover classes: $\frac{60+70+10+10}{1000}=0.15$, so $15$ cM.
step: The interval $b$–$c$ is recombined in $++c$, $ab+$ and both double crossovers: $\frac{55+45+10+10}{1000}=0.12$, so $12$ cM. The total $a$–$c$ distance is $27$ cM.

@example
title: Map distance from a testcross
problem: A testcross of $AB/ab$ gives 450 $AB$, 440 $ab$, 55 $Ab$ and 55 $aB$ offspring. How far apart are the genes?
step: Parental classes are the common ones, $AB$ and $ab$. Recombinants are $Ab$ and $aB$.
step: Total offspring: $450+440+55+55=1000$. Recombinants: $110$.
step: $RF=\frac{110}{1000}=0.11$, which is $11$ cM.

@mistakes
- Counting only the single crossovers in an interval and forgetting to add the double crossovers, which also recombine that interval.
- Choosing the middle gene by looking at the parental classes; it is revealed by comparing the double crossovers with the parental classes.
- Reading an RF of $0.5$ as proof the genes are on different chromosomes; it only shows that they assort independently.
- Computing a LOD with natural logarithms instead of base $10$.

@practice self
prompt: Explain why the recombination frequency between two genes on the same chromosome cannot exceed 50% even when the genes are very far apart.
solution: Between distant genes, many meioses have one, two or more crossovers. Each crossover involves only two of the four chromatids. For an odd number of exchanges between the genes the chromatid is recombinant, and for an even number it returns to the parental arrangement. Averaged over many meioses, recombinant and parental chromatids become equally frequent, so half of gametes are recombinant. Thus RF plateaus at 0.5, the same as for independent assortment, and distances over long intervals are best obtained by summing short intervals.
rubric: States that a crossover involves two of the four chromatids.
rubric: Explains that multiple crossovers can restore the parental arrangement.
rubric: Concludes that RF approaches the 0.5 of independent assortment.
hint: Consider what happens when two crossovers occur between the genes on the same chromatids.
