@article
**Genomics** studies whole genomes. The haploid human genome has about $3.1$ billion base pairs and roughly $20{,}000$ protein-coding genes; coding exons make up only about $1$ to $2\%$ of the sequence. Much of the rest is regulatory DNA, introns, repeats (transposable elements make up about half) and noncoding RNA genes.

### Types of variation

- **Single-nucleotide variants (SNVs)**: the most common; a **SNP** is a variant common in the population (allele frequency of at least $1\%$). Two unrelated people differ at about $1$ in every $1000$ bases.
- **Small insertions and deletions (indels)**.
- **Copy-number variants (CNVs)** and larger **structural variants** (inversions, translocations).
- **Short tandem repeats** and trinucleotide repeat expansions.

### Sequencing strategies

| Approach | Covers | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Targeted panel | Chosen genes | Known disease genes, such as a cardiomyopathy panel |
| Exome | All coding exons (about $1$ to $2\%$ of the genome) | Undiagnosed genetic disease; holds about $85\%$ of known disease-causing variants (a widely quoted estimate; diagnostic yield is much lower) |
| Genome | Everything, including noncoding regions and structural variants | Broad diagnosis, research |

A key measure is **coverage** (read depth): the average number of reads covering a base. Calling a heterozygous variant reliably needs enough depth, typically $30\times$ or more for genomes. **Variant calling** compares reads aligned to a **reference genome**.

### Variant interpretation

The American College of Medical Genetics classifies variants as **pathogenic, likely pathogenic, variant of uncertain significance (VUS), likely benign** or **benign**, using evidence such as population frequency (a variant too common in healthy people cannot cause a rare dominant disease), segregation with disease in a family, computational predictions, functional studies and *de novo* occurrence. A VUS should not change clinical management.

### Sequence alignment

Aligning two sequences reveals homology. In a **pairwise alignment**, matching residues are paired and gaps represent insertions or deletions. **Percent identity** is the number of identical aligned positions divided by the alignment length. A simple score uses $+m$ for each match, $-s$ for each mismatch and $-g$ for each gap position; the alignment with the highest score is best. **Global alignment** (Needleman-Wunsch) aligns the whole length; **local alignment** (Smith-Waterman) finds the best-matching subsequence.

**BLAST** searches a database for similar sequences. Its output gives for each hit a bit score, the percent identity and an **E-value**: the number of hits with at least that score expected by chance in a database of that size. A smaller E-value is more significant; values below about $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-10}$ usually indicate homology. **Homologs** share ancestry: **orthologs** arise by speciation and often keep the same function, while **paralogs** arise by gene duplication. Identity percentages describe similarity, but "homologous" is yes or no.

### Conservation

Residues that are conserved across species are likely to matter for function, so a missense variant at a highly conserved position is more likely pathogenic than one at a position that varies among species.

@example
title: Percent identity and alignment score
problem: Align DNA sequences 5'-ATGCTAGC-3' and 5'-ATGCCAGC-3' (no gaps). Find the number of matches, percent identity, and the score with match $+2$ and mismatch $-3$.
step: Compare position by position: A-A, T-T, G-G, C-C, T-C (mismatch), A-A, G-G, C-C. That gives $7$ matches and $1$ mismatch over $8$ positions.
step: Percent identity $=\frac{7}{8}\times100=87.5\%$.
step: Score $=7(+2)+1(-3)=14-3=11$.

@example
title: Interpreting a variant
problem: An exome in a child with developmental delay finds a heterozygous missense variant in a gene associated with a dominant disorder. It is absent from population databases, is *de novo* (parents are negative), and occurs at a position conserved in all vertebrates. How would you classify it?
step: Absence from population databases supports pathogenicity for a rare dominant disease.
step: *De novo* occurrence with a matching phenotype is strong evidence, and conservation supports functional importance.
step: These data together favor classification as likely pathogenic (or pathogenic if functional studies agree). Confirmation of identity of the parents is needed.

@mistakes
- Treating an E-value like a probability that two genes are related; it is the expected number of chance hits.
- Reporting percent identity without stating the alignment length.
- Acting clinically on a variant of uncertain significance.
- Assuming that exome sequencing detects all variation, including deep intronic changes and many structural variants.

@practice self
prompt: Compare exome and whole-genome sequencing for a child with unexplained developmental delay. State one advantage of each and one limitation of each.
solution: Exome sequencing targets the coding exons, about 1 to 2 percent of the genome, so it is cheaper and gives deeper coverage per exon, and most known pathogenic variants lie in coding regions or splice sites. Its limitations are that it misses deep intronic and regulatory variants, repeat expansions and many structural variants and copy-number changes, and its coverage is uneven. Whole-genome sequencing reads coding and noncoding DNA with more uniform coverage and detects structural variants, copy-number variants and some repeat expansions, but it costs more, produces more data and many variants of uncertain significance in noncoding regions that are hard to interpret.
rubric: States an advantage and a limitation of exome sequencing.
rubric: States an advantage and a limitation of genome sequencing.
rubric: Mentions that noncoding or structural variants are better detected by genome sequencing.
hint: Think about which parts of the genome each method reads and the cost of interpretation.
