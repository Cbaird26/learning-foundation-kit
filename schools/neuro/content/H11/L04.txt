@article
A **summary** reports what one source says. A **comparison** notes how two sources agree or differ. A **synthesis** goes further: it builds a new claim from several sources, using each to support, complicate or qualify a point that is yours. Imagine you are hosting a conversation among authors who have never met. You decide the topic, introduce each voice, show where they agree or disagree, and say what you conclude.

### Organize by idea, not by source

The most common weak synthesis is a "source parade": a paragraph on Source A, then one on Source B, then one on Source C. A real synthesis is organized around ideas. Suppose three sources discuss school start times:

- Source 1 reports that later start times were followed by more sleep.
- Source 2 finds attendance improved in one district.
- Source 3 warns that bus schedules and after-school activities become harder.

A synthesis paragraph about benefits would combine Sources 1 and 2 (sleep and attendance), and a separate one about costs would use Source 3 and perhaps qualify Sources 1 and 2.

### Moves of a synthesis paragraph

1. **Topic sentence with your claim.** It states a point, not a source's name.
2. **Evidence from two or more sources** woven together, each clearly attributed.
3. **Relationship words** that show how sources connect: *similarly, in contrast, however, building on this, although, together these suggest*.
4. **Your analysis** that says what the sources show jointly and what they leave open.

### Attribution and integration

Use **signal phrases** to introduce material: "According to Rivera," "Chen and Patel report," "In a survey of 400 students, one team found." Match the verb to the source's strength: *demonstrates* and *proves* are strong; *suggests* and *proposes* are weaker. Do not upgrade a tentative claim. If a study found an association, do not write that it showed a cause.

Prefer paraphrase and short quotation. A paraphrase restates the idea in your own words and sentence structure and still needs a citation. Quote only when the exact wording matters. Either way, give credit, because ideas belong to their authors even when the words are yours.

### Handling disagreement

Sources will disagree. A good synthesis does not hide that. It may explain the disagreement (different populations, different years, different definitions), weigh the evidence, or conclude that the question remains open. The synthesis is yours: sources support it, but they do not speak for you.

### A model

"Early research links later start times to better sleep and attendance, but benefits depend on how districts manage the logistics. Rivera reports that students in one district slept longer after start times moved, and Chen and Patel find higher attendance. However, Okafor cautions that bus costs and activity schedules can erase these gains if planning is poor. Together, these sources suggest that the schedule change helps most where districts also adjust transportation."

(The names and findings in this model are invented for practice.)

@example
title: Turning three summaries into one synthesis claim
problem: Invented sources on community gardens. Source 1: gardens increased vegetable intake among participants. Source 2: gardens strengthened neighbor relationships. Source 3: gardens often closed within five years when volunteers left. Write a synthesis topic sentence.
step: Find a shared idea. Sources 1 and 2 describe benefits, and Source 3 describes a limit on those benefits.
step: Decide your claim. The benefits are real but depend on sustaining volunteer participation.
step: Write the topic sentence with relationship words. "Although community gardens appear to improve both diet and neighborhood ties, those benefits depend on keeping volunteers engaged."
step: Plan the paragraph by idea: first the benefits (Sources 1 and 2), then the limit (Source 3), then your conclusion about what makes gardens last.

@mistakes
- Writing a source parade: one source per paragraph with no connecting claim.
- Letting the sources do all the talking, with no analysis of your own.
- Strengthening a source's claim (turning "is associated with" into "causes").
- Paraphrasing too closely, changing only a few words, or omitting the citation.

@practice self
prompt: Invented sources on school lunches. Source 1: free lunches raised participation by 18 percent in one district. Source 2: students in the program reported less hunger in the afternoon. Source 3: some schools struggled to afford the program after the first year. Write a six-to-eight sentence synthesis paragraph with an original claim, organized by idea, using signal phrases and relationship words.
hint: Decide your claim first: the lunch benefits (Sources 1 and 2) are real, but Source 3 limits them. Make that your topic sentence.
hint: Group Sources 1 and 2 under benefits and Source 3 under the limit, link them with words like similarly and however, and end with your own conclusion.
solution: Free school lunch programs appear to help students, but their success depends on stable funding. According to Source 1, one district saw a participation increase of 18 percent after meals became free. Similarly, Source 2 reports that students felt less hungry in the afternoon. These findings suggest that removing cost barriers improves both use and daily comfort. However, Source 3 notes that some schools struggled to afford the program after the first year. Together the sources indicate that the benefits are real but fragile. A district that wants the gains should plan its funding before it advertises the program.
rubric: Opens with an original claim, not a source's name.
rubric: Organizes by idea and groups sources accordingly.
rubric: Uses signal phrases and relationship words.
rubric: Includes the writer's own analysis and does not overstate a source.
