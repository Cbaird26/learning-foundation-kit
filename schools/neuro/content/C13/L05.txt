@article
Every sense except smell must pass through a small egg-shaped structure before it reaches the cortex. A lesion the size of a grape in that structure can leave a person numb on one side of the body, and later with pain that no touch should cause. Just below it sits a region weighing about 4 g, a few thousandths of the brain's mass, that sets your body clock, your temperature, your thirst and much of your hormonal state. These are the **thalamus** and the **hypothalamus**, the two parts of the diencephalon you will meet in nearly every pathway.

### The thalamus

The thalamus lies on either side of the third ventricle, above the hypothalamus, medial to the internal capsule. A Y-shaped sheet of white matter, the **internal medullary lamina**, divides it into anterior, medial and lateral groups. Relay nuclei receive a defined input and project, through the internal capsule, to a specific cortical area; the cortex sends back even more fibers than it receives.

| nucleus | main input | cortical target | role |
|---|---|---|---|
| ventral posterolateral (VPL) | medial lemniscus and spinothalamic tract (body) | primary somatosensory cortex (S1) | body touch, proprioception, pain |
| ventral posteromedial (VPM) | trigeminal pathways (face), taste | S1 face area, gustatory cortex | face sensation, taste |
| lateral geniculate (LGN) | retina | primary visual cortex | vision |
| medial geniculate (MGN) | inferior colliculus | primary auditory cortex | hearing |
| ventral lateral and ventral anterior | cerebellum and basal ganglia | motor and premotor cortex | motor relay |
| anterior | mammillary bodies | cingulate gyrus | limbic circuit |
| mediodorsal | amygdala, prefrontal cortex | prefrontal cortex | cognition, emotion |
| pulvinar | superior colliculus, cortex | parietal, temporal and occipital association cortex | attention, higher-order visual processing |
| intralaminar | reticular formation | widespread cortex, striatum | arousal |

The **thalamic reticular nucleus** is a shell of GABAergic neurons around the thalamus that inhibits other thalamic nuclei and does not project to the cortex. A **relay** is not a passive wire: thalamic neurons gate and shape traffic, with firing patterns that change between waking and sleep. Olfaction is the exception, reaching cortex without a thalamic relay.

Damage to VPL and VPM from a stroke in the thalamogeniculate branches of the posterior cerebral artery causes contralateral loss of sensation and, in some patients, later severe pain (**Dejerine–Roussy syndrome**). Infarcts in the paramedian thalamus, supplied through a shared artery in some people, can disturb alertness, vertical gaze and memory.

### The hypothalamus

The **hypothalamus** forms the floor and lower walls of the third ventricle, between the optic chiasm and the mammillary bodies. Rostral to caudal it has preoptic, supraoptic (anterior), tuberal and mammillary regions; medial to lateral it has periventricular, medial and lateral zones.

| region or nucleus | role |
|---|---|
| suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) | circadian clock, entrained by the retinohypothalamic tract |
| supraoptic and paraventricular (magnocellular) | make vasopressin and oxytocin, released from the posterior pituitary |
| paraventricular (parvocellular) | releasing hormones (CRH, TRH), autonomic control |
| arcuate nucleus | feeding circuits, growth-hormone releasing hormone, dopamine |
| ventromedial nucleus | satiety-related circuits |
| lateral hypothalamus | arousal and feeding, including orexin (hypocretin) neurons |
| preoptic area | heat loss, sleep onset, GnRH |
| posterior hypothalamus | heat conservation, wakefulness |
| mammillary bodies | memory circuit |

The hypothalamus controls the pituitary in two ways. The **posterior pituitary** (neurohypophysis) is a neural extension: axons from supraoptic and paraventricular neurons end there and release **vasopressin** (antidiuretic hormone) and **oxytocin** into the blood. The **anterior pituitary** is a gland controlled by releasing hormones carried through the **hypophyseal portal veins**: CRH leads to ACTH and cortisol, TRH to TSH and thyroid hormones, GnRH to LH and FSH, GHRH to growth hormone; dopamine inhibits prolactin. Loss of vasopressin causes **central diabetes insipidus** (large volumes of dilute urine).

The SCN has an intrinsic period near 24 h; carefully controlled studies of people in constant dim light found an average of about 24.2 h (individuals vary). Light resets it each day through a retinal pathway that includes melanopsin-containing ganglion cells.

### Evidence and limits

Classic lesion studies in rats linked the ventromedial hypothalamus to overeating and the lateral hypothalamus to reduced eating, but lesions also destroy fibers passing through and alter hormones such as insulin. Later studies that stimulate or silence specific cell types support, refine and sometimes revise the picture, so a nucleus is better described by its circuits than by a single label.

@example
title: A body clock that runs slow
problem: A person living in constant dim light has an intrinsic period of 24.3 h. After 10 days, how far has their sleep schedule drifted relative to a 24 h clock?
step: Each day the internal clock runs $24.3 - 24 = 0.3$ h longer than the 24 h day.
step: After 10 days the drift is $10 \times 0.3 = 3$ h.
step: Because the period is longer, sleep times occur later each day.
answer: Sleep times are 3 hours later than on day 0.

@example
title: Which nucleus?
problem: A stroke destroys the left VPL nucleus. Which part of the body is affected, and which sensations?
step: VPL receives the medial lemniscus and spinothalamic tracts, which have already crossed.
step: Left VPL therefore represents the right side of the body below the face, and its output goes to left S1.
answer: Loss or distortion of touch, proprioception, pain and temperature on the right side of the body (below the face).

@mistakes
- Treating the thalamus as one structure with one function: each nucleus has its own inputs, cortical targets and role.
- Forgetting that smell is the one sense that reaches cortex without a thalamic relay.
- Mixing up the two pituitary lobes: the posterior lobe is neural tissue releasing hormones made in the hypothalamus, while the anterior lobe is glandular and controlled by releasing hormones through the portal veins.
- Reading a lesion's effect as the function of the damaged nucleus: fibers of passage and hormones also contribute.

@practice self
prompt: Rats whose ventromedial hypothalamus is lesioned eat more and become obese. A classmate concludes that the ventromedial hypothalamus is the brain's satiety center. Explain what other explanations the lesion evidence leaves open, and what kind of additional experiments would test the claim.
hint: Think about what else a lesion destroys besides the cells of the nucleus.
solution: A lesion destroys cells and also fibers passing through the area, and it changes hormones; lesioned animals also show raised insulin and changes in metabolism, so overeating could result from altered metabolism or from damage to passing pathways rather than from loss of a satiety signal. Experiments that would test the claim include selectively lesioning or silencing the nucleus's own cell bodies without damaging fibers of passage, recording or imaging its neurons during meals to see whether they respond to satiety, and stimulating or silencing identified cell types, with controls for metabolic changes. Converging evidence from several methods is stronger than lesions alone.
