@article
A patient's right eye turns down and out, the right eyelid droops, and the left arm and leg are weak. Findings on opposite sides of the body, one from a cranial nerve and one from a long tract, point to one small lesion. To read such a pattern you need to know where each cranial nerve leaves the brainstem and which long tracts pass next to it.

### The brainstem

The brainstem has three parts from rostral to caudal: the **midbrain**, **pons** and **medulla**. The midbrain contains the **tectum** (the superior colliculi for visual orienting and the inferior colliculi for hearing), the **tegmentum** (red nucleus, periaqueductal gray, reticular formation, and the **substantia nigra**) and the **cerebral peduncles**, where the descending corticospinal fibers pass. The pons has a ventral base of pontine nuclei and crossing fibers and a dorsal tegmentum. The medulla contains the **pyramids** (corticospinal tracts) on its ventral side, the olives, and the nuclei that control swallowing, heart rate and breathing. Through all three runs the **reticular formation**, important for arousal, posture and autonomic control.

### Cranial nerves

Twelve pairs attach to the brain. A rough rule, by where their nuclei lie: I and II belong to the forebrain, the midbrain has III and IV, the pons has V, VI and VII, the pontomedullary junction has VIII, and the medulla has IX to XII. (VI, VII and VIII leave the brainstem at the pontomedullary junction.) Motor nuclei lie medial to sensory nuclei, as in the cord, because the sulcus limitans runs along the floor of the fourth ventricle.

| nerve | attached at | main functions |
|---|---|---|
| I olfactory | olfactory bulb | smell; reaches cortex without a thalamic relay first |
| II optic | diencephalon | vision (a CNS tract) |
| III oculomotor | midbrain | four eye muscles and eyelid elevation; parasympathetic pupil constriction and accommodation |
| IV trochlear | midbrain (exits dorsally and crosses) | superior oblique |
| V trigeminal | pons | face sensation; muscles of mastication |
| VI abducens | pontomedullary junction | lateral rectus |
| VII facial | pontomedullary junction | facial expression, taste from the anterior two thirds of the tongue, tears and salivation, stapedius |
| VIII vestibulocochlear | pontomedullary junction | hearing and balance |
| IX glossopharyngeal | medulla | taste from the posterior third of the tongue, pharyngeal sensation, carotid sinus, parotid gland |
| X vagus | medulla | palate, pharynx and larynx muscles; parasympathetic to thorax and abdomen |
| XI accessory | cervical cord (spinal root) | sternocleidomastoid and trapezius |
| XII hypoglossal | medulla | tongue muscles |

Eye movements follow "LR6 SO4": the lateral rectus is supplied by VI, the superior oblique by IV, and all other extraocular muscles by III. The parasympathetic cranial nerves are III, VII, IX and X.

### Reflexes you can test

**Pupillary light reflex:** light on the retina excites ganglion cells whose axons travel in the optic nerve (II) to the **pretectal nucleus**, which projects to the **Edinger–Westphal nuclei** on both sides; their preganglionic fibers run in the oculomotor nerve (III) to the ciliary ganglion, whose neurons constrict the pupil. Because both sides are driven, light in one eye constricts both pupils (the direct and consensual responses). The **corneal reflex** has an afferent limb in V1 and an efferent limb in VII (blinking). The **gag reflex** has an afferent limb in IX and an efferent limb in X.

### Localizing with crossed signs

Tracts that cross below the lesion (the corticospinal tract crosses in the lower medulla; the spinothalamic tract crosses in the cord) give contralateral body signs, while cranial nerve nuclei and fibers affect the same side. **Weber's syndrome** (ventral midbrain, a lesion of the cerebral peduncle and oculomotor fibers) causes an ipsilateral oculomotor palsy and contralateral hemiparesis. **Lateral medullary (Wallenberg) syndrome**, usually from occlusion of the vertebral artery or its posterior inferior cerebellar branch, causes ipsilateral facial loss of pain and temperature (spinal trigeminal tract), contralateral loss of pain and temperature in the body (spinothalamic tract), ipsilateral Horner syndrome, hoarseness and difficulty swallowing (nucleus ambiguus), vertigo and ipsilateral limb ataxia. The corticospinal tract is spared, so there is no hemiparesis.

### Evidence and limits

These patterns come from clinicopathological correlation and imaging. Individual patients vary because vessels and lesions rarely respect nuclear boundaries.

@example
title: The right optic nerve is cut
problem: Light is shone into the right eye of a patient whose right optic nerve has been completely cut. What do the pupils do, and what happens when light is shone into the left eye?
step: With the right optic nerve cut, no signal leaves the right retina, so light in the right eye produces no pupillary response in either eye.
step: Light in the left eye is carried by the intact left optic nerve to the pretectal nuclei, which drive both Edinger–Westphal nuclei.
step: The right oculomotor nerve is intact, so the right pupil constricts (consensual), as does the left.
answer: Light in the right eye: no constriction in either pupil. Light in the left eye: both pupils constrict.

@example
title: Weber or Wallenberg?
problem: A patient has right-sided ptosis with the eye turned down and out, and left-sided weakness of arm and leg. Which syndrome is this, and where is the lesion?
step: The ipsilateral signs (ptosis, eye down and out) are an oculomotor nerve palsy, a midbrain level finding.
step: The contralateral weakness is corticospinal, which has not yet crossed at the midbrain.
step: Together they point to the ventral right midbrain: Weber's syndrome.
answer: Weber's syndrome from a ventral lesion in the right midbrain.

@mistakes
- Thinking that every brainstem lesion causes signs on the opposite side: cranial nerve findings are ipsilateral, and only the crossed long tracts give contralateral signs.
- Forgetting that the light reflex is bilateral: the pretectal nucleus drives both Edinger–Westphal nuclei, so there is a consensual response.
- Assigning the facial nerve to the medulla: VII attaches at the pontomedullary junction, and its motor nucleus lies in the pons.
- Confusing the afferent and efferent limbs: the corneal reflex is V1 in and VII out, and the gag reflex is IX in and X out.

@practice self
prompt: Describe how you would test cranial nerves II, III, IV and VI at the bedside, saying what you ask the patient to do and what each result tells you. State one limitation of the bedside examination.
hint: Match each nerve to a simple action: seeing, light response, eye movement.
solution: For II, test visual acuity and visual fields by confrontation, and look at the optic disc. For the pupillary reflex, shine a light into each eye: afferent II, efferent III; if light in one eye constricts the other pupil but not its own, the oculomotor (efferent) pathway to the unresponsive pupil is damaged. For III, IV and VI, ask the patient to follow a target through the H pattern: failure to abduct indicates VI; failure to look down when the eye is adducted indicates IV; ptosis, a dilated pupil and an eye down and out indicate III. A limitation is that bedside tests only detect substantial deficits and cannot separate nerve, nucleus and muscle disease, so imaging and further testing are often needed.

@quiz mc
prompt: When asked to stick out the tongue, a patient's tongue deviates to the right. The left arm and leg are weak, and the face is spared. Where is the lesion?
choice*: The right medial medulla, involving the pyramid and the hypoglossal fibers.
choice: The left medial medulla.
choice: The right lateral medulla (Wallenberg syndrome).
choice: The right ventral midbrain (Weber's syndrome).
hint: The tongue deviates toward the weak side of the tongue, and the corticospinal tract has not yet crossed in the medulla.
solution: The hypoglossal nerve supplies the ipsilateral tongue muscles, so the tongue deviates toward the lesion (right). The right pyramid carries corticospinal fibers that cross just below, so the left limbs are weak. The face is spared because the corticospinal fibers to the facial nucleus left the tract higher up. A lateral medullary lesion spares the pyramid and the hypoglossal nucleus, and a midbrain lesion would cause an oculomotor palsy.
