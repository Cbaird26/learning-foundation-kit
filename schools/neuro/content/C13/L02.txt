@article
A driver is hit on the side of the head, walks away, and an hour later collapses. A scan shows a lens-shaped collection of blood pressing on the brain from outside the dura. To understand why this is dangerous, and where the blood sits, you need a common language for position and a map of the coverings, fluid spaces and vessels around the brain.

### Directions and planes

Anatomists use the long axis of the nervous system, the **neuraxis**, which bends at the midbrain in humans. **Rostral** means toward the nose and **caudal** toward the tail. Along the spinal cord and brainstem, **dorsal** is toward the back and **ventral** toward the belly. In the forebrain, because of the bend, dorsal is **superior** (toward the top of the head) and ventral is **inferior**. **Medial** is toward the midline and **lateral** away from it; **ipsilateral** means the same side and **contralateral** the opposite side. Three section planes are used: **sagittal** (divides left from right), **coronal** or frontal (divides front from back) and **axial** or horizontal (divides top from bottom).

### The meninges

Three membranes wrap the brain and spinal cord. From the skull inward they are the **dura mater** (thick and fibrous; its two layers separate to form the venous sinuses), the **arachnoid mater** (thin and web-like) and the **pia mater** (adherent to the brain surface). Between them lie spaces:

| space | location | contents | typical bleed |
|---|---|---|---|
| epidural | between skull and dura (a potential space in the skull; contains fat and veins in the spinal canal) | middle meningeal artery lies in it | epidural hematoma, lens-shaped, arterial |
| subdural | between dura and arachnoid (potential) | bridging veins cross it | subdural hematoma, crescent-shaped, venous |
| subarachnoid | between arachnoid and pia | cerebrospinal fluid, large arteries | subarachnoid hemorrhage, often from a ruptured aneurysm |

Dural folds partition the cranium: the **falx cerebri** lies between the hemispheres and the **tentorium cerebelli** between the occipital lobes and the cerebellum. A temporal bone fracture can tear the **middle meningeal artery**, producing the epidural hematoma in the opening case. Some patients have a brief lucid interval before deterioration; the finding is common but not universal.

### Ventricles and cerebrospinal fluid

The ventricular system is the lumen of the neural tube. It comprises two **lateral ventricles**, each with frontal horn, body, atrium, occipital horn and temporal horn; the **third ventricle** between the thalami; the **cerebral aqueduct** through the midbrain; and the **fourth ventricle** behind the pons and medulla. Fluid flows from each lateral ventricle through an **interventricular foramen** to the third ventricle, through the aqueduct to the fourth, and leaves through the median aperture and the two lateral apertures into the **subarachnoid space**. It is absorbed through **arachnoid granulations** into the superior sagittal sinus.

**Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)** is made mainly by the **choroid plexus**, at roughly 500 mL per day (about 0.35 mL per minute) in adults, from a total volume of about 150 mL. It cushions the brain, clears waste and carries solutes. **Hydrocephalus** is excess CSF volume with enlarged ventricles. Blocking the aqueduct (obstructive, or noncommunicating, hydrocephalus) enlarges the lateral and third ventricles but not the fourth; impaired absorption (communicating hydrocephalus) enlarges all four. In the adult, the spinal cord ends near vertebral level L1 to L2, so a **lumbar puncture** at L3 to L4 or L4 to L5 samples CSF from the lumbar cistern without touching the cord.

### Blood supply

The brain is about 2% of body weight but receives roughly 15% of cardiac output and uses about 20% of the body's oxygen at rest; typical blood flow is about 50 mL per 100 g per minute. Two systems supply it. The **internal carotid arteries** (anterior circulation) give the **anterior cerebral artery (ACA)** and **middle cerebral artery (MCA)**. The two **vertebral arteries** join at the pontomedullary junction as the **basilar artery**, which ends as the two **posterior cerebral arteries (PCA)** (posterior circulation). At the base of the brain these are joined into the **circle of Willis** by the **anterior communicating artery** (between the two ACAs) and the two **posterior communicating arteries** (ICA to PCA).

| artery | main territory |
|---|---|
| ACA | medial frontal and parietal cortex, including the leg area of motor and sensory cortex |
| MCA | lateral convexity: face and arm areas, language areas; deep branches (lenticulostriate) supply the internal capsule and basal ganglia |
| PCA | occipital lobe, inferior temporal lobe, much of the thalamus |
| vertebral, basilar and branches | medulla, pons, cerebellum, midbrain |

Brain endothelium forms the **blood–brain barrier** with tight junctions, supported by astrocyte end-feet; it limits entry of many substances. Veins drain into the dural sinuses and then the internal jugular veins.

### Limits

Territories vary between people, and the circle of Willis is complete in only a minority of people, so a given occlusion can have different effects depending on collateral flow.

@example
title: Following a drop of CSF
problem: CSF is made at 0.35 mL per minute and the total volume is 150 mL. About how many hours does it take to make one total volume, and about how many times per day is the volume turned over?
step: Time to make 150 mL: $150 / 0.35 \approx 428.6$ min.
step: Convert: $428.6 / 60 \approx 7.1$ hours.
step: Per day: $0.35 \times 1440 = 504$ mL, and $504 / 150 \approx 3.4$ turnovers.
answer: About 7.1 hours per volume, about 3.4 turnovers per day.

@example
title: Which artery?
problem: A patient has weakness of the opposite leg, more than the arm, and loss of initiative. Which artery is most likely occluded?
step: Leg representations of motor and sensory cortex lie on the medial surface of the hemisphere, in the paracentral region.
step: The medial frontal and parietal surfaces are supplied by the anterior cerebral artery, while the lateral convexity (face and arm) belongs to the MCA.
step: Medial frontal damage also explains reduced initiative (abulia).
answer: The anterior cerebral artery on the side opposite the weak leg.

@mistakes
- Using dorsal and superior as always the same: they match in the forebrain, but in the brainstem and cord, dorsal means posterior.
- Mixing epidural and subdural hematomas: epidural is arterial (middle meningeal artery) and lens-shaped; subdural is venous (bridging veins) and crescent-shaped.
- Thinking CSF is a stagnant bath: it is made continuously, flows in one direction, and is absorbed into venous blood.
- Believing the anterior and middle cerebral arteries come from the vertebral arteries: they come from the internal carotid.

@practice self
prompt: A child has a tumor that blocks the cerebral aqueduct. Predict which ventricles enlarge and which do not, explain why, and contrast this with hydrocephalus caused by failure of arachnoid granulations to absorb CSF.
hint: Ask where CSF is made and where it would back up if the aqueduct were blocked.
solution: CSF is still made by the choroid plexus in all ventricles, but fluid cannot pass the blocked aqueduct, so pressure builds upstream: the lateral and third ventricles enlarge while the fourth ventricle and the subarachnoid space stay normal in size. This is obstructive (noncommunicating) hydrocephalus. If absorption through the arachnoid granulations fails, fluid passes normally through the whole ventricular system into the subarachnoid space but cannot leave, so all four ventricles enlarge and the subarachnoid space may too (communicating hydrocephalus). Imaging showing which ventricles are enlarged therefore localizes the obstruction.
rubric: Predicts enlarged lateral and third ventricles with a normal fourth ventricle for aqueduct blockage.
rubric: Explains that production continues upstream of the block, raising pressure.
rubric: Contrasts communicating hydrocephalus, with all ventricles enlarged and no blockage within the system.
