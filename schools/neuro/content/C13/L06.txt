@article
Look at a human brain from the side and you see a folded sheet of tissue only 2 to 4 mm thick, covering the hemispheres. Unfolded, the cortex would cover roughly 2,000 to 2,500 cm². Some of the most striking findings in neurology come from small injuries to it: a person who can speak fluently but cannot understand, or who ignores the entire left side of space. How is the cortex organized so that location predicts function?

### Lobes and landmarks

Two deep grooves organize the lateral surface: the **central sulcus** separates the **frontal lobe** from the **parietal lobe**, and the **lateral (Sylvian) fissure** separates the **temporal lobe** from both. The **parieto-occipital sulcus** marks the parietal–occipital boundary on the medial surface, where the **occipital lobe** begins. The **insula** lies buried inside the lateral fissure. Medially, the cingulate gyrus and parahippocampal gyrus belong to the **limbic lobe**. Gyri are ridges; sulci are grooves; folding fits a large area into the skull.

### Areas, functions and what is lost

Brodmann (1909) divided the cortex into numbered areas by cell architecture; the numbers are still used as labels.

| area | location | function | damage causes |
|---|---|---|---|
| primary motor cortex (area 4) | precentral gyrus | voluntary movement of the contralateral body | contralateral weakness |
| premotor and supplementary motor (area 6) | anterior to area 4 | planning and sequencing of movement | apraxia, difficulty with sequences |
| prefrontal cortex (areas 9, 10, 46, 11, 47) | anterior frontal lobe | planning, working memory, social behavior | executive and personality changes |
| Broca's area (44, 45) | inferior frontal gyrus, dominant hemisphere | speech production | nonfluent aphasia |
| primary somatosensory cortex (3, 1, 2) | postcentral gyrus | touch, proprioception | contralateral sensory loss |
| parietal association | superior and inferior parietal lobules (including angular and supramarginal gyri) | spatial attention, sensorimotor integration | neglect (right), Gerstmann syndrome (left) |
| primary auditory cortex (41) | Heschl's gyrus, superior temporal plane | hearing | bilateral damage causes cortical deafness |
| Wernicke's area | posterior superior temporal gyrus (area 22) | language comprehension | fluent aphasia with poor comprehension |
| inferior temporal and fusiform | ventral temporal lobe | object and face recognition | visual agnosia, prosopagnosia |
| primary visual cortex (17) | calcarine sulcus, occipital lobe | vision | contralateral visual field loss |

Language is lateralized to the left hemisphere in about 95% of right-handers and about 70% of left-handers, so Broca's and Wernicke's areas refer to the dominant hemisphere. Penfield and Boldrey (1937) showed that stimulating the precentral and postcentral gyri evokes movement and sensation in an orderly map of the body, with the leg medial and the face lateral.

### Layers

Most cortex is **neocortex** with six layers:

| layer | name | main role |
|---|---|---|
| I | molecular | few cells; dendrites and axons |
| II | external granular | small neurons, cortico-cortical |
| III | external pyramidal | cortico-cortical, including callosal output |
| IV | internal granular | main thalamic input |
| V | internal pyramidal | output to striatum, brainstem and spinal cord |
| VI | multiform | output to thalamus |

Sensory areas such as V1 and S1 have a thick layer IV (**granular** cortex); primary motor cortex has a thin layer IV and a thick layer V with giant pyramidal cells (Betz cells), so it is **agranular**. **Allocortex**, such as the hippocampus, has fewer than six layers. The cortex holds roughly 16 billion of the brain's roughly 86 billion neurons.

### Fiber systems

**Association fibers** connect areas within one hemisphere, for example the arcuate fasciculus between temporal and frontal language regions. **Commissural fibers** cross the midline, chiefly in the **corpus callosum** (on the order of 200 million axons), which has a rostrum, genu, body and splenium. **Projection fibers** link the cortex with the thalamus, brainstem and spinal cord through the corona radiata and the internal capsule.

### Evidence and limits

Areas are defined by architecture, connections and function, and their boundaries and exact correspondence to behavior are approximate. Complex functions such as language and attention depend on networks, not single patches, and brain imaging adds correlates that lesion data must confirm.

@example
title: How much cortical volume?
problem: Take the cortex as an unfolded sheet of area 2,000 cm² and mean thickness 2.5 mm. What is its volume in cm³?
step: Convert thickness: 2.5 mm = 0.25 cm.
step: Volume = area × thickness = $2000 \times 0.25 = 500$ cm³.
step: This is an approximation; thickness varies from about 2 to 4 mm across the cortex.
answer: About 500 cm³.

@example
title: Which area is damaged?
problem: A patient understands speech poorly but speaks fluently with many wrong words, and has no weakness. Which cortical area is most likely damaged?
step: Fluent but nonsensical speech with poor comprehension is Wernicke aphasia.
step: This area is in the posterior superior temporal gyrus of the dominant hemisphere (usually left).
step: There is no weakness, so the precentral gyrus is spared.
answer: Wernicke's area in the dominant (usually left) posterior superior temporal gyrus.

@mistakes
- Treating Broca's and Wernicke's areas as fixed in the left hemisphere for everyone: language dominance is left in about 95% of right-handers and fewer left-handers.
- Reversing the layers' roles: layer IV receives thalamic input, while layers V and VI send output to subcortical targets and thalamus.
- Assuming granular cortex is the same everywhere: motor cortex has almost no layer IV.
- Taking a Brodmann number as a precise functional border: the numbers label architecture, and functions overlap.

@practice self
prompt: Explain, using cortical layers, why primary visual cortex and primary motor cortex look different under a microscope, and what each appearance suggests about the area's connections.
hint: Compare the layer that receives thalamic input with the layer that sends output.
solution: Primary visual cortex has a thick layer IV because it receives dense input from the thalamus (the lateral geniculate nucleus), so it appears granular. Primary motor cortex has a thin layer IV but a thick layer V with giant pyramidal cells, because its main task is output to the striatum, brainstem and spinal cord, so it appears agranular. The microscopic appearance therefore follows the dominant connection: input-heavy sensory cortex is granular, and output-heavy motor cortex is agranular. A limit is that layers vary gradually across cortex and the labels are descriptive.
