### Week by week

- **Week 1, development:** neural plate, neural folds, neural tube, neural crest; neuropores close about days 25 and 28; three primary and five secondary vesicles and their derivatives and ventricles; alar plate (sensory, dorsal) and basal plate (motor, ventral); inside-out cortical layering.
- **Week 2, coverings, CSF, blood:** rostral, caudal, dorsal, ventral; planes; dura, arachnoid, pia; epidural versus subdural bleeds; CSF flow from lateral ventricles to the subarachnoid space; ACA, MCA, PCA and the circle of Willis.
- **Week 3, spinal cord:** 31 segments, dorsal (sensory) and ventral (motor) roots, dermatomes; stretch reflex (monosynaptic Ia), reciprocal inhibition, flexion withdrawal; upper versus lower motor neuron signs.
- **Week 4, brainstem:** midbrain, pons, medulla; cranial nerves I to XII; pupillary light, corneal and gag reflexes; Weber and Wallenberg syndromes.
- **Week 5, diencephalon:** thalamic nuclei (VPL, VPM, LGN, MGN, VL/VA, anterior, MD, pulvinar); hypothalamic nuclei and the two pituitary links.
- **Week 6, cortex:** lobes, sulci, Brodmann areas, six layers, association, commissural and projection fibers.
- **Week 7, sensation:** dorsal column-medial lemniscus crosses in the medulla; spinothalamic crosses in the cord; trigeminal pathways; Brown-Séquard.
- **Week 8, movement:** corticospinal tract crosses at the pyramidal decussation; corticobulbar; central versus peripheral facial palsy.
- **Week 9, basal ganglia and cerebellum:** direct and indirect pathways, dopamine; Parkinson and Huntington diseases; cerebellar circuit and ipsilateral signs.
- **Week 10, limbic system:** Papez circuit, trisynaptic loop, H.M., amygdala nuclei.
- **Week 11, vision and hearing:** partial chiasm crossing, field defects; auditory pathway with bilateral projections; ITD.
- **Week 12, localization:** the method.

### Key formulas

- Reflex latency: $t = d_{\text{aff}}/v_{\text{aff}} + d_{\text{eff}}/v_{\text{eff}} + n\delta$.
- Conduction time: distance divided by velocity (convert seconds to ms).
- Interaural time difference: $d\sin\theta/c$.
- Net sign of a loop: the product of the signs of its links.

### Common errors

- Confusing the crossing levels of the dorsal column and spinothalamic pathways.
- Predicting ipsilateral weakness from a cortical or capsular lesion.
- Treating cerebellar signs as contralateral.
- Calling the amygdala the "fear center" and the thalamus a passive relay.
- Mixing the arteries: ACA is medial, MCA lateral, PCA occipital.

@exam num
prompt: A nociceptor in the foot excites an A-delta fiber (1.0 m to the cord at 20 m/s). Three central synapses each add 1.0 ms, and the motor axon (1.0 m at 50 m/s) delivers the command to a flexor muscle. What is the withdrawal reflex latency in ms, ignoring neuromuscular transmission?
answer: 73
unit: ms
check: 1.0 / 20 * 1000 + 3 * 1.0 + 1.0 / 50 * 1000
hint: Convert each conduction time to ms and add the synaptic delays.
solution: Afferent: $1.0/20 = 50$ ms. Central: $3 \times 1 = 3$ ms. Efferent: $1.0/50 = 20$ ms. Total: $50 + 3 + 20 = 73$ ms.

@exam self
prompt: A 66-year-old man has weakness of the right arm and leg with brisk reflexes and an extensor plantar response, loss of vibration and position sense in the right arm and leg with normal pinprick and temperature, normal face movement, and when he protrudes his tongue it deviates to the left. Localize the lesion, explain each finding from the pathways, and name the most likely artery.
hint: Think of the structures lying next to the midline in the medulla, and where each pathway crosses.
solution: The lesion is in the left medial medulla. The left pyramid carries corticospinal fibers that cross below, so damage gives right-sided upper motor neuron weakness with sparing of the face. The left medial lemniscus carries crossed dorsal column fibers, so vibration and position sense are lost on the right, while the spinothalamic tract lies laterally and is spared, so pinprick and temperature are normal. The left hypoglossal nerve or nucleus, a lower motor neuron structure, is damaged, so the left tongue is weak and deviates toward the lesion. The pattern, an ipsilateral cranial nerve sign with contralateral long-tract signs, fits the territory of the anterior spinal artery or vertebral artery branches. Evidence and limit: the pattern follows from the known positions of these tracts, but real lesions vary in size and often extend into neighboring regions.
rubric: Localizes to the left medial medulla and gives the crossing logic for the right-sided corticospinal and medial lemniscal signs.
rubric: Explains the sparing of pain and temperature by the lateral position of the spinothalamic tract.
rubric: Explains the left tongue deviation as a lower motor neuron (hypoglossal) sign toward the lesion and names a plausible artery.
