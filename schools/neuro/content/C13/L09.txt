@article
One patient moves too little: her hand trembles at rest, her steps are small, and starting to walk is a struggle. Another moves too much: his limbs flick and writhe in movements he did not intend. A third moves with good strength but misses: his finger overshoots the target and wobbles near it. All three have intact motor cortex and corticospinal tracts. The first two have damage in the **basal ganglia** and the third in the **cerebellum**, two large structures that shape movement without sending much of it directly to the spinal cord.

### The basal ganglia

The **striatum** is the **caudate nucleus** plus the **putamen**; the putamen and **globus pallidus** together are the lentiform nucleus. The globus pallidus has an **external** segment (GPe) and an **internal** segment (GPi). The **subthalamic nucleus** (STN) lies below the thalamus, and the **substantia nigra** has a dopaminergic **pars compacta** (SNc) and a GABAergic **pars reticulata** (SNr). The ventral striatum includes the nucleus accumbens.

Cortex sends excitatory (glutamatergic) input to the striatum, which is the main input stage. The main outputs are the **GPi and SNr**, whose GABAergic neurons tonically inhibit the **thalamus** (VA and VL), which excites the cortex: a loop from cortex through the basal ganglia and thalamus back to cortex.

| pathway | circuit | net effect on thalamus and cortex |
|---|---|---|
| direct | cortex (+) striatum (D1 neurons) (−) GPi/SNr (−) thalamus (+) cortex | disinhibition; facilitates movement |
| indirect | cortex (+) striatum (D2 neurons) (−) GPe (−) STN (+) GPi/SNr (−) thalamus (+) cortex | more inhibition; suppresses movement |
| hyperdirect | cortex (+) STN (+) GPi/SNr | fast brake |

Each link's sign multiplies: the direct pathway has two inhibitory links (a net positive effect on the thalamus), and the indirect pathway has three (a net negative). **Dopamine** from the SNc excites direct-pathway neurons through D1 receptors and inhibits indirect-pathway neurons through D2 receptors, so it promotes movement through both. The basal ganglia are also central to action selection and reinforcement learning, a theme of later courses.

### Disorders

**Parkinson disease** results from loss of SNc dopaminergic neurons, with Lewy body pathology; by the time of symptoms a large fraction of striatal dopamine has been lost. The features are resting tremor (about 4 to 6 Hz), rigidity, **bradykinesia** and postural instability. Dopamine-restoring drugs and deep brain stimulation of the STN or GPi relieve symptoms; these are treatments for clinicians to manage. **Huntington disease**, an autosomal dominant disorder caused by an expanded CAG repeat in the *HTT* gene (about 36 or more repeats; 40 or more with full penetrance), degenerates the striatum and causes **chorea**, cognitive decline and psychiatric symptoms. **Hemiballismus**, violent flinging of the contralateral limbs, classically follows a lesion (often a small stroke) of the subthalamic nucleus.

### The cerebellum

The cerebellum sits in the posterior fossa behind the pons and medulla, with the fourth ventricle between them. It has about 80% of the brain's neurons (about 69 billion of 86 billion), mostly tiny granule cells. By lobe it divides into anterior, posterior and flocculonodular; by function into three zones:

| zone | location | main role | output nucleus |
|---|---|---|---|
| vestibulocerebellum | flocculonodular lobe | balance, eye movements | vestibular nuclei |
| spinocerebellum | vermis and paravermis | posture, gait, limb movement online | fastigial and interposed |
| cerebrocerebellum | lateral hemispheres | planning, timing | dentate, then VL thalamus |

Three **peduncles** connect it to the brainstem: the **inferior** (input from the spinal cord, olive and vestibular system), the **middle** (input from the pontine nuclei, entirely crossed) and the **superior** (the main output toward the thalamus and red nucleus; its fibers cross in the midbrain).

The cortex has three layers (molecular, Purkinje, granular). **Mossy fibers** excite granule cells, whose **parallel fibers** excite **Purkinje cells**, which are GABAergic and are the sole output of the cortex, inhibiting the deep nuclei. Each Purkinje cell also receives one **climbing fiber** from the inferior olive. Marr (1969) and Albus (1971) proposed that climbing-fiber activity teaches Purkinje cells to adjust their parallel-fiber synapses, and Ito linked this to long-term depression at these synapses.

Cerebellar damage causes **ipsilateral** deficits because of a double crossing: the output crosses in the superior peduncle to the opposite thalamus and motor cortex, and the corticospinal tract crosses back to the side of the lesion. (The input from the cortex also crosses, in the pontine fibers, so each cerebellar hemisphere works with the opposite cerebral hemisphere.) The signs are **dysmetria**, intention tremor, dysdiadochokinesia, a wide-based ataxic gait (vermis lesions), nystagmus, scanning speech and hypotonia.

### Evidence and limits

The direct/indirect model explains many symptoms but is simplified: recordings show both pathways active during movement, and pallidotomy relieves parkinsonism without producing involuntary movements, which a pure firing-rate model would not predict. The learning theory of the cerebellum is supported by eye-blink conditioning and motor adaptation but not all of it is settled.

@example
title: The sign of the direct pathway
problem: Using +1 for excitatory and −1 for inhibitory links, find the net sign from cortex to cortex through the direct pathway.
step: Links: cortex to striatum (+1), striatum to GPi (−1), GPi to thalamus (−1), thalamus to cortex (+1).
step: Multiply: $(+1)(-1)(-1)(+1) = +1$.
answer: +1: the direct pathway facilitates movement.

@example
title: Why a right cerebellar lesion affects the right side
problem: A stroke damages the right cerebellar hemisphere. Which limbs are affected and why?
step: Cerebellar output leaves through the right superior cerebellar peduncle and crosses in the midbrain to the left red nucleus and left VL thalamus.
step: The left motor cortex then sends the corticospinal tract, which crosses in the medulla to the right side of the cord.
step: The two crossings cancel, so the right limbs are affected: ipsilateral ataxia.
answer: Right limbs, with dysmetria and intention tremor.

@mistakes
- Calling the substantia nigra a motor cortex relay: SNc is dopaminergic and SNr is an output nucleus like GPi.
- Predicting contralateral signs from a cerebellar lesion: the double crossing makes them ipsilateral.
- Equating the cerebellum with weakness: cerebellar lesions leave strength intact and impair coordination.
- Treating the direct and indirect pathways as strict on/off switches: both are active in normal movement.
