@article
A man notices that he keeps bumping into people on his left, though each eye separately seems fine. Testing shows that the left half of the visual field is missing in both eyes. Another patient has lost hearing in one ear and has ringing in it. The two disorders differ in a revealing way: a visual lesion behind the eye usually removes half of the field from both eyes, while an auditory lesion above the brainstem almost never deafens one ear. The pathways explain why.

### The visual pathway

Light is transduced by rods and cones in the retina (roughly 120 million rods and 6 million cones in each human eye); signals pass through bipolar cells to **retinal ganglion cells**, whose axons, about a million per eye, form the **optic nerve**. The lens inverts the image, so the **right visual field** falls on the **nasal retina of the right eye** and the **temporal retina of the left eye**.

1. **Optic nerve:** fibers from one eye.
2. **Optic chiasm:** fibers from the nasal half of each retina **cross**; temporal fibers do not. Each **optic tract** now carries the contralateral visual hemifield.
3. **Lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN)** of the thalamus: six layers. Layers 1 and 2 are magnocellular and layers 3 to 6 parvocellular; layers 1, 4 and 6 receive the contralateral eye, and layers 2, 3 and 5 the ipsilateral eye.
4. **Optic radiation** through the posterior limb and sublenticular portion of the internal capsule. Fibers carrying the superior visual field loop forward into the temporal lobe (**Meyer's loop**) before turning back; fibers carrying the inferior visual field run through the parietal lobe.
5. **Primary visual cortex (V1, area 17)** in the walls of the calcarine sulcus: the upper bank (cuneus) receives the inferior field and the lower bank (lingual gyrus) the superior field. The fovea is represented at the occipital pole in a magnified area.

Other retinal targets include the **pretectum** (pupillary light reflex), the **superior colliculus** (orienting eye movements) and the **suprachiasmatic nucleus** (circadian clock).

| lesion | field defect |
|---|---|
| optic nerve | blindness in that eye |
| chiasm (for example pituitary tumor) | bitemporal hemianopia (superior temporal field lost first with compression from below) |
| optic tract, LGN | contralateral homonymous hemianopia |
| Meyer's loop (temporal lobe) | contralateral superior quadrantanopia |
| parietal radiation | contralateral inferior quadrantanopia |
| V1 (for example posterior cerebral artery stroke) | contralateral homonymous hemianopia, often with macular sparing |

Macular sparing is commonly explained by the large cortical representation of the macula and by dual supply from the posterior and middle cerebral arteries at the occipital pole. Some patients with V1 damage can guess the location or movement of stimuli in the blind field without seeing them (blindsight), which suggests other routes, such as the superior colliculus.

### The auditory pathway

Sound makes the basilar membrane move, and **hair cells** of the **organ of Corti** (about 3,500 inner and 12,000 outer hair cells) transduce it; high frequencies are represented at the base and low at the apex (**tonotopy**). **Spiral ganglion** neurons form the cochlear part of cranial nerve VIII.

1. **Cochlear nuclei** (dorsal and ventral), at the pontomedullary junction, ipsilateral.
2. **Superior olivary complex** in the pons, which receives input from both ears through the trapezoid body: the first site of binaural comparison. The medial superior olive uses interaural time differences, and the lateral superior olive interaural level differences.
3. **Lateral lemniscus**, mostly crossed but also uncrossed.
4. **Inferior colliculus** in the midbrain (a nearly obligatory relay).
5. **Medial geniculate nucleus** of the thalamus.
6. **Primary auditory cortex (A1)** in Heschl's gyrus, tonotopically organized.

Because every level above the cochlear nuclei receives input from both ears, a unilateral lesion above them produces only subtle deficits (for example impaired sound localization), while unilateral deafness suggests a lesion of the cochlea, the nerve or the cochlear nucleus. A **vestibular schwannoma** at the cerebellopontine angle causes unilateral sensorineural hearing loss and tinnitus, and can compress the neighboring facial and trigeminal nerves. Bilateral A1 lesions can cause cortical deafness.

### Sound localization by time

For a far-field sound from azimuth $\theta$ with ear separation $d$, the simple model of the interaural time difference is

$$\text{ITD} = \frac{d \sin\theta}{c},$$

where $c \approx 343$ m/s is the speed of sound in air. The model ignores diffraction around the head and so underestimates ITD for real heads.

### Evidence and limits

Pathway maps come from tracing, lesions and imaging. Field defects are rarely perfectly congruous, and exact boundaries between quadrantic defects vary between people.

@example
title: A time difference at the ears
problem: Taking $d = 0.18$ m, what is the maximum ITD predicted by the simple model for a sound directly to one side?
step: Directly to one side, $\theta = 90°$, so $\sin\theta = 1$.
step: $\text{ITD} = 0.18/343 = 5.25 \times 10^{-4}$ s.
step: In milliseconds this is 0.525 ms.
answer: About 0.52 ms.

@example
title: Which field defect?
problem: A tumor presses on the right temporal lobe and cuts Meyer's loop. What field defect results?
step: Meyer's loop carries fibers from the inferior retina, which see the superior visual field.
step: The right optic radiation carries the left visual hemifield.
answer: A left superior quadrantanopia in both eyes.

@mistakes
- Saying the whole optic nerve crosses at the chiasm: only nasal retinal fibers cross, so each tract carries a visual hemifield.
- Confusing field with retina: the field is inverted and reversed on the retina.
- Predicting unilateral deafness from a lesion of the lateral lemniscus or inferior colliculus: bilateral projections spare hearing in each ear.
- Thinking macular sparing means the macula is not in the cortex: it is a property of the vascular supply and representation.
