@article
A man wakes with a drooping right corner of the mouth, but he can still wrinkle his forehead. His right arm is weak, and when he sticks out his tongue it points to the right. A second patient, with a facial palsy that began the previous day, cannot wrinkle the right forehead and cannot close the right eye. The two droops look alike but come from different parts of the motor system, and the corticospinal tract explains the difference.

### Upper and lower motor neurons

**Upper motor neurons** (UMNs) are cortical neurons, mostly layer V pyramidal cells, whose axons descend to brainstem and spinal cord targets. **Lower motor neurons** (LMNs) are the alpha motor neurons of the ventral horn and cranial nerve motor nuclei, whose axons run to muscle. LMN lesions give flaccid weakness, atrophy, fasciculations and reduced reflexes. UMN lesions give weakness with spasticity, exaggerated reflexes and an extensor plantar response.

### The corticospinal tract

About a third of corticospinal axons come from primary motor cortex (area 4, including the giant Betz cells); the rest come from premotor and supplementary motor cortex and from parietal cortex (mostly somatosensory). Each medullary pyramid holds about a million axons. The path, with the crossing marked:

1. Axons leave the cortex in the **corona radiata**.
2. They pass through the **posterior limb of the internal capsule**, roughly with arm fibers anterior to leg fibers.
3. They descend through the middle portion of the **cerebral peduncle** (crus cerebri) of the midbrain.
4. They traverse the **basilar pons** in separated bundles.
5. They gather in the **medullary pyramid**.
6. At the **pyramidal decussation**, at the junction of medulla and cord, about 85 to 90% cross (the percentage varies between people) and descend as the **lateral corticospinal tract** in the lateral funiculus.
7. They end in the ventral horn, often through interneurons; in primates, some synapse directly on alpha motor neurons, especially for the hand, which supports fine finger control.
8. Alpha motor neurons send axons through the ventral root to muscle.

The remaining 10 to 15% descend uncrossed in the **anterior corticospinal tract** for axial and proximal muscles, and cross at the segmental level. Because the main crossing is low in the medulla, a lesion **above** it (cortex, internal capsule, midbrain, pons, upper medulla) causes contralateral weakness, and a lesion **below** it (spinal cord) causes ipsilateral weakness.

### Corticobulbar fibers

The corticobulbar tract projects to brainstem motor nuclei, mostly **bilaterally**. Two classic exceptions are the part of the facial nucleus that moves the lower face, which receives mainly contralateral input, and the hypoglossal nucleus for the genioglossus. A **central (UMN) facial palsy** from a lesion of one hemisphere weakens the contralateral lower face and spares the forehead, which has bilateral cortical input. A **peripheral (LMN) facial palsy** (damage to the nucleus or the nerve) weakens the entire ipsilateral face, forehead included. In a UMN lesion, the tongue deviates toward the weak side, which is contralateral to the lesion; in an LMN lesion of the hypoglossal nerve, it deviates toward the lesion side.

### Other descending systems

| tract | origin | main role |
|---|---|---|
| reticulospinal | pontine and medullary reticular formation | posture, gait, proximal muscle tone |
| vestibulospinal (lateral) | lateral vestibular nucleus | extensor tone and balance |
| rubrospinal | red nucleus | limb flexors; small role in humans |
| tectospinal | superior colliculus | head turning toward stimuli |

### Evidence and limits

The corticospinal course has been established by anatomy, stroke localization and stimulation. Small strokes in the capsule can give pure motor weakness because fibers are packed tightly there. Recovery after UMN lesions involves other pathways and cortical reorganization, so deficits change over time.

@example
title: Following the crossing
problem: A stroke destroys the left posterior limb of the internal capsule. Which side is weak, and why?
step: The corticospinal axons from left motor cortex pass through the left capsule.
step: They have not yet crossed, because the pyramidal decussation is at the medulla–cord junction.
step: They will cross there to descend on the right, controlling right-sided muscles.
answer: The right side of the body is weak, with UMN signs.

@example
title: Is the facial palsy central or peripheral?
problem: A patient cannot move the left side of the lower face but wrinkles both sides of the forehead normally. What is the lesion?
step: Forehead muscles have bilateral cortical input, so a one-sided cortical or capsular lesion spares them.
step: The lower face receives mainly contralateral input, so the weakness is contralateral to the lesion.
answer: A central (UMN) palsy from a lesion in the right hemisphere.

@mistakes
- Predicting ipsilateral weakness from a cortical or capsular lesion: the crossing is below, so the weakness is contralateral.
- Calling a facial palsy that spares the forehead peripheral: sparing the forehead indicates a central lesion.
- Mixing up the tongue rule: a UMN lesion makes the tongue deviate toward the weak side (contralateral to the lesion), and a peripheral lesion toward the damaged side.
- Thinking the whole corticospinal tract crosses: about 10 to 15% remain uncrossed in the anterior tract.
