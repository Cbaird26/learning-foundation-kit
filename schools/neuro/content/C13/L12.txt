@article
A neurologist can often name the site of a lesion before the scan is done. A man who cannot move his right arm and leg and whose speech is slurred has a lesion on one side of the brain; a woman who has lost pain and temperature on her left face and right body has a lesion in the right lateral medulla. The reasoning works because every pathway you have studied has a known location and a known level of crossing, so a pattern of loss points to a place.

### A method

1. **What is lost?** List each deficit precisely: weakness, sensory modality, reflexes, cranial nerve findings, language or attention, and visual fields.
2. **Upper or lower motor neuron?** Spasticity, hyperreflexia and a Babinski sign mean upper motor neuron; flaccidity, atrophy and fasciculations mean lower motor neuron (nerve root, nerve, anterior horn).
3. **Where does each pathway cross?** Corticospinal axons cross at the pyramidal decussation, dorsal column fibers in the medulla, spinothalamic fibers in the cord within a segment or two, and visual fibers in the chiasm. A lesion above the crossing gives contralateral signs; below it, ipsilateral.
4. **Combine nearby structures.** Cranial nerve signs are ipsilateral to a brainstem lesion, long-tract signs are contralateral (or ipsilateral, for dorsal columns below the medulla), and the nerve involved gives the level.
5. **Cortical signs.** Aphasia, neglect, visual field cuts and cortical sensory loss point to the cortex or its immediate white matter.
6. **Check the territory.** Deficits that fit a single arterial territory suggest a vascular cause. Prefer one lesion that explains everything, but be ready to accept more than one.

### Classic patterns

| site | typical findings |
|---|---|
| left MCA cortex | right face and arm weakness and sensory loss, aphasia, right homonymous hemianopia |
| right MCA cortex | left face and arm weakness, left neglect, left hemianopia |
| ACA | contralateral leg weakness and sensory loss, abulia |
| PCA | contralateral homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing |
| internal capsule (lacunar) | contralateral face, arm and leg weakness without cortical signs |
| thalamus (VPL, VPM) | contralateral loss of all sensory modalities, later thalamic pain |
| midbrain (Weber) | ipsilateral CN III palsy, contralateral hemiparesis |
| ventral pons | ipsilateral CN VI (and VII) palsy, contralateral hemiparesis; a large bilateral lesion causes locked-in syndrome |
| lateral medulla (Wallenberg) | ipsilateral face and contralateral body pain and temperature loss, Horner syndrome, dysphagia, ataxia |
| spinal hemisection | ipsilateral weakness and dorsal column loss, contralateral pain and temperature loss |
| central cord | cape loss of pain and temperature, preserved touch |
| cauda equina | lower motor neuron leg weakness, saddle numbness, bladder dysfunction |

**Locked-in syndrome** after bilateral ventral pontine infarction leaves the patient awake and aware but paralyzed apart from vertical eye movements and blinking, because the corticospinal and corticobulbar fibers are interrupted while the reticular system and the midbrain eye-movement pathways are spared. Patients can communicate with eye movements.

### Using evoked potentials

Somatosensory evoked potentials add numbers to the examination. After electrical stimulation of the median nerve at the wrist, recordings show peaks at the brachial plexus (N9), at the level of the cervical cord and lower brainstem (N13) and at primary somatosensory cortex (N20). The **central conduction time** is the interval from N13 to N20; prolongation suggests a lesion of the central sensory pathway between them. Normal values depend on the laboratory and the patient's height.

### Evidence and limits

Localization from signs is a probabilistic inference and has been tested against imaging and pathology: small lesions follow the rules reliably, but large or multifocal lesions, vascular variants and edema make the picture less clean. Signs change over time (for example spinal shock), and some deficits reflect network effects distant from the lesion.

@example
title: Localizing a brainstem lesion
problem: A woman has loss of pain and temperature on the left side of her face and the right side of her body, a left droopy eyelid with a small pupil, hoarseness, and difficulty swallowing. Where is the lesion?
step: Pain and temperature loss in the face on the left means the left spinal trigeminal tract or nucleus is damaged; the right body loss means the left spinothalamic tract (crossed) is damaged.
step: Left Horner syndrome (ptosis and miosis) means left descending sympathetic fibers are damaged; hoarseness and dysphagia mean left nucleus ambiguus involvement.
step: All of these lie in the left lateral medulla, supplied by the left vertebral artery or its posterior inferior cerebellar branch.
answer: The left lateral medulla (Wallenberg syndrome).

@example
title: Prolonged central conduction time
problem: A patient's N13 peak occurs at 13.6 ms and N20 at 21.2 ms after median nerve stimulation. If the laboratory normal is 5.8 ms with a standard deviation of 0.4 ms, is the central conduction time abnormal?
step: Central conduction time: $21.2 - 13.6 = 7.6$ ms.
step: Difference from the normal mean: $7.6 - 5.8 = 1.8$ ms.
step: In standard deviations: $1.8/0.4 = 4.5$, far above the usual abnormal cutoff of about 2.5 to 3 standard deviations.
answer: Yes: 7.6 ms is 4.5 SD above the normal mean, suggesting a lesion between the cervical cord and the cortex.

@mistakes
- Localizing from one sign: always combine findings across motor, sensory, cranial nerve and cortical domains.
- Forgetting the crossing level: the same sign can be ipsilateral or contralateral depending on where the lesion lies relative to the decussation.
- Treating all weakness as upper motor neuron: lower motor neuron signs point to roots, nerves or the anterior horn.
- Equating the function of a region with what is lost after a lesion: a lesion can remove fibers passing through and disconnect distant areas.
