@article
In 1929 Hans Berger showed that voltage fluctuations measured through the human scalp reflect brain activity, and that a rhythm of about 10 cycles per second appears when a person relaxes with the eyes closed. Today **electroencephalography (EEG)** and its magnetic partner **magnetoencephalography (MEG)** are the main tools for watching cognition unfold in milliseconds. Their price is poor spatial precision.

### Where the signal comes from

A single neuron's current is far too small to detect at the scalp. The signal arises from the **postsynaptic currents of thousands to millions of cortical pyramidal cells** that are active together and are aligned in parallel, perpendicular to the cortical surface. Their current flow forms a dipole (an "open field") that sums across neurons. EEG records the resulting voltage difference between scalp electrodes and a reference, in microvolts (µV), typically at 250 to 1000 samples per second. Cells with closed fields, or that fire out of synchrony, contribute little, and action potentials, being brief, contribute less than synaptic currents.

MEG measures the magnetic fields produced by the same currents, using superconducting sensors (SQUIDs) in a magnetically shielded room, because the fields are about a billion times weaker than Earth's. The skull and scalp blur electric potentials but hardly distort magnetic fields. MEG is most sensitive to currents tangential to the scalp, which flow in the walls of sulci, and nearly blind to radial currents in gyral crowns; EEG sees both. The two are complementary.

Neither tells you uniquely where the activity is. Infinitely many source configurations produce the same field outside the head, so the **inverse problem** is ill-posed, and source estimates rely on assumptions (smoothness, a small number of dipoles) and head models. Spatial resolution is on the order of centimeters for EEG and a little better for MEG under favorable conditions.

### Event-related potentials

A single-trial response of a few microvolts is buried in EEG background activity of tens of microvolts. The **event-related potential (ERP)** is the average of epochs time-locked to a stimulus or response. If the noise is independent across trials with standard deviation $\sigma$ and the signal is the same on each trial, the noise in the average of $N$ trials has standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt{N}$, so the signal-to-noise ratio improves by $\sqrt{N}$: 100 trials give a tenfold gain, which is why ERP studies need dozens to hundreds of trials per condition. The assumption fails when latencies vary from trial to trial: jitter smears and reduces the average's peak.

```python
def average_erp(epochs):
    """Point-by-point mean across epochs (lists of equal length)."""
    n = len(epochs)
    return [sum(e[i] for e in epochs) / n for i in range(len(epochs[0]))]
```

Epochs are usually **baseline-corrected** by subtracting each trial's mean in a pre-stimulus interval. Components are named by polarity and latency or order: the N170 (a negative deflection near 170 ms that is larger for faces than for objects at occipito-temporal electrodes), the N400 (larger for words that do not fit their context; Kutas and Hillyard, 1980), and the P300 (larger for rare, task-relevant stimuli). Brain **oscillations** are named by frequency: delta (1 to 4 Hz), theta (4 to 8), alpha (8 to 12), beta (13 to 30) and gamma (above 30), with approximate boundaries.

### Reading a timing claim

What does a difference between waveforms at a given latency show? Suppose faces and objects first differ reliably at 150 ms. Then the brain has **discriminated** the categories by about 150 ms: that is an upper bound on how early the information is available. It does not show that processing begins at 150 ms (earlier stages may not differ), that it ends there, or where it takes place. **Peak latency** is also unreliable as a measure of timing: it depends on amplitude, noise and filtering, and a later peak could mean a slower process or a larger, overlapping component. Overlapping components and the sign cancellation inherent in the scalp potential mean that an ERP component is not the same as a single brain generator. Strong ERP claims use difference waves, onset measures with statistical control, and converging evidence from methods such as intracranial recordings or stimulation.

Well-established: the origin of the signals, the averaging logic and the inverse problem. Debated: the precise generators of most components.

@example
title: Trials needed for a target signal-to-noise ratio
problem: The single-trial EEG noise has a standard deviation of 10 µV and a component has amplitude 5 µV. How many trials must be averaged for the noise in the average to be 1 µV, and what is the signal-to-noise ratio (amplitude/noise) then?
step: The noise after averaging is $\sigma/\sqrt{N}$, so we need $10/\sqrt{N} = 1$.
step: Solve: $\sqrt{N} = 10$, so $N = 100$ trials.
step: Before averaging the ratio was $5/10 = 0.5$; after it is $5/1 = 5$, a gain of $\sqrt{100} = 10$.
answer: 100 trials; the ratio rises from 0.5 to 5.

@example
title: A baseline-corrected average
problem: Two epochs sampled every 10 ms, the first two samples being the baseline, are $[2, 2, 5, 9]$ and $[4, 4, 5, 7]$ µV. Compute the baseline-corrected ERP.
step: Epoch 1 baseline mean is $2$, giving $[0, 0, 3, 7]$.
step: Epoch 2 baseline mean is $4$, giving $[0, 0, 1, 3]$.
step: Average point by point: $[0, 0, 2, 5]$ µV.
step: Without correction the average would be $[3, 3, 5, 8]$: the offset difference between trials would be mistaken for a sustained effect.
answer: $[0, 0, 2, 5]$ µV.

@mistakes
- Treating the scalp location of an effect as the location of its source: the inverse problem has no unique solution, and the skull blurs the potential.
- Reading the latency of a peak as the time at which processing starts: only a difference between conditions bounds when discrimination has occurred, and peak latency depends on noise and overlap.
- Expecting averaging to remove all noise: it reduces independent noise by $\sqrt{N}$ and cannot remove artifacts that are time-locked to the stimulus or smeared by latency jitter.
- Believing that EEG measures action potentials: it mainly reflects synchronous postsynaptic currents.

model:
```python
def corrected_erp(epochs, n_base):
    """Average of epochs after subtracting each epoch's baseline mean."""
    corrected = []
    for e in epochs:
        base = sum(e[:n_base]) / n_base
        corrected.append([v - base for v in e])
    n = len(corrected)
    return [sum(c[i] for c in corrected) / n for i in range(len(corrected[0]))]
```
hint: Compute each epoch's baseline mean first and subtract it from every sample of that epoch, then average across epochs.
solution: Baseline correction is done per epoch before averaging, so offsets that differ between trials do not appear in the ERP. In the last test the three epochs have different baselines but, once corrected, are $[0,0,4]$, $[0,0,0]$ and $[0,0,-4]$, which average to zero.

model:
```python
def peak_latency(erp, dt, t0, start, end, polarity):
    """Latency (ms) of the most negative or positive sample in a window."""
    best_t, best_v = None, None
    for i, v in enumerate(erp):
        t = t0 + i * dt
        if t < start or t > end:
            continue
        if polarity == 'neg':
            v = -v
        if best_v is None or v > best_v:
            best_t, best_v = t, v
    return best_t
```
hint: Loop over samples, compute each sample's time, skip those outside the window, and keep the best one so far; flip the sign for negative peaks and update only on strict improvement.
solution: Restricting to the window matters: the global negative peak is at 20 ms but a window starting at 30 ms must return 30. Using a strict comparison keeps the earliest sample on ties, as in the last test where two samples equal 1.

@quiz self
prompt: A paper states that "face recognition begins at 170 ms, because the N170 peaks at 170 ms and is larger for faces." Write a critique that lists what the data may show, three things the claim goes beyond, and a better way to support a timing claim.
solution: A larger N170 for faces than objects shows that the scalp potential differs by category by about 170 ms, so the information distinguishing them is available by then. The claim goes beyond this in three ways. The peak latency is not an onset: earlier differences may exist, and peaks depend on noise, filtering and overlapping components. The ERP does not say that recognition occurs at that moment, since the N170 might reflect an early stage of face processing or its output without identity recognition. It also does not localize the process, because scalp potentials are blurred and the source problem is ill-posed. Better support would test the onset of the difference with statistical control for multiple comparisons across time points, use difference waves, check for trial-to-trial latency variability, and compare with intracranial recordings or stimulation timing.
