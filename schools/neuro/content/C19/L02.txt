@article
In 1957 a report described a young man whose surgery for epilepsy removed tissue in both medial temporal lobes. He could hold a conversation and repeat a string of digits, yet he could no longer form new memories of events. The patient, known as H.M. (Scoville and Milner, 1957), is among the most studied in neuroscience. His case shows what the **lesion method** can do: damage a structure, look for what is lost, and infer what the structure contributed.

### Single and double dissociation

A **single dissociation** is a pattern in which a patient (or group) is impaired on task A but performs normally on task B. It is suggestive but ambiguous: perhaps task A is simply harder, or more sensitive to any damage, than task B.

A **double dissociation** is a pair of patients (or groups) with opposite patterns: patient 1 is impaired on A and normal on B, patient 2 is impaired on B and normal on A. It is much stronger evidence, because no single difficulty ordering can explain both patterns: A cannot be the harder task for patient 1 and the easier task for patient 2.

A classic double dissociation concerns short-term and long-term memory. H.M. had a normal digit span but could not form new long-term episodic memories. Patient K.F. (Shallice and Warrington, 1970) had the reverse: a severely reduced auditory-verbal digit span, yet apparently preserved ability to form new long-term memories. This supports the view that the two depend on at least partly separable processes, a conclusion also supported by other evidence, and it was an important argument against the idea that short-term memory is simply a gateway to long-term memory.

### Testing one patient

Patients are usually compared with a small control group. With control mean $\bar{x}_c$ and standard deviation $s_c$, a **z score** is $z = (x - \bar{x}_c)/s_c$, and a common (arbitrary) criterion calls a patient impaired when $z < -2$. With few controls, $s_c$ is itself uncertain, so the **Crawford–Howell** test treats the patient as a new observation from the control distribution:

$$t = \frac{x - \bar{x}_c}{s_c \sqrt{(n+1)/n}}, \qquad \text{df} = n - 1,$$

where $n$ is the number of controls. For $n = 20$ controls with mean 50 and $s_c = 8$, a patient scoring 30 has $t = -20/(8 \times 1.0247) \approx -2.44$ with 19 df; the two-tailed 5% critical value is about 2.09, so the deficit is significant.

```python
import math


def crawford_howell(x, mean, sd, n):
    """t statistic for one case against n controls (df = n - 1)."""
    return (x - mean) / (sd * math.sqrt((n + 1) / n))


print(round(crawford_howell(30, 50, 8, 20), 2))
```

This prints `-2.44`. A **classical** dissociation requires an impairment on A and a normal score on B; a **strong** dissociation requires impairment on both but much worse on one; the logic is the same.

### Limits of lesion inference

- **Damage is rarely focal.** Strokes follow blood supply, and surgery or injury may cut white matter pathways; the deficit may come from disconnection of regions not themselves damaged. Lesion–symptom mapping across many patients helps by finding voxels where damage predicts the deficit.
- **Task impurity and difficulty.** Tasks rarely isolate one process. A single dissociation can reflect a difficulty or reliability difference; matching tasks and showing normal control performance on both helps.
- **Compensation and reorganization.** Chronic patients may have relearned strategies, so the deficit understates what the region does; acute and chronic findings can differ.
- **Distant effects.** Damage can reduce activity in connected regions, a phenomenon called **diaschisis**.
- **Models can mimic dissociations.** Some neural-network simulations produced a double dissociation from a single system with differently distributed representations, so a double dissociation supports separable processes without proving separate modules.
- **A lesion shows what is lost, not what the region does.** A region may be necessary for a task without being the seat of the function; a missing fuse stops a radio without being its tuner.

Well-established: the single and double dissociation logic and the cases above. Debated: how far dissociations license claims of modularity.

@example
title: Is it a double dissociation?
problem: Controls score a mean of 50 (sd 10) on task A and 40 (sd 5) on task B. Patient P1 scores 20 on A and 40 on B; P2 scores 50 on A and 25 on B. Using $z < -2$ as impaired, do the data show a double dissociation?
step: Patient P1: $z_A = (20 - 50)/10 = -3$ and $z_B = (40 - 40)/5 = 0$, so P1 is impaired on A and normal on B.
step: Patient P2: $z_A = (50 - 50)/10 = 0$ and $z_B = (25 - 40)/5 = -3$, so P2 is normal on A and impaired on B.
step: The two patients show opposite patterns, each impaired on exactly one task, so this is a double dissociation.
step: It is only as strong as the controls and tasks: it should be checked that A and B are equally reliable and sensitive.
answer: Yes: P1 is impaired on A only and P2 on B only.

@mistakes
- Concluding from a single dissociation that the processes are separate: the impaired task may simply be harder or more sensitive.
- Calling any two patients with different deficits a double dissociation: each must be impaired on one task and normal on the other, and the two tasks must be the same.
- Equating the lesioned region with the function: a deficit shows the region is needed, not that it is the only part of the circuit.
- Using a z score with a small control group as though the control sd were exact: the Crawford–Howell $t$ allows for it.

model:
```python
def classify(patients, norms, cutoff=-2.0):
    """Return 'double', 'single' or 'none' for a set of patient scores."""
    a_only = b_only = False
    for p in patients:
        za = (p['A'] - norms['A'][0]) / norms['A'][1]
        zb = (p['B'] - norms['B'][0]) / norms['B'][1]
        imp_a, imp_b = za < cutoff, zb < cutoff
        if imp_a and not imp_b:
            a_only = True
        if imp_b and not imp_a:
            b_only = True
    if a_only and b_only:
        return 'double'
    if a_only or b_only:
        return 'single'
    return 'none'
```
hint: Compute each patient's two z scores, mark which tasks are impaired, and track whether you have seen an A-only and a B-only patient.
solution: Only patients impaired on exactly one task count, and the cutoff is strict, so a patient at exactly $z = -2$ is not impaired (the edge patient), and a patient impaired on both tasks counts for neither kind.
