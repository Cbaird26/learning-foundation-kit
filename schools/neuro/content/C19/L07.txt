@article
You can look straight at a word and attend to a spot to its side, noticing something there without moving your eyes. Attention is the set of processes that favors some information over the rest. Posner (1980) made it measurable. A cue indicates a likely location; then a target appears either where the cue pointed (a **valid** trial) or elsewhere (**invalid**). On most trials, about 80%, the cue is valid. Participants are faster on valid trials. With invented numbers, a valid cue gives a mean reaction time (RT) of 310 ms, a **neutral** cue 330 ms and an invalid cue 360 ms. The **benefit** is $330 - 310 = 20$ ms, the **cost** is $360 - 330 = 30$ ms, and the **validity effect** is $360 - 310 = 50$ ms.

### Two kinds of attention, three kinds of control

A **peripheral** cue (a flash at the location) draws attention quickly and automatically, within about 100 ms; a **central** cue (an arrow) requires interpretation and attention builds over a few hundred milliseconds under voluntary control. Posner and Petersen (1990) distinguished three functions, each linked to a network: **alerting** (readiness), **orienting** (selecting a location or feature) and **executive control** (resolving conflict). The orienting function is the best studied, and Corbetta and Shulman (2002) proposed two partly separate frontoparietal systems:

- The **dorsal attention network**, the intraparietal sulcus and the frontal eye field in each hemisphere, is engaged when a person voluntarily directs attention, for example after a cue. In imaging studies it is active in the cue-to-target delay, before any target, which suggests that it sets a bias.
- The **ventral attention network**, the temporoparietal junction (TPJ) and ventral frontal cortex, mainly in the right hemisphere, responds to behaviorally relevant but unexpected stimuli, such as a target on an invalid trial, and acts as a circuit breaker that triggers reorienting.

The two-network picture is a useful summary but not final: the networks interact, and the exact roles of the TPJ are debated.

### Neglect

After damage to the right parietal lobe, particularly the right inferior parietal lobule and TPJ (the exact critical sites are debated, and white-matter damage may matter), patients may fail to notice things on the left. This is **hemispatial neglect**. It is not blindness: the visual fields are intact, and a patient may eat food only from the right half of the plate or cross out only right-sided targets. In line bisection the patient marks the midpoint to the right of the true center: on a 200 mm line, a mark 118 mm from the left end deviates by $118 - 100 = 18$ mm, 9% of the line. Posner and colleagues (1984) found that patients with parietal damage were especially slow to respond to a target on the side opposite the lesion after an invalid cue, as if they had trouble disengaging attention. That neglect is more common and severe after right than left lesions suggests a right-hemisphere bias for spatial attention.

### How attention changes neurons

In macaques, responses of neurons in visual area V4 to a stimulus in the receptive field depend on whether the animal attends to it (Moran and Desimone, 1985). The **biased competition** account (Desimone and Duncan, 1995) says that stimuli compete for neural representation and that attention biases the competition toward behaviorally relevant ones. Effects are often summarized with an **attention modulation index**, $(r_{\text{att}} - r_{\text{unatt}})/(r_{\text{att}} + r_{\text{unatt}})$. In humans, attention increases the amplitude of early ERP components (P1 and N1) for stimuli at an attended location, and it lowers **alpha** power (8 to 12 Hz) over the occipital hemisphere contralateral to the attended side and raises it ipsilaterally. A lateralization index is $(\text{ipsi} - \text{contra})/(\text{ipsi} + \text{contra})$, which is positive when alpha is higher over the hemisphere ipsilateral to the attended side, the usual pattern. In fMRI, attention raises responses even in early visual cortex.

### What is established and what is debated

Established: attention changes behavior in the cueing paradigm, and it modulates responses in sensory cortex, with control signals from frontoparietal regions. Debated: whether the effect is best described as a gain change, a sharpening of tuning, or a change in noise; how far early selection occurs; and how to separate attention from expectation, reward and arousal, which cueing designs can confound.

@example
title: Benefit, cost and alpha lateralization
problem: Mean RTs are 310 ms (valid), 330 ms (neutral) and 360 ms (invalid). In a second experiment, when attention is directed to the right, occipital alpha power is 8 (µV$^2$) over the left hemisphere and 12 over the right. Compute the benefit, the cost and the alpha lateralization index.
step: Benefit is the neutral minus valid RT, $330 - 310 = 20$ ms, and cost is invalid minus neutral, $360 - 330 = 30$ ms.
step: With attention to the right, the left hemisphere is contralateral and the right is ipsilateral, so contra $= 8$ and ipsi $= 12$.
step: The index is $(12 - 8)/(12 + 8) = 4/20 = 0.20$.
step: The positive value means alpha is higher over the hemisphere ipsilateral to the attended side, as expected.
answer: Benefit 20 ms, cost 30 ms, alpha index $0.20$.

@mistakes
- Treating neglect as blindness: the sensory fields are intact, and attention to the contralesional side fails.
- Treating a cue-validity effect as pure attention: expectations and response preparation may contribute, so controls matter.
- Reading the dorsal and ventral networks as separate "modules": they interact, and their roles are partly debated.
- Reversing the sign of a modulation index: define which condition is in the numerator and keep to it.
