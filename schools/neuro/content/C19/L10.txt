@article
A patient with frontal damage sits in an examiner's office. On the table are a comb and a cup. Without instruction he picks up the comb and combs his hair, then drinks from the cup, although he was told only to sit. His memory, language and intelligence test scores are near normal. What is missing is not knowledge but control: the ability to select behavior by goal rather than by what the environment invites. This is the domain of **executive function** and of the prefrontal cortex (PFC).

### What executive functions are

Executive functions are processes that organize behavior toward goals, especially in new or conflicting situations. An influential factor analysis (Miyake and colleagues, 2000) found three correlated but separable functions in healthy adults: **inhibition** of dominant responses, **shifting** between tasks or rules, and **updating** the contents of working memory. Typical measures:

- **Stroop task** (Stroop, 1935): name the ink color of words; the **interference effect** is the mean reaction time for incongruent items (the word RED in blue ink) minus congruent ones.
- **Task switching**: the **switch cost** is the RT on switch trials minus repeat trials.
- **Wisconsin Card Sorting Test**: sort cards by color, shape or number, with the rule changing without warning after a run of correct sorts. A **perseverative error** is a continued sort by the old rule. Patients with dorsolateral frontal lesions perseverated more than controls in an early study (Milner, 1963), though poor performance also occurs after other lesions and diseases, so the test is neither specific to the PFC nor a pure test of one function.

### The prefrontal cortex

The PFC lies anterior to motor and premotor cortex and is much larger in primates than in other mammals. Its regions have different roles: **dorsolateral** (working memory, planning, rule use), **ventrolateral** (selection and inhibition), **medial**, including the anterior cingulate cortex (monitoring, effort and motivation), and **orbital and ventromedial** (value, social behavior, impulse control). It is densely connected with sensory and motor cortex, the basal ganglia and the mediodorsal thalamus. It is not a unitary "executive": imaging shows that a similar set of frontal and parietal regions is recruited across many demanding tasks (Duncan and Owen, 2000, the "multiple-demand" pattern), while some specializations remain.

### Working memory in prefrontal neurons

In monkeys doing **delayed-response** tasks, in which a cue is shown, then removed, and the animal must respond after a delay, many neurons in lateral PFC keep firing during the delay (Fuster and Alexander, 1971). In an oculomotor version, a given neuron's delay firing depends on the direction of the remembered location (Funahashi, Bruce and Goldman-Rakic, 1989), which looks like a stored spatial memory. Lesions of the region impair delayed responses.

**It is debated what the activity is for.** One view says PFC holds the remembered content. Another says that the contents are stored in sensory cortex (decodable from visual areas during delays in human imaging) and in parietal cortex, while the PFC supplies **control**, the top-down signal that maintains and protects them. A third notes that memory can sometimes be held in short-term synaptic changes with little spiking ("activity-silent" states). Findings of delay activity, decoding in sensory areas, and the dependence on distractors all have supporters, and the account is not settled.

### Models of control

- **Supervisory attentional system** (Norman and Shallice, 1986): routine behavior runs through competing habit schemas ("contention scheduling"); a supervisory system intervenes in novel or dangerous situations. Damage releases habits: **perseveration** and **utilization behavior**, in which patients use objects in view without instruction (Lhermitte, 1983), as in the opening case.
- **Biased competition and the integrative theory** (Miller and Cohen, 2001): PFC maintains goals and rules and biases activity in other regions toward task-relevant pathways. In the Stroop task, reading is stronger than color naming, so naming needs a goal signal that boosts the weaker pathway; this is why incongruent trials are slower.
- **Conflict monitoring** (Botvinick and colleagues, 2001): the anterior cingulate cortex detects conflict and signals the lateral PFC to increase control. Other accounts link it to expected value of effort or errors.

**Limits.** Frontal lesions are heterogeneous, executive tasks are impure, and patients vary. A deficit shows that the region is part of a network that is needed, not what it computes.

@example
title: Stroop interference and switch cost
problem: Mean RTs are 620 ms (congruent) and 765 ms (incongruent) in a Stroop task, and 540 ms (repeat) and 690 ms (switch) in a task-switching task. Compute the interference effect, the switch cost, and the switch cost as a percent of the repeat RT.
step: Interference is $765 - 620 = 145$ ms.
step: The switch cost is $690 - 540 = 150$ ms.
step: As a percentage of the repeat RT: $150/540 \times 100 \approx 27.8\%$.
step: Both costs reflect control needed to override a dominant response or to reconfigure the task set.
answer: 145 ms, 150 ms and about 27.8%.

@example
title: Explaining a patient's behavior with a model
problem: A patient with frontal damage combs his hair when a comb is placed in front of him, though told to wait. Which model explains this, and what would the model predict for a Stroop task?
step: In the supervisory attentional system model, objects in view trigger routine schemas through contention scheduling; the supervisory system normally suppresses schemas not matching the goal.
step: Damage to the supervisory system leaves the habit unopposed, so the object triggers the action (utilization behavior).
step: In the biasing model, a weak goal signal fails to bias activity toward the instructed behavior, which is the same failure in other terms.
step: Prediction: a large Stroop interference effect and perseverative errors in card sorting, since both require overriding a stronger habit; the prediction should be tested because heterogeneous lesions may not produce it.
answer: Loss of supervisory control; larger interference and perseveration are predicted.

@mistakes
- Treating the PFC as a single "executive" or homunculus: its parts differ, and control involves a distributed network.
- Reading a card-sorting deficit as proof of frontal damage: other lesions and diseases also impair it.
- Concluding from delay-period firing that the PFC stores the memory: the activity may reflect control, attention or preparation.
- Subtracting in the wrong direction: interference is incongruent minus congruent, and switch cost is switch minus repeat.

solution: In the supervisory attentional system model, routine schemas are triggered by objects and context, and the supervisory system selects goal-relevant behavior and overrides habits.
