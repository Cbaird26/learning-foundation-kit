@article
Recording methods tell us when a region is active, but can the region be shown to matter? In 1985 Barker and colleagues introduced **transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS)**: a coil held against the scalp passes a brief, strong current, and the changing magnetic field it makes induces an electric field in the brain beneath, without surgery. Applied over the primary motor cortex, one pulse makes the opposite hand twitch; over the occipital cortex, a person may see a brief spot of light, a phosphene. The brain can now be perturbed painlessly and reversibly in people.

### How TMS works

**Faraday's law** says that a changing magnetic flux $\Phi$ through a loop induces an electromotive force $\mathcal{E} = -d\Phi/dt$. The coil current rises and falls in well under a millisecond, producing a field of the order of 1 to 2 tesla at the coil surface. The induced electric field in the cortex depolarizes the membranes of neurons, mostly axons and axon bends, and can trigger action potentials. As an example, a flux that grows from 0 to $B A$ with $B = 2$ T through $A = 5$ cm$^2$ $= 5 \times 10^{-4}$ m$^2$ in 200 µs gives $\mathcal{E} = 5\times10^{-4} \times 2 / (2\times10^{-4}) = 5$ V around such a loop. Because the field falls quickly with distance, TMS reaches the cortical surface (roughly 2 to 3 cm below the coil, more for deeper coils with weaker focus) and, with a figure-eight coil, stimulates an area on the order of a centimeter.

- **Single-pulse TMS** over the motor cortex evokes a **motor evoked potential (MEP)** in a contralateral hand muscle; the **motor threshold** is the lowest intensity that gives an MEP of at least about 50 µV in at least half the trials, and is used to scale intensity across people.
- **Repetitive TMS (rTMS)**, a train of pulses at a set frequency, can change excitability beyond the stimulation period. Low-frequency stimulation (about 1 Hz) tends to reduce cortical excitability and high-frequency stimulation (5 Hz or more) tends to increase it, but the effects vary a lot across people and sessions. A train of 10 Hz for 4 s has 40 pulses.
- Weak direct current stimulation (tDCS) is a different technique, and its effects on cognition are debated.

### The logic: from perturbation to function

The **virtual lesion** idea is that stimulation applied just before or during a task briefly disrupts a region, so that impaired performance means the region is needed. This is **chronometric** in its refined form: delivering a pulse at different delays (stimulus onset asynchronies, SOAs) shows when the region matters. A larger slowing at 100 and 150 ms than at 50 and 200 ms would say that the region's contribution is critical in that window.

The logic is only as good as its controls:

- **Non-specific effects**: the click, the scalp sensation and muscle twitches can startle or distract. A **control site** (often the vertex) with the same intensity, or a **sham coil**, estimates them. The effect of interest is the difference between the target site and the control.
- **Task specificity**: a **control task** shows that the effect is not general slowing, giving a dissociation between task and site (ideally a double dissociation).
- **Spread and state**: TMS affects connected regions, so an effect shows that the network is disturbed, and the effect depends on the brain's state at the time of the pulse. The "lesion" may add noise or even speed performance, so the label is only a metaphor.
- **Targeting**: coil placement varies with head and scalp-to-cortex distance, and the stimulated neurons are not identified. Neuronavigation guided by an individual MRI improves targeting.

Strengths: causal, reversible, timed. Weaknesses: modest focality, limited depth, uncertain mechanisms, and effects that are often small and variable. Together with fMRI, which finds where, TMS asks whether it matters.

### Safety and use

TMS is considered safe with screening and guidelines; the main serious risk is a seizure, which is rare but is why people with epilepsy, certain implants or related risk factors are excluded from research. TMS is also used clinically, for example in some countries for depression that has not responded to other treatments; decisions about treatment belong to clinicians. Research with people requires ethical review and informed consent.

@example
title: Site-specific effect in a chronometric experiment
problem: Mean reaction times (ms) with TMS over the target site and with TMS over the vertex control site are, by SOA: 50 ms, 505 and 502; 100 ms, 560 and 500; 150 ms, 540 and 498; 200 ms, 503 and 501. Which SOAs show a site-specific effect, and how large?
step: The site-specific effect is the target minus the control: $505 - 502 = 3$, $560 - 500 = 60$, $540 - 498 = 42$, $503 - 501 = 2$ ms.
step: The effect is large at 100 and 150 ms and negligible at 50 and 200 ms.
step: A reasonable conclusion is that the target region contributes between about 100 and 150 ms after the stimulus, provided the task is specific and the effect is reliable across participants.
step: Without the control site we could not exclude a general effect of the click on all SOAs, though that cannot explain why the effect is strong only at two SOAs.
answer: Effects of 3, 60, 42 and 2 ms: clear effects at 100 and 150 ms only.

@mistakes
- Treating TMS as a clean lesion: the effect spreads through connected regions, and its direction can vary.
- Omitting a control site or sham: clicks and scalp sensations alone can slow responses.
- Concluding that a region does a function because TMS impaired it: the result shows that the region, or its network, is needed, not what it computes.
- Assuming rTMS always inhibits at low frequency and excites at high frequency: effects are variable across people and protocols.

@practice self
prompt: A student says that TMS "creates a virtual lesion, so if TMS over region R slows a task, R is the region that performs the task's computation." Evaluate the claim and design a short experiment that would give stronger evidence.
hint: Separate what the pulses show about necessity from what they show about computation, and think of controls and timing.
solution: The first step is valid: if TMS over R impairs the task more than control stimulation, then R or its network is needed in that window. The second step does not follow. R may be part of a network, the disruption may spread to connected regions, TMS may add noise rather than remove a function, and necessity does not specify the computation. A stronger design would use a control site and a control task, deliver pulses at several SOAs to show when R matters, and use fMRI to confirm that R is active in the task. A second experiment could show a double dissociation: stimulation of R impairs task A and not B, and stimulation of another region impairs B and not A.
rubric: Accepts the valid part (necessity of R or its network) and rejects the overreach (computation).
rubric: Names at least two limits (spread, state dependence, noise rather than lesion).
rubric: Proposes controls (site, task, sham) and a timing manipulation or double dissociation.

@quiz self
prompt: Compare TMS and fMRI as sources of evidence about the role of a frontal region in a working memory task: state what each can show and design a combined study.
hint: Ask what each method can show about necessity, location and timing.
solution: fMRI shows that the region is active during the task (for example more with higher load) with good spatial resolution but slowly and only correlationally. TMS can test necessity: if stimulation over the region at specific times impairs performance relative to a control site, the region or its network is needed then. TMS has weaker spatial resolution and uncertain mechanisms. A combined study would first localize the region in each participant with fMRI, then apply TMS to that site and to a control site while participants do the working memory task at high and low load, with pulses during encoding or the delay in different blocks. A load-specific impairment at the fMRI-defined site and in a specific period would give converging evidence of where, whether it matters, and when.
rubric: States that fMRI is correlational and TMS can address necessity.
rubric: Designs a study with individual localization, a control site and a timing or load manipulation.
rubric: Mentions a limit of each method.
