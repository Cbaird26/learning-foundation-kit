@article
A patient has a stroke and can no longer recognize faces, yet she sees well, reads, and recognizes her friends by their voices. What have we learned about the brain? Perhaps that some tissue is specialized for faces. Perhaps only that face recognition is harder than the other tasks she tried. Cognitive neuroscience is the discipline of working out which of these readings is right, and its main skill is **inference**: knowing what a given kind of evidence can and cannot establish.

### Three levels of explanation

Marr (1982) argued that an information-processing system must be explained at three levels.

- The **computational level** says what problem the system solves and why: the goal and the logic of the strategy. For a cash register, the goal is addition, with its properties (order does not matter, adding zero changes nothing).
- The **algorithmic (representational) level** says how: which representation of the input and output, and which procedure. A register could add decimal digits with carries or binary numbers.
- The **implementational level** says how the procedure is physically realized: transistors, or neurons and synapses.

The levels constrain each other but are not interchangeable. Knowing every ion channel of a neuron does not tell you what problem the circuit solves, and a precise computational theory does not say which brain region does it. Cognitive neuroscience tries to connect the levels: behavioral experiments constrain the computation and algorithm, and brain measurements test proposals about the implementation. The mapping is rarely one region to one function; many results show that a function depends on a **network** of regions and that one region takes part in several functions.

### What each method measures

| method | measures | spatial scale | time scale | shows necessity? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| lesion studies | behavior after damage | millimeters to centimeters (set by the lesion) | permanent or slowly changing | yes, with caveats |
| single-neuron recording | spikes of single cells | one neuron | about 1 ms | no |
| EEG and MEG | electric or magnetic fields from synchronous neurons | centimeters | milliseconds | no |
| fMRI | blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signal | voxels of 1 to 3 mm | seconds | no |
| brain stimulation (TMS) | behavior after brief disruption | about 1 cm | pulses timed to the millisecond | yes, with caveats |

**Correlational** methods (recording, EEG, MEG, fMRI) show that activity is associated with a task. They cannot show that the activity is necessary, because a region can be active without contributing, for example when it only receives a copy of a signal. Methods that perturb the brain (lesions, stimulation) can address necessity, but they bring their own problems: damage is rarely clean, and the rest of the brain may compensate or be disrupted indirectly. No single method has good resolution in both space and time, so strong conclusions rest on **converging evidence**: different methods, with different weaknesses, pointing to the same account.

### Forward and reverse inference

A **forward inference** goes from a cognitive process to the brain: if a task engages process $S$, then region $R$ is active. A **reverse inference** goes the other way: region $R$ was active, so process $S$ was engaged. The two probabilities differ, and Bayes' rule links them:

$$P(S \mid R) = \frac{P(R \mid S)\,P(S)}{P(R \mid S)\,P(S) + P(R \mid \neg S)\,\big(1 - P(S)\big)} .$$

Suppose that, across many studies, region $R$ is active in 90% of those that engage $S$, but also in 30% of those that do not ($R$ is active in many tasks). If a new task engages $S$ in 20% of cases a priori, the posterior is $0.9 \times 0.2 / (0.9 \times 0.2 + 0.3 \times 0.8) \approx 0.43$. The activation is useful evidence, since it raises the probability from 0.20 to 0.43, but far from proof. The reverse inference becomes strong only when the region is **selective**: $P(R \mid \neg S)$ small, so that the likelihood ratio $P(R \mid S)/P(R \mid \neg S)$ is large. Here the ratio is $3$.

### Limits

These ideas set the standards of the course: ask which level a claim is at, which method produced the evidence, whether it is correlational or interventional, and whether a reverse inference is being drawn from a region that responds to many things. Well-established: the methods' resolutions above and the logic of Bayes' rule. Debated: how selective any given region is, and whether functions are better described by regions or by distributed networks.

@example
title: How strong is a reverse inference?
problem: Region $R$ is active in 80% of studies that engage working memory and in 40% of studies that do not. A researcher sees $R$ active in a new task that engages working memory with prior probability $0.3$. What is the probability that the task engaged working memory, given the activation?
step: Write the quantities: $P(R \mid S) = 0.8$, $P(R \mid \neg S) = 0.4$, $P(S) = 0.3$, so $P(\neg S) = 0.7$.
step: Compute the numerator: $0.8 \times 0.3 = 0.24$.
step: Compute the denominator: $0.24 + 0.4 \times 0.7 = 0.24 + 0.28 = 0.52$.
step: Divide: $P(S \mid R) = 0.24 / 0.52 \approx 0.46$. The likelihood ratio is only $2$, so the activation moves the probability from $0.30$ to $0.46$.
answer: About $0.46$.

@example
title: Choosing a method for a question
problem: You want to know whether a region is necessary for recognizing faces, and, separately, when in time face-related processing begins. Which methods answer each question?
step: Necessity is a causal question, so use a method that disturbs the region: a lesion study or a brain stimulation study.
step: A timing question needs millisecond resolution, so use EEG, MEG, or single-neuron recording, not fMRI, whose signal is sampled over seconds.
step: Combine them: stimulation at different delays after the stimulus tests both necessity and timing, and fMRI can check that the stimulated region is the one that responds to faces.
answer: Lesions or stimulation for necessity; EEG, MEG or recordings for timing; converging evidence for a strong claim.

@mistakes
- Treating an activation as proof that a region is necessary: activity is correlational, and only interventions such as lesions or stimulation speak to necessity.
- Reading a reverse inference as certain: $P(\text{process} \mid \text{activation})$ is not $P(\text{activation} \mid \text{process})$, and it depends on how selective the region is and on the prior.
- Mixing levels: an ion-channel description is not an answer to what problem a circuit solves, and a computational theory does not say where it is implemented.

@practice num
prompt: Using $P(R \mid S) = 0.56$ and $P(R \mid \neg S) = 0.14$, and a prior $P(S) = 0.25$, what is the probability that the process was engaged, given that the region is active? Give a decimal to two places.
answer: 0.57
tol: 0.01
check: 0.56 * 0.25 / (0.56 * 0.25 + 0.14 * 0.75)
hint: Apply Bayes' rule: the numerator is $P(R \mid S)P(S)$; the denominator adds the same product for $\neg S$.
solution: The numerator is $0.56 \times 0.25 = 0.14$ and the denominator is $0.14 + 0.14 \times 0.75 = 0.245$, so $P(S \mid R) = 0.14/0.245 \approx 0.57$.

@practice self
prompt: A news report says that because a region lit up when volunteers looked at pictures of their children, that region is "the love center." Write a short critique that names the type of inference, explains what additional information about the region would be needed, and proposes one kind of evidence that would strengthen or weaken the claim.
solution: This is a reverse inference from activation to a psychological state. Its strength depends on how selective the region is: if it also responds to unfamiliar faces, to reward, or to other emotional pictures, then $P(\text{activation} \mid \text{not love})$ is large and the likelihood ratio is small. We would need the region's response across many other conditions, or a meta-analytic estimate of the two conditional probabilities. Activation is also correlational, so it does not show that the region is needed for the feeling. A stronger test would compare the children's pictures with pictures of familiar but unloved people and of other positive stimuli, and would add an intervention or a patient study to test necessity.
rubric: Names the inference as a reverse inference (from activation to a mental state).
rubric: Explains that the conclusion depends on the selectivity of the region, with the two conditional probabilities or the likelihood ratio.
rubric: Notes that activation is correlational and proposes a control condition or a causal method.

@quiz mc
prompt: Which statement correctly describes the difference between forward and reverse inference?
choice*: A forward inference goes from a process to the activity it should produce; a reverse inference goes from observed activity to a process, and is only as strong as the region is selective.
choice: A forward inference uses fMRI and a reverse inference uses EEG.
choice: A reverse inference is valid whenever the activation is statistically significant.
choice: A forward inference shows necessity and a reverse inference shows sufficiency.
hint: Think about which conditional probability each one uses.
solution: A forward inference concerns $P(\text{activation} \mid \text{process})$; a reverse inference needs $P(\text{process} \mid \text{activation})$, which also depends on $P(\text{activation} \mid \text{no process})$ and on the prior. Neither is tied to one method, and statistical significance does not make a region selective.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain what it means to say that cognitive neuroscience relies on converging evidence. Use two methods with different strengths and weaknesses to show how together they support a claim that neither supports alone.
solution: Each method has limits: fMRI maps where activity occurs but is correlational and slow; a lesion or stimulation study can address necessity but is imprecise in space or time and may disrupt connected regions. Suppose fMRI shows that a region responds more to faces than to objects, and patients with damage in that region are impaired at face recognition but not object recognition. The imaging result shows where face processing is active in healthy brains, and the patient result shows that the region is needed, so the weakness of each is covered by the other. Because the methods have independent sources of error, agreement is hard to explain by an artifact of one of them.

rubric: Explains how each covers the other's limitation (association versus necessity, or space versus time).
rubric: Concludes that agreement of methods with independent weaknesses is stronger than either result alone.
