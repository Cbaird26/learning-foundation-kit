@article
Bilateral damage to the amygdala is rare, but the patients who have it show something striking: they judge many facial expressions normally but are poor at recognizing fear (Adolphs and colleagues, 1994). Imaging shows the other side of the coin: the amygdala responds more to fearful than neutral faces. So is the amygdala "the fear center"? This lesson shows why that sentence needs care, and it ends with the logic that tests it.

### Emotion and the amygdala

The amygdala is a group of nuclei in the medial temporal lobe, with connections to the sensory cortex, hypothalamus, brainstem and prefrontal cortex. In animals, damage to its nuclei disrupts learned fear responses, and in people bilateral damage impairs physiological responses in fear conditioning and recognition of fearful expressions. Yet the amygdala also responds to positive, novel, ambiguous and arousing stimuli. Two readings compete: that it is specialized for fear, or that it flags stimuli that are significant or uncertain and need attention. Related debates concern whether there are a few **basic emotions** with dedicated circuits, or whether emotions are constructed from more general processes such as core affect and conceptual knowledge, and whether the subjective feeling of fear depends on the same circuits as defensive behavior. These are open.

### Social cognition

Understanding other people's mental states is **theory of mind**. The classic behavioral test is the **false-belief task** (Wimmer and Perner, 1983): a character leaves an object in one place, and while she is away it is moved; where will she look? In fMRI, reading stories about a person's beliefs activates the right **temporoparietal junction** (TPJ) and the **medial prefrontal cortex** more than stories about physical representations of the same structure, such as an outdated photograph (Saxe and Kanwisher, 2003). Whether the TPJ is selective for belief reasoning, or shares computations with attentional reorienting (Lesson 7), is debated.

**Mirror neurons** in macaque premotor area F5 fire both when the animal performs a grasp and when it watches another perform it (di Pellegrino and colleagues, 1992; Gallese and colleagues, 1996). They are an elegant link between action and perception. Their role in understanding actions or intentions is debated, and evidence in humans is indirect.

### Decision making

Choice involves computing the **value** of options. In imaging, the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, orbitofrontal cortex and ventral striatum track subjective value. Dopamine neurons signal a **reward prediction error**, the difference between received and predicted reward (Schultz, Dayan and Montague, 1997). A simple form is $\delta = r - V$; in temporal-difference learning it is $\delta = r + \gamma V(s') - V(s)$ with discount factor $\gamma$. An unexpected reward ($r = 1$, $V = 0$) gives $\delta = +1$; an expected one ($V = 1$) gives 0; an omitted expected reward ($r = 0$, $V = 1$) gives $-1$.

Choices under risk deviate from expected value. Prospect theory (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979) says that outcomes are valued relative to a reference point, with diminishing sensitivity and **loss aversion**. A common form is $v(x) = x^\alpha$ for $x \ge 0$ and $v(x) = -\lambda(-x)^\alpha$ for $x < 0$; estimates from one data set were $\alpha = 0.88$ and $\lambda = 2.25$ (Tversky and Kahneman, 1992), so a loss of 100 has a value of about $-129.5$ against $+57.5$ for a gain of 100. In the Iowa Gambling Task, patients with ventromedial prefrontal damage kept choosing decks with high gains but larger losses (Bechara and colleagues, 1994), interpreted by Damasio's **somatic marker hypothesis** as a failure to use bodily signals of future outcomes; the task's interpretation, and its validity as a measure, are debated.

### Reverse inference, again

The amygdala example is a reverse inference. Suppose an activation occurs with three possible processes, which we treat as exclusive and exhaustive: fear (prior 0.2), general salience (0.5) and memory retrieval (0.3), and the region is active with probabilities 0.9, 0.4 and 0.2 respectively. Bayes' rule gives posteriors proportional to prior times likelihood: $0.18$, $0.20$ and $0.06$, which sum to $0.44$, so $P(\text{fear} \mid \text{active}) \approx 0.41$, $P(\text{salience} \mid \text{active}) \approx 0.45$ and $P(\text{memory} \mid \text{active}) \approx 0.14$. Fear is the process that most reliably activates the region ($0.9$), and yet fear is not even the most probable explanation of the activation.

```python
def posterior_two(prior, like_given, like_not):
    """P(process | activation) for one process against its complement."""
    num = like_given * prior
    return num / (num + like_not * (1 - prior))
```

The limits are in the premises: the priors and likelihoods must come from many studies, the processes are neither exclusive nor exhaustive, and the regions are coarse. The inference becomes stronger when the region is more selective, when the process is more narrowly defined and measured, or when converging methods or independent patterns (rather than one region's mean activation) are used.

@example
title: A reward prediction error in three cases
problem: A cue predicts a reward with value $V = 0.6$ (units of reward). Compute $\delta = r - V$ when the reward is delivered ($r = 1$), when it is omitted ($r = 0$), and when an unpredicted reward arrives ($V = 0$, $r = 1$).
step: Delivered, with a partial prediction: $\delta = 1 - 0.6 = +0.4$.
step: Omitted: $\delta = 0 - 0.6 = -0.6$.
step: Unpredicted: $\delta = 1 - 0 = +1$.
step: The error is positive when the outcome is better than expected, negative when worse, and largest for fully unexpected rewards; dopamine neurons show this pattern in their firing relative to baseline.
answer: $+0.4$, $-0.6$ and $+1$.

@example
title: Posterior probability of a process given activation
problem: A region is active in 80% of fear tasks, 30% of attention tasks and 50% of memory tasks. Priors for the three processes are $0.1$, $0.6$ and $0.3$. What is $P(\text{fear} \mid \text{active})$?
step: Multiply priors by likelihoods: $0.1 \times 0.8 = 0.08$, $0.6 \times 0.3 = 0.18$, $0.3 \times 0.5 = 0.15$.
step: Sum them: $0.08 + 0.18 + 0.15 = 0.41$.
step: Divide: $P(\text{fear} \mid \text{active}) = 0.08/0.41 \approx 0.195$.
step: Although $0.8$ is the highest likelihood, attention (0.44) and memory (0.37) are each more probable than fear.
answer: About $0.20$.

@mistakes
- Treating a region as the "center" of an emotion because it responds to it: the response may be to salience, novelty or arousal in general.
- Reading $P(\text{activation} \mid \text{process})$ as $P(\text{process} \mid \text{activation})$: this ignores priors and the other processes that activate the region.
- Treating mirror neurons as having established that humans understand others through simulation: this is a hypothesis, with debated evidence.
- Forgetting the sign: a reward prediction error is negative when the outcome is worse than predicted.

model:
```python
def posterior(priors, likes):
    """Posterior over exclusive processes given an activation."""
    joint = [p * q for p, q in zip(priors, likes)]
    total = sum(joint)
    if total == 0:
        return None
    return [j / total for j in joint]
```
hint: Multiply each prior by its likelihood, add the products to get the evidence, and divide each product by it.
solution: Normalizing by the total probability of the activation turns the products into posteriors that sum to 1. The third test shows that equal likelihoods leave the priors unchanged, since the activation then carries no information, and the zero-evidence case must be handled before dividing.

model:
```python
def prospect_value(x, alpha=0.88, lam=2.25):
    """Prospect-theory value of a gain or loss relative to zero."""
    if x >= 0:
        return x ** alpha
    return -lam * (-x) ** alpha
```
hint: Handle gains and losses with separate branches; for a loss, raise the magnitude $-x$ to the power and then apply the sign and the loss aversion factor.
solution: Raising a negative number to a fractional power fails or gives a complex number, so losses are evaluated on their magnitude and then multiplied by $-\lambda$. With $\alpha = 1$ and $\lambda = 2$ a loss of 5 is worth $-10$, twice as large as the gain of 5.

@quiz self
prompt: A news article says: "When voters saw a photograph of a candidate, the insula lit up, showing that they feel disgust toward the candidate." Write a critique that identifies the inference, states the probabilities you would need, explains how the posterior could be low even if disgust reliably activates the insula, and proposes a design that would support a stronger claim.
hint: Write the Bayes' rule terms: the likelihood of activation under disgust, under other processes, and the prior.
solution: This is a reverse inference from insula activation to disgust. To judge it one needs $P(\text{insula} \mid \text{disgust})$, $P(\text{insula} \mid \text{other processes})$ and the prior probability that viewing a candidate's photograph engages disgust rather than other processes. The insula is active in many tasks (pain, interoception, attention to salient events, uncertainty, empathy), so the second likelihood is not small. Even if the insula responds in most disgust studies, the posterior for disgust can be low when the competing processes are common and have high priors, as in the amygdala example. A stronger design would include comparison conditions for these alternatives (photographs of other people, salient but positive stimuli), use independent measures (self-report, behavior or physiology) of disgust, and use multivariate patterns trained to discriminate disgust from the alternatives in independent data, with a causal test where possible.
rubric: Identifies the claim as a reverse inference from region activation to a mental state.
rubric: Names the probabilities needed (likelihoods under disgust and under other processes, and the prior) and explains how a low posterior can follow.
