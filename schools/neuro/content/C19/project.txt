@project
title: A research proposal in cognitive neuroscience
overview: You will write a six-page proposal for a study that tests one specific claim about one cognitive function (for example, that a region is necessary for a process, or that a process has a particular time course). You choose a method and defend it against the alternatives with the logic of inference from each method, plan the design and analysis, and say which alternative explanations your design rules out and which it cannot.
goal: Formulate a specific, falsifiable hypothesis about one cognitive function and state the predicted results for it and for its best rival.
goal: Choose among lesions, recordings, EEG or MEG, fMRI and brain stimulation by what each can and cannot show, and justify the choice against the alternatives.
goal: Plan an analysis with correct statistics (controls, multiple comparisons, independent data for any selection) and compute a sample size or number of trials from supplied pilot numbers, written from scratch.
step: Pick a claim and a function (for example, that a region is needed for orienting attention, that face information is available by a given latency, or that hippocampal activity at study predicts later memory because of encoding and not effort). Write the hypothesis in one sentence and the main rival in one sentence.
step: Write a methods comparison table with one row per method (lesion, single-neuron recording, EEG or MEG, fMRI, TMS) and columns for what it measures, its resolution, whether it can show necessity, and its main confounds for your claim. Choose a method, or a combination, and state why each alternative is weaker for this question.
step: Describe the design: participants or patients, conditions, the control condition (control site, control task, matched stimuli), the number of trials per condition, and what is counterbalanced. If your design uses reverse inference anywhere, state the probabilities that would make it strong.
step: Write the analysis plan: the primary contrast, the statistic, how multiple comparisons are handled, which data are used for any region or voxel selection and which for testing, and the pre-specified criterion for a positive result.
step: Using the supplied pilot data, write from scratch in plain Python (no libraries) a program that computes the paired differences, their mean and standard deviation, the paired $t$ statistic, the effect size $d_z$, and the number of participants needed for 80% power at a two-tailed $\alpha = 0.05$ with the normal approximation $n = \big((z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})/d_z\big)^2$, plus 2. Report the results and whether the pilot supports the planned sample size.
step: Write the predicted results as a table or a short description (including what pattern would count against your hypothesis), then the alternative explanations your design rules out and those it cannot, with one follow-up study for each that remains.
step: Assemble the proposal in about six pages (background and hypothesis, method choice, design, analysis, predictions, alternatives and limits), cite the sources you used by title and author, and proofread for the correct use of terms such as dissociation, necessity, and reverse inference.

@review
## Week-by-week key ideas

1. **Levels and inference.** Marr's computational, algorithmic and implementational levels; correlational versus interventional methods; converging evidence; forward versus reverse inference and $P(S \mid R) = P(R \mid S)P(S)/[P(R \mid S)P(S) + P(R \mid \neg S)(1 - P(S))]$.
2. **Lesions.** Single versus double dissociation; z scores and the Crawford–Howell $t = (x - \bar{x}_c)/(s_c\sqrt{(n+1)/n})$; limits: non-focal damage, task difficulty, compensation, diaschisis.
3. **Recording.** Tuning, selectivity index, Fano factor, PSTH (spike count divided by trials times bin width, in Hz), population vector; sampling bias, limited stimuli, correlation.
4. **EEG, ERP, MEG.** Pyramidal-cell dipoles, the inverse problem, averaging gives $\sigma/\sqrt{N}$, baseline correction, what a latency difference bounds (upper bound on discrimination, not onset of processing).
5. **fMRI.** Neurovascular coupling, BOLD, HRF (gamma, peak at $(a-1)b$), convolution and the GLM, multiple comparisons, circular analysis.
6. **TMS.** Faraday's law, motor threshold, rTMS frequencies, virtual lesion and chronometric logic, control site, sham, control task, safety.
7. **Attention.** Cueing benefit, cost and validity effect; dorsal and ventral networks; neglect; biased competition; alpha lateralization.
8. **Ventral stream.** Face selectivity index $(F - O)/(F + O)$, localizers, expertise versus face selectivity, MVPA and RSA ($1 - r$), causal evidence.
9. **Memory.** Declarative and nondeclarative systems, H.M., dissociations, $d'$ and $c$, savings, consolidation accounts, subsequent memory effect.
10. **Executive function.** Inhibition, shifting, updating; Stroop and switch costs; perseveration; prefrontal delay activity and the storage-versus-control debate; supervisory and biasing models.
11. **Language and hemispheres.** Aphasias, dual-stream model, lateralization index $(L - R)/(L + R)$, split-brain logic and interpretations, crossed-uncrossed difference.
12. **Emotion, social cognition, decisions.** Amygdala debates, theory of mind network, reward prediction error $\delta = r + \gamma V' - V$, prospect-theory value, multi-process reverse inference.

## Common errors

Treating activation as necessity; reading a single dissociation as proof of separate processes; taking a peak latency as an onset; equating BOLD with spikes; skipping multiple-comparison correction or selecting voxels circularly; calling TMS a clean lesion; confusing $d'$ with criterion; treating neglect as blindness; calling the PFC a single executive; over-reading the split brain; reading $P(R \mid S)$ as $P(S \mid R)$.

@exam num
prompt: An ERP study has a single-trial noise standard deviation of 18 µV and a component with an amplitude of 3 µV. If 144 trials are averaged per condition, what is the signal-to-noise ratio (amplitude divided by the noise standard deviation of the average)?
answer: 2
check: 3 / (18 / sqrt(144))
hint: First compute the noise in the average, $\sigma/\sqrt{N}$, then divide the amplitude by it.
solution: The noise in the average is $18/\sqrt{144} = 18/12 = 1.5$ µV, so the ratio is $3/1.5 = 2$. Averaging has improved the ratio from $3/18 \approx 0.17$ by a factor of $\sqrt{144} = 12$.

@exam ms
prompt: A study claims that region X is necessary for face discrimination but not for object discrimination. Which combined results would give the strongest evidence for this claim?
choice*: TMS over X slows face discrimination but not object discrimination, and TMS over a control site slows neither.
choice*: Patients with lesions that include X are impaired at faces and normal at objects, and patients with damage elsewhere show the opposite pattern.
choice: X responds more to faces than objects in fMRI, which by itself shows that X is necessary for faces.
choice: X is active in the face task according to BOLD, whatever happens in the object task.
choice: The ERP over X is larger in the face task, with no control task.
hint: Look for evidence from interventions with controls, and for evidence of a dissociation.
solution: The strongest evidence combines interventions with controls (stimulation with a control site, and lesions with opposite patterns, forming a double dissociation). Activation alone, even when selective, or an ERP without a control task, is correlational.

@exam self
prompt: A researcher reports that a frontal region is more active in an fMRI study when participants resolve conflict in a Stroop task, and concludes that the region "performs conflict monitoring." Using ideas from several weeks of the course, write a critique that covers the inference from the method, a reverse inference, the multiple comparisons and selection issues, and a study that would give stronger evidence.
hint: Go through the course's list: what fMRI can show, selectivity and Bayes' rule, correction and independent data, then a causal or converging method.
solution: fMRI is correlational, so activity during conflict does not show that the region is needed, and BOLD is a slow, indirect measure of synaptic activity. Concluding that the region "performs conflict monitoring" is a reverse inference, whose strength depends on how selective the region is: frontal regions are active in many demanding tasks (the multiple-demand pattern), so the likelihood of activation without conflict monitoring is high and the posterior for the process is modest. The study should show that voxels were selected with data independent of those used to estimate the effect and that the result survives correction for whole-brain multiple comparisons, since selecting voxels in the same data inflates effects and uncorrected thresholds yield false positives. Stronger evidence would come from patients or stimulation (TMS at the site with a control site and control task) showing that disruption specifically increases interference effects, and from EEG or MEG for timing, together with other conflict tasks and non-conflict control tasks of matched difficulty.
rubric: Notes that fMRI activity is correlational and does not show necessity.
rubric: Identifies the reverse inference and explains the dependence on selectivity and the multiple-demand pattern.
rubric: Raises multiple comparisons and circular selection.
rubric: Proposes a stronger design with a causal or converging method and controls.
