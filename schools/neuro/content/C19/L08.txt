@article
Within a fraction of a second you can tell a face from a chair, and a friend from a stranger, whatever the viewpoint, size or lighting. The retinal image of the same face varies enormously, yet recognition stays stable. How does the brain do it, and is there tissue just for faces?

### The ventral stream

Visual information flows from the retina through the thalamus to primary visual cortex (V1), and then along two partly separate pathways (Ungerleider and Mishkin, 1982). The **ventral stream** runs from V1 through V2 and V4 to the inferior temporal (IT) cortex and is concerned with object identity, "what"; the **dorsal stream** runs to the parietal cortex and supports spatial and action-related vision. Along the ventral stream, receptive fields grow, from edge detectors in V1 (Hubel and Wiesel, 1959 and 1962) to cells in IT that prefer complex shapes and respond across changes of position and size (tolerance, or invariance). Deep networks trained to recognize objects have been used as models of this hierarchy (Yamins and colleagues, 2014).

### Face cells and the fusiform face area

Neurons in the macaque temporal cortex that respond best to faces were reported in the 1970s and 1980s (Gross and colleagues, 1972; Desimone and colleagues, 1984), and fMRI later showed several face-selective patches in the monkey's temporal lobe, with cells inside them strongly face-selective (Tsao and colleagues, 2006). In humans, Kanwisher, McDermott and Chun (1997) found in each of most participants a region in the right mid-fusiform gyrus, the **fusiform face area (FFA)**, that responded more to faces than to objects, houses and other categories, using a **localizer**: a task defining the region in each person for later tests. Neighboring regions are selective for places (the parahippocampal place area; Epstein and Kanwisher, 1998) and bodies.

Selectivity is quantified, for percent signal changes $F$ to faces and $O$ to non-face objects, by the **face selectivity index**

$$\text{FSI} = \frac{F - O}{F + O}, $$

defined when $F + O > 0$. It is 0 for equal responses and approaches 1 when the region responds only to faces; an FSI of $1/3$ means $F = 2O$, and in general $F/O = (1 + \text{FSI})/(1 - \text{FSI})$. A response greater for faces than for objects is **selectivity**, not exclusivity: the region still responds to other things.

### Debates

- **Face-specificity or expertise?** Gauthier and colleagues (1999 and 2000) reported that the response of the FFA grew with expertise: bird and car experts showed larger responses to their category, and people trained to recognize novel objects (greebles) responded more. The expertise view holds that the area supports fine, within-category discrimination in which we are experts, with faces the standard case. Critics note that the face preference persists with matched experience, appears early in development, and that monkeys raised without seeing faces did not develop typical face patches (Arcaro and colleagues, 2017), suggesting experience shapes selectivity. Both experience and a predisposition may contribute; the question is open.
- **Region or pattern?** Haxby and colleagues (2001) showed that **multivariate pattern analysis (MVPA)** can classify the category viewed from the pattern across ventral temporal voxels, even after removing the most selective voxels, arguing that information is distributed. Others reply that selective regions carry the main category signal and that patterns do not rule out modules. **Representational similarity analysis (RSA)** (Kriegeskorte, Mur and Bandettini, 2008) summarizes patterns by a dissimilarity matrix, with entries $1 - r$, where $r$ is the Pearson correlation between the activity patterns for two stimuli.
- **Necessity.** Patients with acquired **prosopagnosia** have lost face recognition with relatively preserved object recognition, typically after right occipitotemporal damage, and cases in the opposite direction (object agnosia with spared faces) have been reported, a possible double dissociation, though the expertise view argues that faces are the hardest within-category task. In one epilepsy patient, electrical stimulation of face-selective sites in the fusiform gyrus distorted perceived faces (Parvizi and colleagues, 2012); TMS over occipital face-selective cortex impairs face discrimination. These causal results are limited in number.
- **Timing.** Face-sensitive ERP components such as the N170 begin around 130 to 170 ms (Lesson 4).

Established: category-selective regions exist and replicate across people and species. Debated: whether selectivity reflects specialization for faces or expertise, and how distributed the code is.

```python
def pearson(u, v):
    """Pearson correlation of two equal-length lists."""
    n = len(u)
    mu, mv = sum(u) / n, sum(v) / n
    suv = sum((a - mu) * (b - mv) for a, b in zip(u, v))
    suu = sum((a - mu) ** 2 for a in u)
    svv = sum((b - mv) ** 2 for b in v)
    return suv / (suu * svv) ** 0.5
```

title: Selectivity index of a voxel
problem: A voxel responds with 2.0% signal change to faces and 1.0% to objects. Compute its face selectivity index and say how many times larger the face response is.
step: $\text{FSI} = (2.0 - 1.0)/(2.0 + 1.0) = 1/3 \approx 0.33$.
step: The ratio follows from $F/O = (1 + \text{FSI})/(1 - \text{FSI}) = (4/3)/(2/3) = 2$.
step: Interpretation: the voxel prefers faces by a factor of two, but responds to objects too, so it is selective and not exclusive.
answer: FSI $\approx 0.33$; the face response is twice the object response.

@example
title: A dissimilarity between two patterns
problem: The activity patterns over four voxels are $[1, 2, 3, 4]$ for stimulus 1 and $[2, 1, 4, 3]$ for stimulus 2. What is the dissimilarity $1 - r$?
step: The means are both $2.5$, so the centered patterns are $(-1.5, -0.5, 0.5, 1.5)$ and $(-0.5, -1.5, 1.5, 0.5)$.
step: The sum of products is $0.75 + 0.75 + 0.75 + 0.75 = 3$; each sum of squares is $5$.
step: $r = 3/5 = 0.6$, so the dissimilarity is $1 - 0.6 = 0.4$.
answer: $0.4$.

@mistakes
- Reading "selective" as "exclusive": a face-selective region still responds to non-face stimuli.
- Concluding from activation alone that a region is needed for face recognition: necessity needs lesion or stimulation evidence.
- Taking one data set as settling face-specificity versus expertise: both views predict some of the results, and the evidence is mixed.
- Computing a pattern dissimilarity without centering each pattern: the correlation subtracts the pattern's mean.

```python
def rdm(patterns):
    """Matrix of 1 - Pearson r between all pairs of patterns."""
    def corr(u, v):
        n = len(u)
        mu, mv = sum(u) / n, sum(v) / n
        suv = sum((a - mu) * (b - mv) for a, b in zip(u, v))
        suu = sum((a - mu) ** 2 for a in u)
        svv = sum((b - mv) ** 2 for b in v)
        return suv / (suu * svv) ** 0.5

    n = len(patterns)
    out = [[0.0] * n for _ in range(n)]
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(i + 1, n):
            d = 1 - corr(patterns[i], patterns[j])
            out[i][j] = out[j][i] = d
    return out
```
hint: Loop over pairs, compute the correlation of each pair after centering, and fill both `[i][j]` and `[j][i]`.
solution: Scaling a pattern does not change its correlation, so the first and third patterns in the third test have dissimilarity 2 (a perfect negative correlation) even though the third has larger values and a different mean. Setting the diagonal to exactly 0.0 and filling the matrix symmetrically gives the required output.

```python
def face_selective(voxels, thr):
    """Indices of voxels whose face selectivity index exceeds thr."""
    out = []
    for i, (face, obj) in enumerate(voxels):
        total = face + obj
        if total > 0 and (face - obj) / total > thr:
            out.append(i)
    return out
```
hint: Compute the sum first and skip voxels where it is not positive; then compare the index with a strict inequality.
solution: The sum condition avoids dividing by zero or by a negative number: the voxel $(1, -1)$ has sum 0, and $(-3, -1)$ has a negative sum, for which the formula would give a misleading positive value of $0.5$. The index $0.5$ in the second test is not strictly greater than the threshold.

@quiz self
prompt: A supplied study reports that a region in the right fusiform gyrus responds more strongly to photographs of faces than to photographs of houses, and concludes that it is "a face-specific module." Evaluate the evidence: say what it supports, list the controls and additional evidence you would require, and name one alternative account.
hint: Consider the comparison categories, the form of the selectivity, causal evidence, and the expertise account.
solution: The result supports selectivity: the region responds more to faces than to houses. To support "face-specific" one needs more comparison categories (hands, bodies, animals, cars, objects matched for low-level features such as contrast and spatial frequency, scrambled images), a measure of selectivity such as an index that shows how much more than the other categories, replication in independent data and participants with a localizer, and checks that the effect is not due to attention or to low-level confounds. To show involvement one needs causal evidence, from lesion cases or stimulation, and behavioral links such as face recognition ability. An alternative account is the expertise hypothesis, that the region supports fine discrimination in which we are experts, so training or expertise with other categories should raise its response; another is a distributed code across ventral temporal cortex. A "module" would also require that the response is highly selective and not just preferential, which the single contrast cannot show.
