### From voltage to spikes to codes

An extracellular electrode records voltage changes near cells. **Spike sorting** separates the waveforms of different neurons, so that each **unit** has a list of spike times. For one stimulus repeated over many trials, the unit gives a list of spike times per trial. Standard summaries:

- **Firing rate** $r$: spike count divided by the counting window, in Hz (spikes per second). A count of 12 in 300 ms is $12/0.3 = 40$ Hz.
- **Tuning curve**: mean rate as a function of a stimulus parameter (orientation, direction, identity). Many cells in motor cortex of the monkey have broad, roughly cosine-shaped tuning for movement direction: $r(\theta) = r_0 + a\cos(\theta - \theta_{\text{pref}})$, with preferred direction $\theta_{\text{pref}}$ (Georgopoulos and colleagues, 1982).
- **Selectivity index**: with preferred-stimulus rate $r_p$ and mean rate $\bar r_o$ over the other stimuli, $\text{SI} = (r_p - \bar r_o)/(r_p + \bar r_o)$, which is 0 for no preference and approaches 1 for a cell firing only to one stimulus. It depends on which stimuli were tested.
- **Fano factor**: variance of the spike count across repeats divided by its mean. A Poisson process has Fano factor 1; many cortical neurons are near or above 1 for counts over a few hundred milliseconds.

The **peristimulus time histogram (PSTH)** averages over trials: divide time into bins, count spikes in each bin across trials, and divide by (number of trials $\times$ bin width in seconds).

```python
def psth(trials, t_max, bin_ms):
    """Rate in Hz for each bin; trials are lists of spike times in ms."""
    n_bins = int(t_max // bin_ms)
    counts = [0] * n_bins
    for spikes in trials:
        for t in spikes:
            if 0 <= t < n_bins * bin_ms:
                counts[int(t // bin_ms)] += 1
    return [c / (len(trials) * bin_ms / 1000) for c in counts]


print(psth([[12, 15, 55], [14, 60], [11, 16, 18]], 100, 50))
```

This prints `[40.0, 13.333333333333334]`: six spikes in 0 to 50 ms over three trials is $6/(3 \times 0.05) = 40$ Hz, and two in 50 to 100 ms is 13.3 Hz.

### Populations

Single neurons are noisy and broadly tuned, but a population can carry a precise signal. In the **population vector** method (Georgopoulos, Schwartz and Kettner, 1986), each neuron votes for its preferred direction, weighted by its rate; the decoded direction is that of the vector sum $\sum_i r_i (\cos\theta_i, \sin\theta_i)$. Decoding from many neurons is an example of **multivariate** analysis; modern recordings with silicon probes or imaging of calcium indicators give hundreds to thousands of simultaneous cells. In people, rare recordings in the medial temporal lobe of patients being evaluated for epilepsy surgery found cells that respond to a particular individual, whether shown as photographs or as the written name (Quiroga and colleagues, 2005), often called concept cells.

### What recordings can and cannot show

- Spiking is the output of a neuron, so recording gives the best spatial and temporal resolution of any method: single cells, millisecond precision.
- **Sampling bias**: electrodes favor large, active cells, and experimenters often keep only responsive cells; the fraction of neurons with a property is therefore hard to estimate.
- **Limited stimuli**: a cell that responds best to one of 50 tested images may respond better to an untested one. Selectivity is relative to the set.
- **Correlation**: a neuron that fires with a percept or a decision need not be necessary for it; causation needs a perturbation.
- **Decoding above chance** shows that information is present in the activity, not that the brain reads it out that way.

Well-established: tuning, selectivity and the value of population codes. Debated: how much of the code is carried by single cells compared with distributed patterns.

@example
title: Selectivity and variability of one neuron
problem: A neuron fires at 40 Hz to its preferred picture and at 10, 6, 8 and 4 Hz to four others. Over five repeats of one picture it fires 8, 12, 10, 9 and 11 spikes in a window. Compute the selectivity index and the Fano factor (using the sample variance).
step: Mean of the other rates: $(10 + 6 + 8 + 4)/4 = 7$ Hz.
step: $\text{SI} = (40 - 7)/(40 + 7) = 33/47 \approx 0.70$.
step: Mean count: $(8 + 12 + 10 + 9 + 11)/5 = 10$. Squared deviations: $4, 4, 0, 1, 1$, so the sample variance is $10/4 = 2.5$.
step: Fano factor $= 2.5/10 = 0.25$, well below the Poisson value of 1, so this (invented) neuron is more regular than a Poisson neuron.
answer: $\text{SI} \approx 0.70$; Fano factor $0.25$.

@mistakes
- Treating a selectivity index as a property of the neuron alone: it depends on the stimuli tested.
- Dividing a spike count by the window in milliseconds and calling it hertz: convert the window to seconds, or multiply by 1000.
- Concluding that a neuron that fires with a percept causes it: recording is correlational.
- Forgetting to wrap angles: a decoded direction of $-90°$ and $270°$ are the same direction, but a decoder should report one convention.

model:
```python
import math


def decode(prefs, rates):
    """Population-vector direction (degrees in [0, 360)) or None."""
    if all(r == 0 for r in rates):
        return None
    x = sum(r * math.cos(math.radians(p)) for p, r in zip(prefs, rates))
    y = sum(r * math.sin(math.radians(p)) for p, r in zip(prefs, rates))
    return math.degrees(math.atan2(y, x)) % 360
```
hint: Use `math.atan2(y, x)` for the angle and `% 360` to wrap negative angles; convert degrees to radians before `cos` and `sin`.
solution: Each neuron adds a vector of length equal to its rate pointing along its preferred direction. `atan2` returns an angle between $-180°$ and $180°$, so the modulo maps it into the range from 0 to 360 (the fourth test needs it), and all-zero rates give no direction.
