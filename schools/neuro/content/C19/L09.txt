@article
After the surgery described by Scoville and Milner (1957), patient H.M. could repeat seven digits and talk sensibly, but a few minutes after meeting a person he had no memory of it. Yet over several days of practice he improved at tracing a shape seen in a mirror, though each day he said he had never done the task. One person, then, held several kinds of memory that behave differently. What are they, and which brain structures support each?

### A taxonomy of memory

Squire and others proposed a division, now widely used, between two broad classes.

- **Declarative (explicit) memory**, what can be consciously recalled, includes **episodic memory** of events in their time and place and **semantic memory** of facts (Tulving, 1972). It depends on the **medial temporal lobe**: the hippocampus and the surrounding entorhinal, perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices.
- **Nondeclarative (implicit) memory** shows up in performance and not in recollection: **skills and habits** (basal ganglia and cerebellum), **priming** (neocortical areas that process the stimulus), and **conditioning** (amygdala, cerebellum).

The evidence is a set of dissociations. Amnesic patients with medial temporal damage have impaired declarative memory and relatively intact skill learning and priming. In a probabilistic classification task (the weather prediction task), patients with Parkinson's disease, who have basal ganglia dysfunction, were impaired at learning across trials while amnesic patients learned at a similar rate early on, but amnesic patients remembered less about the training episodes (Knowlton, Mangels and Squire, 1996). Together these suggest a double dissociation between declarative and habit memory systems, with the usual caveats about patient groups, task impurity and the fact that normal people probably use both systems at once.

### What the hippocampus does, and the debates

H.M.'s bilateral medial temporal removal produced profound **anterograde amnesia** (inability to form new episodic memories), preserved short-term memory and skills, and some **retrograde amnesia** for the years just before surgery. Several accounts of the hippocampus' role exist: it binds the elements of an event, including items and context, into one memory; it supports spatial maps (place cells; O'Keefe and Dostrovsky, 1971); and it supports recollection of detailed episodes.

Two debates matter here.

- **Consolidation.** The **standard (systems) consolidation** account says that the hippocampus is needed to store a new memory for a limited time, after which the neocortex holds it independently; it predicts a temporal gradient in which recent memories are lost and remote ones spared. The **multiple trace** theory (Nadel and Moscovitch, 1997) holds that detailed episodic memories always depend on the hippocampus, while semantic or gist-like memories may become independent. Patients' retrograde gradients vary across studies, and the question is unsettled.
- **Episodic versus semantic.** Children with early bilateral hippocampal damage (Vargha-Khadem and colleagues, 1997) had poor episodic memory in daily life but attended ordinary schools and acquired language and factual knowledge, which some take to show semantic memory can develop without a normal hippocampus. Others argue that the damage is incomplete or that the cortical regions around the hippocampus compensate.
- **Recollection and familiarity.** Dual-process accounts say recognition depends on two processes, recollection of details and a feeling of familiarity, perhaps supported by the hippocampus and perirhinal cortex respectively; single-process signal detection accounts propose one strength dimension. Both have evidence.

### Imaging encoding

In the **subsequent memory paradigm**, fMRI during study is sorted by whether each item is later remembered; regions more active for later-remembered items (parahippocampal cortex, hippocampus and left inferior prefrontal cortex) support encoding (Wagner and colleagues, 1998; Brewer and colleagues, 1998). The effect is correlational: attention or effort at study could raise both the activity and later memory.

### Measuring memory

Recognition sensitivity, $d' = z(H) - z(F)$, uses the hit rate $H$ and false alarm rate $F$ with the inverse normal $z$. Hits of $0.84$ and false alarms of $0.16$ give $z(0.84) \approx 0.994$ and $z(0.16) \approx -0.994$, so $d' \approx 1.99$. The criterion is $c = -(z(H) + z(F))/2$, here 0. **Savings** (Ebbinghaus, 1885) compares trials to learn a list originally and later: $\text{savings} = (n_{\text{orig}} - n_{\text{re}})/n_{\text{orig}}$.

@example
title: Sensitivity of controls and a patient
problem: A control group has a hit rate of $0.84$ and a false alarm rate of $0.16$ in recognition. An amnesic patient has hits $0.69$ and false alarms $0.31$. Use $z(0.84) = 0.994$, $z(0.69) = 0.496$. Compare their sensitivities.
step: By symmetry $z(0.16) = -z(0.84) = -0.994$ and $z(0.31) = -z(0.69) = -0.496$.
step: Control: $d' = 0.994 - (-0.994) = 1.99$.
step: Patient: $d' = 0.496 - (-0.496) = 0.99$.
step: The patient's sensitivity is about half the control's, though the criterion $c = 0$ is the same: the patient is less able to discriminate old from new, not just more cautious.
answer: $d' \approx 1.99$ for the controls and $0.99$ for the patient.

@example
title: A savings score
problem: A list took 20 repetitions to learn; a week later it took 8 to relearn. Compute the savings.
step: Subtract the relearning trials from the original: $20 - 8 = 12$ trials saved.
step: Divide by the original: $12/20 = 0.60$.
step: Savings is 60% although the person could not recall the list at the start of relearning, an implicit trace.
answer: 60%.

@mistakes
- Calling all amnesic patients' memory loss total: skills, priming and short-term memory are largely spared in classic amnesia.
- Treating the subsequent memory effect as proof that the hippocampus is necessary: it is correlational.
- Mixing up $d'$ and the criterion: $d'$ is sensitivity (the difference of the $z$ scores) and $c$ is bias (their negative average).
- Describing consolidation as settled: the standard and multiple trace accounts predict different things, and the data are mixed.
