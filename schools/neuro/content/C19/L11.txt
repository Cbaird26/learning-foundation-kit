@article
In 1861 Paul Broca described a patient (Leborgne) who understood speech but could say almost only one syllable; after the patient's death, the lesion was in the left inferior frontal region. In 1874 Carl Wernicke described patients with fluent but meaningless speech and poor comprehension, with damage further back in the left temporal lobe. Both lesions were in the **left** hemisphere. Since then, language has been the best-known case of **hemispheric specialization**.

### The language network and the aphasias

**Aphasia** is an acquired language disorder after brain damage, not due to weakness of the speech muscles or to general intellectual loss. Classic types:

| type | speech | comprehension | repetition | typical lesion |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Broca's | nonfluent, effortful, agrammatic | relatively good for simple sentences | impaired | left inferior frontal region and surrounding tissue |
| Wernicke's | fluent, with wrong or invented words | impaired | impaired | left posterior superior temporal region |
| conduction | fluent | good | impaired | often the arcuate fasciculus or nearby tissue |

The classic **Wernicke–Geschwind** model joined the two areas by the arcuate fasciculus. It remains a useful map but is incomplete. A lesion confined to Broca's area usually causes milder, transient problems; lasting Broca's aphasia typically involves wider damage including insula and white matter. The **dual-stream** model (Hickok and Poeppel, 2007) proposes a ventral temporal stream that maps sound to meaning and a dorsal temporal-parietal-frontal stream that maps sound to articulation. Event-related potentials separate stages: the N400 (Kutas and Hillyard, 1980) is larger for words that do not fit their sentence context, showing sensitivity to meaning. The role of the left inferior frontal gyrus in syntax or in selecting among competing meanings is debated, and since that region is active in many tasks, a reverse inference from its activation to "syntax" is weak.

### Lateralization

In about 90% of people the right hand is preferred, and language is represented mainly in the left hemisphere in the large majority of right-handers (about 95% in common estimates) and in a majority, roughly 70%, of left-handers; the rest have bilateral or right-hemisphere dominance. Dominance has been assessed with the **Wada test**, in which one hemisphere is briefly anesthetized before epilepsy surgery, and now often with fMRI, summarized by a **lateralization index**, $\text{LI} = (L - R)/(L + R)$, with $L$ and $R$ the number of active voxels (or the activation) in each hemisphere. $\text{LI} = +1$ is entirely left and $-1$ entirely right; values above about $+0.2$ are commonly called left-lateralized (a convention, with threshold-dependence problems). With 120 left and 40 right voxels, $\text{LI} = 80/160 = 0.5$. The right hemisphere contributes to prosody, discourse and some other aspects of language comprehension; "left-brain language" does not mean the right hemisphere is silent.

### The split brain

The two hemispheres are connected by the **corpus callosum**, of the order of 200 million axons. In some patients with severe epilepsy, surgeons cut it. Sperry and Gazzaniga studied such patients from the 1960s. The method uses the anatomy of vision: with the eyes fixed on a central point, a stimulus in the right visual field goes to the left hemisphere, and one in the left field to the right hemisphere. If it is flashed for under about 200 ms, there is no time for eye movement to bring it to both.

- A word flashed to the right field is read aloud. A word flashed to the left field is not: the patient may say nothing was seen, but the left hand (controlled mainly by the right hemisphere) can pick out the named object from a hidden set by touch.
- The left hemisphere's speech system may invent an explanation for an action initiated by the right, which has been called the **interpreter**.

In healthy people the same logic appears in the **crossed-uncrossed difference** (CUD): respond with one hand to a flash in either field; responses are a few milliseconds faster when the hand and the field are on the same side (uncrossed, no callosal crossing needed), and the CUD, the crossed minus uncrossed mean RT, is used to estimate callosal transfer time.

### Interpretations that are debated

The original reading is that the disconnection produced two separate minds or streams of awareness, one of which speaks. Other interpretations hold that perception remains largely unified, with many processes lower in the system, and the limited tasks and the response channel reveal a break in communication mainly at the level of cortical report; some careful later testing finds that patients can respond to stimuli in either field in some ways, and others question that. Cross-cueing and subcortical routes can transfer some information. Evidence limits: few patients, who had long-standing epilepsy and sometimes early brain damage, so the extension to healthy brains is uncertain. The popular idea that people are "left-brained" or "right-brained" is not supported: in intact brains the hemispheres exchange information constantly.

@example
title: Lateralization index and crossed-uncrossed difference
problem: A language task activates 150 voxels in the left hemisphere and 50 in the right. In a Poffenberger task, mean RTs are 248 ms for crossed and 244 ms for uncrossed responses. Compute the LI and the CUD.
step: $\text{LI} = (150 - 50)/(150 + 50) = 100/200 = 0.5$.
step: This is above the conventional $0.2$, so the task is left-lateralized by the convention.
step: $\text{CUD} = 248 - 244 = 4$ ms.
step: The positive CUD means the crossed condition is slower, consistent with a callosal transfer time of a few ms.
answer: $\text{LI} = 0.5$ (left-lateralized); CUD $= 4$ ms.

@mistakes
- Equating Broca's area with speech production and Wernicke's area with comprehension and nothing else: the classic model is a first approximation, and the networks are wider.
- Concluding that the right hemisphere is irrelevant to language: it contributes to prosody and discourse.
- Taking split-brain results as proof that healthy people use two independent minds: the patients are few, their brains atypical, and interpretations differ.
- Flipping the sign of the LI: a positive value means left dominance when $L$ is in the numerator.

@practice self
prompt: Present the strongest case for each of two interpretations of the split-brain results (two separate streams of awareness versus a unified perception with a disconnected report), name the evidence each cites, and say what further evidence would help to decide between them.
hint: Use the dissociation between speech and hand responses for one view, and the evidence of unified performance and alternative routes for the other.
solution: The two-streams view argues that stimuli in the left field produce coherent, goal-directed behavior by the left hand (matching, choosing and sometimes acting against the speaking hemisphere's intent), without being available to speech; the knowledge appears to be held in one hemisphere separately from the other, as if two systems. The unified-perception view argues that patients behave as integrated people in daily life, that tests of simple responses sometimes show that either field's stimuli can be reported by either response channel, that attention and arousal are shared by subcortical routes, and that the apparent split arises because the speech system, mainly in the left hemisphere, cannot read out what the right hemisphere sees, a limit on report rather than on perception. Evidence that would help includes tests with many response channels and careful control of cross-cueing, tests in more patients including those with different surgical extents, and comparison with healthy people's behavior under tasks that load the callosum.
