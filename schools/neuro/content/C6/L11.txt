@article
Constants used here: $hc = 1240\ \mathrm{eV\,nm}$, $1\ \mathrm{eV} = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}$, hydrogen ground-state energy $-13.6\ \mathrm{eV}$, $1\ \mathrm{u} = 931.5\ \mathrm{MeV}/c^2$, proton mass $1.007276\ \mathrm{u}$, neutron mass $1.008665\ \mathrm{u}$.

### Quantized atoms

Atoms emit and absorb light only at discrete frequencies. In the **Bohr model** of hydrogen the electron's allowed energies are

$$E_n = -\frac{13.6\ \mathrm{eV}}{n^2}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3,\dots$$

A transition from level $n_i$ down to $n_f$ emits a photon of energy equal to the difference, $E_\gamma = E_{n_i} - E_{n_f} = 13.6\left(\frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2}\right)\ \mathrm{eV}$, and wavelength $\lambda = hc/E_\gamma$. The Bohr model works for hydrogen-like atoms; full quantum mechanics describes electron clouds (orbitals). Characteristic X-rays arise from transitions of inner electrons in heavy atoms, which is why X-ray tubes with tungsten targets emit lines on top of a continuous spectrum.

### The nucleus

A nucleus of element $X$ with $Z$ protons and $N$ neutrons has mass number $A = Z + N$, written ${}^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X}$. **Isotopes** share $Z$ but differ in $N$. The strong force binds nucleons. The nucleus has less mass than its separated parts; this **mass defect** $\Delta m$ corresponds to the **binding energy**

$$E_B = \Delta m\,c^2 = \Delta m(\mathrm{u}) \times 931.5\ \mathrm{MeV}.$$

### Radioactive decay

Unstable nuclei change spontaneously. In every decay, charge and mass number are conserved.

- **Alpha decay**: emits a helium nucleus ${}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}$; $Z$ falls by 2 and $A$ by 4. Alpha particles are heavily ionizing but stopped by paper or skin; dangerous mainly when the source is inside the body.
- **Beta-minus decay**: a neutron becomes a proton, emitting an electron and an antineutrino; $Z$ rises by 1, $A$ unchanged. **Beta-plus** (positron) decay does the reverse; the positron annihilates with an electron to produce two $511\ \mathrm{keV}$ photons, the basis of PET imaging.
- **Gamma decay**: a nucleus in an excited state emits a high-energy photon with no change in $Z$ or $A$. Technetium-99m emits a $140\ \mathrm{keV}$ gamma photon and is the workhorse of nuclear medicine.

### Half-life and activity

A large sample of identical nuclei decays randomly, but the number remaining follows

$$N(t) = N_0\left(\tfrac12\right)^{t/T_{1/2}} = N_0\,e^{-\lambda t}, \qquad \lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{T_{1/2}}.$$

The **activity** is the number of decays per second, $A = \lambda N$, in becquerels ($1\ \mathrm{Bq} = 1$ decay/s). The older unit is the curie. Activity decays with the same half-life as the number of nuclei. Medical isotopes are chosen with half-lives of hours to days: long enough to prepare and image, short enough to limit the patient's dose. The *biological* half-life (elimination from the body) combines with the physical one to give an effective half-life shorter than either.

@example
title: Fraction of technetium-99m remaining
problem: Technetium-99m has half-life $6.0\ \mathrm{h}$. What fraction of the original activity remains after $15\ \mathrm{h}$?
step: The number of half-lives elapsed is $t/T_{1/2} = 15/6.0 = 2.5$.
step: The fraction remaining is $(1/2)^{2.5} = 1/(2^{2}\cdot\sqrt2) = 1/5.657$.
step: Fraction $\approx 0.177$, so about $18\%$ of the original activity remains. Activity and number of nuclei fall by the same factor.

@mistakes
- Thinking that after two half-lives everything has decayed; half remains after one half-life, a quarter after two.
- Writing the daughter nucleus without checking that both $A$ and $Z$ balance.
- Mixing up the sign of energies in Bohr levels: bound levels are negative and the emitted photon energy is the positive difference.
- Confusing activity (decays per second) with absorbed dose (energy per kilogram).

@practice num
prompt: Using nuclear masses $m_p = 1.007276\ \mathrm{u}$, $m_n = 1.008665\ \mathrm{u}$ and a helium-4 nuclear mass of $4.001506\ \mathrm{u}$, and $1\ \mathrm{u} = 931.5\ \mathrm{MeV}/c^2$, find the binding energy of helium-4 in MeV.
answer: 28.3
tol: 1%
unit: MeV
check: (2*1.007276 + 2*1.008665 - 4.001506) * 931.5
hint: Helium-4 has 2 protons and 2 neutrons. Compute the mass defect in u.
solution: $\Delta m = 2(1.007276) + 2(1.008665) - 4.001506 = 0.030376\ \mathrm{u}$, so $E_B = 0.030376 \times 931.5 \approx 28.3\ \mathrm{MeV}$.

hint: Which particle transformation raises $Z$ without changing $A$?

@quiz num
prompt: A sample contains $1.0\times10^{12}$ nuclei of iodine-131, whose half-life is $8.02$ days. Find its activity in becquerels (decays per second), using $A = \lambda N$ with $\lambda = \ln 2/T_{1/2}$.
answer: 1.0003e6
tol: 1%
unit: Bq
check: math.log(2) / (8.02 * 24 * 3600) * 1.0e12
hint: Convert the half-life to seconds before finding $\lambda$.
solution: $T_{1/2} = 8.02 \times 86400 = 6.93\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{s}$, so $\lambda = 0.693/6.93\times10^{5} = 1.00\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $A = \lambda N \approx 1.0\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{Bq}$.
