@article
Constants used here: at body temperature ($37\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$) the quantity $RT/F \approx 26.7\ \mathrm{mV}$, where $R$ is the gas constant, $T$ the absolute temperature and $F$ the Faraday constant. Concentrations are in mmol/L. Sign convention: membrane potential $V_m$ is the inside potential minus the outside potential; outward positive current is a flow of positive charge from inside to outside.

### Where the resting potential comes from

Cells keep different ion concentrations inside and outside: high $\mathrm{K^+}$ inside (about $140\ \mathrm{mM}$ versus $5\ \mathrm{mM}$ outside), high $\mathrm{Na^+}$ and $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ outside. The membrane has ion channels, and at rest it is much more permeable to $\mathrm{K^+}$ than to other ions. Potassium leaves down its concentration gradient, leaving the inside negative; the growing electric field opposes further exit. The balance point is the **Nernst potential** (equilibrium potential) of the ion:

$$E_X = \frac{26.7\ \mathrm{mV}}{z}\,\ln\frac{[X]_{out}}{[X]_{in}},$$

where $z$ is the charge number of the ion ($+1$ for $\mathrm{K^+}$, $+2$ for $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$, $-1$ for $\mathrm{Cl^-}$). With $\mathrm{K^+}$ at $5$ outside and $140$ inside, $E_K \approx -89\ \mathrm{mV}$. The actual resting potential, near $-70\ \mathrm{mV}$, is a weighted average of the equilibrium potentials of the permeant ions. The $\mathrm{Na^+/K^+}$ pump uses ATP to maintain the gradients over the long term.

### Ion current and the circuit model

For each ion, the net current is driven by the difference between the membrane potential and that ion's Nernst potential: $I_X = g_X\,(V_m - E_X)$, where $g_X$ is the conductance. With the convention above, a positive $I$ is outward positive current. The Nernst potential acts as a battery in series with the conductance. The lipid bilayer in parallel acts as a capacitor $C_m$. The simplest model of a patch is therefore a resistor $R_m$ in parallel with a capacitor $C_m$.

### Time constant

A capacitor discharging through a resistor decays exponentially: $V(t) = V_0\,e^{-t/\tau}$ with

$$\tau = R_m C_m.$$

After one time constant the voltage has fallen to about $37\%$ of its starting value. Membranes with a large $\tau$ respond slowly; this limits how rapidly voltage changes can be followed.

### Conduction along an axon

An axon is a long cable: a core of conducting axoplasm with resistance per unit length $r_i$, surrounded by a leaky, capacitive membrane. A local depolarization spreads passively along the axon but decays with distance as $e^{-x/\lambda}$, where the **length constant** is

$$\lambda = \sqrt{\frac{r_m}{r_i}},$$

with $r_m$ the membrane resistance times unit length ($\Omega\,\mathrm{m}$) and $r_i$ the axoplasm resistance per unit length ($\Omega/\mathrm{m}$). A larger $\lambda$ means the signal spreads farther. An action potential is regenerated by voltage-gated $\mathrm{Na^+}$ channels as it travels, so its amplitude does not decay.

### Myelin

In myelinated axons, the membrane between nodes of Ranvier is wrapped in many layers of insulating myelin. This raises the effective membrane resistance and lowers the effective membrane capacitance, so depolarization spreads farther between nodes and charges the next node faster. The action potential effectively jumps from node to node (**saltatory conduction**), which is faster and uses less energy. In demyelinating diseases such as multiple sclerosis, loss of myelin shortens the length constant and slows or blocks conduction.

@example
title: Equilibrium potential of chloride
problem: For $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ with $[\mathrm{Cl^-}]_{out} = 110\ \mathrm{mM}$ and $[\mathrm{Cl^-}]_{in} = 10\ \mathrm{mM}$, use $RT/F = 26.7\ \mathrm{mV}$ to find the Nernst potential.
step: Chloride has $z = -1$, so $E = \dfrac{26.7}{-1}\ln\dfrac{110}{10}$ mV.
step: $\ln(11) \approx 2.398$.
step: $E \approx -26.7 \times 2.398 \approx -64\ \mathrm{mV}$. The negative sign means that at this membrane potential chloride is at equilibrium, with the inside negative.

@mistakes
- Forgetting the sign of the charge number $z$ for anions in the Nernst equation, or inverting the concentration ratio.
- Mixing the sign convention for current: with $V_m - E_X > 0$ a positive ion current flows out of the cell.
- Thinking that the resting potential equals $E_K$ exactly; the small $\mathrm{Na^+}$ permeability pulls it above $E_K$.
- Believing the action potential weakens along a healthy axon; it is regenerated, while passive spread decays with $\lambda$.
