@article
Constants and conventions used here: normal near point $25\ \mathrm{cm}$ (the conventional distance for comfortable viewing), normal far point at infinity, refractive index of air $1.000$. Lens equation $1/f = 1/d_o + 1/d_i$ and power $P = 1/f$ (in meters, giving diopters, D). Sign convention as in the previous lesson: converging lens $f>0$; a virtual image has $d_i<0$. In the simplified thin-lens model used here, the effective distance from the lens to the retina is $2.0\ \mathrm{cm}$, so a relaxed normal eye has power about $50\ \mathrm{D}$ in this model (a real eye has about $60\ \mathrm{D}$ because the refracting media are not air).

### How the eye forms an image

Light is refracted at the **cornea** (which provides about two thirds of the eye's focusing power, since the index change from air to the cornea is large) and then by the **crystalline lens**. The image is real and inverted on the **retina**, which contains rods and cones; the brain interprets it as upright. The iris controls the pupil and thus the amount of light and the depth of focus.

To see nearby objects the eye **accommodates**: the ciliary muscle contracts, the lens becomes more curved, and its power rises. The **near point** is the closest object distance for sharp focus (about $25\ \mathrm{cm}$ in young adults); the **far point** is the farthest. In **presbyopia**, which develops with age, the lens stiffens and the near point recedes.

With the image distance fixed at $2.0\ \mathrm{cm}$, focusing an object at $25\ \mathrm{cm}$ requires $P = 1/0.25 + 1/0.020 = 54\ \mathrm{D}$ in this model, $4\ \mathrm{D}$ more than the relaxed power.

### Refractive errors and corrections

**Myopia (nearsightedness).** The eye is too long or too powerful; distant objects focus in front of the retina and the far point is closer than infinity. A **diverging** lens corrects it: it must form a virtual image of a distant object at the person's far point. For an object at infinity, $d_i = -d_{far}$, so

$$P = \frac{1}{f} = -\frac{1}{d_{far}} \quad (\text{negative diopters}).$$

**Hyperopia (farsightedness).** The eye is too short or too weak; near objects focus behind the retina and the near point is too far. A **converging** lens corrects it: it must form a virtual image at the person's near point $d_{near}$ of an object placed at the desired reading distance $d_o = 25\ \mathrm{cm}$:

$$P = \frac{1}{d_o} + \frac{1}{-d_{near}} = 4.0\ \mathrm{D} - \frac{1}{d_{near}}.$$

**Astigmatism** results from unequal curvature in different meridians and is corrected by a cylindrical lens. These formulas ignore the small distance between a spectacle lens and the eye (the vertex distance), which matters for strong prescriptions and contact lenses.

### Instruments

A **magnifier** is a converging lens that forms an enlarged virtual image of an object placed just inside its focal length. Its angular magnification when the image is at infinity is $M = 25\ \mathrm{cm}/f$. A **compound microscope** uses two lenses, an objective that forms a real enlarged image and an eyepiece that acts as a magnifier; the total magnification is the product. A **refracting telescope** has a long-focal-length objective and a short-focal-length eyepiece, with angular magnification $|M| = f_o/f_e$.

### Resolution of the eye

Even a perfect lens cannot focus to a point because of diffraction. The limit on angular resolution comes in the next lesson; for the eye the pupil size and the spacing of cones set the practical limit.

@example
title: Glasses for a nearsighted eye
problem: A person's far point is $50\ \mathrm{cm}$. Find the power of the lens that lets them see distant objects clearly (ignoring the vertex distance).
step: The lens must take an object at infinity and form a virtual image at the far point, $50\ \mathrm{cm}$ in front of the lens. So $d_o = \infty$ and $d_i = -0.50\ \mathrm{m}$.
step: $\dfrac{1}{f} = \dfrac{1}{\infty} + \dfrac{1}{-0.50} = -2.0\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$.
step: $P = -2.0\ \mathrm{D}$: a diverging lens, as expected for myopia.

@mistakes
- Using a converging lens (positive power) for myopia; nearsightedness needs diverging lenses.
- Forgetting that the image formed by a corrective lens is virtual, so $d_i$ is negative.
- Using centimeters rather than meters when computing power in diopters.
- Confusing angular magnification (viewing angle) with image-size magnification $m = -d_i/d_o$.

@practice num
prompt: A myopic person has a far point of $80\ \mathrm{cm}$. Using $P = -1/d_{far}$ (ignore vertex distance), find the power of the corrective lens in diopters.
answer: -1.25
tol: 1%
unit: D
check: -1 / 0.80
hint: The lens should form a virtual image of a distant object at $80\ \mathrm{cm}$.
solution: $d_o\to\infty$ and $d_i = -0.80\ \mathrm{m}$, so $P = 1/d_i = -1.25\ \mathrm{D}$.

@practice num
prompt: A farsighted person has a near point of $75\ \mathrm{cm}$ and wants to read at $25\ \mathrm{cm}$. Find the power of the corrective lens in diopters, using $P = 1/d_o + 1/d_i$ with the virtual image at the near point (ignore vertex distance).
answer: 2.667
tol: 1%
unit: D
check: 1/0.25 + 1/(-0.75)
hint: The object is at $0.25\ \mathrm{m}$; the image is virtual at $0.75\ \mathrm{m}$ on the same side, so $d_i = -0.75\ \mathrm{m}$.
solution: $P = 1/0.25 - 1/0.75 = 4.00 - 1.33 = +2.67\ \mathrm{D}$, a converging lens.

unit: D

solution: $P = 1/0.50 + 1/0.020 = 2.0 + 50 = 52\ \mathrm{D}$.

@practice num
prompt: A simple magnifying glass has focal length $5.0\ \mathrm{cm}$. Using the standard near point of $25\ \mathrm{cm}$, find its angular magnification when the image is formed at infinity.
answer: 5
tol: 1%
check: 25 / 5.0
hint: $M = 25\ \mathrm{cm}/f$.
solution: $M = 25/5.0 = 5.0$, so the object appears $5\times$ larger in angle than at the near point.
