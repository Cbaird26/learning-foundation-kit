@article
Constants used here: $k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{N\,m^2/C^2}$, $e = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}$, $\varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{F/m}$. Sign convention: a positive charge gains potential energy when moved toward higher potential; an electron moving toward higher potential loses potential energy.

### Potential energy and potential

Moving a charge against an electric force takes work, which is stored as electric potential energy $U$. The **electric potential** $V$ is the potential energy per unit charge, $V = U/q$, measured in volts ($1\ \mathrm{V} = 1\ \mathrm{J/C}$). Only **differences** matter physically: moving charge $q$ through a potential difference $\Delta V$ changes its energy by

$$\Delta U = q\,\Delta V.$$

A useful unit is the **electron-volt**: $1\ \mathrm{eV} = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}$, the energy of one elementary charge across one volt. A point charge $Q$ gives potential $V = kQ/r$ (zero at infinity; the sign of $Q$ is kept). In a uniform field, $E = |\Delta V|/d$, and the field points from high to low potential. That is why voltages across thin membranes correspond to huge fields.

### Capacitors

A **capacitor** stores separated charge. Two conductors carrying $+Q$ and $-Q$ with potential difference $V$ between them have capacitance

$$C = \frac{Q}{V}, \qquad \text{units: farad } (1\ \mathrm{F} = 1\ \mathrm{C/V}).$$

For two parallel plates of area $A$ separated by distance $d$, with an insulator of dielectric constant $\kappa$ between them,

$$C = \kappa\,\varepsilon_0\,\frac{A}{d}.$$

The stored energy is $U = \tfrac12 CV^2 = \tfrac12 QV = Q^2/(2C)$. This is the principle behind a defibrillator, which stores energy in a capacitor and delivers it through the chest in milliseconds.

### Combinations

Capacitors in **parallel** share the same voltage and their capacitances add: $C_{eq} = C_1 + C_2 + \cdots$. Capacitors in **series** carry the same charge and their reciprocals add: $1/C_{eq} = 1/C_1 + 1/C_2 + \cdots$. Parallel combinations increase capacitance; series combinations decrease it.

### The membrane as a capacitor

A cell membrane is a lipid bilayer about $7$ to $8\ \mathrm{nm}$ thick, a thin insulator separating two conducting salt solutions. Treat it as a parallel-plate capacitor with dielectric constant $\kappa \approx 7$ (a typical textbook value for the lipid layer). The capacitance **per unit area** is $C/A = \kappa\varepsilon_0/d$, which comes out near $0.8\ \mu\mathrm{F/cm^2}$, close to the measured value of about $1\ \mu\mathrm{F/cm^2}$. A resting potential difference of about $70\ \mathrm{mV}$ across such a thin layer produces a field near $10^7\ \mathrm{V/m}$, which is why membranes are close to electrical breakdown yet remain stable. Changing the membrane voltage means moving charge $Q = CV$ onto the membrane, a very small number of ions relative to the total in the cell.

@example
title: Capacitance and field of a membrane patch
problem: A membrane is modeled as a parallel-plate capacitor with thickness $d = 7.5\ \mathrm{nm}$, dielectric constant $\kappa = 7.0$ and $\varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{F/m}$. Find the capacitance per unit area in $\mu\mathrm{F/cm^2}$ and the field across it when the potential difference is $70\ \mathrm{mV}$.
step: Capacitance per area is $C/A = \kappa\varepsilon_0/d = (7.0)(8.854\times10^{-12})/(7.5\times10^{-9}) \approx 8.26\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{F/m^2}$.
step: Convert: $1\ \mathrm{F/m^2} = 10^{6}\ \mu\mathrm{F}/10^{4}\ \mathrm{cm^2} = 100\ \mu\mathrm{F/cm^2}$, so $C/A \approx 0.83\ \mu\mathrm{F/cm^2}$.
step: The field is $E = V/d = 0.070/(7.5\times10^{-9}) \approx 9.3\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{V/m}$.

@mistakes
- Mixing up potential $V$ (volts, per charge) with potential energy $U$ (joules).
- Adding capacitances in series or adding reciprocals in parallel.
- Forgetting to convert nanometers or millivolts to SI units before using $C = \kappa\varepsilon_0 A/d$ or $E = V/d$.
- Thinking a capacitor stores net charge; it stores equal and opposite charges, so its net charge is zero.
