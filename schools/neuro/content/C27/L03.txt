@article
You want to know which part of the brain responds to faces. You could show faces in long runs, twenty seconds of faces and then twenty seconds of rest. Or you could show single faces at random moments among other pictures. Both put the same faces on the screen. Which design finds the activation better, and which is better for finding out how the response unfolds in time? The answer depends on how the design interacts with the slow, smoothing hemodynamic response from the last lesson.

### Block and event-related designs

In a **block design** the conditions alternate in extended periods (blocks) of many trials, typically 10 to 30 seconds each. In an **event-related design** trials are brief and separated by intervals that vary. The **interstimulus interval** (ISI) is the time between trials; making it vary from trial to trial is called **jittering**. A **periodic** event-related design uses a fixed ISI.

Because the HRF blurs each event over about 15 seconds, a block of closely spaced events produces a response that adds up to a large, sustained change. A regular train of widely spaced events, by contrast, produces small ripples that rise and fall around the baseline.

### Efficiency

How well can a design estimate an effect? In the general linear model of week 5, the variance of an estimated contrast $\mathbf{c}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$ is $\sigma^2\,\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{c}^\top$, where $\mathbf{X}$ is the design matrix and $\sigma^2$ is the noise variance. The **efficiency** of the design for that contrast is

$$\text{eff}(\mathbf{c}) = \frac{1}{\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{c}^\top}.$$

Larger efficiency means a smaller variance, so a higher chance of detecting a real effect with the same number of scans.

For a single task regressor $\mathbf{r}$ (the event sequence convolved with the HRF) plus a constant column, and $\mathbf{c}$ selecting the task, this simplifies to the sum of squared deviations of the regressor from its mean,

$$\text{eff} = \sum_{i}(r_i - \bar r)^2,$$

so the efficient design is the one whose predicted signal varies the most.

For two conditions A and B with mean-centered regressors, let $s_{11} = \sum a_i^2$, $s_{22} = \sum b_i^2$ and $s_{12} = \sum a_i b_i$. For the contrast $\mathbf{c} = (1, -1)$, A minus B, the efficiency is

$$\text{eff}_{A-B} = \frac{s_{11}s_{22} - s_{12}^2}{s_{11} + s_{22} + 2 s_{12}}.$$

If A and B regressors are nearly collinear, the numerator goes to zero and the contrast cannot be estimated.

### An example comparison

Take TR $=2$ s, 16 scans, and the HRF sampled as $h = [0,\ 0.5,\ 1.0,\ 0.5,\ 0,\ -0.25]$. Three designs for one condition against baseline:

| design | events (task onsets) | task scans | efficiency |
|---|---|---|---|
| block, 8 s on and 8 s off, twice | scans 0 to 3 and 8 to 11 | 8 | 11.75 |
| periodic events, one every 4 scans | scans 0, 4, 8, 12 | 4 | 2.15 |
| jittered events | scans 0, 3, 5, 9, 10, 13 | 6 | 3.25 |

The block design wins by a large factor for **detection**: the convolved regressor swings between 0 and 2.0, whereas the event regressors never exceed 1.5. The jittered design with six events beats the periodic design with four, partly because it has more events and partly because the irregular timing lets the responses overlap and add in places.

### What each design is good for

- **Detection power.** Block designs are best at detecting that a region responds at all. They are also robust to errors in the assumed HRF shape.
- **Estimation of the response shape.** If you want to know when the response peaks or how wide it is, you need events whose timing varies relative to the scans, so the design samples the HRF at many delays. Jittered event-related designs do this.
- **Flexibility and psychology.** Event-related designs allow random trial order, so participants cannot predict the trial type, and they allow trials to be sorted after the fact (remembered versus forgotten items, correct versus error trials). Blocks can induce a sustained mental set, strategies, boredom and drift, which are confounded with the condition.
- **Mixed designs** combine blocks of one type with events inside them to estimate both sustained and transient effects.

For a contrast between two event types, randomizing their order and jittering the ISI keeps their regressors from becoming collinear. For a rapid event-related design, a few hundred events with an ISI as short as 2 to 4 seconds can be efficient, but only if the order is randomized, and the extra overlap makes the linearity assumption more important.

@example
title: Efficiency of a small regressor
problem: A task regressor (already convolved) over six scans is $\mathbf{r} = [0,\ 1,\ 2,\ 1,\ 0,\ 0]$. Compute its efficiency for detecting the task when a constant column is also in the model.
step: Compute the mean: $\bar r = (0 + 1 + 2 + 1 + 0 + 0)/6 = 4/6 \approx 0.667$.
step: Compute the sum of squares of the raw values: $0 + 1 + 4 + 1 + 0 + 0 = 6$.
step: Use the identity $\sum (r_i - \bar r)^2 = \sum r_i^2 - n\bar r^2 = 6 - 6 \times 0.4444 = 6 - 2.667$.
step: The result is $3.333$.
answer: The efficiency is about $3.33$, in units of squared signal change.

@mistakes
- Judging a design only by the regressor of one condition. For a contrast between two conditions, what matters is how correlated their regressors are; two conditions in separate halves of the run, or always in the same order, are confounded with drift.
- Concluding that block designs are always better because their efficiency is higher. They are better for detection but poor for estimating response shape and for any question about single trials.
- Forgetting that efficiency depends on the HRF: it is computed from the convolved regressors, not the raw event times.

model:
```python
def design_efficiency(x, h):
    """Sum of squared deviations of the convolved regressor from its mean."""
    n = len(x)
    r = []
    for i in range(n):
        total = 0.0
        for k in range(len(h)):
            if 0 <= i - k < n:
                total += x[i - k] * h[k]
        r.append(total)
    mean = sum(r) / n
    return sum((v - mean) ** 2 for v in r)
```
hint: Build the regressor with a double loop over output index and lag, then compute the sum of squared deviations from its mean.
solution: Convolve, truncate to `len(x)`, subtract the mean and square. The last test has a response that is cut off by the end of the run, so truncating to `len(x)` matters: the regressor is $[0, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1]$ with mean $5/6$ and efficiency $5/6$.

model:
```python
def diff_efficiency(r1, r2):
    """Efficiency of the A-minus-B contrast for two convolved regressors."""
    n = len(r1)
    m1 = sum(r1) / n
    m2 = sum(r2) / n
    a = [v - m1 for v in r1]
    b = [v - m2 for v in r2]
    s11 = sum(v * v for v in a)
    s22 = sum(v * v for v in b)
    s12 = sum(u * v for u, v in zip(a, b))
    det = s11 * s22 - s12 ** 2
    if abs(det) < 1e-12:
        return 0.0
    return det / (s11 + s22 + 2 * s12)
```
hint: Center both regressors first, form the three sums, and check the determinant before dividing.
solution: The three sums are the entries of $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$ for the centered regressors, and the formula is $1/(\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{c}^\top)$ worked out for a 2 by 2 matrix. Perfectly collinear regressors, such as a regressor and its negative, have a zero determinant and cannot give a contrast estimate, so the efficiency is 0.

model:
```python
import math


def corr(r1, r2):
    """Pearson correlation of two equal-length lists; 0.0 if either is constant."""
    n = len(r1)
    m1 = sum(r1) / n
    m2 = sum(r2) / n
    num = sum((a - m1) * (b - m2) for a, b in zip(r1, r2))
    d1 = sum((a - m1) ** 2 for a in r1)
    d2 = sum((b - m2) ** 2 for b in r2)
    if d1 == 0 or d2 == 0:
        return 0.0
    return num / math.sqrt(d1 * d2)
```
hint: Subtract each list's mean first, and guard against a zero denominator.
solution: The correlation is the covariance sum divided by the geometric mean of the two variance sums. A constant regressor has zero variance, so the guard avoids dividing by zero.
