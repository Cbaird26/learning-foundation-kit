@article
After fitting the GLM, a voxel has one weight for every regressor. The experimental question, though, is never "what is $\beta_3$?". It is "does the response to faces exceed the response to houses?" or "is there any response to the task?" A **contrast** turns weights into the answer to a question, and a **t statistic** says how large the answer is compared with its uncertainty.

### Contrasts

A contrast is a vector $\mathbf{c}$ with one number for each column of the design matrix; the estimated effect is $\mathbf{c}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$ (a weighted sum of weights). With columns ordered (A, B, constant):

| question | $\mathbf{c}$ | estimated effect |
|---|---|---|
| A above baseline? | $(1, 0, 0)$ | $\hat\beta_A$ |
| B above baseline? | $(0, 1, 0)$ | $\hat\beta_B$ |
| A larger than B? | $(1, -1, 0)$ | $\hat\beta_A - \hat\beta_B$ |
| does either A or B differ from baseline? | rows $(1,0,0)$ and $(0,1,0)$ tested together with an F test | |

The coefficients of a contrast that asks about a difference sum to zero, so a constant shift in all conditions cancels.

### The t statistic

Under the assumptions of the previous lesson (independent, equal-variance noise), the variance of $\mathbf{c}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$ is $\sigma^2\,\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{c}^\top$, so

$$t = \frac{\mathbf{c}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}}{\sqrt{\hat\sigma^2\,\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{c}^\top}}, \qquad \text{df} = n - p.$$

If the true effect is zero, $t$ follows Student's $t$ distribution with $n - p$ degrees of freedom. The quantity under the root is the standard error of the effect, so a large $t$ means an effect large compared with its noise.

### A worked voxel

Eight scans, regressors A $= [0,1,2,1,0,0,0,0]$ and B $= [0,0,0,0,0,1,2,1]$, and a constant; the time series is $\mathbf{y} = [100.2,\ 101.0,\ 102.1,\ 101.2,\ 99.9,\ 100.6,\ 101.1,\ 100.4]$. Solving the normal equations gives $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (1.0417,\ 0.5083,\ 100.0375)$, RSS $= 0.0929$, and with $n - p = 5$ degrees of freedom $\hat\sigma^2 = 0.01858$. Here $(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}$ has diagonal entries $0.3333$ for A and B and off-diagonal $0.1667$ between them, so

| contrast | effect | $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{c}^\top$ | $t$ (df $=5$) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 1.0417 | 0.3333 | 13.24 |
| B | 0.5083 | 0.3333 | 6.46 |
| A minus B | 0.5333 | 0.3333 | 6.78 |

For a two-sided test at 0.05 with 5 degrees of freedom, the critical value of $t$ is 2.571, so all three are significant. For A minus B the variance factor is $0.3333 + 0.3333 - 2 \times 0.1667 = 0.3333$.

### Statistical maps

The same design is fitted at every voxel, and the contrast $t$ value is stored in that voxel, which gives a **statistical map** ($t$ map). The design matrix is the same for all voxels, so $(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}$ is computed once; only $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$ and $\hat\sigma^2$ differ. A threshold is then applied to find "active" voxels, which raises the problem of thousands of simultaneous tests, covered in lesson 7. A $t$ value can be converted to a $z$ value with the same tail probability, which makes maps from different degrees of freedom comparable. For large degrees of freedom, a $t$ value is close to a standard normal $z$ value; $z = 2$ corresponds to a two-sided p of about 0.0455.

### Differences must be tested as differences

A common error is to find that A is significant (A against baseline) and B is not, and to conclude that A exceeds B. The difference between "significant" and "not significant" is not itself a significant difference: B's effect may be just under threshold and almost as large as A's. The question "A greater than B?" needs the contrast $(1, -1, 0)$.

### Group level

A group study fits the model separately in each participant (the **first level**) and then tests the contrast estimates across participants (the **second level**). With subject effects $b_1, \dots, b_N$, the one-sample t statistic is

$$t = \frac{\bar b}{s_b/\sqrt{N}}, \qquad \text{df} = N - 1,$$

where $s_b$ is the standard deviation of the $b_i$ with $N - 1$ in the denominator. This treats participants as a random sample, so the result generalizes to the population they were drawn from (a **random-effects** analysis). A test that pooled all scans from all participants as if they were independent would generalize only to those participants. A $t$ is not an effect size: with many subjects a trivial effect can give a large $t$, so the effect estimate itself (for example in percent signal change) should always be reported.

### In code

```python
def contrast_effect(c, betas):
    """Estimated effect: the weighted sum of the betas."""
    return sum(ci * bi for ci, bi in zip(c, betas))

print(round(contrast_effect([1, -1, 0], [1.0417, 0.5083, 100.0375]), 4))
```

This prints `0.5334`, matching the 0.5333 of the table up to rounding of the inputs. In practice, packages compute the whole map for every voxel at once.

title: A t statistic from its parts
problem: At one voxel, a contrast has estimated effect $\mathbf{c}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = 0.90$ percent signal change. The residual variance is $\hat\sigma^2 = 0.64$ and $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{c}^\top = 0.25$. The model has $p = 4$ regressors and $n = 104$ scans. Find $t$ and its degrees of freedom.
step: Compute the variance of the effect: $0.64 \times 0.25 = 0.16$.
step: Take the square root, the standard error: $\sqrt{0.16} = 0.40$.
step: Divide: $t = 0.90/0.40 = 2.25$.
step: Degrees of freedom: $n - p = 104 - 4 = 100$.
answer: $t = 2.25$ with 100 degrees of freedom.

@mistakes
- Concluding that A exceeds B because A is significant and B is not. Test the contrast $(1, -1)$ directly.
- Forgetting the design factor $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{c}^\top$: two contrasts with the same effect and the same noise have different $t$ values if their designs estimate them with different precision.
- Using the number of scans as the degrees of freedom of the group test. At the second level the degrees of freedom are the number of participants minus one.
- Reading a large $t$ as a large effect. It combines effect size, noise and sample size.

model:
```python
import math


def t_stat(X, y, c):
    """t statistic for the contrast c of the least-squares weights."""
    n, p = len(X), len(X[0])
    A = [[sum(X[i][a] * X[i][b] for i in range(n)) for b in range(p)]
         + [1.0 if a == b else 0.0 for b in range(p)] for a in range(p)]
    for col in range(p):
        pivot = max(range(col, p), key=lambda r: abs(A[r][col]))
        A[col], A[pivot] = A[pivot], A[col]
        div = A[col][col]
        A[col] = [v / div for v in A[col]]
        for r in range(p):
            if r != col:
                f = A[r][col]
                A[r] = [v - f * w for v, w in zip(A[r], A[col])]
    inv = [row[p:] for row in A]
    xty = [sum(X[i][a] * y[i] for i in range(n)) for a in range(p)]
    beta = [sum(inv[a][b] * xty[b] for b in range(p)) for a in range(p)]
    rss = sum((y[i] - sum(X[i][a] * beta[a] for a in range(p))) ** 2
              for i in range(n))
    sigma2 = rss / (n - p)
    effect = sum(ci * bi for ci, bi in zip(c, beta))
    factor = sum(c[a] * inv[a][b] * c[b] for a in range(p) for b in range(p))
    return effect / math.sqrt(sigma2 * factor)
```
hint: Reduce the matrix $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$ next to the identity to get its inverse, then follow the formulas for the weights, RSS, $\hat\sigma^2$ and the design factor.
solution: Invert $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$ once, use it both for the weights and for the design factor $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{c}^\top$, estimate the variance with $n - p$ degrees of freedom, and divide the contrast effect by its standard error. The last test checks that reversing the contrast flips the sign of $t$.

model:
```python
import math


def group_t(effects):
    """One-sample t statistic of the participants' effects against zero."""
    n = len(effects)
    mean = sum(effects) / n
    var = sum((e - mean) ** 2 for e in effects) / (n - 1)
    if var == 0:
        return None
    return mean / math.sqrt(var / n)
```
hint: Compute the mean, the sample variance with $N-1$, and divide the mean by $\sqrt{s^2/N}$.
solution: This is the second level of a summary-statistics analysis: each participant contributes one number, and the test treats participants as the random sample. Dividing by $N$ instead of $N - 1$ in the variance gives a larger $t$ and fails the first test.

```python
import math


def two_sided_p(z):
    """Two-sided tail probability of the standard normal distribution."""
    return 1.0 - math.erf(abs(z) / math.sqrt(2))
```
hint: Use the absolute value of $z$ so that negative values give the same probability.
solution: The probability of a standard normal value exceeding $|z|$ in either direction is $1 - \operatorname{erf}(|z|/\sqrt{2})$. The tests check symmetry about zero, $p = 1$ at $z = 0$, and the familiar values at 1, 2 and 3.

@quiz self
prompt: A study has 20 participants with 400 scans each. An analyst pools all 8,000 scans in one GLM (treating scans as independent) and gets a very small p value for a task effect; a colleague runs a group analysis on the 20 participants' effects and finds a much weaker result. Explain the difference and say which result supports a claim about people in general.
hint: Ask what is treated as the sample in each analysis.
solution: In the pooled analysis the degrees of freedom come from the 8,000 scans, so the test asks whether the effect is nonzero in these particular 20 participants (a fixed-effects inference) and ignores that scans from one person are correlated and that people differ in the size of their effects. The group analysis treats the 20 participants as a random sample, with 19 degrees of freedom, and its standard error includes the between-person variability in the effect, so a result that is significant there is expected to hold for the population they represent. The group result supports a claim about people in general; the pooled result supports only a claim about these participants. The effect size, in percent signal change, should be reported in both cases, since p values alone reflect sample size.
rubric: Identifies the pooled analysis as a fixed-effects (within-sample) inference and the group analysis as a random-effects inference.
