@article
Two regions that are not touching each other, and are anatomically far apart, can have BOLD time series that rise and fall together even when a person lies still with eyes closed. Does that mean they are connected? **Functional connectivity** is the statistical dependence between the time series of different regions, usually the Pearson correlation. It is a description of co-fluctuation, and the first thing to learn is how far a description can be pushed.

### Correlation between regions

For two series $x$ and $y$ of $n$ scans, the correlation is

$$r_{xy} = \frac{\sum_t (x_t - \bar x)(y_t - \bar y)}{\sqrt{\sum_t (x_t - \bar x)^2\ \sum_t (y_t - \bar y)^2}}.$$

In a **seed-based** analysis, one region's average time series is correlated with the time series of every other voxel, giving a map. For a set of $K$ regions, all pairwise correlations form a symmetric $K \times K$ **correlation matrix** with ones on the diagonal; this is the starting point for network analyses. Resting-state analyses usually band-pass filter the data (commonly about 0.01 to 0.1 Hz) so that slow fluctuations dominate, and they remove nuisance signals before correlating.

### The Fisher transformation

Correlations are bounded by $\pm 1$ and their sampling distribution is skewed, so averaging them directly is biased. The **Fisher transformation** $z = \operatorname{arctanh}(r) = \tfrac12\ln\frac{1+r}{1-r}$ maps $r$ to an unbounded scale on which averages and t tests behave better; its inverse is $r = \tanh(z)$. To average the correlations of participants, transform each, average, and transform back. For $r = 0.5$, $z = 0.549$; for $r = 0.9$, $z = 1.472$. The standard error of $z$ is about $1/\sqrt{n_\text{eff} - 3}$, where $n_\text{eff}$ is the effective number of independent scans. Because BOLD series are autocorrelated, $n_\text{eff}$ is smaller than the number of scans, and a test that uses $n$ gives p values that are too small.

### Common input and partial correlation

Take three regions with time series $x$, $y$ and $z$ over eight scans. Region $z$ (say, a sensory region driven by a stimulus) is $z = (0, 1, 2, 1, 0, -1, -2, -1)$. The other two are

$$x = (-0.4,\ 0.4,\ 2.6,\ 0.5,\ -0.6,\ -1.1,\ -1.6,\ -1.8),$$
$$y = (-0.4,\ 0.7,\ 2.7,\ 0.9,\ 0.7,\ -1.7,\ -2.3,\ -0.7).$$

Their correlations are $r_{xy} = 0.895$, $r_{xz} = 0.935$ and $r_{yz} = 0.955$. Is there a link between $x$ and $y$ beyond what they share with $z$? The **partial correlation** removes the linear effect of $z$ from both:

$$r_{xy\cdot z} = \frac{r_{xy} - r_{xz}\,r_{yz}}{\sqrt{(1 - r_{xz}^2)(1 - r_{yz}^2)}} = \frac{0.895 - 0.935 \times 0.955}{\sqrt{(1 - 0.874)(1 - 0.912)}} \approx 0.02$$

(0.025 when computed from the unrounded correlations).

Here the strong correlation between $x$ and $y$ is explained by their shared dependence on $z$. This is why task-evoked responses must be modeled and removed before reading correlations as an interaction between regions: two regions that both respond to the same stimuli will be correlated whether or not they communicate.

### What functional connectivity is not

- It is **not causal**: a correlation does not tell the direction of influence. Methods of **effective connectivity** (such as dynamic causal modeling) model direction, with strong assumptions.
- It is **not anatomical connectivity**: regions without direct axons can be strongly correlated through a third region, and connected regions can be weakly correlated.
- It is sensitive to **confounds**: head motion produces spurious, distance-dependent correlations; breathing and heart rate produce widespread fluctuations; the global signal (the mean over the brain) is contentious, since removing it makes some correlations negative and changes the results, so the choice should be justified and reported.
- It varies with state, so the common practice of **sliding-window** correlation (compute $r$ in a window of $w$ scans, then slide it) is used to look at changes over time, but short windows give very noisy estimates, and fluctuations of this kind can be produced by stationary noise, so claims about "dynamics" need a null model.

A related task-based method, **psychophysiological interaction** (PPI), tests whether the coupling between a seed's time series and a voxel's changes with the task, by adding the product of the seed series and the task regressor to a GLM that also contains the seed series and the task regressor themselves (full implementations form the product at the level of estimated neural activity rather than BOLD).

title: A partial correlation from three correlations
problem: Three regions have $r_{xy} = 0.70$, $r_{xz} = 0.80$ and $r_{yz} = 0.60$. Find the partial correlation of $x$ and $y$ controlling for $z$.
step: Numerator: $r_{xy} - r_{xz}r_{yz} = 0.70 - 0.80 \times 0.60 = 0.70 - 0.48 = 0.22$.
step: Denominator: $\sqrt{(1 - 0.64)(1 - 0.36)} = \sqrt{0.36 \times 0.64} = \sqrt{0.2304} = 0.48$.
step: Divide: $0.22/0.48 = 0.458$.
step: Interpret: the correlation drops from 0.70 to 0.46 when the shared dependence on $z$ is removed, so part of the original connectivity was common input.
answer: The partial correlation is about $0.458$.

@mistakes
- Reading a correlation between regions as a causal connection or a direct anatomical pathway.
- Averaging raw correlations across participants instead of Fisher-transforming first.
- Interpreting correlations without removing the task-evoked response, head motion, or physiological noise, so shared stimulation and artifacts masquerade as connectivity.
- Treating every scan as an independent sample when computing p values for a correlation. Autocorrelation reduces the effective degrees of freedom.

```python
import math


def _corr(a, b):
    n = len(a)
    ma, mb = sum(a) / n, sum(b) / n
    saa = sum((u - ma) ** 2 for u in a)
    sbb = sum((v - mb) ** 2 for v in b)
    sab = sum((u - ma) * (v - mb) for u, v in zip(a, b))
    return sab / math.sqrt(saa * sbb)


def partial_corr(x, y, z):
    """Partial correlation of x and y controlling for z."""
    if sum((v - sum(z) / len(z)) ** 2 for v in z) == 0:
        return None
    rxy, rxz, ryz = _corr(x, y), _corr(x, z), _corr(y, z)
    den = (1 - rxz ** 2) * (1 - ryz ** 2)
    if den < 1e-12:
        return None
    return (rxy - rxz * ryz) / math.sqrt(den)
```
hint: Write a small Pearson correlation helper first, then combine the three correlations; check the denominator before dividing, and a constant `z` has no correlation at all.
solution: The partial correlation removes what $z$ explains of both series. A `z` that is a multiple of `x` makes $r_{xz} = 1$ and the denominator zero, and a constant `z` has zero variance, so its correlations are undefined; both cases return `None`.

```python
import math


def group_mean_r(rs):
    """Average of correlations through the Fisher transformation."""
    zs = [0.5 * math.log((1 + r) / (1 - r)) for r in rs]
    return math.tanh(sum(zs) / len(zs))
```
hint: Transform each correlation, take the ordinary mean of the transformed values, then transform back with `math.tanh`.
solution: Averaging on the Fisher scale weights large correlations more heavily than a plain mean does, which is why $0.4928$ exceeds $0.4667$. The symmetric pair $0.3$ and $-0.3$ averages to zero on either scale, and a single correlation is returned unchanged.

model:
```python
import math


def sliding_corr(x, y, w):
    """Pearson correlation of x and y in each window of w scans."""
    out = []
    for start in range(len(x) - w + 1):
        a = x[start:start + w]
        b = y[start:start + w]
        ma, mb = sum(a) / w, sum(b) / w
        saa = sum((u - ma) ** 2 for u in a)
        sbb = sum((v - mb) ** 2 for v in b)
        if saa == 0 or sbb == 0:
            out.append(0.0)
        else:
            sab = sum((u - ma) * (v - mb) for u, v in zip(a, b))
            out.append(sab / math.sqrt(saa * sbb))
    return out
```
hint: Loop over window start positions, slice both series, and compute the correlation of the two slices, guarding zero variance.
solution: Each window is a separate correlation with its own means. A window in which one series is constant has an undefined correlation, which is recorded as 0.0 here. When `w` exceeds the length, `range(len(x) - w + 1)` is empty, so the result is an empty list.
