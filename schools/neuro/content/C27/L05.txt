@article
Lesson 2 gave you a predicted time course for a task: the event sequence convolved with the HRF. A real voxel's time series is that prediction, scaled by some unknown amount, riding on a baseline, plus noise. How much of the voxel's variation does the task explain, and how large is the task effect? Fitting a **general linear model** (GLM) to each voxel answers both questions. It is the workhorse of fMRI analysis.

### The model

For one voxel with $n$ scans, let $\mathbf{y}$ be the time series (length $n$), and let $\mathbf{X}$ be the $n \times p$ **design matrix** whose columns are regressors: the convolved task regressors, a constant (the baseline), and nuisance regressors such as a slow drift or motion. The model is

$$\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon, \qquad \boldsymbol\varepsilon \sim \text{noise with mean } 0 \text{ and variance } \sigma^2.$$

The unknown weights $\boldsymbol\beta$ (the **betas**) say how much of each regressor is in the data. We choose $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$ to minimize the residual sum of squares $\text{RSS} = \|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta\|^2$. Setting the derivative with respect to $\boldsymbol\beta$ to zero, $-2\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta) = \mathbf{0}$, gives the **normal equations**

$$\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\,\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = \mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}, \qquad \hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}.$$

The inverse exists when the columns of $\mathbf{X}$ are linearly independent; if two regressors are perfectly correlated, the individual betas cannot be separated.

### A worked fit

Eight scans (TR $= 2$ s) with a convolved task regressor $\mathbf{r} = [0, 1, 2, 2, 1, 0, 0, 0]$ (arbitrary units) and a constant column, and a time series in scanner units, $\mathbf{y} = [10.1,\ 10.9,\ 12.2,\ 11.8,\ 11.1,\ 10.2,\ 9.9,\ 10.1]$. Then

$$\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} = \begin{pmatrix} 10 & 6 \\ 6 & 8 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y} = \begin{pmatrix} 70 \\ 86.3 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The determinant is $10 \times 8 - 6 \times 6 = 44$, so

$$\hat\beta_\text{task} = \frac{8 \times 70 - 6 \times 86.3}{44} = 0.959, \qquad \hat\beta_0 = \frac{10 \times 86.3 - 6 \times 70}{44} = 10.068.$$

The fitted values are $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$ = $[10.07,\ 11.03,\ 11.99,\ 11.99,\ 11.03,\ 10.07,\ 10.07,\ 10.07]$. The task weight 0.959 means the voxel's signal rises by 0.959 scanner units for each unit of the regressor, so at the regressor's peak of 2 the model predicts a rise of 1.92 units above the baseline of 10.07, about 19%; this is a large effect chosen for easy arithmetic, as real effects are often below 2%.

### Residuals and fit

The **residuals** are $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}}$. Here RSS $= 0.1495$. The noise variance is estimated with the **degrees of freedom** $n - p$ (scans minus fitted parameters):

$$\hat\sigma^2 = \frac{\text{RSS}}{n - p} = \frac{0.1495}{8 - 2} = 0.0249.$$

The proportion of variance explained is $R^2 = 1 - \text{RSS}/\text{TSS}$, where $\text{TSS} = \sum (y_i - \bar y)^2 = 5.209$; here $R^2 = 0.971$.

### What goes in $\mathbf{X}$

- **A constant** column is almost always needed: the raw signal is far from zero, and without it the task regressor would be asked to explain the baseline.
- **Drift** regressors (a few low-frequency cosines or polynomials) absorb slow scanner drift. Without them, drift ends up in the residuals, inflating $\hat\sigma^2$.
- **Motion** regressors can absorb part of the leftover motion effect.
- Each experimental condition gets its own convolved regressor (and, if wanted, a derivative regressor).

The betas for a regressor depend on the other columns. If two regressors overlap, a beta measures only the part of its regressor that the others do not explain. A beta's units are signal units per regressor unit, so to report a percent signal change the regressor is usually scaled to a known peak height (for example 1) and the beta divided by the baseline.

### The assumptions

The standard inference assumes that the noise is independent across scans with equal variance. fMRI noise is in fact **autocorrelated** (a scan resembles its neighbors), so software models the autocorrelation (pre-whitening) or the p-values will be too small. The model is also *linear*, so its HRF assumption matters.

### In code

```python
def fit_two(r, y):
    """Least-squares weights of y on [r, 1] by the 2 by 2 normal equations."""
    n = len(r)
    srr = sum(a * a for a in r)
    sr = sum(r)
    sry = sum(a * b for a, b in zip(r, y))
    sy = sum(y)
    det = srr * n - sr * sr
    return ((n * sry - sr * sy) / det, (srr * sy - sr * sry) / det)

r = [0, 1, 2, 2, 1, 0, 0, 0]
y = [10.1, 10.9, 12.2, 11.8, 11.1, 10.2, 9.9, 10.1]
print([round(b, 3) for b in fit_two(r, y)])
```

This prints `[0.959, 10.068]`. For more regressors the same equations need a solver for a $p \times p$ linear system, which you will write as an exercise; in NumPy it is `np.linalg.lstsq(X, y)`.

@example
title: Solving a two-weight GLM by hand
problem: A voxel with $n = 5$ scans has a task regressor and a constant. The sums are $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y} = \begin{pmatrix} 9 \\ 20 \end{pmatrix}$. Find the task weight and the baseline.
step: Compute the determinant: $6 \times 5 - 4 \times 4 = 14$.
step: Task weight: $\hat\beta_1 = (5 \times 9 - 4 \times 20)/14 = (45 - 80)/14 = -2.5$.
step: Baseline: $\hat\beta_0 = (6 \times 20 - 4 \times 9)/14 = (120 - 36)/14 = 6$.
step: Check in the normal equations: $6(-2.5) + 4(6) = 9$ and $4(-2.5) + 5(6) = 20$. Both hold.
answer: The task weight is $-2.5$ and the baseline is $6$.

@mistakes
- Leaving out the constant (or using a regressor that is not centered while also omitting the constant), so the task regressor must absorb the baseline.
- Reading a beta as an effect on its own when regressors are correlated: it is the effect after the other regressors in the model have taken their share.
- Dividing the residual sum of squares by $n$ rather than by $n - p$ to estimate the noise variance, which underestimates it.
- Treating the p-values as exact when the noise is autocorrelated and the model has not accounted for it.

```python
def fit_glm(X, y):
    """Least-squares weights via the normal equations and Gauss-Jordan."""
    n, p = len(X), len(X[0])
    A = [[sum(X[i][a] * X[i][b] for i in range(n)) for b in range(p)]
         + [sum(X[i][a] * y[i] for i in range(n))] for a in range(p)]
    for col in range(p):
        pivot = max(range(col, p), key=lambda r: abs(A[r][col]))
        A[col], A[pivot] = A[pivot], A[col]
        div = A[col][col]
        A[col] = [v / div for v in A[col]]
        for r in range(p):
            if r != col:
                f = A[r][col]
                A[r] = [v - f * w for v, w in zip(A[r], A[col])]
    return [A[i][p] for i in range(p)]
```
hint: Build the augmented matrix whose rows are the rows of $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$ followed by the matching entry of $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}$; then eliminate column by column.
solution: Build $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$ and $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}$ with sums over scans, append the right-hand side as an extra column, and for each column pick the largest pivot, scale the row so the pivot is 1, and subtract multiples of it from the other rows. The weights are the last column. The fourth test lists the constant last, so a solver that assumes the first column is the constant fails it.

```python
def residual_variance(X, y, betas):
    """Residual sum of squares divided by n minus p."""
    n, p = len(X), len(betas)
    rss = 0.0
    for row, target in zip(X, y):
        fit = sum(x * b for x, b in zip(row, betas))
        rss += (target - fit) ** 2
    return rss / (n - p)
```
hint: Compute each fitted value as a dot product of a row with the weights, accumulate the squared residuals, and divide by $n - p$.
solution: The degrees of freedom are the number of scans minus the number of fitted parameters, here `len(X) - len(betas)`. In the first test the fitted values are 1, 2, 3, 4, so the residuals are 0, 0, -1, 0 and RSS is 1 over 2 degrees of freedom. Dividing by `n` instead would give 0.25.

model:
```python
def orthogonalize(a, b):
    """Remove from a its projection onto b."""
    bb = sum(v * v for v in b)
    if bb == 0:
        return [float(v) for v in a]
    k = sum(u * v for u, v in zip(a, b)) / bb
    return [u - k * v for u, v in zip(a, b)]
```
hint: Compute the dot product of `a` and `b` over the dot product of `b` with itself, then subtract that multiple of `b` from `a`.
solution: The coefficient is the least-squares weight of `b` for explaining `a`; subtracting it leaves the part of `a` that `b` cannot explain. The second test shows an `a` that is entirely explained by `b`, which leaves zeros, and the fourth shows the guard for an all-zero `b`.

@quiz self
prompt: A student fits a GLM to a voxel with a task regressor whose peak height is 1, a constant column that has the value 1200 (scanner units), and a drift column. The task beta is 6.0. Explain what this beta means, convert it to a percent signal change, and say what would change if the regressor were rescaled so its peak height were 2.
hint: A beta is signal per unit of its regressor.
solution: The task beta is the change in the fitted signal, in scanner units, when the task regressor rises by one unit, which is its peak height here, so at its peak the task raises the voxel signal by 6.0 units. Relative to a baseline of 1200 this is $100 \times 6.0/1200 = 0.5\%$. If the regressor were rescaled to a peak of 2, the fit would be identical, but the beta would be halved to 3.0, because one regressor unit is now twice as large; the percent signal change at the peak is still 0.5%, which is why the regressor's scale must be known before betas are compared across studies or described as effect sizes. Correlation with the drift and constant columns matters too: the beta is the effect of the task after those columns have taken their share.
