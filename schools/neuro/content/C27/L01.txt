@article
A scanner contains no electrodes and touches no neurons, yet it produces pictures of the working brain. How can a magnet and a radio antenna tell you which region was busy while a person looked at faces? The answer has two halves: how a scanner gets any signal at all from tissue, and why that signal changes when neurons become active. This lesson gives both at the level you need to analyze the data, not at the level needed to build a scanner.

### From protons to an image, in outline

The nucleus of a hydrogen atom, a proton, behaves like a tiny spinning magnet. Tissue is mostly water, so it is full of them. In a strong static magnetic field $B_0$ (measured in tesla, T) the protons precess at the **Larmor frequency**

$$f = \bar{\gamma}\, B_0, \qquad \bar{\gamma} \approx 42.58\ \text{MHz/T for hydrogen.}$$

| field $B_0$ (T) | Larmor frequency (MHz) |
|---|---|
| 1.5 | 63.87 |
| 3 | 127.74 |
| 7 | 298.06 |

A brief radio-frequency (RF) pulse at that frequency tips the net magnetization away from the field. As it relaxes, it induces a signal in a receiver coil. Two relaxation processes matter. **T1** is the recovery of magnetization along the field. **T2** is the loss of coherence between protons across the plane; it is a property of the tissue. In practice, small variations in the local field add extra dephasing, so the signal decays faster, with time constant **T2\***, which is always shorter than T2. For a gradient-echo image read out at **echo time** TE (ms) the signal is

$$S(\mathrm{TE}) = S_0\, e^{-\mathrm{TE}/T_2^*}.$$

Position is encoded by additional **gradient** fields, which make the precession frequency and phase depend on location, so that each voxel (a small box, often a few millimeters on a side) contributes its own signal to the recording. A fast readout called echo-planar imaging collects an entire slice in a few tens of milliseconds, and the scanner repeats this for all slices. The time to collect one whole volume is the **repetition time** (TR), typically 1 to 3 seconds for fMRI. An fMRI data set is therefore a list of volumes, or equivalently one time series per voxel, one sample per TR.

### The BOLD signal

Hemoglobin changes its magnetic properties with oxygen. Deoxyhemoglobin is paramagnetic and distorts the local field around blood vessels, which speeds dephasing and shortens T2\*; oxyhemoglobin is much less magnetic. Ogawa and colleagues (1990) showed that images sensitive to T2\* therefore show **blood-oxygenation-level-dependent** (BOLD) contrast.

When a patch of cortex becomes active, local blood flow rises by much more than oxygen consumption does. The blood leaving the patch is then more oxygenated than at rest, deoxyhemoglobin falls, T2\* becomes longer, and the T2\*-weighted signal gets a little *brighter*. The change is small: typically a fraction of a percent to a few percent. We express it as the **percent signal change**

$$\text{PSC} = 100\,\frac{S - S_\text{base}}{S_\text{base}}.$$

For example, if $T_2^*$ rises from 45 ms to 46 ms and TE $=30$ ms, then $S$ goes from $S_0 e^{-30/45} = 0.5134\,S_0$ to $S_0 e^{-30/46} = 0.5209\,S_0$, a change of 1.46%.

The choice of TE matters. The difference between the active and resting signal, $S_0\big(e^{-\mathrm{TE}/T_{2b}^*} - e^{-\mathrm{TE}/T_{2a}^*}\big)$ with $T_{2b}^* > T_{2a}^*$ (b for "blood-oxygenated, active"), is largest at

$$\mathrm{TE}_\text{opt} = \frac{T_{2a}^* T_{2b}^*}{T_{2b}^* - T_{2a}^*}\ln\frac{T_{2b}^*}{T_{2a}^*},$$

which lies close to the tissue's $T_2^*$ itself (45.50 ms for the pair 45 and 46 ms). Too short a TE has little contrast; too long a TE has little signal.

### What BOLD is and is not

BOLD is an indirect, vascular signal. It is better related to synaptic input and local field potentials than to the spiking of single neurons alone, but it is not a direct count of either. It is slow (it lags neural activity by seconds, which is the subject of the next lesson), it is sensitive to the size and arrangement of veins, and it is a relative signal: one scan's raw intensity has arbitrary units, so we always analyze changes relative to a baseline.

### In code

```python
import math

def signal(S0, TE, T2star):
    """Gradient-echo signal (arbitrary units) at echo time TE (ms)."""
    return S0 * math.exp(-TE / T2star)

rest = signal(1000.0, 30.0, 45.0)
active = signal(1000.0, 30.0, 46.0)
print(round(100 * (active - rest) / rest, 2))
```

This prints `1.46`. Real analyses never see $T_2^*$ directly; they see only the time series of $S$ and compute changes in it.

@example
title: How much does the signal change?
problem: A gradient-echo sequence uses TE $=40$ ms and $S_0 = 1000$ (arbitrary units). A voxel's resting $T_2^*$ is 50 ms and rises to 52 ms during activation. Find the two signals and the percent signal change.
step: Resting signal: $1000\,e^{-40/50} = 1000\,e^{-0.8} \approx 449.33$.
step: Active signal: $1000\,e^{-40/52} \approx 463.37$.
step: Percent signal change: $100\,(463.37 - 449.33)/449.33 \approx 3.12\%$.
step: Check the direction: a longer $T_2^*$ means slower decay, so the active signal must be larger; it is.
answer: The signal rises from about 449.3 to 463.4, a change of about $3.12\%$.

@mistakes
- Thinking that active neurons produce more deoxyhemoglobin and so more BOLD signal. The flow increase overshoots the oxygen use, so deoxyhemoglobin falls and the signal rises.
- Treating BOLD as a direct, instantaneous measure of spiking. It is a slow, indirect, vascular signal, and its raw units are arbitrary until you express it relative to a baseline.
- Using the wrong baseline or the wrong units: a percent signal change divides by the baseline signal, not by the active one, and gives a percent, not a fraction.

```python
import math


def best_te(T2_rest, T2_active):
    """Echo time (ms) on a 0.1 ms grid that maximizes BOLD contrast."""
    best_k, best_diff = 10, -1.0
    for k in range(10, 1001):
        te = k / 10
        diff = math.exp(-te / T2_active) - math.exp(-te / T2_rest)
        if diff > best_diff:
            best_k, best_diff = k, diff
    return best_k / 10
```
hint: Loop over the integer `k`, compute `te = k / 10`, and keep the `k` with the largest difference so far.
solution: Evaluate the signal difference on the grid and keep the best. The grid answer should agree with the closed form $T_{2a}T_{2b}\ln(T_{2b}/T_{2a})/(T_{2b}-T_{2a})$ to within half a grid step, which the last test checks.

```python
import math


def tsnr(series):
    """Mean divided by the sample standard deviation."""
    n = len(series)
    mean = sum(series) / n
    var = sum((s - mean) ** 2 for s in series) / (n - 1)
    return mean / math.sqrt(var)
```
hint: Compute the mean, then the sum of squared deviations divided by $n - 1$, then its square root.
solution: The sample variance divides by $n - 1$. Dividing by $n$ instead gives a larger ratio and fails the tests. A voxel with low tSNR needs a larger BOLD effect to be detected.

@quiz self
prompt: A colleague says "the BOLD signal in this region went up, so the neurons there fired more." Explain what BOLD measures, why this inference is not guaranteed, and what other evidence would strengthen it.
hint: Think about the chain from synaptic activity to blood flow to deoxyhemoglobin.
solution: BOLD reflects the local concentration of deoxyhemoglobin, which depends on blood flow, blood volume and oxygen consumption, all coupled to neural activity through a slow vascular response. It tracks synaptic input and local field potentials at least as well as spiking, so a rise can come from input to the region, from inhibitory as well as excitatory activity, or from neuromodulation, and it also varies with vessel anatomy and baseline physiology. So the rise is consistent with more firing but does not prove it. Stronger evidence would come from converging methods, such as electrophysiological recordings in the same region and task (in animals or in patients with recordings made for clinical reasons), or from MEG and EEG signals with matching time courses, together with controls for vascular differences between conditions.
rubric: States that BOLD depends on blood flow, volume and oxygen use, not directly on spikes.
rubric: Gives at least one reason a BOLD increase could occur without more spiking in that region.
rubric: Proposes converging evidence from another measurement or a control.
