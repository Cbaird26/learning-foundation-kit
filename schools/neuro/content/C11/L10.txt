@article
In 1861 Broca examined a patient who could understand speech but could say almost nothing but a single syllable. After the patient died, Broca found a lesion in the left frontal lobe. A few years later Wernicke (1874) described patients with the opposite pattern: fluent speech but poor understanding, with damage farther back. The two cases launched the idea that the cortex has regions specialized for language. How far does that idea hold up?

### The aphasias

**Aphasia** is a language disorder caused by brain damage, not by paralysis of the speech muscles or by hearing loss.

| | Broca's aphasia | Wernicke's aphasia |
|---|---|---|
| classical lesion | left inferior frontal gyrus | left posterior superior temporal cortex |
| speech | effortful, slow, few small words | fluent, normal rhythm, often empty or jumbled |
| comprehension | relatively good for simple speech | poor |
| repetition | impaired | impaired |

The classical model links the two regions by a fiber bundle, the arcuate fasciculus, and predicts a third syndrome, conduction aphasia, with poor repetition when that bundle is damaged. In most right-handed people and in many left-handed people, language depends mainly on the left hemisphere: the neurosurgical Wada test, which temporarily anesthetizes one hemisphere, was used to establish this.

Limits: classical lesion boundaries are crude, since real strokes are large and vary; Broca's patient also had more extensive damage than the inferior frontal gyrus alone; and imaging shows that language engages a **distributed network** in the left frontal, temporal and parietal lobes and their connections, with some right-hemisphere contribution. Aphasias are a strong case that damage to specific regions disrupts specific language functions, but not that a single area performs a function.

### Split-brain studies

The two hemispheres are linked by the **corpus callosum**. In the 1960s, patients whose callosum had been cut to control severe epilepsy were studied by Sperry and Gazzaniga. When a word was flashed to the left visual field, which projects to the right hemisphere, patients could not say what they saw, because speech is controlled by the left hemisphere, yet their left hand, controlled by the right hemisphere, could select the matching object. The experiments show that each hemisphere can have information the other lacks when the callosum is cut. They do not show that people are "left-brained" or "right-brained": in intact brains the hemispheres communicate continuously, and the popular personality types have no support.

### Attention and association cortex

Damage to the right parietal lobe often causes **hemispatial neglect**: patients ignore the left side of space, failing to eat food on the left of a plate, shave the left of the face or draw the left side of a clock, even though their vision is intact. Neglect is not blindness; it shows that parietal cortex contributes to attending to space. Most of the cortex is **association cortex**, between primary sensory and motor areas, which combines information; the prefrontal cortex supports working memory, planning and control, and temporal regions support recognition. A well-studied specialization is the **fusiform face area**, a region that responds more strongly to faces than to objects (Kanwisher, McDermott and Chun, 1997); whether it is specialized for faces or for expertise with fine visual categories is debated.

### Reasoning about localization

A **double dissociation** supports separate systems when two patients show opposite patterns. Patient A fails at task 1 but passes task 2; patient B passes 1 and fails 2. A single dissociation can be explained by task difficulty, so the double is stronger. A **lateralization index** measures hemispheric dominance in imaging data:

$$\text{LI} = \frac{L - R}{L + R},$$

where $L$ and $R$ are the amounts of activation (for example, the number of active voxels) in homologous left and right regions. LI runs from $-1$ (entirely right) to $+1$ (entirely left); values above about $+0.2$ are usually called left-lateralized, though thresholds depend on the method.

@example
title: A lateralization index
problem: In a language task, a participant has 120 active voxels in a left frontal region and 280 in the matching right region. Compute the lateralization index and interpret it.
step: $L = 120$ and $R = 280$, so $L - R = -160$ and $L + R = 400$.
step: $\text{LI} = -160/400 = -0.4$.
step: The negative value means more activation on the right: this participant is right-lateralized for this task in this region. This is an unusual pattern for language, so the researcher would check the data and the region.
answer: $\text{LI} = -0.4$, right-lateralized.

@example
title: Interpreting two patients
problem: Patient A cannot read words aloud but recognizes faces normally; patient B cannot recognize faces but reads words normally. What does this pair of patients show?
step: The deficits are opposite: A fails reading and passes faces, B fails faces and passes reading.
step: This is a double dissociation, which makes it unlikely that one task is just harder than the other.
step: It supports partly separate systems for the two functions, but it does not show that each depends on a single area, or that the systems share no processing.
answer: A double dissociation, which suggests partly separate systems.

@mistakes
- Equating Broca's aphasia with loss of understanding: comprehension of simple speech is relatively spared, whereas fluent Wernicke's aphasia has poor comprehension.
- Concluding from split-brain patients that normal people are left- or right-brained: the studies concern patients with a cut callosum.
- Treating neglect as blindness: the visual system is intact and attention to one side of space is impaired.
- Treating a lesion as the proof that the region does the function: damage to fibers or networks can produce the same deficit.

@quiz self
prompt: Summarize the evidence that Broca's area, or the left inferior frontal region, is specialized for speech production, then give three limits on that conclusion. Say what other evidence would test it.
hint: Use lesion, imaging and the idea of distributed networks, and include the idea of necessity versus sufficiency.
solution: Evidence: patients with lesions in the left inferior frontal region show effortful, nonfluent speech with relatively spared comprehension, and the left hemisphere's dominance for language is supported by the Wada test and imaging, which show frontal activation during speech production. Limits: lesions in these patients are usually larger than the classical area and include underlying white matter, so the deficit may reflect damaged connections; imaging shows that frontal activation occurs in many tasks, so activation does not show specificity for speech production; and language relies on a distributed left-hemisphere network, with right-hemisphere contributions and recovery after damage, so the region is involved but not the only site. Further tests: patients with focal lesions selected by lesion mapping, temporary disruption of the region in healthy people, which would show necessity, and a double dissociation with patients whose damage elsewhere impairs comprehension without impairing production.
