@article
Open a human skull and you find about 1.4 kg of tissue, roughly 2% of body mass, that uses about 20% of the body's energy at rest. How is it organized, and does each region have its own job? The organization is easiest to learn as a nested set of divisions.

### Divisions of the nervous system

The **central nervous system (CNS)** is the brain and spinal cord; the **peripheral nervous system (PNS)** is everything else: nerves and ganglia. The PNS has a **somatic** part, carrying sensory information in and commands to skeletal muscle out, and an **autonomic** part controlling glands, the heart and smooth muscle. The autonomic system has two main branches. The **sympathetic** branch mobilizes the body for action (faster heart rate, dilated pupils); the **parasympathetic** branch, including the vagus nerve, supports resting functions (slower heart rate, digestion). Most organs get input from both. There are 12 pairs of cranial nerves and 31 pairs of spinal nerves.

In the spinal cord, **gray matter** (cell bodies and dendrites) forms a butterfly-shaped core, surrounded by **white matter**, the myelinated axons running up and down. Sensory axons enter through the **dorsal root** and motor axons leave through the **ventral root**.

### Directions and planes

**Rostral** means toward the nose and **caudal** toward the tail; **dorsal** toward the back (top of the head in humans) and **ventral** toward the belly; **medial** toward the midline and **lateral** away from it. A **sagittal** plane divides left from right, a **coronal** plane divides front from back, and a **horizontal** plane divides top from bottom.

### The brain by embryonic divisions

| division | main structures | main functions |
|---|---|---|
| hindbrain | medulla, pons, cerebellum | medulla: breathing, heart rate, reflexes; pons: relays to the cerebellum, sleep; cerebellum: coordination and motor learning |
| midbrain | tectum, tegmentum (including dopamine-producing areas) | orienting reflexes, eye movements, movement and reward |
| forebrain: diencephalon | thalamus, hypothalamus | thalamus: relay for most sensory input to cortex; hypothalamus: homeostasis, hormones, drives |
| forebrain: telencephalon | cerebral cortex, basal ganglia, hippocampus, amygdala | cortex: perception, action, thought; basal ganglia: action selection; hippocampus: memory; amygdala: emotion |

The medulla, pons and midbrain together form the **brainstem**, which also contains nuclei that regulate arousal and carries every long pathway between brain and body. Counts of neurons are surprising: the cerebral cortex has about 16 billion neurons and the cerebellum about 69 billion, so the small cerebellum holds roughly 80% of all neurons.

### The cerebral cortex

The cortex is a folded sheet, about 2.5 mm thick in the human, with a surface area of roughly 2,000 cm$^2$; folding fits it into the skull. The raised folds are **gyri** and the grooves **sulci**. The **central sulcus** separates the **frontal lobe** from the **parietal lobe**, and the **lateral sulcus** separates both from the **temporal lobe**; the **occipital lobe** lies at the back. Primary areas include:

- **primary motor cortex** (precentral gyrus, frontal lobe) and **primary somatosensory cortex** (postcentral gyrus, parietal lobe), each with a body map: Penfield and Boldrey (1937) charted them by electrical stimulation of conscious patients during surgery;
- **primary visual cortex** (occipital lobe) and **primary auditory cortex** (upper temporal lobe).

The frontal lobe also contains prefrontal cortex, important for planning, working memory and control of behavior. Most cortex is not primary sensory or motor: association areas combine information.

### Evidence for localization, and its limits

**Lesions** (damage by stroke, injury or surgery) show what a region is necessary for; **stimulation** shows what a region can produce; **recording and imaging** show what activity accompanies a function (Lesson 12). A famous historical case is Phineas Gage, a railroad worker who in 1848 survived an iron rod passing through the front of his brain and was reported to show changes in personality; accounts of his later life vary and are not well documented, so the case is a suggestion about the frontal lobe, not a precise measurement.

Localization is real but coarse. A lesion that abolishes a function shows that the region is necessary for it, not that the region alone performs it: the damaged region may be a relay or a connection, and other regions may compensate. Functions are carried out by distributed networks, and the same region contributes to several. **Double dissociations** (lesion A impairs function 1 but not 2, lesion B impairs 2 but not 1) give stronger evidence that two functions depend on partly separate systems, but even they do not show a single "center."

@example
title: The brain's disproportionate energy use
problem: A person's brain has mass 1.4 kg and the person's body 70 kg. If the brain uses 20% of the body's resting energy, how many times more energy per kilogram does brain tissue use than the body average?
step: The brain's share of mass is $1.4/70 = 0.02$, or 2%.
step: The brain's share of energy is 20%, or 0.20.
step: Energy per kilogram relative to the average: $0.20/0.02 = 10$.
answer: About 10 times the body average per kilogram.

@example
title: Neurons by region
problem: Using 86 billion neurons in the whole human brain, 16 billion in the cerebral cortex and 69 billion in the cerebellum, what percent of all neurons are in the cerebellum, and what percent are in neither structure?
step: Cerebellum: $69/86 = 0.802$, or 80.2%.
step: Cortex: $16/86 = 18.6\%$.
step: Neither: $86 - 69 - 16 = 1$ billion, so about $1.2\%$ lies in the rest of the brain (brainstem, thalamus, basal ganglia and others); with these rounded counts the true share is a little under 1%.
answer: 80.2% in the cerebellum, about 1.2% elsewhere.

@mistakes
- Treating a region as the "center" for a function: lesions and activation show necessity or involvement, not that the region works alone.
- Mixing up sympathetic and parasympathetic effects: remember action for sympathetic (faster heart, dilated pupils) and rest functions for parasympathetic (slower heart, digestion).
- Confusing dorsal and ventral roots: sensory information comes in dorsally; motor commands leave ventrally.
- Treating the cerebellum as minor because of its size: it holds about four in five of the brain's neurons.

@quiz self
prompt: A patient with damage to region X cannot do task T. A classmate concludes that X is the brain's center for T. Explain why this conclusion is not justified, name two other explanations, and describe the evidence that would strengthen the claim that X is specialized for T, including a double dissociation.
hint: Think about what a lesion shows (necessity) and what it does not (sufficiency, exclusivity).
solution: A lesion shows that X, or fibers passing through it, is necessary for T under the patient's conditions; it does not show that X alone carries out T. Other explanations: X may be a relay or contain fibers connecting other regions that do the work; the damage may extend beyond X; T may depend on a network in which X is one member; or the deficit may reflect a more general impairment such as attention. Evidence for specialization includes a double dissociation, in which patients with damage to X are impaired on T but not on task U, and patients with damage to Y are impaired on U but not T; converging methods such as imaging showing that X is active during T in healthy people and stimulation or temporary inactivation of X disrupting T; and tests with matched difficulty so that T is not just harder than U.
rubric: States that a lesion shows necessity, not that X alone performs T.
rubric: Names at least two alternative explanations such as fibers of passage, network involvement, or extent of damage.
rubric: Describes a double dissociation correctly and at least one converging method.
