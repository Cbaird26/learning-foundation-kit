@article
A fertilized egg becomes a brain of tens of billions of neurons wired with trillions of connections, and the genome is far too small to specify each connection. How does the wiring come about, and why is a kitten, or a child, much more sensitive to its early experience than an adult?

### Building the brain

The nervous system begins as a sheet of ectoderm that folds into the **neural tube**; its front end swells into the forebrain, midbrain and hindbrain. The main stages overlap:

1. **Neurogenesis:** stem cells lining the tube divide and produce neurons and glia; most cortical neurons are born before birth.
2. **Migration:** young neurons move along guides to their final positions; the cortex fills from the inside out, with later-born neurons passing earlier ones.
3. **Axon guidance:** a **growth cone** at the tip of each axon follows attractive and repulsive chemical cues to its targets.
4. **Synapse formation and refinement:** more neurons and synapses are produced than survive. In many regions about half of the neurons die by programmed cell death, and synapses that are active together are retained while others are **pruned**, so activity and experience sculpt the wiring.
5. **Myelination:** continues for years; the prefrontal cortex matures last, into the twenties.

Experience-independent mechanisms (molecular cues) set up the rough plan, and activity-dependent mechanisms refine it. Developmental disorders can follow disruption at any stage.

### Critical periods

Hubel and Wiesel found that closing one eye of a kitten for the first weeks of life shifts the responses of neurons in visual cortex toward the open eye, and the closed eye remains functionally weak even after it is reopened. The same deprivation in an adult cat has little effect. The effect is in the cortex, not the eye: the retina of the deprived eye is intact. This is a **critical period**: a time when experience has an unusually strong, lasting effect on circuit wiring. In humans, a cataract or misaligned eyes in early childhood can cause **amblyopia**, a reduced acuity that is hard to reverse if untreated; hence early eye screening. Critical periods end partly through molecular brakes on plasticity, and research shows that some can be partially reopened under experimental conditions, but how far this holds in humans is not established.

### Learning the sounds and structure of language

Werker and Tees (1984) showed that infants about six to eight months old distinguish speech sounds that are not used in their parents' language, and that by about ten to twelve months this ability declines for non-native contrasts while it improves for native ones: **perceptual narrowing**. Saffran, Aslin and Newport (1996) showed that eight-month-old infants listening for a couple of minutes to a continuous stream of syllables can pick out which syllable sequences were "words" by the **transitional probability** between syllables,

$$\text{TP}(B \mid A) = \frac{\text{count}(AB)}{\text{count}(A)},$$

which is high inside words and lower across word boundaries. This **statistical learning** appears in several domains and species, but it does not show that language is learned by statistics alone; how much of language is innate is a contested question. Likewise, young children succeed at false-belief tasks (predicting where a person will look for an object that was moved without their knowledge, Wimmer and Perner, 1983) at about four to five years, but whether younger children have an implicit understanding is debated.

### Plasticity and aging in adults

The adult brain remains plastic: after amputation, the cortical area that represented a limb is taken over by neighboring body parts, and practice enlarges representations. Learning involves synaptic changes (Lesson 7). **Adult neurogenesis**, the birth of new neurons, is well established in the hippocampal dentate gyrus and olfactory system of adult rodents; whether it continues in adult humans, and how much, is debated, with studies reaching different conclusions.

Normal aging brings gradual loss of brain volume, slower processing and lower working memory, while vocabulary and general knowledge are often preserved. **Dementia**, such as Alzheimer's disease (Lesson 12), is a disease, not normal aging. Lifelong activity and education are associated with better outcomes; the causal contribution of each is still studied.

@example
title: Transitional probabilities in a syllable stream
problem: In a speech stream the syllable pair "bi da" occurs 60 times and "bi" occurs 60 times in total; the syllable "ku" occurs 60 times, and it is followed by "pa" in 20 of those cases. Compute the two transitional probabilities and say which pair looks like it is inside a word.
step: $\text{TP}(\text{da} \mid \text{bi}) = 60/60 = 1.0$.
step: $\text{TP}(\text{pa} \mid \text{ku}) = 20/60 = 0.33$.
step: A high transitional probability suggests that the syllables belong together; a drop marks a likely word boundary. So "bi da" looks like a word-internal pair and "ku pa" crosses a boundary.
answer: 1.0 for bi-da, about 0.33 for ku-pa.

@example
title: Ocular dominance after deprivation
problem: In a sample of 80 cortical cells recorded from a kitten that had one eye closed early in life, 60 respond better to the open eye, 12 respond better to the closed eye and 8 respond equally. What percent respond better to the closed eye?
step: Divide: $12/80 = 0.15$.
step: Convert to percent: 15%. The fraction driven mainly by the deprived eye is small, which indicates a shift toward the open eye.
step: Such a number is compared with a control group of animals with both eyes open, where a larger share would respond to each eye.
answer: 15%.

@mistakes
- Believing infants' brains only add synapses: pruning and cell death are central to development.
- Treating a critical period as a sudden closing: sensitivity declines gradually, and the length depends on the function.
- Assuming that adult human neurogenesis is settled: it is established in adult rodents and debated in humans.
- Equating normal aging with dementia: most older adults keep much of their cognitive function, and dementia is a disease.

@quiz self
prompt: Design a looking-time experiment to test whether infants of about eight months learn word-like units from the transitional probabilities between syllables. State the stimuli, the procedure, the measure, the prediction and one control. The study must be low risk and voluntary with parental consent.
hint: Use familiarization, then test words against non-words or part-words, and measure how long infants look toward a sound source.
solution: Familiarization: play a continuous stream of syllables made from several three-syllable nonsense words in random order with no pauses or stress cues, so that transitional probabilities are 1.0 inside words and lower between words, for two to three minutes. Test: play, in separate trials, the words and part-words (sequences that occurred across word boundaries but with the same syllables), with counterbalanced order, and measure how long each infant looks toward the speaker, with an experimenter blind to the trial type. Prediction: if infants learn the statistics, they will discriminate the two kinds of test items, shown as a reliable difference in looking time (the direction can differ between studies). Control: a second group hears a different set of words built from the same syllables so that part-words and words swap roles, which shows that the difference comes from exposure to the statistics and not from the sounds. Procedures follow ethics review, with parental consent and the option to stop whenever the infant is fussy.
