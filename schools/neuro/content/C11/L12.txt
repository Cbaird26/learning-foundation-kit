@article
Brain disorders are common and costly, and neuroscientists learn about the healthy brain partly by studying them. What do we know about the main disorders, and what can the available methods tell us about the brain?

### Disorders at textbook level

Diagnosis and treatment of every condition below belong to clinicians. Classifications change: the DSM-5-TR was published in 2022 and the ICD-11 came into effect on 1 January 2022, so check the current versions.

- **Stroke:** loss of blood supply to part of the brain, from a blocked artery (ischemic, the more common type) or a bleed (hemorrhagic). Deficits follow the region (aphasia after left-hemisphere damage, Lesson 10), and early treatment improves outcomes.
- **Epilepsy:** recurrent seizures from abnormal, excessive and synchronous neuronal firing, often visible in the EEG; causes range from genetic to injury.
- **Alzheimer's disease:** the commonest cause of dementia, with extracellular amyloid plaques and intracellular tau tangles, loss of synapses and neurons, beginning in the entorhinal cortex and hippocampus and so producing early memory loss.
- **Parkinson's disease and Huntington's disease** (Lesson 6): degeneration of the substantia nigra dopamine neurons and of the striatum, respectively.
- **Psychiatric conditions** such as depression, schizophrenia and autism involve many genes, development and environment. Brain differences are found on average, and drug mechanisms are known, but no biological test diagnoses them, and how well current categories reflect distinct biological conditions, and how much brain-based explanations can deliver, are debated.

### Methods: what each can and cannot show

| method | spatial resolution | temporal resolution | what it can show | main limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| lesion study | the damaged region (coarse) | permanent | necessity | damage is large and compensation occurs |
| single-neuron recording | one cell | sub-millisecond | what a neuron encodes | few cells; invasive, so mostly animals |
| EEG, MEG | centimeters | milliseconds | timing of activity | poor localization |
| fMRI (BOLD) | millimeters | seconds | where activity changes | indirect, slow |
| TMS (magnetic pulses) | about a centimeter | milliseconds to minutes | causal effect of disrupting a region | superficial cortex, nonspecific spread |
| optogenetics | cell type | milliseconds | causal role of defined cells | mostly in animals |

The **BOLD signal** (Ogawa and colleagues, 1990) is a change in the magnetic signal from blood oxygenation. Active neurons raise local blood flow by more than their oxygen use, so oxygenated blood increases, and it peaks about 4 to 6 seconds after the neural activity. It reflects synaptic input and local processing more than spiking itself, and it is indirect. **Forward inference** (this task activates this region) is sound; **reverse inference** (this region is active, so the person used this process) is weak when the region participates in many tasks. Multiple comparisons are a further risk: an image tests tens of thousands of voxels, so the threshold must be corrected, for instance by dividing the significance level by the number of tests (**Bonferroni correction**); circular analysis (Kriegeskorte and colleagues, 2009) and cluster inference failures (Eklund, Nichols and Knutsson, 2016) show how analysis choices can create false findings. The patch clamp (Neher and Sakmann, 1976) and optogenetics (Boyden and colleagues, 2005) are methods for cells and circuits.

Converging evidence is the rule: a claim is strong when lesions (necessity), imaging (involvement) and stimulation (causal effect) agree. Research with people is reviewed by institutional review boards under rules such as the Common Rule, and research with animals follows the 3Rs of replacement, reduction and refinement. The Declaration of Helsinki was last revised in October 2024; check current rules.

### Spike data in code

Recordings from neurons over repeated trials are summarized by the **peristimulus time histogram (PSTH)**: the mean firing rate in successive time bins aligned to the stimulus. The **Fano factor**, variance over mean of the spike counts across trials, measures variability: about 1 for a Poisson process.

```python
def psth(trials, t_start, t_end, bin_ms):
    """Mean rate (Hz) per bin; trials hold spike times in ms."""
    n_bins = int(round((t_end - t_start) / bin_ms))
    counts = [0] * n_bins
    for spikes in trials:
        for t in spikes:
            if t_start <= t < t_end:
                counts[int((t - t_start) // bin_ms)] += 1
    return [c / len(trials) / (bin_ms / 1000) for c in counts]

print([round(r, 1) for r in psth([[12, 35, 48], [8, 41], [30, 44, 47]], 0, 60, 20)])
```

This prints `[33.3, 33.3, 66.7]`.

@example
title: Reading a PSTH by hand
problem: Four trials have these spike times (ms after stimulus onset): [4, 22], [9, 25, 27], [3, 8, 26], [15, 29]. Use two 15 ms bins from 0 to 30 ms (half-open intervals) and compute the mean rate in each bin.
step: Bin [0, 15): spikes at 4, 9, 3 and 8, so 4 spikes. Bin [15, 30): 22, 25, 27, 26, 15 and 29, so 6 spikes (15 belongs here, because a spike at 15 is not less than 15).
step: Rate = count / trials / bin width in seconds: bin 1: $4/4/0.015 = 66.7$ Hz; bin 2: $6/4/0.015 = 100$ Hz.
step: The rate rises from about 67 Hz to 100 Hz, the shape of an increasing response.
answer: 66.7 Hz and 100 Hz.

@example
title: A Bonferroni threshold
problem: A study tests 100,000 voxels at a family-wise level of 0.05. What per-test threshold does Bonferroni give?
step: Divide: $0.05 / 100{,}000 = 5 \times 10^{-7}$.
step: Each voxel must reach $p < 5 \times 10^{-7}$. The method is conservative because neighboring voxels are correlated, and methods that use this correlation have more power.
answer: $5 \times 10^{-7}$.

@mistakes
- Treating BOLD as a direct count of spikes: it is a slow, indirect blood-oxygen signal related to synaptic activity.
- Concluding from activity in a region that a particular mental process occurred (reverse inference): the same region is active in many tasks.
- Forgetting multiple comparisons in imaging, which yields many false positives at an uncorrected threshold.
- Treating a psychiatric diagnosis as a single brain lesion: the evidence points to many contributing causes and varying categories.

model:
```python
def fano_factor(trials, t_start, t_end):
    """Sample variance over mean of the spike counts across trials."""
    if len(trials) < 2:
        return None
    counts = [sum(1 for t in spikes if t_start <= t < t_end)
              for spikes in trials]
    mean = sum(counts) / len(counts)
    if mean == 0:
        return None
    var = sum((c - mean) ** 2 for c in counts) / (len(counts) - 1)
    return var / mean
```
hint: First reduce each trial to a count in the half-open window, then compute the mean and the sample variance of those counts.
solution: The half-open window excludes a spike exactly at `t_end`. In the last test the counts are 2, 1 and 3, so the mean is 2 and the sample variance 1, giving 0.5; dividing by `n` instead of `n - 1` would give a different value.

@quiz self
prompt: Compare what three methods (a lesion study, fMRI and single-neuron recording) can and cannot show about the function of a brain region. For each, state one claim it supports and one it cannot support, and say how you would combine them.
hint: Use necessity, involvement and neural coding, and mention resolution and the logic of inference.
solution: A lesion study shows what a region is necessary for, since a deficit follows its damage; it cannot show that the region alone performs the function, because damage may include fibers, other regions may compensate, and large lesions blur the localization. fMRI shows where activity changes during a task with millimeter resolution in healthy people, giving evidence of involvement; it is indirect and slow and cannot show necessity, and reverse inference from activation to process is weak. Single-neuron recording shows what individual cells encode, for instance tuning to a stimulus, at millisecond resolution; it samples few cells, is mostly done in animals, and does not show that the cells are necessary. Combining them: fMRI in humans can find a candidate region, recordings in animals can show what its neurons encode, and lesions or temporary stimulation test necessity; a claim backed by all three is more secure than one backed by a single method.
