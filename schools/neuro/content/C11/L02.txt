@article
Every neuron is a tiny battery. Insert a fine glass electrode and the inside of the cell reads about $-70$ mV compared with the outside. Then, for about a millisecond, the voltage shoots up to roughly $+40$ mV and falls back: an **action potential**, or spike. Where do the $-70$ and the $+40$ come from?

### Ions on two sides of a membrane

Ions are unevenly distributed. Typical mammalian values (mM) are:

| ion | outside | inside |
|---|---|---|
| Na$^+$ | 145 | 15 |
| K$^+$ | 5 | 140 |
| Cl$^-$ | 110 | 10 |

The **sodium–potassium pump** keeps this up: using ATP it moves 3 Na$^+$ out and 2 K$^+$ in per cycle. Apart from the pump, ions cross the membrane only through channels, and the membrane at rest has many open K$^+$ channels.

### The Nernst equation

If the membrane were permeable only to K$^+$, K$^+$ would leave the cell down its concentration gradient, leaving negative charge behind, until the electrical pull inward exactly balances the chemical push outward. At that voltage there is no net flow: the **equilibrium potential**. For an ion of charge $z$ it is the **Nernst equation**

$$E_{\text{ion}} = \frac{RT}{zF}\ln\frac{[\text{ion}]_{\text{out}}}{[\text{ion}]_{\text{in}}}.$$

At 37 °C, for a monovalent ion, $\frac{RT}{F}\ln 10 \approx 61.5$ mV, so $E = \frac{61.5\ \text{mV}}{z}\log_{10}\frac{[\text{out}]}{[\text{in}]}$. From the table: $E_{\text{K}} = 61.5\log_{10}(5/140) \approx -89$ mV and $E_{\text{Na}} = 61.5\log_{10}(145/15) \approx +61$ mV. The equation holds only for a membrane permeable to one ion at equilibrium.

### The resting potential

The real membrane is permeable to several ions. The **Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz (GHK) equation** weights each ion by its permeability $P$:

$$V_m = 61.5\log_{10}\frac{P_{\text{K}}[\text{K}]_{o} + P_{\text{Na}}[\text{Na}]_{o} + P_{\text{Cl}}[\text{Cl}]_{i}}{P_{\text{K}}[\text{K}]_{i} + P_{\text{Na}}[\text{Na}]_{i} + P_{\text{Cl}}[\text{Cl}]_{o}} \text{ mV}.$$

Chloride appears with inside and outside swapped because it is an anion. With $P_{\text{K}}:P_{\text{Na}}:P_{\text{Cl}} = 1:0.04:0.45$ the table values give $V_m \approx -67$ mV: near $E_{\text{K}}$ because potassium dominates permeability, pulled slightly upward by a small sodium leak. If $P_{\text{Na}}$ were huge the potential would swing toward $E_{\text{Na}}$, which is what happens in a spike. The resting potential is a steady state, not an equilibrium: small Na$^+$ and K$^+$ leaks flow continually and the pump offsets them.

### The action potential

The **Hodgkin–Huxley model** (1952), built from voltage-clamp measurements on the giant axon of the squid, explains the spike with voltage-gated channels.

1. **Depolarization to threshold** (about $-55$ mV in many neurons): some Na$^+$ channels open.
2. **Rising phase:** Na$^+$ entry depolarizes the membrane further, opening more Na$^+$ channels, a regenerative loop, and $V_m$ heads toward $E_{\text{Na}}$, peaking near $+40$ mV.
3. **Falling phase:** Na$^+$ channels **inactivate** within about a millisecond, and delayed voltage-gated K$^+$ channels open, so K$^+$ leaves and $V_m$ falls.
4. **Undershoot:** K$^+$ channels close slowly, so $V_m$ dips below rest, nearer $E_{\text{K}}$, before recovering.

Because Na$^+$ channels stay inactivated for a short time, there is an **absolute refractory period** (no second spike is possible, roughly 1–2 ms) followed by a **relative refractory period** (a stronger stimulus is needed); this limits firing to at most several hundred hertz in most neurons and prevents spikes running backward. Spikes are **all-or-none**: once threshold is crossed, the shape does not depend on the stimulus. Stimulus strength is conveyed by the **rate** and pattern of spikes. The toxin tetrodotoxin blocks voltage-gated Na$^+$ channels and abolishes spikes, leaving the resting potential nearly unchanged.

### Conduction

A spike regenerates itself along the axon because current flowing ahead of it depolarizes the next patch to threshold. In myelinated axons, current flows passively beneath the insulation and the spike is regenerated at nodes of Ranvier, so it appears to jump from node to node (**saltatory conduction**): faster, and cheaper in ions pumped.

### Limits

Hodgkin–Huxley describes one cell type at one temperature; mammalian neurons have many more channel types, giving bursting, adaptation and spikes in dendrites. Equilibrium potentials depend on temperature and on concentrations that change with activity.

```python
import math

def nernst(z, c_out, c_in, kT=61.5):
    """Equilibrium potential (mV) of an ion of valence z at 37 C."""
    return kT / z * math.log10(c_out / c_in)

print(round(nernst(1, 5, 140), 1))
```

This prints `-89.0`.

title: Equilibrium potentials and driving forces
problem: A neuron has K$^+$ at 5 mM outside and 140 mM inside, and Na$^+$ at 145 mM outside and 15 mM inside. At rest $V_m = -70$ mV. Find $E_{\text{Na}}$ and the driving force on Na$^+$, $V_m - E_{\text{Na}}$, and say which way Na$^+$ moves when channels open.
step: Apply Nernst: $E_{\text{Na}} = 61.5\log_{10}(145/15) = 61.5 \times 0.985 \approx +60.6$ mV.
step: Driving force: $V_m - E_{\text{Na}} = -70 - 60.6 = -130.6$ mV.
step: A negative value means the net force pushes the cation inward: Na$^+$ flows into the cell, depolarizing it. The driving force on K$^+$ is only $-70 - (-89) = +19$ mV, outward, much smaller.
answer: $E_{\text{Na}} \approx +60.6$ mV; driving force $-130.6$ mV, inward.

@example
title: Resting potential from permeabilities
problem: Using the same concentrations, find $V_m$ from the GHK equation if $P_{\text{K}}:P_{\text{Na}}:P_{\text{Cl}} = 1:0.05:0$.
step: Numerator: $1(5) + 0.05(145) = 5 + 7.25 = 12.25$.
step: Denominator: $1(140) + 0.05(15) = 140 + 0.75 = 140.75$.
step: $V_m = 61.5\log_{10}(12.25/140.75) = 61.5 \times (-1.0603) \approx -65.2$ mV.
step: Sodium leak raises $V_m$ above $E_{\text{K}} = -89$ mV, while it is still far below $E_{\text{Na}}$.
answer: About $-65.2$ mV.

@mistakes
- Treating the resting potential as the equilibrium potential of potassium: it is near $E_{\text{K}}$ but a small Na$^+$ permeability makes it a steady state with small continual flows.
- Swapping inside and outside in the Nernst equation, or in the chloride term of GHK, which flips the sign.
- Thinking a stronger stimulus makes a bigger spike: spikes are all-or-none, and intensity is carried by the firing rate.
- Believing the sodium–potassium pump produces each spike: spikes use ions flowing through channels, and the pump restores gradients over longer times.

```python
import math


def ghk_voltage(perm, out, inn):
    """Resting potential (mV) from the GHK voltage equation at 37 C."""
    top = (perm['K'] * out['K'] + perm['Na'] * out['Na']
           + perm['Cl'] * inn['Cl'])
    bottom = (perm['K'] * inn['K'] + perm['Na'] * inn['Na']
              + perm['Cl'] * out['Cl'])
    return 61.5 * math.log10(top / bottom)
```
hint: Build the numerator from K and Na outside plus Cl inside, and the denominator from K and Na inside plus Cl outside, each weighted by its permeability.
solution: Chloride's concentrations are swapped because it carries negative charge. The checks include a pure-sodium membrane, which must give the sodium equilibrium potential, and a chloride-permeable case that fails if the swap is forgotten.
