@article
In 1921 Loewi stimulated the vagus nerve of one frog heart, which slowed it, and collected the fluid bathing that heart. When he applied the fluid to a second heart, the second heart slowed too. Something chemical had been released by the nerve and had carried the message: **chemical transmission**. The gap between neurons, which Sherrington named the **synapse** in 1897, is crossed by molecules.

### Steps of transmission

1. An action potential invades the **presynaptic terminal** and opens voltage-gated Ca$^{2+}$ channels.
2. Ca$^{2+}$ entry triggers **vesicles** filled with transmitter to fuse with the membrane (a process mediated by the SNARE proteins) and release their contents by exocytosis.
3. Transmitter diffuses across the cleft (about 20 nm, a fraction of a millisecond) and binds **receptors** on the postsynaptic membrane.
4. Receptor activation changes the postsynaptic membrane potential.
5. The transmitter is removed: by **reuptake** into terminals or glia through transporters, by enzymatic breakdown (acetylcholine is broken down by acetylcholinesterase), or by diffusion.

### Quanta

Fatt and Katz (1952), and del Castillo and Katz (1954), recording from the frog neuromuscular junction, found tiny spontaneous potentials of nearly equal size even without nerve stimulation, and evoked responses that came in whole multiples of that size. Transmitter is released in packets, **quanta**, each corresponding to the contents of about one vesicle. If a terminal has $n$ release sites, each releasing one quantum with probability $p$ and each quantum producing an amplitude $q$, then the mean response is $\mathbb{E}[\text{response}] = n\,p\,q$. The number of quanta is binomial with variance $n p (1-p)$, so trial-to-trial variability tells researchers about $n$ and $p$ separately.

### Receptors, EPSPs and IPSPs

**Ionotropic receptors** are ligand-gated ion channels: fast (a millisecond or so) and brief. **Metabotropic receptors** act through G proteins and second messengers: slower (tens of milliseconds to seconds), longer lasting and modulatory.

| transmitter | main receptors | usual effect |
|---|---|---|
| glutamate | AMPA, NMDA (ionotropic); metabotropic | excite (main fast excitatory transmitter in the brain) |
| GABA | GABA$_A$ (ionotropic, Cl$^-$); GABA$_B$ (metabotropic) | inhibit (main inhibitory transmitter in the brain) |
| glycine | ionotropic, Cl$^-$ | inhibit, especially in the spinal cord and brainstem |
| acetylcholine | nicotinic (ionotropic); muscarinic (metabotropic) | excites muscle at the neuromuscular junction; modulates the brain |
| dopamine, serotonin, norepinephrine | mostly metabotropic | modulate |

A transmitter does not itself excite or inhibit: the receptor's channel does. Opening channels with a reversal potential near 0 mV (AMPA receptors pass Na$^+$ and K$^+$) moves $V_m$ upward, an **excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP)**. Opening Cl$^-$ channels with $E_{\text{Cl}}$ near $-65$ mV holds $V_m$ near that value and resists depolarization: an **inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP)**. PSPs are **graded** and decay with distance, unlike spikes. The neuron **integrates**: **temporal summation** adds PSPs that arrive close together in time, **spatial summation** adds PSPs from different synapses, and the sum at the axon hillock must cross threshold to produce a spike.

### Drugs and toxins

At textbook level, drugs act at a small number of steps.

- **Receptor agonists and antagonists:** curare blocks the nicotinic receptors at the neuromuscular junction, causing paralysis; nicotine activates nicotinic receptors; benzodiazepines enhance the action of GABA at GABA$_A$ receptors, which reduces neuronal firing.
- **Release:** botulinum toxin blocks acetylcholine release.
- **Removal:** cocaine blocks the dopamine transporter, prolonging dopamine's action; selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors block the serotonin transporter; physostigmine-like drugs block acetylcholinesterase.
- **Precursors:** L-DOPA is converted to dopamine and is used in Parkinson's disease (Lesson 6).

Clinical use of any such drug is a matter for clinicians. The effects of a drug in a real brain depend on dose, brain region, receptor subtypes and adaptation over days; "this drug raises neurotransmitter X" is a description of one step, not an explanation of a mood or disorder.

### Limits

The simple scheme omits transmitters released from dendrites, co-release of several transmitters, electrical synapses, and plastic changes in $n$, $p$ and $q$ over time. Quantal numbers are best established at the neuromuscular junction; at central synapses, which may have one or a few release sites, estimating $n$, $p$ and $q$ is harder.

@example
title: Mean and variability of a quantal response
problem: A terminal has $n = 20$ release sites, each releasing a quantum with probability $p = 0.3$ per spike, and each quantum depolarizes the postsynaptic cell by $q = 0.4$ mV. Find the mean response and its standard deviation across spikes.
step: Mean: $n p q = 20 \times 0.3 \times 0.4 = 2.4$ mV.
step: The number of quanta released has variance $n p (1-p) = 20 \times 0.3 \times 0.7 = 4.2$, so its standard deviation is $\sqrt{4.2} \approx 2.05$ quanta.
step: Each quantum counts $q = 0.4$ mV, so the response has standard deviation $2.05 \times 0.4 \approx 0.82$ mV.
answer: Mean 2.4 mV, standard deviation about 0.82 mV.

@example
title: Temporal summation
problem: An EPSP peaks at 1.0 mV and decays exponentially with time constant 20 ms. A second identical EPSP begins 10 ms after the first. Assuming linear addition, what is the peak voltage change after the second?
step: At 10 ms the first EPSP has decayed to $1.0\,e^{-10/20} = 0.607$ mV.
step: The second EPSP adds its own 1.0 mV: $1.0 + 0.607 = 1.607$ mV.
step: A single EPSP reaches only 1.0 mV, so closely spaced inputs sum to more; if the interval were 100 ms, the first would have decayed to $1.0\,e^{-5} \approx 0.007$ mV and almost no summation would occur.
answer: About 1.61 mV.

@mistakes
- Calling a transmitter excitatory or inhibitory in itself: the receptor decides (acetylcholine excites at the neuromuscular junction through nicotinic receptors and slows the heart through muscarinic ones).
- Treating an EPSP as a small action potential: an EPSP is graded, local and decays with distance, while a spike is all-or-none.
- Explaining a psychiatric condition by a single "chemical imbalance": a drug's effect on one transmitter step does not by itself show that this step causes the condition.
- Forgetting that release needs Ca$^{2+}$ entering the terminal: with no Ca$^{2+}$ entry, spikes arrive but no transmitter is released.

@practice num
prompt: A neuron rests at $-70$ mV with threshold at $-55$ mV. Assume that PSPs add linearly. Each active excitatory synapse contributes $+1.5$ mV and each active inhibitory synapse contributes $-2.0$ mV. If 2 inhibitory synapses are active at the same moment, what is the smallest whole number of excitatory synapses that must be active at the same moment to reach threshold?
answer: 13
unit: synapses
check: ceil((15 + 2 * 2.0) / 1.5)
hint: The net depolarization must reach 15 mV; add back what the inhibition removes.
solution: The excitation must supply $15 + 4 = 19$ mV, and $19/1.5 = 12.67$, so 13 excitatory synapses are needed (12 give only 18 mV).

@quiz num
prompt: Three identical EPSPs of 1.2 mV peak amplitude arrive at one synapse 10 ms apart, each decaying exponentially with time constant 15 ms. Assuming linear addition, what is the total depolarization at the peak of the third EPSP, in millivolts to two decimal places?
answer: 2.13
tol: 0.01
unit: mV
check: 1.2 * (1 + exp(-10 / 15) + exp(-20 / 15))
hint: At the third peak, the first EPSP is 20 ms old and the second is 10 ms old.
solution: $1.2\,(1 + e^{-10/15} + e^{-20/15}) = 1.2\,(1 + 0.5134 + 0.2636) = 1.2 \times 1.7770 \approx 2.13$ mV.

@quiz self
prompt: Design an experiment, using intracellular recording from a postsynaptic cell, to show that Ca$^{2+}$ entry into the presynaptic terminal is required for transmitter release. State the manipulation, the measurement, the expected result, and a control showing that the postsynaptic cell was still able to respond.

solution: Stimulate the presynaptic axon at a steady rate and record the postsynaptic potential. Lower the extracellular Ca$^{2+}$ concentration, or apply a blocker of voltage-gated Ca$^{2+}$ channels. If Ca$^{2+}$ entry triggers release, the postsynaptic potential shrinks or vanishes, although a presynaptic recording shows that the spike still reaches the terminal. Controls: restoring normal Ca$^{2+}$ restores the response (reversibility), and applying the transmitter directly to the postsynaptic cell, for example from a nearby pipette, still evokes a normal response, showing that receptors and postsynaptic membrane work and that the failure lies at release. A stronger design also graded Ca$^{2+}$ levels and showed that the response grows steeply with Ca$^{2+}$ concentration.
