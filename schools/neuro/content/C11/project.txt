@review
**Week by week.**

1. **Neurons and glia.** Dendrites, soma, axon hillock, axon, terminals; neuron doctrine (Cajal) and its amendment by gap junctions; astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, Schwann cells, microglia; conduction time $t = L/v$ and velocity from latency differences.
2. **Electrical signals.** Nernst: $E = (61.5/z)\log_{10}([\text{out}]/[\text{in}])$ mV at 37 °C; GHK weights by permeability (chloride inside and outside swapped); action potential phases, inactivation, refractoriness, all-or-none, saltatory conduction.
3. **Synapses.** Ca$^{2+}$ entry, vesicle release, receptors (ionotropic fast, metabotropic slow), EPSP and IPSP, summation, quantal mean $npq$ and variance $np(1-p)$, drug actions on release, receptors and removal.
4. **Organization.** CNS and PNS, sympathetic and parasympathetic, forebrain, midbrain and hindbrain, lobes and primary areas, lesion logic and double dissociation.
5. **Senses.** Transduction, receptive fields, retina to LGN to V1, Weber's law $\Delta I/I = k$, Stevens $\psi = k\phi^a$, $d' = z(H) - z(F)$ and $c = -\tfrac12(z(H)+z(F))$.
6. **Movement.** Motor unit, neuromuscular junction, stretch reflex, corticospinal tract, basal ganglia (Parkinson's disease), cerebellum (ataxia).
7. **Learning and memory.** Memory systems, H.M., LTP and the NMDA receptor, Rescorla-Wagner $\Delta V = \alpha\beta(\lambda - \sum V)$ and blocking, forgetting $e^{-t/S}$.
8. **Emotion and reward.** Amygdala and fear conditioning, HPA axis, dopamine and the TD error $\delta = r + \gamma V(s') - V(s)$, competing views of dopamine.
9. **Sleep and consciousness.** EEG rhythms, sleep stages, SCN and circadian period, homeostatic pressure, no consensus on consciousness.
10. **Language and cortex.** Broca and Wernicke, split-brain studies, neglect, lateralization index $(L-R)/(L+R)$.
11. **Development and plasticity.** Neural tube to myelination, pruning, critical periods, perceptual narrowing, transitional probability, adult neurogenesis debated.
12. **Disorders and methods.** Stroke, epilepsy, Alzheimer's, resolution trade-offs, BOLD, forward and reverse inference, PSTH, Fano factor, Bonferroni.

**Self-check.** Can you (a) compute an equilibrium potential and say why rest lies near $E_{\text{K}}$; (b) explain what a lesion shows and does not show; (c) trace a sensation and a movement through the nervous system; (d) apply Rescorla-Wagner and TD updates by hand; (e) say which method answers which question; (f) state what is established and what is debated about dopamine, adult neurogenesis and consciousness?

**Common errors.** Swapping inside and outside in Nernst or the chloride term in GHK; treating an EPSP as all-or-none; calling a region the center of a function; treating dopamine as the pleasure chemical; confusing declarative and nondeclarative memory; using percent correct instead of $d'$; reverse inference from imaging; forgetting multiple comparisons.

@exam num
prompt: A neuron rests at $-70$ mV and fires when its membrane potential reaches $-55$ mV. Each active excitatory input releases quanta from $n = 40$ release sites with probability $p = 0.25$ per site, and each quantum depolarizes the cell by $q = 0.3$ mV. Assuming that PSPs from simultaneously active inputs add linearly and each input has its mean response, how many inputs must be active at once to reach threshold?
answer: 5
tol: 0.01
unit: inputs
check: (-55 - (-70)) / (40 * 0.25 * 0.3)
hint: Find the mean response per input, then divide the depolarization needed by it.
solution: Mean response per input: $n p q = 40 \times 0.25 \times 0.3 = 3.0$ mV. The cell must depolarize by $15$ mV, so $15/3.0 = 5$ inputs.

@exam ms
prompt: A study claims that a cortical region R is the center for a function F. Select all findings that would strengthen the claim that R is necessary for F, and not just involved.
choice*: Patients with focal damage to R are impaired on F but not on a matched control task.
choice*: Temporary disruption of R in healthy volunteers impairs F, with a control site showing no effect.
choice*: A double dissociation in which damage to another region impairs the control task but not F.
choice: R is more active in fMRI during F than during rest.
choice: R is active whenever F is performed in a published meta-analysis.
hint: Necessity needs evidence from interference with the region, not only activity.
solution: Focal lesions with a matched control task, temporary disruption with a control site, and a double dissociation each address necessity. Higher fMRI activity and a meta-analysis show involvement and association, not that R is required.

@exam self
prompt: Trace what happens in the nervous system from the moment a tap strikes the tendon below your kneecap to the kick, and then compare it with what happens when you decide to kick a ball. Cover the receptors, the neurons and their signals (resting potential, action potential, synapse and transmitter), the circuits (spinal cord, motor cortex, basal ganglia, cerebellum) and give one number of the kind you computed in this course.
hint: Start with the muscle spindle and Ia afferent, then the monosynaptic synapse onto the motor neuron; then add the descending path for the voluntary kick.
solution: The tap stretches the quadriceps and activates muscle spindle receptors, whose transduction depolarizes the sensory endings. The Ia afferent fires action potentials (sodium-channel dependent, all-or-none, regenerated at nodes of Ranvier) that travel at about 70 m/s into the spinal cord, where the terminal releases glutamate onto alpha motor neurons: an EPSP that, if it reaches threshold, triggers spikes in the motor axon. At the neuromuscular junction acetylcholine acts on nicotinic receptors, an end-plate potential triggers a muscle spike, Ca$^{2+}$ release lets actin and myosin slide, and the leg extends; an inhibitory interneuron relaxes the opposing muscle. With 1.0 m of axon at 70 m/s and a 1.0 ms synaptic delay the arc takes about 15.3 ms. A deliberate kick begins in premotor and supplementary motor cortex, where the plan is formed, with the basal ganglia helping to select and start the action; primary motor cortex pyramidal neurons send spikes down the corticospinal tract, crossing in the medulla, to the same motor neurons, and the cerebellum compares the intended with the actual movement using sensory feedback and corrects errors. The reflex is faster and automatic and involves a single central synapse; the voluntary kick adds cortical planning, descending pathways and cerebellar and basal ganglia loops and takes longer.
rubric: Traces the reflex arc correctly with receptor, afferent, synapse, motor neuron, neuromuscular junction and contraction.
rubric: Explains the signals at each step accurately (graded EPSP, all-or-none spike, transmitter) and includes a correct number.
rubric: Adds the descending voluntary pathway with the roles of motor cortex, basal ganglia and cerebellum, and contrasts it with the reflex.
