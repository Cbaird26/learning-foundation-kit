@article
Why does a faint star vanish when you stare straight at it and reappear in the corner of your eye? Why is a whisper audible in a quiet room and not in a noisy one? Sensory systems turn physical energy into spikes, and what the brain reports depends both on those spikes and on a decision.

### Transduction and receptive fields

A **sensory receptor** converts a stimulus (light, pressure, sound, chemicals) into a change in membrane potential: **transduction**. Many receptors **adapt**: their response fades under a constant stimulus, which is why you stop feeling your clothes. The **receptive field** of a sensory neuron is the region of the stimulus space (a patch of retina or skin, a range of sound frequencies) where a stimulus changes its firing. Receptive fields get more complex as signals climb the pathway.

### Vision

The human retina has about 120 million **rods**, which are sensitive and used in dim light, and about 6 million **cones**, which mediate color and fine detail; cones are packed in the **fovea**. Photoreceptors synapse onto bipolar cells, which synapse onto **retinal ganglion cells**, whose axons form the optic nerve. Many ganglion cells have **center–surround** receptive fields: an ON-center cell is excited by light in a small central region and inhibited by light in the surrounding ring. Because excitation and inhibition nearly cancel, such a cell responds poorly to uniform light and strongly to contrast, such as edges.

Axons from the nasal half of each retina cross at the **optic chiasm**, so the left visual field is represented in the right hemisphere. Most ganglion cell axons reach the **lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN)** of the thalamus, which projects to **primary visual cortex (V1)**. Hubel and Wiesel (1959, 1962), recording from cat V1, found **simple cells** that respond best to a bar of a particular orientation at a particular position, and **complex cells** that respond to the preferred orientation over a range of positions. Beyond V1, a ventral stream through temporal cortex is associated with recognizing objects, and a dorsal stream through parietal cortex with space and the guidance of action.

### Other senses

In hearing, sound vibrates the **basilar membrane** of the cochlea, whose base responds best to high frequencies and apex to low; hair cells transduce the vibration, giving a **tonotopic** map. Humans hear roughly 20 to 20,000 Hz. In touch, mechanoreceptors in skin project through the spinal cord and thalamus to the somatosensory cortex, whose body map is **somatotopic**.

### Psychophysics: how sensation scales

**Weber's law**: the smallest detectable change $\Delta I$ is proportional to the baseline: $\Delta I / I = k$, the Weber fraction. **Fechner's law** derives $S = c\log I$ from it under assumptions. **Stevens's power law** (1957) describes perceived magnitude as $\psi = k\,\phi^{a}$, with exponent $a$ about 0.67 for loudness (sound pressure) and about 3.5 for electric shock: an exponent below 1 compresses the stimulus, above 1 expands it. Both laws hold approximately over a middle range and fail near threshold and at extremes.

### Signal detection theory

A listener decides "signal present" when internal evidence exceeds a criterion; noise alone sometimes produces high evidence, so errors are inevitable. With **hit rate** $H$ and **false-alarm rate** $F$ and the inverse of the standard normal cumulative distribution $z(\cdot)$, signal detection theory (Green and Swets, 1966) with equal-variance Gaussian evidence gives

$$d' = z(H) - z(F), \qquad c = -\tfrac{1}{2}\big(z(H) + z(F)\big).$$

$d'$ measures **sensitivity**, the separation of the signal and noise distributions in standard deviations. $c$ measures **bias**; positive $c$ is a conservative criterion. Percent correct mixes the two: a lenient observer raises both hits and false alarms without any change in $d'$.

```python
from statistics import NormalDist

def d_prime(h, f):
    """Sensitivity from a hit rate h and a false-alarm rate f."""
    z = NormalDist().inv_cdf
    return z(h) - z(f)

print(round(d_prime(0.84, 0.16), 2))
```

This prints `1.99`. A rate of exactly 0 or 1 makes $z$ infinite, so researchers adjust counts slightly before computing.

### Limits

Receptive fields measured with simple stimuli in anesthetized animals do not fully predict responses to natural scenes. The equal-variance Gaussian assumption is often wrong, and $d'$ then misstates sensitivity.

@example
title: A center-surround response
problem: An ON-center cell weights the center pixel of a 3 by 3 patch by $+8$ and each of the 8 surrounding pixels by $-1$. Its response is the weighted sum of pixel intensities. Compute the response to a patch with a bright spot (center 9, all others 2), and to a uniform patch of intensity 5.
step: Bright spot: center $8 \times 9 = 72$; surround $-1 \times (8 \times 2) = -16$; total $56$.
step: Uniform patch: center $8 \times 5 = 40$; surround $-8 \times 5 = -40$; total $0$.
step: The weights sum to zero, so uniform illumination cancels and only contrast gives a response, which is why such a cell signals spots and edges, not overall brightness.
answer: 56 for the spot and 0 for the uniform patch.

@example
title: Sensitivity and bias
problem: In a detection task, an observer has hit rate $H = 0.90$ and false-alarm rate $F = 0.20$. Compute $d'$ and $c$.
step: Look up the $z$ scores: $z(0.90) = 1.282$ and $z(0.20) = -0.842$.
step: $d' = 1.282 - (-0.842) = 2.124$ (2.12 to two decimals).
step: $c = -\tfrac12(1.282 + (-0.842)) = -0.5 \times 0.440 = -0.22$.
step: The negative $c$ means a lenient criterion: more "yes" responses than an unbiased observer with this $d'$ would give.
answer: $d' \approx 2.12$ and $c \approx -0.22$.

@mistakes
- Using percent correct as sensitivity: two observers with the same percent correct can differ in $d'$ and bias; $d'$ separates them.
- Reading Weber's law as a fixed threshold: the just-noticeable difference grows in proportion to the baseline, so the same change is detected at low but not at high baseline.
- Thinking the left eye sends information to the right hemisphere: it is the visual field that crosses, since nasal fibers cross at the chiasm.
- Treating the receptive field as fixed: it depends on stimulus, state and adaptation.

model:
```python
from statistics import NormalDist


def dprime_c(hits, misses, fas, crs):
    """Sensitivity d' and criterion c with the loglinear correction."""
    z = NormalDist().inv_cdf
    h = (hits + 0.5) / (hits + misses + 1)
    f = (fas + 0.5) / (fas + crs + 1)
    return z(h) - z(f), -0.5 * (z(h) + z(f))
```
hint: Compute the two corrected rates first, convert each to a $z$ score, and then combine them.
solution: The correction keeps the rates strictly between 0 and 1, so a perfect hit rate or zero false alarms does not give an infinite $z$. In the third test the correction makes both rates equal, so $d'$ is zero, and in the fourth the hit and false-alarm rates are equal, again giving $d' = 0$ with a lenient criterion because the common rate is above one half.
