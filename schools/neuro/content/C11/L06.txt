@article
Tap the tendon below your kneecap and your leg kicks before you decide anything. Reach for a cup and the same muscles move in a smooth, planned arc. How does the nervous system produce both a reflex and a voluntary reach, and what fails when parts of it are damaged?

### From motor neuron to muscle

A **motor unit** is one **alpha motor neuron** in the spinal cord or brainstem plus all the muscle fibers it innervates. Muscles that need fine control have small motor units (extraocular muscles, with on the order of ten fibers per neuron); large muscles of the leg have motor units with hundreds to thousands of fibers. Motor neurons are recruited from small to large as force rises, so fine control is available at low force.

Each action potential in a motor neuron releases acetylcholine at the **neuromuscular junction**, which activates nicotinic receptors and produces a large end-plate potential that reliably triggers a muscle spike. The spike releases Ca$^{2+}$ inside the fiber, which lets the proteins **myosin** and **actin** slide past each other in each **sarcomere** (the repeating contractile unit, roughly 2 to 3 µm long): the **sliding-filament** mechanism. A muscle fiber is made of thousands of sarcomeres in series, and the fiber shortens by the sum of their shortening.

### Reflexes

The **stretch reflex** is the knee-jerk. A tap stretches the quadriceps, which activates **muscle spindle** receptors; their Ia afferent axons enter the spinal cord and synapse directly on the alpha motor neurons of the same muscle, which contract it: a **monosynaptic** reflex, with a single synapse. Other branches of the Ia axon excite an inhibitory interneuron that relaxes the opposing muscle (reciprocal inhibition). Reflexes work without the brain, but the brain adjusts their gain, so reflex testing in a clinic probes spinal and peripheral circuits and the descending control of them.

### Descending control

Voluntary movement is organized hierarchically. **Primary motor cortex (M1)**, in the precentral gyrus, has a body map in which hands and face occupy a disproportionately large area; Penfield and Boldrey (1937) charted it by stimulation. **Premotor cortex** and the **supplementary motor area** are involved in planning and sequencing. M1 pyramidal neurons send axons down the **corticospinal tract**, which crosses at the **pyramids of the medulla**, so the left motor cortex mainly controls the right side of the body. Single motor-cortex neurons are tuned to the direction of reaching, and the direction is better predicted from the activity of a population of neurons than a single neuron.

### The basal ganglia

The **basal ganglia** include the striatum (caudate and putamen), the globus pallidus, the subthalamic nucleus and the substantia nigra. They form loops with the cortex through the thalamus and are involved in selecting and initiating actions and in habit and reward-based learning (Lesson 8). In the textbook scheme, a **direct pathway** facilitates movement by disinhibiting the thalamus and an **indirect pathway** suppresses it. **Parkinson's disease** results from the degeneration of dopamine neurons in the substantia nigra, which project to the striatum, and produces rest tremor, rigidity and slowness of movement. L-DOPA, which is converted to dopamine, and deep brain stimulation are treatments; decisions about them belong to clinicians. **Huntington's disease**, an inherited disorder (a repeated CAG sequence in one gene), degenerates the striatum and produces involuntary movements.

### The cerebellum

The **cerebellum** compares intended with actual movement and corrects errors. Damage causes **ataxia**: uncoordinated, poorly timed movement, overshoot or undershoot of targets (dysmetria), and tremor during goal-directed movement, but not paralysis. The cerebellum is also essential for **motor learning**, such as adapting reaching when vision is shifted by prism goggles. The standard view is that it learns from error signals, but evidence also implicates it in timing and in some cognitive functions, which remains an active area of study.

### Limits

The direct–indirect scheme is a useful simplification; real basal ganglia circuits have more pathways, and dopamine's effects are complex. Motor cortex neurons correlate with direction, but whether they encode direction or other features such as force and muscle activation is debated.

title: Latency of the knee-jerk reflex arc
problem: Estimate the time for the signal in a monosynaptic reflex arc if the afferent and efferent axons together are 1.0 m long, they conduct at 70 m/s, and the single central synapse adds a 1.0 ms delay. Ignore receptor and muscle delays.
step: Conduction time: $t = L/v = 1.0/70 = 0.01429$ s, or 14.3 ms.
step: Add the synaptic delay: $14.3 + 1.0 = 15.3$ ms.
step: A disynaptic or polysynaptic reflex adds at least one more synaptic delay per synapse, so the latency increase tells researchers how many synapses lie in the circuit.
answer: About 15.3 ms.

@example
title: Force from recruited motor units
problem: A muscle has motor units that each contain 200 fibers, with each active fiber contributing 0.0025 N. How much force does the muscle produce when 12 such units are active?
step: Force per unit: $200 \times 0.0025 = 0.5$ N.
step: Total for 12 units: $12 \times 0.5 = 6$ N.
step: Real muscles recruit units of different sizes and fire them at different rates, so the force is not simply proportional to the count of units.
answer: 6 N.

@mistakes
- Thinking the reflex needs the brain: the stretch reflex runs through the spinal cord, although the brain modulates it.
- Thinking Parkinson's disease is loss of motor neurons: it is the loss of dopamine neurons in the substantia nigra, upstream of the cortex.
- Confusing the signs of basal ganglia and cerebellar damage: rest tremor and slowness fit Parkinson's disease; ataxia and intention tremor fit the cerebellum.
- Saying the left motor cortex controls the left side: the corticospinal tract mostly crosses at the medulla.
