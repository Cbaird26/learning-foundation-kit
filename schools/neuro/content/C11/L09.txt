@article
Place electrodes on the scalp and the brain's electrical activity looks like a restless, noisy line. Berger recorded the first human electroencephalogram (EEG) in 1929 and noticed that the line changes with state: closing the eyes and relaxing produces a regular wave of about 10 cycles per second. Sleep occupies about a third of our lives, yet its purposes are still debated.

### EEG rhythms and sleep stages

The EEG measures summed postsynaptic currents of many aligned neurons, mainly cortical pyramidal cells, through the skull. Its rhythms are named by frequency:

| rhythm | frequency (Hz) | typical state |
|---|---|---|
| delta | 0.5 to 4 | deep non-REM sleep |
| theta | 4 to 8 | drowsiness, light sleep |
| alpha | 8 to 12 | relaxed wakefulness, eyes closed |
| beta | 13 to 30 | alert, active thinking |
| gamma | above 30 | various cognitive processes |

Sleep has two main kinds. **Non-REM (NREM) sleep** is divided into stages N1, N2 and N3. In N2, the EEG shows brief bursts called **sleep spindles** (about 12 to 15 Hz) and sharp **K-complexes**; N3, or slow-wave sleep, is dominated by high-amplitude delta waves. **REM sleep** has a fast, low-amplitude EEG resembling wakefulness, **rapid eye movements** and loss of muscle tone (atonia), and is associated with vivid dreams. A night consists of four to six cycles of about 90 minutes: slow-wave sleep is concentrated in the first half of the night and REM periods lengthen toward the morning. Most adults need about seven to nine hours of sleep, although individuals vary.

### The circadian clock

Humans have a **circadian rhythm** of close to 24 hours, generated internally: people kept without time cues drift to a period of slightly more than 24 hours, around 24.2 hours on average in careful studies. The master clock in mammals is the **suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN)** of the hypothalamus, whose neurons contain a molecular feedback loop of clock genes. Light entrains it daily through retinal ganglion cells containing the pigment melanopsin, which project to the SCN; the pineal gland releases **melatonin** at night under SCN control. Jet lag and shift work arise when external time and the internal clock disagree.

### Sleep pressure and the two-process model

Alongside the clock, a **homeostatic** pressure for sleep builds with wakefulness and discharges with sleep. Adenosine accumulating in the brain is one candidate signal; caffeine blocks adenosine receptors. In the **two-process model** of sleep regulation, a circadian process $C$ and a homeostatic process $S$ jointly determine when sleep occurs. A toy version lets $S$ (scaled 0 to 1) rise toward 1 during wake with time constant $\tau_{\text{up}}$ and fall toward 0 during sleep with time constant $\tau_{\text{down}}$:

$$S_{\text{wake}}(t) = 1 - (1 - S_0)e^{-t/\tau_{\text{up}}}, \qquad S_{\text{sleep}}(t) = S_0\,e^{-t/\tau_{\text{down}}}.$$

The values used here ($\tau_{\text{up}} = 18$ h, $\tau_{\text{down}} = 4$ h) are illustrative. The model predicts that sleep is deeper after long wake and shorter once pressure is low, and that the circadian drive can keep a person awake or asleep against the pressure.

```python
import math

def evolve(s, hours, awake, tau_up=18.0, tau_down=4.0):
    """Sleep pressure S after `hours` awake or asleep, starting from s."""
    if awake:
        return 1 - (1 - s) * math.exp(-hours / tau_up)
    return s * math.exp(-hours / tau_down)

print(round(evolve(0.2, 14, True), 3))
```

This prints `0.632`.

### Why do we sleep?

Established: sleep loss impairs attention, mood and memory, and extended deprivation is dangerous. Proposed functions include consolidating memory, restoring synaptic balance, clearing metabolic waste and regulating energy; each has supporting evidence and none is accepted as the whole answer, and what dreams do is unknown. Narcolepsy type 1 involves the loss of orexin-producing neurons in the hypothalamus, which stabilize wakefulness.

### Consciousness in outline

Consciousness varies with state: reduced in deep NREM sleep and anesthesia, altered in REM dreaming. Neuroscientists look for the **neural correlates of consciousness**, the minimal brain activity that accompanies a conscious experience, using methods such as comparing seen and unseen stimuli. Competing theories, among them the global workspace theory (Baars, 1988; Dehaene and Naccache, 2001) and integrated information theory (Tononi, 2004), are being tested against each other. As of 2026 there is no scientific consensus on how consciousness arises, nor on which animals or machines are conscious.

@mistakes
- Treating the human clock period as exactly 24 hours: it is close to, and slightly longer than, 24, and daily light resets it.
- Thinking REM sleep is the deepest sleep because dreams are vivid: slow-wave NREM sleep has the highest arousal threshold, and REM EEG looks like wakefulness.
- Presenting a function of sleep as established fact: several proposals have evidence and none is accepted as the sole function.
- Saying neuroscience has shown how consciousness arises: there is no consensus, and correlates are not explanations.

model:
```python
import math


def hours_awake_to_reach(s0, target, tau_up=18.0):
    """Hours of wakefulness for S to rise from s0 to target (toy model)."""
    if s0 >= target:
        return 0.0
    if target >= 1.0:
        return None
    return -tau_up * math.log((1 - target) / (1 - s0))
```
hint: Solve $1 - (1-s_0)e^{-t/\tau} = $ target for $t$ by isolating the exponential and taking the logarithm; check the special cases first.
solution: Rearranging gives $t = -\tau\ln\big((1-\text{target})/(1-s_0)\big)$. The special cases come first: no time is needed if the pressure already meets the target, and a target of 1 or more is never reached because $S$ only approaches 1.

prompt: Explain the two-process model of sleep regulation. Describe what each process is, how they interact to determine when a person falls asleep and wakes, and what it predicts about sleeping after a night of deprivation and about a person working night shifts.
hint: One process builds with time awake and falls in sleep; the other is a roughly 24-hour oscillation.
solution: Process S is a homeostatic pressure for sleep that increases with time awake and dissipates during sleep (in the toy model, exponential rise toward a ceiling and exponential fall). Process C is a circadian drive from the suprachiasmatic nucleus that varies over about 24 hours and promotes wakefulness in the biological day and sleep at night. Sleep is likely when S is high and C favors sleep, and waking occurs as S falls and C rises. After a night without sleep S is higher than normal, so the next sleep tends to be deeper, with more slow-wave activity, and longer or more intense than usual, although the circadian drive can still wake a person in the biological morning. A night-shift worker must sleep during the day, when C promotes waking, so S falls less completely, sleep is shorter and lighter, and pressure builds during work at night when C is low; the mismatch between S and C explains tiredness and poor sleep, and adapting the clock with light exposure helps only slowly.

rubric: Predicts deeper or longer recovery sleep after deprivation.
rubric: Explains shift-work problems as a misalignment of S and C.
