@article
A rat hears a tone, then receives a mild shock. After a few pairings the tone alone makes it freeze and its heart rate and stress hormones rise. Nothing in the tone is dangerous; the brain has learned what it predicts. The same logic of prediction runs through reward: dopamine neurons fire when a reward is better than expected, not simply when it arrives.

### Fear and the amygdala

In **fear conditioning**, a neutral cue (the conditioned stimulus) is paired with an aversive outcome. Sensory information reaches the **lateral amygdala**, which projects through other amygdala nuclei to the **central nucleus**, which drives freezing, autonomic arousal and hormone release through the brainstem and hypothalamus. Evidence that the amygdala matters: lesioning or inactivating it in rodents blocks learning or expression of conditioned fear, and in humans, bilateral damage reduces the recognition of fearful faces and physiological fear conditioning. Limits: the amygdala is not a "fear center." It also responds to positive and novel stimuli, and fear depends on a wider network (hippocampus for context, prefrontal cortex for extinction). Whether the subjective feeling of fear, as distinct from defensive responses, is produced by the amygdala is debated. Theories of emotion differ on whether bodily responses cause, accompany or follow the feeling, and the evidence supports each in part.

### The hypothalamus and the stress axis

The **hypothalamus** regulates body state. During stress, it releases corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), which makes the pituitary release ACTH, which makes the adrenal cortex release the hormone **cortisol**: the **HPA axis** (hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal). Cortisol feeds back to shut the axis down. Sympathetic activation separately releases epinephrine from the adrenal medulla within seconds. For hunger, neurons in the arcuate nucleus of the hypothalamus respond to signals such as leptin, a hormone released by fat tissue; activating one group of these neurons drives feeding in mice, and the older idea of single "hunger" and "satiety" centers is now seen as too simple.

### Reward and dopamine

Dopamine neurons in the midbrain (the **ventral tegmental area** and substantia nigra) project to the **nucleus accumbens**, striatum and prefrontal cortex. Electrical self-stimulation of these pathways is rewarding to rats, and drugs of abuse such as cocaine and amphetamine increase dopamine's action in the nucleus accumbens. But recordings gave a sharper picture. Schultz, Dayan and Montague (1997) recorded dopamine neurons in monkeys receiving juice. Before training, the neurons fire when the juice is delivered. After a cue reliably predicts the juice, they fire at the cue and no longer at the juice; if the predicted juice is omitted, firing dips below baseline at the time it was due. This is the pattern of a **reward prediction error**.

The **temporal-difference (TD) error** of Sutton (1988) captures it. With value $V(s)$ for state $s$, reward $r$ on the step from $s$ to $s'$, and a discount factor $\gamma$ ($0 \le \gamma \le 1$),

$$\delta = r + \gamma V(s') - V(s), \qquad V(s) \leftarrow V(s) + \alpha\,\delta,$$

where $\alpha$ is a learning rate. A positive $\delta$ means things are better than predicted; negative means worse; zero means fully predicted. Early in learning $\delta$ occurs at the reward, and with learning it moves back to the earliest predictive cue.

```python
def td_chain(n, episodes, alpha, gamma, reward=1.0):
    """Values of a chain of n states; reward on leaving the last state."""
    v = [0.0] * n
    for _ in range(episodes):
        for s in range(n):
            nxt = v[s + 1] if s + 1 < n else 0.0
            r = reward if s == n - 1 else 0.0
            v[s] += alpha * (r + gamma * nxt - v[s])
    return v

print([round(x, 3) for x in td_chain(3, 20, 0.3, 0.9)])
```

This prints `[0.781, 0.893, 0.999]`, approaching the discounted values $0.81$, $0.9$ and $1.0$.

### Competing interpretations

The prediction-error account is well supported for many dopamine neurons in conditioning tasks, but it is not the whole story. Other findings show dopamine responses to novelty and to aversive or salient events, and some dopamine neurons ramp up as a reward approaches. A different proposal holds that dopamine mediates the motivational pull of cues, "wanting", distinct from "liking" a reward, supported by evidence that animals depleted of dopamine can still show pleasure-related reactions to sweet tastes but do not work for food. These views are not exclusive, and which one best describes a given population of dopamine neurons is an active research question. Neither claims that dopamine "is" pleasure.

@example
title: A temporal-difference error
problem: A state has value $V(s) = 0.2$. The animal moves to a state with value $V(s') = 0.0$ and receives a reward $r = 1$, with $\gamma = 0.9$. Find $\delta$ and, with $\alpha = 0.1$, the updated $V(s)$.
step: Compute the error: $\delta = 1 + 0.9 \times 0 - 0.2 = 0.8$.
step: Update: $V(s) \leftarrow 0.2 + 0.1 \times 0.8 = 0.28$.
step: The error is large and positive: the reward was much better than predicted.
answer: $\delta = 0.8$ and the new value is $0.28$.

@example
title: An omitted reward
problem: After training, a state before the reward has $V(s) = 0.9$, and the next state is terminal ($V(s') = 0$). The expected reward is omitted ($r = 0$). Find $\delta$.
step: $\delta = 0 + \gamma \times 0 - 0.9 = -0.9$.
step: A negative $\delta$ corresponds to the dip in dopamine firing seen when a predicted reward fails to come.
answer: $\delta = -0.9$.

@mistakes
- Saying dopamine "is the pleasure chemical": the recordings show that it tracks prediction errors, and its role in liking and wanting is debated.
- Treating the amygdala as the brain's fear center: it participates in many functions, and fear involves a network.
- Expecting a response to a fully predicted reward: after learning, the TD error at the reward is near zero and the response moves to the cue.
- Forgetting the discount factor $\gamma$ or the next state's value in the error: the error compares what happened with what was predicted, including the future value.

model:
```python
def td_errors(n, rewards, alpha=0.2, gamma=1.0):
    """TD error at the last state of a chain, for each episode."""
    v = [0.0] * n
    errors = []
    for reward in rewards:
        for s in range(n):
            last = s == n - 1
            nxt = 0.0 if last else v[s + 1]
            r = reward if last else 0.0
            delta = r + gamma * nxt - v[s]
            v[s] += alpha * delta
            if last:
                errors.append(delta)
    return errors
```
hint: Keep the value list across episodes, update each state as you pass it, and record the error only on the last state.
solution: Values persist across episodes, so a reward that was reliably delivered makes the last state's value high, and omitting it produces a negative error equal to minus that value. The tests include a single-state chain and an empty list of episodes.

@quiz self
prompt: Two interpretations of dopamine: it signals reward prediction errors for learning, or it signals incentive salience ("wanting"). State the evidence for each, say what data each handles less well, and describe one experiment that could help distinguish them. Do not claim that either is settled.
hint: Think of recordings in conditioning tasks, dopamine depletion and the dissociation of "wanting" from learning.
solution: Prediction-error evidence: recordings in monkeys show a burst at unpredicted reward, a shift to the cue after training, and a dip at omission, matching a TD error, and dopamine manipulations can change learning from outcomes. Wanting evidence: animals depleted of dopamine can still show taste-related reactions that indicate liking but do not work for food, and dopamine rises with cues that drive motivation. Each handles some data less well: pure prediction error has trouble with dopamine responses to novelty, salience, and ramps near rewards, while a pure wanting account has to explain the precise error-like timing in conditioning. A distinguishing experiment would manipulate dopamine neurons at the time of the outcome in a task that separates learning from motivation, for example stimulating them when a reward is fully predicted, and ask whether later cue-driven approach and learning about that cue differ, with the learning measured separately from the effort animals will pay. The views may also be partly compatible, if a signal serves both functions in different circuits.
