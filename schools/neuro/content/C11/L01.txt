@article
Look at brain tissue under a microscope with an ordinary stain and you see a dense tangle: the cell bodies are visible, but the long thin processes that connect them are not. Is the brain one continuous web, like the blood vessels, or a collection of separate cells that touch? For decades in the nineteenth century this was a real dispute. The **reticular theory**, defended by Golgi, held that the processes fuse into a continuous network. The **neuron doctrine**, argued by Cajal in the late nineteenth century, held that the nervous system is built from discrete cells, the **neurons**.

### The evidence for separate cells

Golgi himself supplied the tool that settled it: a silver stain that, for reasons still not fully understood, darkens a small fraction of cells completely, including every branch, while leaving their neighbors clear. Cajal used it to draw cells with free, ending branches rather than fusing ones. Three further lines of evidence followed.

- **Degeneration.** When an axon is cut, the part separated from its cell body degenerates, while the cell it contacted usually survives; the damage respects cell boundaries.
- **Electron microscopy** (1950s) showed that at most synapses the membranes of the two cells are separated by a gap, the **synaptic cleft**, roughly 20 nm wide.
- **Physiology.** Transmission at most synapses is one-way and delayed by a fraction of a millisecond, as expected if a chemical must cross a gap.

The doctrine needed one amendment. At **electrical synapses** (gap junctions), channels link the cytoplasm of two neighboring neurons directly, and current passes with almost no delay. The cells are still separate cells with their own membranes; the doctrine survives, but cells can be electrically coupled.

### Anatomy of a neuron

A typical neuron has four regions (see the table). Information normally flows in one direction, the principle of **dynamic polarization**: inputs arrive on dendrites and soma, and output leaves along the axon.

| region | what it is | main job |
|---|---|---|
| dendrites | branching processes, often covered with small protrusions called spines | receive synaptic input |
| soma (cell body) | holds the nucleus and most protein-making machinery | integrates input, supports the cell |
| axon hillock and initial segment | where the axon leaves the soma | usually where action potentials start |
| axon and terminals | a single long process, branching at its end into terminals | carries action potentials to other cells and releases transmitter |

By function, **sensory neurons** carry information from the body or world toward the central nervous system, **motor neurons** carry commands to muscles and glands, and **interneurons** connect neurons to neurons within the brain and spinal cord; interneurons are by far the most numerous. Cortical neurons are often divided into excitatory **pyramidal cells** and a diverse set of mostly inhibitory interneurons. Modern single-cell methods reveal many more types than the classical anatomy did, and how best to define a "type" is still debated.

### Glia

Neurons share the tissue with **glial cells**, which do not fire action potentials in the way neurons do but are far from passive.

- **Astrocytes** take up potassium and the transmitter glutamate from the extracellular space, help form the blood–brain barrier, and supply neurons with metabolic fuel.
- **Oligodendrocytes** wrap axons in the central nervous system with **myelin**, a fatty insulating sheath; one oligodendrocyte myelinates segments of several axons.
- **Schwann cells** do the same job in peripheral nerves; each myelinates one segment of one axon.
- **Microglia** are the brain's resident immune cells; they remove debris and prune and monitor synapses.

Myelin comes in segments separated by small gaps, the **nodes of Ranvier**, where the action potential is regenerated (Lesson 2). Myelinated axons conduct at tens of meters per second, up to about 100 m/s; thin unmyelinated axons conduct at around 1 m/s. In multiple sclerosis the immune system attacks central myelin, and conduction slows or fails.

### How many cells?

A widely cited estimate for the adult human brain is about 86 billion neurons and a similar number of non-neuronal cells. The older claim that glia outnumber neurons ten to one is not supported by modern counting methods. Counts vary by region: the cerebellum holds most of the brain's neurons although it is a small part of its volume.

### Limits

The doctrine is a statement about structure, not a claim that the neuron is the only information-processing unit: astrocyte calcium signaling, for example, is an active research area whose functional importance is debated. And "a neuron fires or it does not" ignores that dendrites compute: they integrate inputs and in some cells generate local spikes.

@example
title: Conduction time down a long axon
problem: A motor neuron's axon runs 0.8 m from the spinal cord to a foot muscle. Compare the conduction time if the axon is myelinated and conducts at 70 m/s with the time if it were unmyelinated and conducted at 1 m/s.
step: Time is distance divided by speed: $t = L/v$.
step: Myelinated: $t = 0.8/70 \approx 0.0114$ s, or about 11.4 ms.
step: Unmyelinated: $t = 0.8/1 = 0.8$ s, or 800 ms.
step: The ratio is $800/11.4 \approx 70$, the ratio of the speeds. Myelin turns a nearly one-second delay into a delay of about a hundredth of a second.
answer: About 11.4 ms with myelin and 800 ms without.

@example
title: Estimating how many non-neuronal cells
problem: Suppose the old claim of ten glia per neuron were true for a brain with 86 billion neurons. How many glia would that imply, and how does it compare with a modern estimate of about 85 billion non-neuronal cells?
step: Multiply: $10 \times 86 \times 10^9 = 8.6 \times 10^{11}$, or 860 billion.
step: Compare with the modern estimate: $860/85 \approx 10$, so the old claim overstates the count about tenfold.
step: The modern ratio of non-neuronal cells to neurons is $85/86 \approx 0.99$, close to one to one.
answer: The old claim implies 860 billion glia, about ten times the modern estimate.

@mistakes
- Equating "glia" with "support only": glia take up transmitter, regulate the chemistry of the extracellular fluid, form myelin and defend the tissue.
- Believing the neuron doctrine says neurons never touch electrically: gap junctions couple some neurons directly, and the doctrine still holds because the cells remain distinct.
- Mixing up the two myelin-forming cells: oligodendrocytes myelinate central axons (several at once), Schwann cells myelinate peripheral axons (one segment each).
- Treating all conduction as equally fast: speed depends on myelination and axon diameter, and ranges from about 1 m/s to about 100 m/s.

@quiz self
prompt: A classmate says that the discovery of gap junctions proves the reticular theory was right after all. Evaluate this claim: say what the reticular theory and the neuron doctrine each claim, what a gap junction is, and what evidence shows that the doctrine still holds.
hint: Distinguish a continuous network of fused cytoplasm from coupled but separate cells.
solution: The reticular theory said that nerve cell processes fuse into one continuous network; the neuron doctrine said the nervous system is built from separate cells, each with its own membrane. A gap junction is a cluster of channels that connect the cytoplasm of two neighboring neurons so that ions and current pass directly. That is a form of electrical coupling, not fusion: each cell keeps its own membrane, nucleus and boundaries, and the channels can open and close. Evidence for separateness includes Golgi-stained cells with free-ending branches, degeneration confined to the cell that is cut off, and electron micrographs of chemical synapses with a visible cleft between two membranes. So gap junctions add a second way for separate cells to communicate, and the doctrine stands with an amendment.
