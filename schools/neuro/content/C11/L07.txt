@article
In 1953 a young man, known in the literature as H.M., had parts of both medial temporal lobes removed to treat severe epilepsy. The seizures improved. But afterward he could not form new lasting memories of events: people he met, conversations, what he had eaten. Yet he could hold a conversation, remember his childhood, and learn new motor skills. What does this one case reveal about how memory is organized?

### Kinds of memory

Memory is not one thing. Miller (1956) summarized evidence that people hold about seven items in short-term memory, and Atkinson and Shiffrin (1968) proposed a **modal model** with sensory, short-term and long-term stores. Baddeley and Hitch (1974) replaced the passive short-term store with **working memory**, an active system for holding and manipulating information. Long-term memory divides into **declarative** (explicit) memory for facts and events, subdivided by Tulving (1972) into **semantic** (facts) and **episodic** (personal events), and **nondeclarative** memory: skills and habits, priming, and conditioned responses, which are expressed in performance rather than recollection. Ebbinghaus (1885) measured how fast lists were forgotten: retention falls steeply at first, then slowly.

### Patient H.M.

Scoville and Milner (1957) described H.M.'s surgery, which removed the medial temporal lobes on both sides, including the hippocampus and nearby cortex and the amygdala. The results were:

- severe **anterograde amnesia**: almost no new declarative memories;
- some **retrograde amnesia** for the years just before surgery, with older memories largely intact;
- normal digit span and conversation over seconds (working memory spared) and a normal IQ;
- the ability to learn a motor skill, such as mirror drawing, across days without remembering the practice sessions.

The case supported three conclusions: the medial temporal lobe is necessary for forming new declarative memories; it is not the site of all long-term storage, since old memories survived; and declarative and nondeclarative memory depend on different systems. Limits: the lesion included several structures, so the case cannot say which one is responsible, and it was one patient with a history of epilepsy; later studies of patients with more restricted damage and of animals refined the picture.

### Synaptic plasticity

Hebb (1949) proposed that when one cell repeatedly helps to fire another, the connection between them strengthens. Bliss and Lømo (1973) found a physical correlate: in the hippocampus of anesthetized rabbits, a brief high-frequency train of stimuli to the perforant path produced a lasting increase, lasting hours, in the response of dentate granule cells: **long-term potentiation (LTP)**. At many hippocampal synapses, LTP depends on the **NMDA receptor**, which acts as a **coincidence detector**: its channel opens only when glutamate is bound and the postsynaptic membrane is depolarized enough to expel a Mg$^{2+}$ ion that blocks the pore. Ca$^{2+}$ entering through it triggers changes that increase the number and function of AMPA receptors. Low-frequency stimulation can instead produce **long-term depression (LTD)**.

How strong is the evidence that LTP underlies memory? It is correlational and pharmacological in parts: blocking NMDA receptors impairs spatial learning in rodents, and learning produces LTP-like changes in some circuits. But drugs affect more than LTP, and a causal chain from synaptic strength to a specific memory is still being tested. Place cells in the rat hippocampus (O'Keefe and Dostrovsky, 1971), which fire in a specific location, show that the hippocampus represents space.

### Learning rules and forgetting

**Classical conditioning** pairs a cue (a tone) with an outcome (food, shock). The **Rescorla–Wagner** model (1972) says that learning is driven by surprise. For each cue present on a trial, the associative strength $V$ is updated by

$$\Delta V = \alpha\beta\,(\lambda - \textstyle\sum V),$$

where $\sum V$ is the total strength of all cues present, $\lambda$ is the maximum strength the outcome supports (zero if no outcome) and $\alpha\beta$ is a learning rate. It explains **blocking**: if cue A already predicts the outcome, the error is near zero when B is added, so B learns almost nothing. A simple model of forgetting is exponential, $R(t) = e^{-t/S}$, with memory strength $S$, though power laws often fit retention data better.

```python
def rw_acquisition(n_trials, ab, lam=1.0):
    """Associative strength of one cue after each of n_trials."""
    v, history = 0.0, []
    for _ in range(n_trials):
        v += ab * (lam - v)
        history.append(v)
    return history

print([round(v, 3) for v in rw_acquisition(4, 0.3)])
```

This prints `[0.3, 0.51, 0.657, 0.76]`: each trial closes a fraction 0.3 of the remaining gap.

@example
title: One Rescorla–Wagner step and trials to reach 0.9
problem: A cue has $V = 0.4$. A trial pairs it with the outcome ($\lambda = 1$) with $\alpha\beta = 0.25$. Find the new $V$ and the number of trials for $V$ to pass 0.9 starting from 0 with $\alpha\beta = 0.25$.
step: Error: $\lambda - V = 1 - 0.4 = 0.6$.
step: Update: $V = 0.4 + 0.25 \times 0.6 = 0.55$.
step: Starting from 0, $V_n = 1 - (1-0.25)^n = 1 - 0.75^n$. Need $0.75^n < 0.1$, so $n > \ln 0.1/\ln 0.75 = 8.00$, which means $n = 9$ trials ($0.75^8 = 0.1001$, so $V_8 = 0.8999$, just short).
answer: $V = 0.55$ after the step; 9 trials to pass 0.9.

@example
title: How fast is memory forgotten?
problem: Retention is modeled as $R(t) = e^{-t/S}$ with $S = 20$ hours. How long until retention is 50%, and what is retention at 10 hours?
step: Solve $e^{-t/S} = 0.5$: $t = S\ln 2 = 20 \times 0.693 = 13.9$ hours.
step: At 10 hours, $R = e^{-10/20} = e^{-0.5} = 0.607$.
answer: 50% at about 13.9 hours; 60.7% at 10 hours.

@mistakes
- Saying H.M. "lost memory": working memory, old memories and skill learning were spared, and the loss was of forming new declarative memories.
- Treating the hippocampus as the store of all memories: older memories survived its removal, so consolidation and storage involve other regions.
- Treating LTP as proven to be memory: it is a candidate mechanism with strong supporting evidence and open questions.
- Letting each cue learn independently in Rescorla–Wagner: the error uses the summed strength of all cues present.

model:
```python
def rw_two_cues(trials, ab=(0.3, 0.3), lam=1.0):
    """Final (V_A, V_B) under the Rescorla-Wagner rule."""
    v = [0.0, 0.0]
    for a, b, reinforced in trials:
        present = (a, b)
        total = sum(v[i] for i in range(2) if present[i])
        error = (lam if reinforced else 0.0) - total
        for i in range(2):
            if present[i]:
                v[i] += ab[i] * error
    return v[0], v[1]
```
hint: Sum the strengths of present cues only, and update only those cues, using the same error for both.
solution: Using the sum makes the error shared, which produces blocking: in the fourth test, A already predicts the outcome, so B gains almost nothing. The last test catches solutions that update an absent cue or ignore which cues are present.
