@article
Why does a gray patch look darker next to a white card than next to a black one? The patch sends the same light to your eye in both cases. The retina is not a camera sensor that reports each pixel on its own: it compares each point with its surroundings. This lesson follows light into the eye and shows where that comparison begins.

### Optics and the retina

The cornea and the lens focus light onto the retina at the back of the eye. The cornea supplies about two-thirds of the focusing power, and the lens adjusts it (**accommodation**) to focus near objects. The pupil sets how much light enters. The retina is a thin sheet of neural tissue built back to front: light passes through the ganglion and bipolar cell layers to reach the **photoreceptors** at the back. There are about 120 million **rods**, which are very sensitive and work in dim light, and about 6 million **cones**, which need brighter light and give color vision (next week). Cones are packed densely in the **fovea**, the small central pit used for fine detail; the fovea has no rods, and rods dominate the periphery. Where the optic nerve leaves the eye there are no photoreceptors, producing the blind spot.

### Photoreceptors: light hyperpolarizes

In darkness a photoreceptor is depolarized: cyclic-GMP-gated channels are open and the cell releases glutamate steadily. Absorbing a photon activates a visual pigment, starts an enzyme cascade that lowers cyclic GMP, closes the channels, and **hyperpolarizes** the cell, reducing glutamate release. More light means a larger change in the cell's potential, up to a limit. Bipolar cells come in two kinds. **ON bipolar cells** depolarize when light increases (a sign-inverting glutamate receptor reverses the photoreceptor's signal), and **OFF bipolar cells** depolarize when light decreases. Horizontal and amacrine cells spread signals sideways.

### Center-surround receptive fields

Kuffler (1953) recorded from retinal ganglion cells and found that each responds to light in a small region, its **receptive field**, which is organized in two concentric zones with opposite effects. An **ON-center** cell fires faster for light in the middle and slower for light in the surrounding ring; an **OFF-center** cell does the reverse. Light over both zones nearly cancels, so the cell responds weakly to a uniform field and strongly to local contrast. A standard model is a **difference of Gaussians**: a narrow excitatory Gaussian minus a broader inhibitory one, weighted so that the weights sum to zero. The surround arises largely from horizontal cells and from lateral connections. The one-million-odd ganglion cell axons form the optic nerve, so the retina compresses its roughly 126 million receptors into a signal about local differences. Parvocellular and magnocellular ganglion cells (smaller and larger, more and less sensitive to color and fine detail) start two parallel pathways.

### Lateral inhibition and edges

Hartline's work on the horseshoe crab eye showed **lateral inhibition**: each receptor is inhibited by its neighbors. A simple model subtracts a fraction $w$ of the neighbors' input: $y_i = x_i - w\,(x_{i-1} + x_{i+1})$. At a luminance edge this produces an undershoot on the dark side and an overshoot on the light side, the kind of exaggeration that Mach bands show.

### Light adaptation and sampling

Retinal responses cover a range of light levels far wider than the voltage range of a neuron, so the retina **adapts**. A neuron's response is often described by the **Naka–Rushton** function $R = R_{\max}\,I^n/(I^n + \sigma^n)$, where $\sigma$ is the intensity giving half the maximum. Adaptation shifts $\sigma$ toward the background intensity, keeping the working range centered on the current light level. Then an increment of fixed proportion $\Delta I/I_b$ produces the same response change at any background, which is the neural counterpart of Weber's law. Finally, cones sample the image at a limited spacing. Foveal cones are about 0.5 arcminute apart, and a regular sampling lattice with spacing $s$ cannot represent a grating with more than $1/(2s)$ cycles per degree (the **Nyquist limit**): roughly 60 cycles per degree, close to the best human acuity.

### In code

```python
def lateral_inhibition(row, w):
    """y_i = x_i - w * (left + right), repeating the end pixels."""
    n = len(row)
    out = []
    for i in range(n):
        left = row[max(i - 1, 0)]
        right = row[min(i + 1, n - 1)]
        out.append(row[i] - w * (left + right))
    return out


edge = [2, 2, 2, 2, 8, 8, 8, 8]
print([round(v, 2) for v in lateral_inhibition(edge, 0.4)])
```

This prints `[0.4, 0.4, 0.4, -2.0, 4.0, 1.6, 1.6, 1.6]`. Far from the edge each flat region gives a constant output ($2(1 - 0.8) = 0.4$ and $8(1 - 0.8) = 1.6$), but beside the edge the dark side drops to $-2.0$ and the light side rises to $4.0$: contrast is enhanced.

@example
title: Adaptation and Weber's law
problem: A photoreceptor-like response is $R = I/(I + \sigma)$ with $\sigma$ set equal to the background intensity $I_b$. Compare the response change to a 20% increment on backgrounds of 20 and 200 (arbitrary units).
step: On background 20, $\sigma = 20$ and the increment is $4$: $R(24) = 24/44 = 0.5455$, while $R(20) = 0.5$, so the change is $0.0455$.
step: On background 200, $\sigma = 200$ and the increment is $40$: $R(240) = 240/440 = 0.5455$, again with $R(200) = 0.5$.
step: The changes are equal, $0.0455$, because each background scales $I$ and $\sigma$ together and $R$ depends only on $I/\sigma$.
answer: Both give a response change of $0.0455$: a fixed proportion of the background gives a fixed response, which is Weber's law.
@mistakes
- Thinking photoreceptors depolarize in light: they hyperpolarize, and the sign flip to depolarization happens at the ON bipolar cell.
- Expecting a ganglion cell to signal absolute brightness: with balanced center and surround it signals local contrast, and its response to a uniform field is near zero.
- Forgetting the edge pixels in a filtering computation: decide how to treat the borders (repeat the end pixel, or compute only interior positions) and state it.

```python
import math


def dog_weights(half, sigma_c, sigma_s):
    """Difference-of-Gaussians weights that sum to zero."""
    xs = range(-half, half + 1)
    gc = [math.exp(-x * x / (2 * sigma_c ** 2)) for x in xs]
    gs = [math.exp(-x * x / (2 * sigma_s ** 2)) for x in xs]
    sc, ss = sum(gc), sum(gs)
    return [a / sc - b / ss for a, b in zip(gc, gs)]
```
hint: Build both Gaussians over the same positions, normalize each by its own sum, then subtract.
solution: Normalizing each Gaussian to sum to 1 means their difference sums to zero, so a uniform input gives no response. The narrow center Gaussian has the larger peak, giving a positive middle weight and negative flanks, an ON-center profile.

model:
```python
def surround_response_grid(image):
    """Center pixel minus the mean of its 8 neighbors, interior only."""
    rows, cols = len(image), len(image[0])
    out = []
    for r in range(1, rows - 1):
        line = []
        for c in range(1, cols - 1):
            total = 0.0
            for dr in (-1, 0, 1):
                for dc in (-1, 0, 1):
                    if dr != 0 or dc != 0:
                        total += image[r + dr][c + dc]
            line.append(image[r][c] - total / 8)
        out.append(line)
    return out
```
hint: Sum the eight neighbors with two nested loops over offsets -1, 0, 1, skipping the offset (0, 0), then divide by 8.
solution: For each interior pixel, add the eight surrounding values and divide by 8 to get the surround mean, then subtract it from the center. A uniform image gives zero everywhere, while a vertical edge gives a negative response on the dark side and a positive response on the light side.

model:
```python
def increment_response(i_b, d_i, n=1):
    """Response change for an increment on an adapted background."""
    def r(i):
        return i ** n / (i ** n + i_b ** n)
    return r(i_b + d_i) - r(i_b)
```
hint: Define the response with `sigma` equal to the background, then subtract the response to the background alone, which is 0.5.
solution: With the semi-saturation constant set to the background, $R$ depends only on the ratio $I/I_b$, so an increment of the same fraction gives the same response change at any background, for any exponent $n$. The tests show this scaling: 10 with a step of 1 behaves like 100 with a step of 10.
