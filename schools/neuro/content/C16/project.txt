**Week by week**

1. **Methods and thresholds.** A threshold is the level at a stated proportion correct on a psychometric function. Methods: adjustment, limits, constant stimuli, adaptive staircases. A two-down one-up staircase targets $0.5^{1/2} = 70.7\%$; average reversals after discarding the first.
2. **Weber and scaling.** $\Delta I/I = k$ (Weber); $S = c\ln(I/I_0)$ (Fechner); $\psi = a\varphi^b$ (Stevens), with $b$ the log–log slope.
3. **Signal detection.** $d' = z(H) - z(F)$, $c = -\tfrac12(z(H) + z(F))$; handle rates of 0 and 1 with a stated correction; 2AFC $d' = \sqrt{2}\,z(P_c)$; the ROC area is $\Phi(d'/\sqrt{2})$.
4. **Psychometric functions.** $\Psi(x) = \gamma + (1-\gamma-\lambda)F(x;\alpha,\beta)$; maximum likelihood with binomial log-likelihood; inverse $x_p = \alpha + \beta\ln(q/(1-q))$ for the logistic; bootstrap for uncertainty.
5. **Eye and retina.** Light hyperpolarizes photoreceptors; ON and OFF bipolar cells; center-surround ganglion cells; lateral inhibition; Naka–Rushton adaptation and Weber's law; Nyquist limit $1/(2s)$.
6. **Color.** Three cone classes, $r_c = \sum s_c(\lambda)p(\lambda)$; metamers; color matching as a linear system; dichromacy as one missing class; opponent channels.
7. **Visual cortex.** Simple cells: rectified linear filter; complex cells: energy $u_{\text{even}}^2 + u_{\text{odd}}^2$; orientation columns; retinotopy and magnification.
8. **Recognition.** Ventral stream; template matching plus max pooling gives position tolerance; face space; nearest-mean decoding and leave-one-out accuracy.
9. **Motion and depth.** Reichardt detector; aperture problem and intersection of constraints; disparity $\eta \approx I\Delta d/d^2$; Gestalt principles.
10. **Hearing.** $L = 20\log_{10}(p/p_0)$; tonotopy; Woodworth ITD $= (r/c)(\theta + \sin\theta)$; delay-line coincidence detection.
11. **Touch, pain, taste, smell.** Four mechanoreceptors; A-delta and C fibers; gate control; combinatorial odor codes.
12. **Inference.** Posterior mean $=$ reliability-weighted average; cue weights $w_i = r_i/\sum r$; combined $\sigma^2 = \sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2/(\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)$.

**Self-check.** Can you (a) state what proportion correct a staircase rule targets, (b) compute $d'$ and $c$ from counts and say what a negative $c$ means, (c) invert a psychometric function at a given proportion, (d) compute a cone response and decide whether two spectra match for a protanope, (e) compute a simple- and a complex-cell response on a small grid, (f) get an ITD from an angle, and (g) combine two cues and predict the combined variance?

**Common errors.** Reporting a threshold without its criterion; using hit rate in place of $d'$; passing rates of 0 or 1 to the inverse normal; using degrees where radians are needed; forgetting rectification in the simple-cell model; comparing a staircase estimate with the wrong point of the psychometric function; weighting cues by $1/\sigma$ instead of $1/\sigma^2$; reading a bootstrap interval from too few simulations.

model:
```python
import math
from statistics import NormalDist


def level_at_dprime(levels, correct, trials, target):
    """Level where the 2AFC d-prime first reaches the target."""
    z = NormalDist().inv_cdf
    d = []
    for k in correct:
        p = k / trials
        if p <= 0:
            p = 1 / (2 * trials)
        if p >= 1:
            p = 1 - 1 / (2 * trials)
        d.append(math.sqrt(2) * z(p))
    for i, di in enumerate(d):
        if di >= target:
            if i == 0:
                return levels[0]
            frac = (target - d[i - 1]) / (di - d[i - 1])
            return levels[i - 1] + frac * (levels[i] - levels[i - 1])
    return None
```

@exam self
prompt: A volunteer detects a faint patch more often after you tell them that patches are very common. A friend says that their vision has become more sensitive. Using signal detection theory, explain how you would decide between a change in sensitivity and a change in response bias, say what data you need and which quantities you would compute, and name one design change that would make the experiment immune to the bias.
hint: Think about what happens to false alarms, and which quantities separate sensitivity from bias.
solution: A change in the expected frequency of patches should move the criterion, not the stimulus distributions. To test it, include catch trials with no patch and record both the hit rate and the false-alarm rate in each condition (before and after the instruction, or low-frequency and high-frequency blocks). Compute $d' = z(H) - z(F)$ and $c = -(z(H) + z(F))/2$ for each condition, with a stated correction for rates of 0 or 1. If hits and false alarms both rise while $d'$ stays about the same and $c$ becomes more negative (more liberal), the change is in bias, which is the prediction when expectation changes. If $d'$ increases, sensitivity changed. Hits alone cannot tell the two apart, because a liberal observer scores more hits and more false alarms. A design change that removes the effect of bias is two-alternative forced choice, in which the patch is in one of two intervals and the volunteer picks the interval; proportion correct then depends on $d'$ through $P_c = \Phi(d'/\sqrt{2})$ and not on willingness to say yes (assuming no bias toward an interval). With few trials, report uncertainty on $d'$ and $c$, for instance by bootstrap.
