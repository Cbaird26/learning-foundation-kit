@article
Look at a yellow patch on a monitor. It contains only red and green light, with no light of the wavelength we call yellow, yet it looks the same as a pure yellow light. How can two physically different lights be indistinguishable? The answer lies in the eye. A rainbow contains a continuum of wavelengths, but the retina does not measure wavelength: it samples the spectrum with only three kinds of cone.

### Trichromacy and cone responses

In the 1800s Young and Helmholtz proposed, and Maxwell's color-matching experiments supported, that normal color vision is **trichromatic**: observers can match any light by mixing three suitably chosen primary lights. The explanation is the three **cone** classes. The **S** (short-wavelength), **M** (medium) and **L** (long) cones have pigments that absorb best near about 420, 530 and 560 nm. A cone class's response to a light with power $p(\lambda)$ at each wavelength is the sum of power times sensitivity:

$$r_c = \sum_\lambda s_c(\lambda)\,p(\lambda), \qquad c \in \{S, M, L\}.$$

A key property is **univariance**: a single cone's output depends only on the number of photons it absorbs, not on their wavelength. A dim light at its peak wavelength and a bright light at an off-peak wavelength can give the same response, so one cone type cannot tell wavelength from intensity. Color needs a comparison across cone classes. The table gives invented sensitivities (relative units) at five wavelengths, enough to see the idea.

| wavelength (nm) | 450 | 500 | 550 | 600 | 650 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| S | 0.80 | 0.15 | 0.00 | 0.00 | 0.00 |
| M | 0.10 | 0.65 | 0.95 | 0.25 | 0.02 |
| L | 0.05 | 0.35 | 1.00 | 0.80 | 0.15 |

### Metamers and color matching

Two lights with different spectra that give the same three cone responses look identical: they are **metamers**. This is why a monitor with three phosphors can display so many colors. In a color-matching experiment the observer adjusts the amounts $a_1, a_2, a_3$ of three primaries until their mixture matches a test light. If $A_{cj}$ is the response of cone $c$ to a unit of primary $j$ and $\mathbf{t}$ holds the cone responses to the test light, the match solves the linear system $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{t}$. With primaries at 450, 550 and 650 nm and a unit 500 nm test light, the solution is $\mathbf{a} \approx (0.1875,\ 0.7173,\ -2.5115)$. A **negative** amount means the primary cannot be subtracted from the mixture, so it is added to the test light instead and the match is made between the two mixtures. No set of three real primaries can match every light with positive amounts.

### Color deficiency

People with **color deficiency** have fewer or altered cone classes. A **dichromat** lacks one: **protanopia** (no L cones) and **deuteranopia** (no M cones) cause red–green confusions, and the rarer **tritanopia** (no S cones) causes blue–yellow confusions. With only two cone classes a dichromat can match any light with two primaries, and lights that differ only in the missing class look the same to them. In **anomalous trichromacy** all three classes are present but one pigment is shifted toward another, making matches differ from the typical observer's. The genes for the L and M pigments lie on the X chromosome, so red–green deficiencies are much commoner in men (about 8% in several well-studied populations) than in women (under 1%). Diagnosis and advice belong to clinicians.

### Opponent coding

Cone signals are recombined early. Retinal and thalamic neurons are **cone-opponent**: one common description has a red–green channel $L - M$, a blue–yellow channel $S - (L + M)/2$, and a luminance channel $L + M$, in line with Hering's opponent-process proposal and the later work of Hurvich and Jameson. This explains why we never see a reddish green, and it compresses three correlated cone signals into less redundant ones. Real color appearance also depends on context and color constancy, which a cone-response model does not explain.

### In code

```python
def cone_responses(spectrum, sens):
    """[S, M, L] responses: sum of power * sensitivity per cone class."""
    return [sum(s * p for s, p in zip(row, spectrum)) for row in sens]


def are_metamers(spec_a, spec_b, sens, tol=1e-9):
    """True if two spectra give the same response in all three cones."""
    ra = cone_responses(spec_a, sens)
    rb = cone_responses(spec_b, sens)
    return all(abs(x - y) <= tol for x, y in zip(ra, rb))


sens = [[0.80, 0.15, 0.00, 0.00, 0.00],
        [0.10, 0.65, 0.95, 0.25, 0.02],
        [0.05, 0.35, 1.00, 0.80, 0.15]]
print(cone_responses([0, 1, 0, 0, 0], sens))
```

This prints `[0.15, 0.65, 0.35]`, the responses to a unit light at 500 nm. Spectra are lists of power at the five wavelengths. Color scientists use finely sampled sensitivity tables, 3-by-N matrices and NumPy, but the sums are exactly these.

```python
def solve_linear(A, b):
    """Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting."""
    n = len(A)
    m = [list(map(float, row)) + [float(b[i])] for i, row in enumerate(A)]
    for c in range(n):
        p = max(range(c, n), key=lambda r: abs(m[r][c]))
        m[c], m[p] = m[p], m[c]
        for r in range(c + 1, n):
            f = m[r][c] / m[c][c]
            for k in range(c, n + 1):
                m[r][k] -= f * m[c][k]
    x = [0.0] * n
    for i in range(n - 1, -1, -1):
        s = sum(m[i][j] * x[j] for j in range(i + 1, n))
        x[i] = (m[i][n] - s) / m[i][i]
    return x
```
hint: Build an augmented copy of the matrix, eliminate below each pivot, then back-substitute from the last row up.
solution: Copying into an augmented matrix leaves the caller's `A` unchanged (one test checks this). Swapping in the largest pivot avoids dividing by zero when a diagonal entry is 0, as in the second and last tests. After elimination the matrix is upper triangular, and back-substitution finds the unknowns from the last to the first.

```python
def dichromat_match(sens, cones, primaries, test):
    """Amounts of two primaries matching a test light for two cone classes."""
    c1, c2 = cones
    p1, p2 = primaries
    a, b = sens[c1][p1], sens[c1][p2]
    c, d = sens[c2][p1], sens[c2][p2]
    det = a * d - b * c
    if abs(det) < 1e-12:
        return None
    t1, t2 = sens[c1][test], sens[c2][test]
    return ((t1 * d - b * t2) / det, (a * t2 - c * t1) / det)
```
hint: Cramer's rule gives $a_1 = (t_1 d - b t_2)/\det$ and $a_2 = (a t_2 - c t_1)/\det$ for the matrix with rows $(a, b)$ and $(c, d)$.
solution: Each present cone class gives one equation in the two unknown amounts. When the two primaries are the same (or proportional in both cone classes) the determinant is zero and no unique match exists, which the function reports as `None`. A negative amount would mean that primary is added to the test light.
@practice self
prompt: Explain, using cone responses, (a) why three primary lights can match any test light, (b) why the match of a trichromat does not hold for a protanope, and (c) why a protanope can still match any light with only two primaries. Include one numerical illustration with the cone responses of two lights.
solution: (a) Each light is reduced by the eye to three numbers, the S, M and L cone catches, so any two lights with equal catches are indistinguishable. Three primaries give three adjustable amounts, enough to equate three cone responses; the linear system $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{t}$ has a solution, which may need a negative amount (the primary is added to the test light). (b) A protanope lacks L cones, so lights are compared on S and M only. A match made for a trichromat equates S, M and L; one that has equal S and M but a different L catch, such as responses (0, 0.95, 1.00) and (0, 0.95, 3.04), looks the same to a protanope but different to a trichromat, so the trichromat's matches are not unique for a protanope. Conversely, the protanope accepts matches that a trichromat rejects. (c) With two cone classes there are two responses to equate, so two adjustable primaries suffice (a 2 by 2 system). Univariance explains why one cone class would not suffice: wavelength and intensity would be confounded.

```python
def monochromat_match(spec_a, spec_b, sens, cone):
    """Intensity factor that equates two spectra in one cone class."""
    row = sens[cone]
    ra = sum(s * p for s, p in zip(row, spec_a))
    rb = sum(s * p for s, p in zip(row, spec_b))
    if rb == 0:
        return None
    return ra / rb
```
hint: Compute the cone's response to each spectrum; the factor is their ratio.
solution: Scaling a spectrum by $k$ scales the cone response by $k$, so $k\,r_b = r_a$ gives $k = r_a/r_b$. Any two lights that the cone responds to can be matched this way, which is why one cone class cannot signal color. If the cone does not respond to `spec_b` at all, no intensity works.
