@article
Hold a coin in your palm without moving your fingers and, after a while, you stop feeling it; slide the coin, and the sensation returns at once. A tap makes you feel its onset, not the steady weight. Your sense of touch does not report a constant pressure, it reports changes, and some touch receptors do exactly that. Pain, taste and smell are the other body senses, and each uses a different kind of receptor and code.

### Touch

The glabrous (hairless) skin of the hand has four main **mechanoreceptor** types. They differ in how quickly they adapt and in how large an area each covers.

| receptor | adaptation | receptive field | best signals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Merkel cell complex | slowly adapting (SA I) | small | sustained pressure, edges, fine form |
| Meissner corpuscle | rapidly adapting (RA I) | small | light touch, slip, flutter |
| Pacinian corpuscle | rapidly adapting (RA II) | large | high-frequency vibration |
| Ruffini ending | slowly adapting (SA II) | large | skin stretch |

Spatial acuity depends on the density of receptors with small fields. The **two-point threshold**, the smallest separation at which two simultaneous touches are felt as two, is a few millimeters at the fingertip and several centimeters on the back. A touch signal reaches the brain along the dorsal-column pathway, crossing in the medulla, then through the thalamus to primary somatosensory cortex in the postcentral gyrus. The cortical map of the body surface (Penfield and Boldrey, 1937) is **somatotopic**, and regions with dense receptors, such as the hand and lips, claim far more cortex than the trunk.

### Pain

**Nociceptors** are free nerve endings that respond to damaging or potentially damaging stimuli. Two fiber types matter. **A-delta fibers** are thinly myelinated, conduct at roughly 5 to 30 m/s, and carry the quick, sharp "first pain". **C fibers** are unmyelinated, conduct at about 0.5 to 2 m/s, and carry the dull, burning "second pain" that arrives later: this is why a stubbed toe hurts twice. One nociceptor channel, TRPV1, responds to noxious heat and to capsaicin, the molecule that makes chili peppers feel hot. Pain signals ascend in the spinothalamic pathway, which crosses in the spinal cord. Pain is not the same as nociception: the experience depends on attention, expectation, context and mood, and brain regions such as the insula and anterior cingulate contribute to its unpleasantness. In the **gate-control theory** (Melzack and Wall, 1965), activity in large touch fibers and descending signals from the brain can reduce the transmission of pain signals in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord, which fits the relief of rubbing a bumped elbow. The details have been revised, but the principle that signals are modulated at the first synapse remains. Chronic pain and its treatment are matters for clinicians.

### Taste

Taste receptor cells in taste buds on the tongue and palate detect five basic qualities: sweet, sour, salty, bitter and umami (savory). Sweet, bitter and umami are detected by G-protein-coupled receptors, sour by proton-sensitive channels and salt by sodium-permeable channels. Signals travel through cranial nerves VII, IX and X to the nucleus of the solitary tract in the brainstem, the thalamus, and the insular and opercular cortex. What we call flavor combines taste with smell, texture and temperature. Whether quality is carried by dedicated "labeled lines" or by patterns across many cells is debated.

### Smell

Olfactory receptor neurons in the nasal epithelium each express one type of receptor, chosen from a family of about 400 functional genes in humans (the family was identified by Buck and Axel, 1991). Neurons with the same receptor send their axons to the same few **glomeruli** in the olfactory bulb, so each odorant produces a spatial pattern across glomeruli. Because every receptor responds to several molecules and every molecule activates several receptors, odors are coded **combinatorially**: the identity is in the pattern, not in a single cell. The bulb projects to olfactory (piriform) cortex without passing through the thalamus first.

### In code

```python
def touch_responses(depth, sa_gain, ra_gain):
    """Slowly adapting rate follows depth; rapidly adapting follows change."""
    sa = [sa_gain * d for d in depth]
    ra = []
    previous = 0
    for d in depth:
        ra.append(ra_gain * abs(d - previous))
        previous = d
    return sa, ra


depth = [0, 0, 1, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0]
print(*touch_responses(depth, 40, 100))
```

Depth is the indentation in millimeters at successive 10 ms samples, and the rates are in hertz. This prints `[0, 0, 40, 40, 40, 40, 0, 0] [0, 0, 100, 0, 0, 0, 100, 0]`: the slowly adapting cell fires throughout the press, and the rapidly adapting cell fires only at onset and offset.

@example
title: Identifying an odor from a receptor pattern
problem: Three receptors respond to a sample with the pattern $(3, 0, 4)$. A library gives rose the pattern $(1, 0, 1)$ and lemon the pattern $(0, 2, 0)$. Which does the sample match by cosine similarity?
step: Cosine similarity is $\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}/(\|\mathbf{x}\|\|\mathbf{y}\|)$, and $\|\mathbf{x}\| = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$.
step: For rose, $\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y} = 3 + 4 = 7$ and $\|\mathbf{y}\| = \sqrt{2} = 1.414$, so the cosine is $7/(5 \times 1.414) = 0.990$.
step: For lemon, $\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y} = 0$, so the cosine is $0$.
step: The sample matches rose; cosine ignores overall strength, so a diluted rose with the pattern $(1.5, 0, 2)$ would match as well.
answer: Rose (cosine $0.99$ against $0$).
@mistakes
- Equating pain with nociceptor activity: the nociceptive signal is one input, and attention, expectation and emotion change the experience, so the same stimulus can hurt more or less.
- Thinking each odorant has its own receptor: odors are coded by the pattern of activity across many receptor types, and one receptor responds to many odorants.
- Saying a slowly adapting receptor "does not respond to touch" after a while: it continues to fire during the press, whereas a rapidly adapting receptor fires only when the stimulus changes.

```python
def adapting_response(x, alpha):
    """Rectified response to the difference from a running adaptation state."""
    a = 0.0
    out = []
    for v in x:
        out.append(max(0.0, v - a))
        a += alpha * (v - a)
    return out
```
hint: Compute the output first, using the old state, and update the state afterwards.
solution: The state `a` is a running average of the stimulus, so the output `v - a` is large when the stimulus has just changed and decays as the state catches up, as a rapidly adapting receptor does. Setting `alpha = 0` keeps the state at zero, so the output follows the stimulus (a slowly adapting cell), and the rectification keeps the rate from going negative when the stimulus is removed.

```python
def two_point_resolved(afferents, p1, p2, radius):
    """True if a silent afferent separates two responding ones."""
    responding = [abs(a - p1) <= radius or abs(a - p2) <= radius
                  for a in sorted(afferents)]
    on = [i for i, r in enumerate(responding) if r]
    if len(on) < 2:
        return False
    return any(not responding[j] for j in range(on[0], on[-1] + 1))
```
hint: Sort the afferents, mark which respond, and look for a silent one between the first and last responder.
solution: The two touches are felt as separate only if the pattern of activity has a gap, a silent afferent between active ones. Wide receptive fields (a large radius) or sparse afferents fill or miss the gap, which is why acuity is poor where fields are large. Sorting first matters because the last test lists the afferents out of order.

model:
```python
import math


def identify_odor(pattern, library):
    """Odor whose reference pattern has the highest cosine similarity."""
    n_pat = math.sqrt(sum(v * v for v in pattern))
    if n_pat == 0:
        return None
    best_name, best_cos = None, None
    for name in sorted(library):
        ref = library[name]
        n_ref = math.sqrt(sum(v * v for v in ref))
        if n_ref == 0:
            continue
        cos = sum(a * b for a, b in zip(pattern, ref)) / (n_pat * n_ref)
        if best_cos is None or cos > best_cos + 1e-12:
            best_name, best_cos = name, cos
    return best_name
```
hint: Compute the norm of the pattern once, loop over the names in sorted order, and keep the best similarity with a small tolerance for ties.
solution: Cosine similarity compares the direction of the activity pattern and ignores its overall size, so a weak and a strong version of one odor match the same entry. Looping in sorted order with a strict improvement test makes ties go to the alphabetically first name, and entries with a zero norm have no direction and are skipped.
