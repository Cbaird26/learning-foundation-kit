@article
You judge the width of a box by looking at it and by holding it. Usually the two agree, but now suppose they disagree by a few millimeters. What do you perceive? The first guess, to trust vision, is a good one when the box is in sharp focus. In fog, the weight of the hands would count for more. The brain seems to know how reliable each sense is at the moment and to weigh the senses accordingly. This is the idea behind treating **perception as inference**, which goes back to Helmholtz's "unconscious inference" in the nineteenth century.

### Bayes' rule for perception

A sensory measurement $x$ is a noisy sample caused by an unknown world property $s$. The observer wants $p(s\mid x)$, which by **Bayes' rule** is proportional to the likelihood times the prior:

$$p(s\mid x) \propto p(x\mid s)\,p(s).$$

Suppose the likelihood is Gaussian, $x = s + \text{noise}$ with noise standard deviation $\sigma$, and the **prior** is Gaussian with mean $\mu_p$ and standard deviation $\sigma_p$. The posterior is Gaussian with

$$\hat s = \frac{x/\sigma^2 + \mu_p/\sigma_p^2}{1/\sigma^2 + 1/\sigma_p^2}, \qquad \sigma_{\text{post}}^2 = \frac{1}{1/\sigma^2 + 1/\sigma_p^2}.$$

The **reliability** (precision) $r = 1/\sigma^2$ of each source acts as its weight, so the estimate is a reliability-weighted average, pulled from the measurement toward the prior. When the measurement is unreliable, the prior counts more. This explains a classic effect: a low-contrast moving pattern looks slower than a high-contrast one, as if the brain used a prior favoring slow speeds when the evidence is weak (Weiss, Simoncelli and Adelson, 2002).

### Combining two cues

With two independent Gaussian cues $x_1$ and $x_2$ about the same quantity, with standard deviations $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_2$ and a flat prior, the posterior mean and the **maximum-likelihood** estimate coincide:

$$\hat s = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2,\qquad w_i = \frac{r_i}{r_1 + r_2},\qquad \sigma^2 = \frac{1}{r_1 + r_2} = \frac{\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2}{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2}.$$

The weights sum to 1. The combined variance is smaller than either single-cue variance, which is the benefit of combining: the combined estimate is at least as reliable as the better cue. Ernst and Banks (2002) tested these predictions with visual and haptic judgments of the height of a ridge. They measured thresholds with each sense alone (the standard deviation $\sigma$ of the cumulative-Gaussian psychometric function of week 4), predicted the weights and the combined threshold, and then measured them. When they added noise to the visual stimulus, the weight of vision fell, and the combined threshold was close to the prediction. A related finding: vision dominates sound location when the visual spot is sharp, but blurring it shifts the weight toward hearing (Alais and Burr, 2004).

### Testing the prediction

The prediction has two testable parts. The **weights** can be measured in a **cue-conflict** experiment, where the two cues are made to disagree by a small amount and the point of subjective equality of the combined percept shifts toward the more reliable cue by the predicted fraction. The **variance** prediction is tested by comparing the measured combined $\sigma$ with $\sqrt{\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2/(\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)}$ computed from the single-cue values. Agreement is evidence for optimal use of reliability, but both sides have uncertainty, so a comparison should include confidence intervals or a bootstrap.

### Assumptions and limits

The derivation assumes Gaussian noise, independent across cues, and a common cause. When the cues disagree a lot, observers may stop combining and treat them as coming from different sources, which is the subject of causal-inference models (Körding and colleagues, 2007). Some tasks show weights that deviate from the optimal ones, and an observer need not compute with explicit probabilities even when behavior is close to optimal. One proposal for how neurons could represent probability distributions is probabilistic population coding; whether and how the brain does this is an active research question.

### In code

```python
def combine_cues(estimates, sigmas):
    """Reliability-weighted estimate, its standard deviation and weights."""
    reliabilities = [1 / s ** 2 for s in sigmas]
    total = sum(reliabilities)
    weights = [r / total for r in reliabilities]
    estimate = sum(w * x for w, x in zip(weights, estimates))
    return estimate, (1 / total) ** 0.5, weights


est, sigma, w = combine_cues([55.0, 60.0], [2.0, 4.0])
print(round(est, 2), round(sigma, 3), [round(v, 2) for v in w])
```

This prints `56.0 1.789 [0.8, 0.2]`: vision says 55 mm with $\sigma = 2$ mm, touch says 60 mm with $\sigma = 4$ mm, so the combined estimate is 56 mm, much closer to the more reliable cue, with a standard deviation of 1.79 mm, below both. The function works for any number of cues.

problem: Vision gives a width of 50 mm ($\sigma_v = 2$ mm) and touch gives 56 mm ($\sigma_h = 3$ mm). Find the optimal combined estimate and its standard deviation.
step: Reliabilities: $r_v = 1/4 = 0.25$ and $r_h = 1/9 = 0.1111$; the total is $0.3611$.
step: Weights: $w_v = 0.25/0.3611 = 0.6923$ and $w_h = 0.3077$.

step: Standard deviation: $\sqrt{1/0.3611} = 1.664$ mm, below both 2 and 3 mm.

@example
title: A slow-speed prior
problem: A moving low-contrast pattern gives a speed measurement of 10 deg/s with $\sigma = 4$ deg/s. A prior favoring slow speeds has mean 0 and $\sigma_p = 8$ deg/s. What speed does the posterior mean give, and how reliable is it?
step: Reliabilities: measurement $1/16 = 0.0625$, prior $1/64 = 0.015625$; the total is $0.078125$.
step: The weight on the measurement is $0.0625/0.078125 = 0.8$.
step: Posterior mean: $0.8 \times 10 + 0.2 \times 0 = 8$ deg/s, slower than the measurement.
step: Posterior standard deviation: $\sqrt{1/0.078125} = 3.58$ deg/s.
answer: $8$ deg/s with $\sigma \approx 3.58$ deg/s: the pattern appears slower than it is.
@mistakes
- Averaging two cues with equal weights: the optimal weights are proportional to the reliabilities $1/\sigma^2$, not to $1/\sigma$ and not equal.
- Concluding that the combined estimate must lie between the cues, then ignoring its improved precision: the combined variance is below the smaller single-cue variance.
- Treating good agreement with optimal combination as proof that the brain computes Bayes' rule: it shows that behavior is consistent with reliability weighting, which several mechanisms could produce.

```python
def posterior_with_prior(x, sigma, mu_p, sigma_p):
    """Posterior mean and sd for a Gaussian measurement and Gaussian prior."""
    r = 1 / sigma ** 2
    r_p = 1 / sigma_p ** 2
    total = r + r_p
    mean = (r * x + r_p * mu_p) / total
    return mean, (1 / total) ** 0.5
```
hint: Convert both standard deviations to reliabilities, weight each mean by its reliability, and divide by the total reliability.
solution: The posterior mean is the reliability-weighted average of the measurement and the prior mean, and the posterior reliability is the sum of the two reliabilities, so the posterior is narrower than either source. A precise measurement (tiny `sigma`) is hardly changed by the prior, and a very precise prior (tiny `sigma_p`) overrides a noisy measurement, as the third and fourth tests show.

```python
import math


def combined_prediction(a, b, c):
    """Predicted and observed sd of a combined estimate, and cue A's weight."""
    def sd(v):
        m = sum(v) / len(v)
        return math.sqrt(sum((t - m) ** 2 for t in v) / (len(v) - 1))

    sa, sb = sd(a), sd(b)
    predicted = math.sqrt(sa ** 2 * sb ** 2 / (sa ** 2 + sb ** 2))
    weight_a = (1 / sa ** 2) / (1 / sa ** 2 + 1 / sb ** 2)
    return predicted, sd(c), weight_a
```
hint: Write a small helper for the sample standard deviation first, then apply the three formulas to its results.
solution: The sample standard deviation divides the summed squared deviations by $n - 1$. The prediction uses only the single-cue spreads, and comparing it with the observed spread of the two-cue condition is the test of optimal combination; in the first data set the observed spread (3.54) is larger than the predicted 2.89, so combination is less efficient than optimal, with only eight trials per list the difference is itself uncertain. Equal spreads give equal weights and a predicted spread of $s/\sqrt{2}$.

```python
def simulate_combination(true_s, sigma1, sigma2, noise1, noise2):
    """Combined estimates for supplied noise draws."""
    r1, r2 = 1 / sigma1 ** 2, 1 / sigma2 ** 2
    w1 = r1 / (r1 + r2)
    w2 = 1 - w1
    out = []
    for n1, n2 in zip(noise1, noise2):
        x1 = true_s + sigma1 * n1
        x2 = true_s + sigma2 * n2
        out.append(w1 * x1 + w2 * x2)
    return out
```
hint: Compute the two weights once from the sigmas, then loop over the paired noise values and form the weighted average on each trial.
solution: Each cue is the truth plus its noise scaled by its own standard deviation, and the combined estimate is the reliability-weighted average. With equal sigmas and opposite noise the errors cancel exactly, and when cue 2 is almost useless the estimate follows cue 1. Passing the random numbers in as lists keeps the function deterministic, so the standard deviation of the output could be compared with the prediction.
