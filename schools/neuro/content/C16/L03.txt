@article
Two observers listen for a faint tone. One says "yes" on 90% of the trials with a tone, and also on 40% of the trials with no tone. The other says "yes" on 70% of tone trials and 10% of silent trials. Who hears better? Is the first observer more sensitive to the tone, or just more willing to say "yes"? Counting correct answers cannot tell: a bold observer scores many hits and many false alarms, a cautious one few of each. **Signal detection theory** (Green and Swets, 1966) separates **sensitivity**, how well the signal is distinguished from noise, from **bias**, the readiness to say "yes".

### The four outcomes

On a trial the signal is present or absent, and the observer says yes or no.

| | says "yes" | says "no" |
|---|---|---|
| signal present | hit | miss |
| signal absent | false alarm | correct rejection |

The **hit rate** is $H = P(\text{yes}\mid\text{signal})$ and the **false-alarm rate** is $F = P(\text{yes}\mid\text{noise})$. The other two rates follow: miss rate $1-H$ and correct-rejection rate $1-F$.

### The equal-variance Gaussian model

The model assumes that on each trial the observer has a single number, the **decision variable**, such as the strength of internal evidence. On noise trials it has a Gaussian distribution with mean 0 and standard deviation 1; on signal trials it has a Gaussian with the same standard deviation and mean $d'$. The observer says "yes" when the variable exceeds a **criterion** $k$. Then

$$F = 1 - \Phi(k), \qquad H = 1 - \Phi(k - d'),$$

where $\Phi$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution. Inverting with $z = \Phi^{-1}$ (in Python, `NormalDist().inv_cdf`) gives the two measures:

$$d' = z(H) - z(F), \qquad c = -\tfrac{1}{2}\big(z(H) + z(F)\big).$$

The sensitivity $d'$ is the separation of the two distributions in standard deviations; $d' = 0$ is chance. The criterion $c$ is measured from the midpoint between the distributions: $c = 0$ is unbiased, $c > 0$ is conservative (few yes responses), and $c < 0$ is liberal. Useful values: $z(0.5)=0$, $z(0.8)=0.8416$, $z(0.9)=1.2816$, $z(0.95)=1.6449$, and $z(1-p) = -z(p)$.

| criterion $k$ | false-alarm rate | hit rate | $d'$ | $c$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 0.309 | 0.933 | 2 | −0.5 |
| 1.0 | 0.159 | 0.841 | 2 | 0 |
| 1.5 | 0.067 | 0.691 | 2 | 0.5 |

Here the observer's sensitivity is fixed at $d' = 2$ and only the criterion moves, which is what happens when payoffs or the proportion of signal trials change. The two observers above differ in both measures: sensitivities of $z(0.9) - z(0.4) = 1.53$ and $z(0.7) - z(0.1) = 1.81$, and criteria of $-0.51$ and $0.38$: the first observer is more liberal and the second more sensitive.

### Rates of 0 and 1, and forced choice

A rate of exactly 0 or 1 has $z = \mp\infty$. Two common fixes: the **log-linear rule** adds 0.5 to the hit and false-alarm counts and 1 to each total, so $H = (\text{hits} + 0.5)/(N_s + 1)$; or replace a rate of 0 by $1/(2N)$ and a rate of 1 by $1 - 1/(2N)$. State which you used. In a **two-alternative forced-choice** task the observer compares two intervals, which removes bias, and the proportion correct is $P_c = \Phi(d'/\sqrt{2})$, so $d' = \sqrt{2}\,z(P_c)$.

### The ROC curve

Sweeping $k$ from large to small traces the **receiver operating characteristic**: hit rate against false-alarm rate. At $d' = 0$ it is the diagonal; as $d'$ grows it bows toward the top-left corner. The area under it equals $\Phi(d'/\sqrt{2})$, the forced-choice proportion correct. If the two variances differ, the ROC plotted in $z$-coordinates has a slope other than 1, one reason to check the model rather than assume it.

### In code

```python
from statistics import NormalDist


def dprime_c(hits, n_signal, false_alarms, n_noise):
    """d-prime and criterion c, with the log-linear correction."""
    z = NormalDist().inv_cdf
    h = (hits + 0.5) / (n_signal + 1)
    f = (false_alarms + 0.5) / (n_noise + 1)
    return z(h) - z(f), -(z(h) + z(f)) / 2


d, c = dprime_c(84, 100, 16, 100)
print(round(d, 2), round(c, 2))
```

This prints `1.96 0.0`: 84 hits in 100 signal trials and 16 false alarms in 100 noise trials give $d' \approx 1.96$ and an unbiased observer.

title: Sensitivity and bias from counts
problem: In a yes/no detection task an observer said yes on 51 of 60 signal trials and on 3 of 60 noise trials. Use $z(0.85)=1.0364$ and $z(0.05)=-1.6449$ to compute $d'$ and $c$, and say whether the observer is liberal or conservative.
step: Convert counts to rates: $H = 51/60 = 0.85$ and $F = 3/60 = 0.05$.
step: Apply the formulas: $d' = z(H) - z(F) = 1.0364 + 1.6449 = 2.681$.
step: The criterion is $c = -\tfrac12 (1.0364 - 1.6449) = 0.304$.
step: Interpret: $c > 0$ means the observer needs more evidence than the midpoint before saying yes, so is conservative.
answer: $d' \approx 2.68$ and $c \approx 0.30$, a conservative observer.
@example
title: A perfect hit rate
problem: An observer said yes on all 20 signal trials and on 2 of 20 noise trials. Compute $d'$ with the log-linear rule.
step: A raw hit rate of 1 has an infinite $z$, so correct the counts: $H = (20 + 0.5)/21 = 0.9762$ and $F = (2 + 0.5)/21 = 0.1190$.
step: Convert to $z$-scores: $z(0.9762) = 1.9808$ and $z(0.1190) = -1.1798$.
step: Subtract: $d' = 1.9808 + 1.1798 = 3.16$.
step: The criterion is $c = -\tfrac12(1.9808 - 1.1798) = -0.40$, a liberal observer.
answer: $d' \approx 3.16$, $c \approx -0.40$. The correction makes the value an estimate, not an exact one.
@mistakes
- Using the hit rate (or percent correct) as a measure of sensitivity: a liberal criterion raises hits and false alarms together, so only $d'$ separates the two effects.
- Getting the sign of the criterion backward: with $c = -(z(H)+z(F))/2$, a positive $c$ means conservative, a negative $c$ liberal.
- Passing rates of exactly 0 or 1 to the inverse normal function, which fails or gives infinity, without stating a correction.

```python
from statistics import NormalDist


def roc_points(dprime, criteria):
    """(false-alarm rate, hit rate) for each criterion."""
    phi = NormalDist().cdf
    return [(1 - phi(k), 1 - phi(k - dprime)) for k in criteria]
```
hint: The false-alarm rate depends only on the criterion; the hit rate uses the criterion measured from the signal mean, $k - d'$.
solution: Noise trials are standard normal, so $F = P(x > k) = 1 - \Phi(k)$. Signal trials have mean $d'$, so $H = P(x > k) = 1 - \Phi(k - d')$. With $d' = 0$ the two rates are equal, which puts every point on the diagonal.

```python
def roc_area(points):
    """Trapezoid area under an ROC curve through the given points."""
    pts = sorted(list(points) + [(0.0, 0.0), (1.0, 1.0)])
    area = 0.0
    for (f0, h0), (f1, h1) in zip(pts, pts[1:]):
        area += (f1 - f0) * (h0 + h1) / 2
    return area
```
hint: Sorting must happen after adding the corner points, or the trapezoids will be mismatched.
solution: Sorting by false-alarm rate puts the points in order along the curve, and each trapezoid has width $f_{i+1} - f_i$ and mean height $(h_i + h_{i+1})/2$. With no points the curve is the diagonal from $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$ and the area is 0.5, the chance value.

model:
```python
from statistics import NormalDist


def dprime_from_trials(truth, resp):
    """d-prime and criterion c from trial lists, with 1/(2N) clipping."""
    z = NormalDist().inv_cdf
    n_sig = sum(truth)
    n_noise = len(truth) - n_sig
    hits = sum(1 for t, r in zip(truth, resp) if t == 1 and r == 1)
    fas = sum(1 for t, r in zip(truth, resp) if t == 0 and r == 1)

    def rate(count, n):
        p = count / n
        if p == 0:
            return 1 / (2 * n)
        if p == 1:
            return 1 - 1 / (2 * n)
        return p

    h = rate(hits, n_sig)
    f = rate(fas, n_noise)
    return z(h) - z(f), -(z(h) + z(f)) / 2
```
hint: Count hits among trials where `truth` is 1 and false alarms among trials where it is 0, and divide by the right totals before clipping.
solution: The two rates have different denominators (signal trials and noise trials), so each is clipped with its own $N$. A perfect observer in the third test gets $H = 7/8$ and $F = 1/8$, giving $d' = 2z(0.875) = 2.30$, and swapping every response flips the sign of $d'$.
