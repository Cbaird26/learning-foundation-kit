@article
Hold a 40 g weight in your palm and add a 1 g coin: you notice. Hold a 4 kg bag of flour and add the same coin: you do not. The physical change is identical, yet the sensory change is not. How much must a stimulus change before you notice? This is the **difference threshold**, and its unit is the **just noticeable difference** (JND), the smallest change in intensity that you detect on a stated proportion of trials.

### Weber's law

Ernst Weber found in the nineteenth century that the JND grows in proportion to the baseline intensity $I$:

$$\frac{\Delta I}{I} = k .$$

The constant $k$ is the **Weber fraction**. It is dimensionless, so it compares senses: it is small for judging the brightness of a light of moderate intensity and larger for other qualities. A lower $k$ means finer discrimination. Suppose a light's luminance is varied around four baselines and a staircase yields the thresholds below.

| baseline $I$ (cd/m²) | JND $\Delta I$ (cd/m²) | $\Delta I / I$ |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | 0.31 | 0.031 |
| 20 | 0.62 | 0.031 |
| 40 | 1.30 | 0.0325 |
| 80 | 2.40 | 0.030 |

The ratio is nearly constant, about $0.031$, so Weber's law holds well here. Near the absolute threshold it fails: the ratio rises at very low intensities, where internal noise dominates, and a common repair adds a small constant to the baseline. The law also fails at extreme intensities.

### Fechner's law

Gustav Fechner added an assumption: every JND adds the same small increment $c$ to sensation $S$. Then a change of intensity $dI$ adds $dS = c\,dI/I$ to sensation, since the JND grows with $I$. Integrating from the absolute threshold $I_0$, where sensation is zero, gives

$$S = c \ln \frac{I}{I_0} .$$

Equal ratios of intensity add equal amounts of sensation, so sensation grows with the logarithm of intensity. Counting JNDs is an indirect way to measure sensation. From $I$ to $I(1+k)$ is one JND, so the number of JNDs between $I_1$ and $I_2$ is $\ln(I_2/I_1)/\ln(1+k)$.

### Stevens's power law

S. S. Stevens (1957) asked people directly. In **magnitude estimation** they assign numbers to stimuli ("if this is 10, how much is that?"), and in cross-modality matching they set the intensity of one sense to match another. The estimates $\psi$ followed a power function of the physical intensity $\varphi$:

$$\psi = a\,\varphi^{\,b} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \log\psi = \log a + b\log\varphi .$$

On log–log axes this is a straight line whose slope is the **exponent** $b$. Typical exponents depend on the continuum: loudness grows with sound pressure with an exponent near 0.67, the brightness of a small target in the dark near 0.33, apparent length near 1, and electric shock near 3.5. When $b < 1$ the response is compressive (doubling the stimulus less than doubles the sensation), and when $b > 1$ it is expansive. The two laws are not strictly rivals: they come from different tasks, and a power law with a small exponent resembles a logarithm over a limited range. Whether they measure the same internal quantity is debated, and magnitude estimates depend on the instructions and the range of stimuli.

### In code

```python
def weber_fraction(baselines, jnds):
    """Mean of jnd / baseline over the supplied pairs."""
    ratios = [j / b for b, j in zip(baselines, jnds)]
    return sum(ratios) / len(ratios)


print(round(weber_fraction([10, 20, 40, 80], [0.31, 0.62, 1.30, 2.40]), 4))
```

This prints `0.0311`. To fit a power law without NumPy, take logarithms of both variables and fit a straight line by least squares; the slope is the exponent. NumPy's `polyfit` does the same in one call, and the same least-squares formula appears in the practice items.
@example
title: Predicting a difference threshold
problem: A person lifts a 200 g weight and a staircase puts the JND at 6 g. Assuming Weber's law, find the Weber fraction and the JND for a 500 g weight.
step: The Weber fraction is $k = \Delta I / I = 6/200 = 0.03$.
step: Weber's law says $\Delta I = k I$ at any baseline, so at 500 g the JND is $0.03 \times 500 = 15$ g.
step: Check: the baseline is 2.5 times larger, so the JND should be 2.5 times larger, and $6 \times 2.5 = 15$ g.
answer: $k = 0.03$ and a JND of $15$ g.
@example
title: A power-law exponent from two estimates
problem: A volunteer rates the apparent intensity of a stimulus as 6 at physical level 20 (arbitrary units) and as 12 at level 80. Assuming Stevens's power law, find the exponent and the rating expected at level 180.
step: The exponent is the log–log slope: $b = \ln(12/6)/\ln(80/20) = \ln 2/\ln 4 = 0.5$.
step: The ratio of the levels is $180/20 = 9$, so the rating scales by $9^{0.5} = 3$.
step: Starting from the known rating of 6 at level 20, the predicted rating is $6 \times 3 = 18$.
answer: $b = 0.5$, and a rating of $18$ at level 180.
@mistakes
- Confusing the JND with the Weber fraction: the JND $\Delta I$ has the units of the stimulus and grows with $I$, while the Weber fraction $\Delta I/I$ is a dimensionless constant.
- Reading a power law's exponent from a plot with a linear axis: the exponent is the slope of $\log\psi$ against $\log\varphi$, not the slope of $\psi$ against $\varphi$.
- Treating Weber's law as exact: it fails near the absolute threshold and at very high intensities, so fit it only over the range where the ratio is flat.

```python
def count_jnds(i_low, i_high, k):
    """Number of whole JNDs of fraction k between two intensities."""
    n = 0
    level = i_low
    while level * (1 + k) <= i_high * (1 + 1e-9):
        level *= 1 + k
        n += 1
    return n
```
hint: Each pass of a loop multiplies the level by $1 + k$; stop when the next level would overshoot `i_high`.
solution: The loop counts how many multiplications by $1+k$ fit under `i_high`, the floor of $\ln(i_{high}/i_{low})/\ln(1+k)$. The case `(1.0, 2.0, 1.0)` lands exactly on 2.0, which a strict comparison without slack could miss in floating point, and the reversed case never enters the loop.

model:
```python
import math


def power_exponent(phi, psi):
    """Least-squares slope of log10(psi) against log10(phi)."""
    x = [math.log10(v) for v in phi]
    y = [math.log10(v) for v in psi]
    mx = sum(x) / len(x)
    my = sum(y) / len(y)
    num = sum((a - mx) * (b - my) for a, b in zip(x, y))
    den = sum((a - mx) ** 2 for a in x)
    return num / den
```
hint: Take logarithms of both lists first, center each on its mean, then divide the cross-product sum by the sum of squares of the centered x values.
solution: The fitted line in log–log coordinates has the least-squares slope $\sum (x-\bar x)(y-\bar y)/\sum (x-\bar x)^2$, and that slope is $b$ in $\psi = a\varphi^b$. Because the slope is a ratio of logarithms, the base of the logarithm does not matter. A flat set of estimates gives an exponent of zero.

model:
```python
def weber_from_staircases(baselines, reversal_lists, skip):
    """Mean Weber fraction from staircase reversals at several baselines."""
    fractions = []
    for base, revs in zip(baselines, reversal_lists):
        kept = revs[skip:]
        threshold = sum(kept) / len(kept) - base
        fractions.append(threshold / base)
    return sum(fractions) / len(fractions)
```
hint: Handle one baseline at a time: slice off the first `skip` reversals, average, subtract the baseline, divide by the baseline.
solution: Each staircase gives a threshold level; subtracting the baseline gives the increment $\Delta I$, and dividing by the baseline gives that baseline's Weber fraction. Averaging the fractions summarizes how well Weber's law holds. The result changes with `skip` because early reversals are biased by the starting level, as the second and third tests show.
