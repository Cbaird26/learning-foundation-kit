@article
Close your eyes and a friend clapping in the room is easy to point to, even though your ears are just two small pressure sensors on opposite sides of your head. Where does direction come from? Mostly from comparing the two ears: a sound from the left reaches the left ear slightly earlier and slightly louder than the right.

### From pressure to the cochlea

Sound is a wave of air pressure. Pitch relates to its frequency (in hertz; humans hear roughly 20 Hz to 20 kHz) and loudness to its amplitude. Levels are expressed in decibels of sound pressure level: $L = 20\log_{10}(p/p_0)$ with $p_0 = 20\ \mu\text{Pa}$, roughly the quietest sound a young listener detects at a mid frequency. Doubling the pressure adds about 6 dB; a tenfold pressure increase adds 20 dB. Loud sound can permanently damage the ear, which is why any listening experiment must stay at comfortable levels.

The pinna and ear canal funnel sound to the eardrum. The three **ossicles** of the middle ear transmit its vibration to the fluid-filled cochlea, matching the impedance of air to fluid. Inside the cochlea, the **basilar membrane** is narrow and stiff at the base and wide and floppy at the apex, so a tone of a given frequency produces a traveling wave that peaks at one place: high frequencies near the base, low frequencies near the apex (von Békésy's traveling-wave work). This is the **tonotopic** map, and it is kept through the auditory pathway. On the membrane lies the organ of Corti, with about 3,500 **inner hair cells**, which transduce vibration and send most of the signal to the brain, and about 12,000 **outer hair cells**, which amplify and sharpen the response by changing their length. Bending a hair cell's stereocilia opens mechanically gated channels, and potassium entry depolarizes the cell. Frequency is coded by place and also by timing: auditory nerve fibers fire at a preferred phase of a low-frequency tone (phase locking, up to about 4 to 5 kHz).

### The auditory pathway

Signals pass from the cochlear nuclei to the **superior olivary complex** in the brainstem, the first stage with input from both ears, then to the inferior colliculus, the medial geniculate nucleus of the thalamus, and primary auditory cortex on the upper surface of the temporal lobe.

### Locating a sound

Three cues locate a sound. The **interaural time difference** (ITD) is the difference in arrival time at the two ears and is useful at low frequencies, where the wave's phase can be compared. The **interaural level difference** (ILD) arises because the head casts an acoustic shadow, mostly for high frequencies. **Spectral cues** from the folds of the pinna change with elevation and help with up–down and front–back judgments. For a spherical head of radius $r$ in the horizontal plane, a distant source at azimuth $\theta$ (0 straight ahead, 90° directly to one side) produces

$$\text{ITD} = \frac{r}{c}\,(\theta + \sin\theta),\qquad \theta\ \text{in radians},$$

the **Woodworth** formula, where $c$ is the speed of sound (about 343 m/s in air). The extra path around the head, $r\theta$, plus the straight-line part, $r\sin\theta$, divided by $c$ gives the delay. For $r = 0.0875$ m the maximum, at 90°, is about 656 µs. Humans can detect ITD changes of about ten microseconds. For a pure tone, an ITD becomes a phase difference, $360^\circ f\,\text{ITD}$, which is ambiguous when it exceeds half a cycle, so ITD works best below about 1.5 kHz.

Jeffress (1948) proposed that the brain compares the ears with **delay lines** and **coincidence detectors**: each detector receives input from the two ears through different delays and fires most when the internal delay cancels the external ITD. The barn owl brainstem looks much like this (Carr and Konishi, 1990). Whether mammals use the same scheme or a different population code is still debated.

### In code

```python
import math


def woodworth_itd(theta_deg, r=0.0875, c=343.0):
    """ITD in microseconds for a source at azimuth theta_deg."""
    t = math.radians(theta_deg)
    return r / c * (t + math.sin(t)) * 1e6


print([round(woodworth_itd(a), 1) for a in (0, 30, 60, 90)])
```

This prints `[0.0, 261.1, 488.1, 655.8]`: the ITD grows with azimuth but flattens near 90°, where the source is directly to one side. Real heads and ears differ from the sphere, so this is a model.

title: ITD for a source at 75 degrees
problem: A distant source is 75 degrees to the right of straight ahead. Find the ITD for a spherical head of radius 0.0875 m with $c = 343$ m/s, and say which ear is reached first.
step: Convert the angle: $\theta = 75° = 1.3090$ rad, and $\sin\theta = 0.9659$.
step: The sum is $\theta + \sin\theta = 2.2749$.
step: Scale by $r/c = 0.0875/343 = 2.551\times10^{-4}$ s: $\text{ITD} = 2.551\times10^{-4} \times 2.2749 = 5.803\times10^{-4}$ s.
step: That is about $580$ µs, and the sound reaches the right ear first, since the source is on the right.
answer: About $580$ µs, right ear leading.
@example
title: A delay-line estimate
problem: The left ear signal is $[0, 2, 4, 2, 0, 0]$ and the right ear signal is $[0, 0, 0, 2, 4, 2]$, sampled every 25 µs. Compute $\sum_i l_i\, r_{i+d}$ for lags $d = 0, 1, 2, 3$ and find the delay.
step: For $d = 0$: only $i = 3$ overlaps nonzero values: $2 \times 2 = 4$.
step: For $d = 1$: $4\times2 + 2\times4 = 16$; for $d = 2$: $2\times2 + 4\times4 + 2\times2 = 24$; for $d = 3$: $2\times4 + 4\times 2 = 16$.
step: The score is largest at $d = 2$ samples, which is $2 \times 25 = 50$ µs.
step: The right signal lags the left one, so the sound reached the left ear first and the source is on the left.
answer: A delay of $50$ µs with the left ear leading.
@mistakes
- Treating the decibel as a unit of pressure: it is a logarithm of a ratio, and for sound pressure $L = 20\log_{10}(p/p_0)$, whereas for power the factor is 10.
- Applying the Woodworth formula with the angle in degrees: the angle $\theta$ must be in radians inside the formula.
- Assuming one cue does everything: ITD works at low frequencies, ILD at high frequencies, and spectral cues are needed for elevation and front–back, so a pure tone from the front and from the back has nearly the same ITD and ILD.

model:
```python
def best_lag(left, right, max_lag):
    """Lag (in samples) at which the right signal best matches the left."""
    best_d, best_score = None, None
    for d in range(-max_lag, max_lag + 1):
        score = sum(left[i] * right[i + d] for i in range(len(left))
                    if 0 <= i + d < len(right))
        if best_score is None or score > best_score + 1e-12:
            best_d, best_score = d, score
    return best_d
```
hint: Loop over lags, compute the cross-correlation sum for each with a bounds check on the index, and keep the lag with the highest sum.
solution: The score is the cross-correlation at each lag, the quantity a bank of coincidence detectors with different internal delays computes. The bounds check handles the ends of the signals, and a strict `>` comparison keeps the first of any tied lags, so the all-zero test returns the first lag scanned. Multiply the lag by the sampling interval to get the ITD.

model:
```python
def coincidence_counts(left, right, delays, window):
    """Number of left-right spike pairs matching each internal delay."""
    counts = []
    for d in delays:
        n = 0
        for a in left:
            for b in right:
                if abs((b - a) - d) <= window:
                    n += 1
        counts.append(n)
    return counts
```
hint: For every delay, loop over all left-right pairs and count those whose time difference is within the window of the delay.
solution: Each internal delay is a coincidence detector that counts spike pairs whose arrival-time difference matches it, within the window. The detector whose delay equals the true ITD collects the most coincidences; in the first test it is the 300 µs detector, which gets four. The comparison `<=` includes pairs exactly at the window edge, as the second test needs.

model:
```python
import math


def azimuth_from_itd(itd_us, r=0.0875, c=343.0):
    """Azimuth (degrees) whose Woodworth ITD equals itd_us."""
    def f(th):
        return r / c * (th + math.sin(th)) * 1e6

    sign = 1 if itd_us >= 0 else -1
    target = abs(itd_us)
    if target >= f(math.pi / 2):
        return 90.0 * sign
    lo, hi = 0.0, math.pi / 2
    for _ in range(60):
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        if f(mid) < target:
            lo = mid
        else:
            hi = mid
    return sign * math.degrees((lo + hi) / 2)
```
hint: The ITD increases steadily with azimuth on 0 to 90 degrees, so bisection works: keep the half-interval that contains the target.
solution: Because $\theta + \sin\theta$ increases monotonically on $[0, \pi/2]$, each halving keeps the interval that contains the target ITD, and 60 halvings pin the angle to machine precision. The sign handles sources on the other side, and an ITD at or beyond the 90-degree value is clamped because no azimuth in this model produces it.
