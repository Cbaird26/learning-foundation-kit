@article
The **skeleton** supports the body, protects organs, provides levers for movement, stores calcium and phosphate, and houses bone marrow, where blood cells are made. Muscles pull on bones to produce movement.

### Bone

Bone is connective tissue with a matrix of collagen and **hydroxyapatite** (calcium phosphate crystals). **Compact (cortical) bone** forms the dense outer shell; **spongy (trabecular) bone** forms a lattice inside, with marrow in the spaces. Three bone cell types work together: **osteoblasts** build matrix, **osteocytes** are mature cells that maintain it, and **osteoclasts** resorb it. Bone is continually **remodeled**, and the balance determines bone mass: in osteoporosis, resorption outpaces formation, increasing fracture risk. Long bones grow in length at the **epiphyseal plates** (growth plates) until adolescence. Cartilage covers joint surfaces. Joints may be fibrous (skull sutures), cartilaginous (intervertebral discs) or synovial (knee, shoulder), where a lubricated capsule allows movement. **Tendons** connect muscle to bone; **ligaments** connect bone to bone.

### Calcium homeostasis

Plasma calcium is held at roughly $8.5$–$10.5$ mg/dL, because it is essential for nerve and muscle function and clotting. When calcium falls, the parathyroid glands release **parathyroid hormone (PTH)**, which stimulates osteoclast activity (releasing calcium from bone), increases calcium reabsorption in the distal kidney tubule, and promotes activation of **vitamin D** (calcitriol) in the kidney, which raises calcium absorption in the intestine. Rising calcium then suppresses PTH. This is a classic negative feedback loop. Calcitonin from thyroid C cells lowers calcium modestly and is of limited importance in adults.

About 40 to 45% of plasma calcium is bound to proteins, mostly albumin, and only ionized calcium (about half of the total) is active. When albumin is low, total calcium falls without a change in ionized calcium. A common estimate is
$$\text{corrected Ca} = \text{measured Ca} + 0.8\,(4.0 - \text{albumin}),$$
with calcium and albumin in mg/dL and g/dL.

### Muscle contraction

Skeletal muscle fibers are packed with **myofibrils**, made of repeating units called **sarcomeres**, bounded by Z discs. Thin filaments contain **actin** with **tropomyosin** and **troponin**; thick filaments are made of **myosin**. In the **sliding-filament model**, thin filaments slide over thick filaments, shortening the sarcomere without shortening the filaments themselves. The cycle:

1. An action potential at the neuromuscular junction (acetylcholine) depolarizes the fiber and travels down T-tubules.
2. The sarcoplasmic reticulum releases **Ca$^{2+}$**.
3. Ca$^{2+}$ binds troponin C, moving tropomyosin off the myosin-binding sites on actin.
4. Myosin heads bind actin, perform a **power stroke** and ADP is released.
5. **ATP** binds myosin, which detaches from actin, and ATP hydrolysis re-cocks the head.

Contraction ends when Ca$^{2+}$ is pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Without ATP, myosin stays bound to actin, which explains rigor mortis. Skeletal muscle gets ATP from creatine phosphate, glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation. Smooth and cardiac muscle differ in their regulation; cardiac muscle relies on Ca$^{2+}$ entry from outside the cell.

### Thyroid feedback in one account

A complete explanation of the thyroid axis has a chain and a loop: TRH raises TSH, TSH raises T$_4$/T$_3$, and T$_4$/T$_3$ suppress TRH and TSH. Disease can break the chain at the gland, the pituitary or the feedback signal, and the TSH and free T$_4$ pattern shows which.

@example
title: Correcting calcium for low albumin
problem: A patient has a total calcium of $7.0$ mg/dL and an albumin of $3.0$ g/dL. Estimate the corrected calcium.
step: Use $\text{corrected Ca} = \text{measured Ca} + 0.8\,(4.0 - \text{albumin})$.
step: The albumin deficit is $4.0 - 3.0 = 1.0$, so the correction is $0.8 \times 1.0 = 0.8$.
step: Corrected Ca $= 7.0 + 0.8 = 7.8$ mg/dL.
step: This is still low, so the hypocalcemia is real, not just a result of low albumin.

@mistakes
- Stating that filaments shorten during contraction; the thin and thick filaments keep their length and slide past each other.
- Thinking ATP is needed only to start the power stroke; it is needed for detachment, and its absence causes rigor.
- Mixing up osteoblasts (build) and osteoclasts (resorb).
- Interpreting a low total calcium as hypocalcemia without checking albumin or ionized calcium.

@practice self
prompt: Explain, using the thyroid as the example, how negative feedback keeps a hormone level stable. Then say what you would expect TSH and free T$_4$ to do if the thyroid gland is destroyed.
hint: Write the chain hypothalamus, pituitary, thyroid, then add the arrow that returns to the top.
solution: The hypothalamus releases TRH, which stimulates the anterior pituitary to release TSH. TSH stimulates the thyroid to make T$_4$ and T$_3$. These hormones inhibit TRH and TSH release, so when thyroid hormone is high, TSH falls and output falls; when it is low, inhibition is removed, TSH rises and output increases. This keeps hormone levels near a set point. If the thyroid is destroyed, free T$_4$ falls, feedback is lost, and TSH rises markedly (primary hypothyroidism).

rubric: States that thyroid hormone inhibits TRH and TSH (the feedback arrow).
rubric: Explains that a fall in hormone raises TSH and a rise lowers it.
rubric: Predicts low free T$_4$ and high TSH after gland destruction.
