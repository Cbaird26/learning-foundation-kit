@article
The **endocrine system** uses hormones, chemical messengers released into the blood, to coordinate processes over minutes to months. The nervous system is fast and local; the endocrine system is slower and widespread. Each hormone affects only cells that have its **receptor**.

### Classes of hormones

- **Peptide and protein hormones** (insulin, glucagon, growth hormone, ADH) are water-soluble, travel free in blood, and bind **cell-surface receptors** that activate second messengers such as cAMP or kinase cascades. Effects are rapid.
- **Steroid hormones** (cortisol, aldosterone, estrogen, testosterone, vitamin D) are made from cholesterol, are lipid-soluble, travel bound to carrier proteins, and bind **intracellular receptors** that regulate gene transcription. Effects are slower and longer.
- **Thyroid hormones** (T$_3$ and T$_4$) are amino-acid derivatives but behave like steroids: they cross the membrane and act on nuclear receptors.
- **Catecholamines** (epinephrine, norepinephrine) are amine hormones acting on cell-surface receptors.

### Hypothalamus and pituitary

The hypothalamus controls the pituitary. The **posterior pituitary** stores and releases **antidiuretic hormone (ADH)** and **oxytocin**, which are made in the hypothalamus and travel down axons. The **anterior pituitary** makes its own hormones under the control of releasing and inhibiting hormones delivered through a portal blood system: **TSH**, **ACTH**, **FSH**, **LH**, **growth hormone** and **prolactin**. Many axes follow the same pattern:
$$\text{hypothalamus} \to \text{anterior pituitary} \to \text{target gland} \to \text{hormone}\ (\text{inhibits upstream}).$$

### The thyroid axis as a model of feedback

**TRH** from the hypothalamus stimulates the anterior pituitary to release **TSH**, which stimulates the thyroid to take up iodide and make and release **T$_4$** (thyroxine, the main product) and **T$_3$** (the more active form, mostly generated from T$_4$ in tissues). T$_3$ and T$_4$ increase metabolic rate and heat production. Free T$_4$ and T$_3$ exert **negative feedback**, suppressing TRH and TSH. The pattern of lab results therefore locates the problem:

| Condition | TSH | Free T$_4$ | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary hypothyroidism | High | Low | Hashimoto thyroiditis |
| Primary hyperthyroidism | Low | High | Graves disease |
| Central (pituitary) hypothyroidism | Low or inappropriately normal | Low | Pituitary disease |

In Graves disease, antibodies stimulate the TSH receptor, so the thyroid makes hormone without regard to TSH, and feedback suppresses TSH. Chronic TSH stimulation, as with iodine deficiency, can enlarge the gland (goiter). Hypothyroidism is treated with **levothyroxine**; a typical full replacement dose for study is about $1.6\ \mu\text{g}/\text{kg}$ per day in adults, adjusted by TSH. Hormone levels are monitored by TSH because it is the sensitive index of the feedback loop.

### Other axes and glucose

The adrenal cortex makes **cortisol** (ACTH-driven, with negative feedback), **aldosterone** (regulated mainly by the renin–angiotensin system and potassium) and androgens; the adrenal medulla makes catecholamines. Blood glucose is kept near $70$–$100$ mg/dL when fasting by the pancreas: **insulin** from beta cells lowers glucose by promoting uptake and storage, and **glucagon** from alpha cells raises it by stimulating glycogen breakdown and gluconeogenesis. Both are negative feedback loops, since each response reverses the stimulus that evoked it. Diabetes mellitus results from absent insulin (type 1) or resistance and relative deficiency (type 2).

@example
title: Interpreting thyroid tests
problem: A $45$-year-old woman is tired, cold-intolerant and has gained weight. Her TSH is high and her free T$_4$ is low. Where is the problem, and why is TSH high?
step: Free T$_4$ is low, so thyroid hormone is deficient, consistent with her symptoms of low metabolic rate.
step: Normally T$_4$ inhibits the pituitary. With low T$_4$, the negative feedback is weakened, so the pituitary releases more TSH.
step: High TSH with low T$_4$ means the pituitary is responding appropriately, and the defect is in the thyroid itself: primary hypothyroidism.
step: A common cause is autoimmune Hashimoto thyroiditis, and treatment is levothyroxine, titrated to bring TSH to normal.

@mistakes
- Assuming low thyroid hormone must mean low TSH; in primary hypothyroidism, TSH is high because of lost feedback.
- Saying the posterior pituitary makes ADH and oxytocin; it stores and releases them, and they are made in the hypothalamus.
- Treating steroid and peptide hormones as acting the same way; the receptor location explains the speed and duration of the response.
- Confusing insulin and glucagon directions: insulin lowers blood glucose and glucagon raises it.
