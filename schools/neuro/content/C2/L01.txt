@article
**Evolution** is a change in the heritable characteristics of a population over generations. It is a property of populations, not of individuals: a single organism does not evolve, but the frequency of an allele in a population can change from one generation to the next.

### The logic of natural selection

Natural selection follows from four observations that you can check in almost any population.

1. Individuals vary in their traits.
2. Some of that variation is **heritable**, passed on through genes.
3. More offspring are produced than the environment can support, so individuals compete.
4. Individuals with certain heritable traits survive and reproduce more than others.

When all four hold, alleles associated with higher reproductive success become more common. A trait that raises reproductive success in a given environment is an **adaptation**. Selection acts on phenotypes, but only heritable variation produces a response across generations. A large muscle gained by exercise is not inherited, which is why the inheritance of acquired characteristics does not occur. Selection is also not goal-directed: it favors whatever works in the current environment, and the environment can change.

A clinical example is antibiotic resistance. Mutations that confer resistance arise at random in a bacterial population. Before drug exposure they are rare and may carry a cost. During treatment the drug kills susceptible cells, and resistant cells leave more descendants. The drug did not cause the mutations; it changed which ones were favored.

### Other mechanisms of change

Natural selection is not the only cause of evolution. **Mutation** creates new alleles. **Gene flow** moves alleles between populations. **Genetic drift** is random change in allele frequency, strongest in small populations. Only natural selection reliably produces adaptation.

### Evidence

Evolution is supported by the fossil record, by **homologous structures** (the same bones in a human arm, a bat wing and a whale flipper, inherited from a common ancestor), by shared genetic code and conserved genes, by biogeography, and by observed evolution in fast-reproducing organisms. **Analogous** structures, like insect and bird wings, share a function but not an ancestry.

### Measuring selection

**Fitness** is reproductive success, usually counted as the number of offspring that survive to reproduce. **Relative fitness** $w$ scales each genotype against the most successful one, so the best genotype has $w = 1$. The **selection coefficient** is
$$s = 1 - w.$$
In a haploid population with allele $A$ at frequency $p$ and fitness $w_A$, and allele $a$ at frequency $1-p$ and fitness $w_a$, the mean fitness is $\bar w = p\,w_A + (1-p)\,w_a$, and the frequency of $A$ in the next generation is
$$p' = \frac{p\,w_A}{\bar w}.$$
Because $w_A > w_a$ makes $\bar w < w_A$, the favored allele increases. Each generation the change is small if $s$ is small, but it compounds.

@example
title: One generation of selection
problem: In a haploid population, allele $A$ has frequency $p = 0.5$ and relative fitness $w_A = 1$. Allele $a$ has relative fitness $w_a = 0.9$. Find the frequency of $A$ in the next generation.
step: Write the frequency of $a$: $1 - p = 0.5$.
step: Mean fitness is $\bar w = (0.5)(1) + (0.5)(0.9) = 0.95$.
step: The new frequency is $p' = \dfrac{p\,w_A}{\bar w} = \dfrac{0.5}{0.95} = \dfrac{10}{19}$.
step: This is about $0.526$, an increase from $0.5$. The favored allele rose even though selection against $a$ was only $s = 0.1$.

@mistakes
- Saying individuals evolve or adapt during their lifetime; evolution is change in allele frequencies in a population.
- Treating "fitness" as strength or health rather than reproductive success of offspring that themselves reproduce.
- Believing selection has a goal or that organisms change because they "need" to; variation arises first, and selection then sorts it.
- Confusing homologous structures (shared ancestry) with analogous structures (shared function).

@quiz mc
prompt: A hospital finds that after years of heavy use of a drug, most bacteria in a ward are resistant. Which explanation best fits evolution by natural selection?
choice: Individual bacteria learned to resist the drug and passed on what they learned.
choice: The drug induced resistance mutations in the bacteria that needed them.
choice*: Resistant variants already existed or arose by random mutation, and the drug killed susceptible cells so resistant cells left more descendants.

hint: Ask whether the variation or the selection came first.
solution: Mutation produces variation without regard to need. The drug is the selective pressure that raises the frequency of resistant cells. Resistance is often costly without the drug, so it is not "better" in every environment.
