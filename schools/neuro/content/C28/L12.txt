@article
The ear converts sound pressure waves into neural signals. The tympanic membrane and the three ossicles (malleus, incus, stapes) transmit vibrations to the oval window of the cochlea. This amplifies pressure by **impedance matching**: the tympanic membrane is much larger than the oval window (area ratio roughly 17) and the ossicles act as a lever. In the cochlea the vibration travels as a wave along the basilar membrane; the base is stiff and narrow and responds to high frequencies, the apex to low frequencies. Hair cells of the organ of Corti bend, opening channels that admit K$^+$ from the potassium-rich endolymph; the resulting depolarization releases glutamate onto the cochlear nerve (CN VIII).

### Hearing loss

- **Conductive loss** arises in the external or middle ear: cerumen, otitis media with effusion, perforation, otosclerosis (stapes fixation) and cholesteatoma.
- **Sensorineural loss** arises in the cochlea or CN VIII: presbycusis (age-related high-frequency loss), noise exposure, ototoxic drugs (aminoglycosides, loop diuretics, cisplatin), Meniere disease, vestibular schwannoma, and infection (meningitis).

| Test | Normal | Conductive loss | Sensorineural loss |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rinne | Air conduction better than bone | Bone better than air in the affected ear | Air better than bone (both reduced) |
| Weber | Midline | Lateralizes to the **affected** ear | Lateralizes to the **better** ear |

Audiometry confirms and grades loss. A sudden sensorineural hearing loss over 72 hours or less is an emergency treated with oral corticosteroids.

Sound intensity level is $L = 10\log_{10}(I/I_0)$ dB; every 10 dB is a tenfold increase in intensity. Normal conversation is about 60 dB, and noise above about 85 dB over prolonged periods causes hearing loss.

### Vertigo

First decide whether the cause is peripheral (inner ear or vestibular nerve) or central (brainstem or cerebellum).

- **Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV):** brief (under a minute) spells of vertigo with head position changes from displaced otoconia, usually in the posterior canal. The **Dix-Hallpike maneuver** reproduces vertigo with torsional, upbeating nystagmus that fatigues. Treat with the **Epley maneuver**.
- **Vestibular neuritis:** acute continuous vertigo over days after a viral illness with no hearing loss; with hearing loss it is labyrinthitis.
- **Meniere disease:** episodic vertigo lasting 20 minutes to hours, fluctuating low-frequency sensorineural hearing loss, tinnitus and aural fullness from endolymphatic hydrops; manage with salt restriction and diuretics.
- **Vestibular schwannoma:** gradual unilateral sensorineural hearing loss and tinnitus; MRI of the internal auditory canals with gadolinium; bilateral in neurofibromatosis type 2.
- **Central causes:** cerebellar or brainstem stroke. The **HINTS** examination in acute continuous vertigo with nystagmus points to a central cause when the head impulse test is normal, nystagmus changes direction, or there is skew deviation. Other red flags are new severe headache, inability to walk, and other neurologic signs. These should be imaged with MRI.

### Infections

- **Acute otitis media:** bulging, erythematous tympanic membrane with effusion and pain, most often after a viral upper respiratory infection; organisms are *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, nontypeable *Haemophilus influenzae* and *Moraxella catarrhalis*. Treat with high-dose amoxicillin (about 80 to 90 mg/kg/day in two divided doses, a typical pediatric dose for study) when antibiotics are indicated, with analgesia; recurrent disease may need tympanostomy tubes.
- **Otitis externa** ("swimmer's ear"): pain on tragal pressure; *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* and *Staphylococcus aureus*; topical antibiotic drops. In older diabetic patients, severe pain with granulation tissue suggests **malignant otitis externa**, a skull-base osteomyelitis requiring systemic antipseudomonal therapy.
- **Cholesteatoma:** keratinizing squamous epithelium in the middle ear with chronic discharge, destroying ossicles; surgery.

### Facial nerve connection

The facial nerve passes through the temporal bone; Bell palsy and Ramsay Hunt syndrome (herpes zoster oticus with vesicles) can follow ear disease.

@example
title: Interpreting tuning fork tests
problem: A 45-year-old woman has progressive hearing loss in the right ear. Weber lateralizes to the right ear. On the right, bone conduction is better than air conduction, and the tympanic membrane looks normal. What is the type of loss and a likely cause in a woman of this age?
step: Rinne with bone conduction louder than air conduction is abnormal and indicates a conductive component.
step: Weber lateralizes to the affected ear in conductive loss, which agrees.
step: Conductive loss with an intact tympanic membrane in an adult woman suggests otosclerosis, from stapes fixation.
step: Treatment is a hearing aid or stapedectomy.

@mistakes
- Believing Weber lateralizes to the ear with worse sensorineural loss; it goes to the better ear.
- Applying the Dix-Hallpike test to diagnose central vertigo; HINTS is used for continuous vertigo.
- Dismissing continuous vertigo with vascular risk factors as peripheral without a HINTS examination or MRI.
- Prescribing aminoglycosides without considering ototoxicity.
