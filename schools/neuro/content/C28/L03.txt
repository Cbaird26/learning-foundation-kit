@article
Stroke is sudden focal neurological deficit from a vascular cause and is a leading cause of death and disability. About 85 to 87% are **ischemic** (vessel occlusion); the rest are **hemorrhagic** (intracerebral hemorrhage and subarachnoid hemorrhage). The management principle is "time is brain": roughly 2 million neurons die each minute in a large-vessel occlusion, so the first hour is organized around rapid imaging and reperfusion.

### Mechanisms of ischemic stroke

- **Large-artery atherosclerosis** (carotid, intracranial stenosis): artery-to-artery embolism or hypoperfusion.
- **Cardioembolism:** atrial fibrillation, recent myocardial infarction, valvular disease, endocarditis, patent foramen ovale with paradoxical embolism.
- **Small-vessel (lacunar) disease:** hypertension and diabetes damage penetrating arteries.
- **Other:** arterial dissection (young patients, neck trauma), hypercoagulable states, vasculitis.

A **transient ischemic attack (TIA)** is a brief episode of focal deficit with no infarct on imaging; it carries a high short-term stroke risk. The ABCD2 score (Age 60 or over: 1; BP 140/90 or higher: 1; Clinical: unilateral weakness 2, speech disturbance without weakness 1; Duration 60 minutes or more: 2, 10 to 59 minutes: 1; Diabetes: 1) estimates this risk.

### The first hour: assessment

1. Establish **last known well** time, not just the time symptoms were noticed.
2. ABCs, check **point-of-care glucose** immediately (hypoglycemia mimics stroke; treat if below 60 mg/dL).
3. **Non-contrast head CT** to exclude hemorrhage, which is the decision that matters before any thrombolysis. Add CT angiography (head and neck) to find a large-vessel occlusion; perfusion imaging or MRI selects patients in extended windows.
4. Score severity with the **NIH Stroke Scale (NIHSS)**, 0 to 42.
5. Labs: glucose, electrolytes, creatinine, CBC with platelets, coagulation (INR), troponin. Do not delay thrombolysis for labs other than glucose unless a coagulopathy is suspected. ECG for atrial fibrillation.

### Reperfusion

- **IV thrombolysis** (alteplase or tenecteplase) within **4.5 hours** of last known well in eligible patients with a disabling deficit. Typical alteplase dosing, for study: 0.9 mg/kg to a maximum of 90 mg, 10% as a bolus over 1 minute and the rest over 60 minutes. Blood pressure must be below **185/110** mmHg before lysis and below **180/105** for 24 hours after.
- **Mechanical thrombectomy** for a large-vessel occlusion (internal carotid or proximal MCA) within 6 hours, and up to **24 hours** in patients selected by perfusion or MRI criteria showing salvageable tissue. Thrombectomy can follow or occur without lysis.
- Major contraindications to lysis: any intracranial hemorrhage on CT, ischemic stroke or severe head trauma within 3 months, intracranial surgery within 3 months, active internal bleeding, platelets below 100,000/$\mu$L, INR above 1.7, use of a direct oral anticoagulant within 48 hours, glucose below 50 mg/dL, blood pressure that cannot be lowered below 185/110, and aortic dissection suspicion.

### After reperfusion decisions

- If not treated with lysis, permit **permissive hypertension** up to 220/120 mmHg for the first 24 to 48 hours unless there is another indication to lower it.
- **Aspirin** 160 to 325 mg within 24 to 48 hours (delayed 24 hours after lysis and after a repeat scan excludes hemorrhage). For minor stroke (NIHSS 3 or less) or high-risk TIA, short-term dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin and clopidogrel for 21 days.
- Secondary prevention: high-intensity statin, blood pressure control, diabetes control, smoking cessation, **anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation** (timing varies with infarct size; after the ELAN and TIMING trials it is often started within days, and sooner for small infarcts), and **carotid endarterectomy** (or stenting) for symptomatic internal carotid stenosis of 70 to 99%, ideally within 2 weeks.

### Hemorrhagic stroke

**Intracerebral hemorrhage** presents with headache, vomiting and rapidly progressive deficit; hypertension is the usual cause (basal ganglia, thalamus, pons, cerebellum) and cerebral amyloid angiopathy causes lobar bleeds in the elderly. Lower systolic pressure to about 130 to 150 mmHg and reverse anticoagulation: vitamin K plus four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate for warfarin, idarucizumab for dabigatran, four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate for factor Xa inhibitors (andexanet alfa was withdrawn from the U.S. market in December 2025).

**Subarachnoid hemorrhage** is the "worst headache of life" with sudden (thunderclap) onset, often from a ruptured saccular aneurysm. Non-contrast CT within 6 hours of onset is nearly 100% sensitive; if CT is negative and suspicion remains, lumbar puncture looks for xanthochromia. Treat with aneurysm coiling or clipping, **nimodipine** for 21 days to reduce delayed cerebral ischemia (vasospasm peaks at days 3 to 14), and monitor for hydrocephalus and hyponatremia.

@example
title: Calculating thrombolysis dose and time window
problem: A 70-kg man was last known well at 07:00 and arrives at 08:20. He has a disabling deficit and the CT shows no hemorrhage. Is he within the 4.5-hour window, and what total alteplase dose, bolus and infusion should be given?
step: Elapsed time at arrival: 07:00 to 08:20 is 80 minutes, well inside 270 minutes (4.5 hours), leaving 190 minutes.
step: Total dose is $0.9 \times 70 = 63$ mg, below the 90 mg maximum.
step: Bolus is 10%: $6.3$ mg over 1 minute.
step: The remaining $63 - 6.3 = 56.7$ mg runs over 60 minutes. Blood pressure must be under 185/110 beforehand.

@mistakes
- Using the time of symptom discovery instead of last known well, particularly for wake-up strokes.
- Giving aspirin or anticoagulants before a head CT excludes hemorrhage.
- Treating blood pressure aggressively in untreated ischemic stroke; permissive hypertension protects the penumbra up to 220/120.
- Believing thrombectomy is limited to 6 hours; selected patients benefit up to 24 hours.
