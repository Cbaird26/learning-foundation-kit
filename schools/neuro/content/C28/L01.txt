@article
Neuroanatomy is clinical logic: every sign points to a place, and every place has a blood supply. Learn the map once and a stroke examination becomes a puzzle you can solve. As a reminder from your anatomy and physiology courses, the cerebral hemispheres control the **contralateral** body because the corticospinal tract decussates in the caudal medulla, while the cerebellum acts on the **ipsilateral** body.

### Two circulations

The brain receives about 15% of cardiac output through two systems that join at the base of the brain.

- **Anterior circulation:** the internal carotid arteries (ICA) give off the ophthalmic artery, then divide into the **anterior cerebral artery (ACA)** and the **middle cerebral artery (MCA)**.
- **Posterior circulation:** the two vertebral arteries unite to form the **basilar artery**. Branches include the posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA, from the vertebral), the anterior inferior cerebellar artery (AICA) and superior cerebellar artery, pontine perforators, and finally the two **posterior cerebral arteries (PCA)**.

The **circle of Willis** (anterior communicating artery, ACAs, ICAs, posterior communicating arteries, PCAs) connects the two systems, but a complete, functional circle is present in only a minority of people, so collateral flow is unreliable.

### Cortical territories

| Artery | Territory | Typical deficit |
|---|---|---|
| ACA | Medial frontal and parietal lobes | Contralateral leg weakness and sensory loss, abulia, urinary incontinence |
| MCA | Lateral cortex, including motor and sensory areas for face and arm, and the basal ganglia and internal capsule via lenticulostriate branches | Contralateral face and arm weakness greater than leg; aphasia if the dominant (usually left) hemisphere; hemineglect if the nondominant hemisphere; contralateral homonymous hemianopia; gaze deviation toward the lesion |
| PCA | Occipital lobe, medial temporal lobe, thalamus | Contralateral homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing; alexia without agraphia in a left PCA lesion with splenial involvement |

The homunculus explains the pattern: the leg lies medially (ACA) and the face and arm laterally (MCA).

### Deep structures and lacunes

Small penetrating arteries supply deep structures: lenticulostriates from the MCA supply the basal ganglia and the internal capsule, and thalamoperforators from the PCA and posterior communicating artery supply the thalamus. Occlusion from chronic hypertension produces a **lacunar infarct**, classically a pure motor hemiparesis of face, arm and leg together (posterior limb of the internal capsule) with no cortical signs, meaning no aphasia, neglect or visual field loss.

### Brainstem syndromes

Brainstem lesions produce **crossed findings**: cranial nerve signs on the side of the lesion and long-tract signs on the opposite side of the body.

- **Lateral medullary (Wallenberg) syndrome** (PICA or vertebral artery): ipsilateral facial pain and temperature loss (spinal trigeminal nucleus), contralateral body pain and temperature loss (spinothalamic tract), ipsilateral Horner syndrome, dysphagia and hoarseness (nucleus ambiguus), vertigo and nystagmus (vestibular nuclei), ipsilateral ataxia. Strength is spared.
- **Medial medullary syndrome** (anterior spinal artery branches): contralateral hemiparesis sparing the face, contralateral loss of vibration and proprioception (medial lemniscus), ipsilateral tongue deviation (CN XII).
- **Weber syndrome** (paramedian midbrain, PCA or basilar branches): ipsilateral oculomotor palsy (eye down and out, ptosis, dilated pupil) with contralateral hemiparesis.
- **Basilar artery occlusion:** decreased consciousness, quadriparesis, and in the locked-in state preserved vertical eye movement and blinking only. It is an emergency with high mortality.

### Blood pressure and perfusion

Mean arterial pressure (MAP) is estimated as $\text{MAP} = \frac{\text{SBP} + 2\,\text{DBP}}{3}$ because diastole occupies about two thirds of the cardiac cycle. Cerebral autoregulation keeps flow constant across a MAP of roughly 60 to 150 mmHg in a normotensive person; beyond that range flow follows pressure, and infarcted tissue loses autoregulation entirely.

### Venous drainage

Cortical veins drain to the dural sinuses (superior sagittal, transverse, sigmoid) and out through the internal jugular veins. Venous sinus thrombosis causes headache, seizures and venous infarcts that cross arterial territories, a clue to the diagnosis.

@example
title: Localizing a stroke from its deficits
problem: A 66-year-old right-handed man with atrial fibrillation develops sudden right face and arm weakness greater than leg weakness, a right homonymous hemianopia, and speech that is halting with preserved comprehension. Which artery is occluded?
step: Lateralize. Right-sided weakness and a right-sided field cut mean a lesion of the left hemisphere, since the motor pathway and the visual pathway beyond the chiasm both serve the opposite side.
step: Look at the pattern. Face and arm worse than leg is the lateral cortex, which is the MCA territory; the leg area is medial and spared.
step: Language. A right-handed person has a dominant left hemisphere, so halting speech with preserved comprehension (Broca-type aphasia) fits a left MCA superior division infarct.
step: Cause. Atrial fibrillation favors cardioembolism, and an embolus typically lodges in the MCA. Answer: left middle cerebral artery.

@example
title: Mean arterial pressure from a cuff reading
problem: A patient has a blood pressure of 130/70 mmHg. Estimate the MAP.
step: Use $\text{MAP} = \frac{\text{SBP} + 2\,\text{DBP}}{3}$.
step: Substitute: $\frac{130 + 2(70)}{3} = \frac{270}{3}$.
step: $\text{MAP} = 90$ mmHg, within the autoregulatory range.

@mistakes
- Forgetting that cortical lesions give contralateral signs but cerebellar lesions give ipsilateral signs.
- Attributing leg-predominant weakness to the MCA; leg predominance points to the ACA.
- Expecting weakness in lateral medullary syndrome; it produces sensory, bulbar and cerebellar signs with preserved strength.
- Averaging systolic and diastolic pressure for MAP instead of weighting diastole twice.
