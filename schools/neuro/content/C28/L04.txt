@article
A **seizure** is a transient event from abnormal, excessive, synchronous neuronal activity. **Epilepsy** is a tendency to unprovoked seizures: two unprovoked seizures more than 24 hours apart, or one with a high recurrence risk such as an epileptogenic lesion on MRI or an epileptiform EEG. Mechanistically, seizures reflect too much excitation (glutamate) or too little inhibition (GABA), which is why many drugs act on sodium channels or GABA.

### Classification

- **Focal seizures** begin in one hemisphere. They may be aware or with impaired awareness, and may spread (focal to bilateral tonic-clonic). Temporal lobe seizures bring an epigastric rising sensation, déjà vu, automatisms (lip smacking) and postictal confusion; hippocampal sclerosis is the classic lesion.
- **Generalized seizures** involve both hemispheres at onset: tonic-clonic, absence, myoclonic, atonic.
- **Absence seizures** (childhood, 4 to 10 years): 5 to 10 seconds of staring without postictal state, provoked by hyperventilation, with 3-Hz generalized spike-and-wave on EEG. First-line is ethosuximide (or valproate if tonic-clonic seizures coexist).
- **Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy:** morning myoclonic jerks in adolescents, with generalized tonic-clonic seizures, triggered by sleep deprivation; treat with valproate (avoid in women who may become pregnant), levetiracetam or lamotrigine. Carbamazepine can worsen it.
- **Infantile spasms (West syndrome):** flexor spasms in clusters at 3 to 12 months, hypsarrhythmia on EEG, developmental regression; tuberous sclerosis is a cause. Treat with ACTH or oral corticosteroids, or vigabatrin (especially in tuberous sclerosis).
- **Febrile seizures:** generalized, brief, in children 6 months to 5 years with fever and no CNS infection; benign and not requiring antiseizure drugs.

### Provoked causes to exclude

Hypoglycemia, hyponatremia, hypocalcemia, alcohol or sedative withdrawal, drug toxicity (bupropion, tramadol, cocaine), eclampsia (treat with magnesium sulfate), head trauma, stroke, CNS infection and tumor. Treat the cause rather than starting long-term drugs.

### Evaluating a first seizure

History with witness account, glucose and electrolytes, toxicology as indicated, pregnancy test, **MRI brain** and **EEG**. A prolactin level within 20 minutes or a raised lactate supports a convulsive event. A **Todd paralysis** (focal weakness lasting minutes to hours after a seizure) can mimic stroke; in an uncertain case treat as stroke until imaging says otherwise. Psychogenic nonepileptic events often have eyes closed, long duration and asynchronous movements.

### Antiseizure drugs

| Drug | Mechanism | Notable adverse effects |
|---|---|---|
| Phenytoin | Na$^+$ channel | Nystagmus, ataxia, gingival hyperplasia, hirsutism, teratogenicity; zero-order kinetics near the therapeutic range |
| Carbamazepine | Na$^+$ channel | Hyponatremia (SIADH), aplastic anemia, Stevens-Johnson syndrome (HLA-B*15:02), CYP450 induction |
| Valproate | Na$^+$ channel, GABA | Hepatotoxicity, pancreatitis, neural tube defects, tremor, weight gain |
| Lamotrigine | Na$^+$ channel | Stevens-Johnson syndrome (titrate slowly; valproate doubles its level) |
| Levetiracetam | SV2A | Irritability and mood change |
| Topiramate | Multiple | Kidney stones, weight loss, acute angle-closure glaucoma, metabolic acidosis |
| Ethosuximide | T-type Ca$^{2+}$ channel | GI upset; used only for absence |

Carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine or lamotrigine are first line for focal seizures, as is levetiracetam. Phenytoin binds albumin: in hypoalbuminemia the total level underestimates the free level. The **Sheiner-Tozer** correction is $C_{\text{normal}} = \frac{C_{\text{observed}}}{0.2 \times \text{albumin} + 0.1}$ with albumin in g/dL (valid when renal function is normal; some sources use 0.25 in place of 0.2, and a different factor, for example $0.1 \times \text{albumin} + 0.1$, in renal failure, so check the local reference).

### Status epilepticus

Defined as a seizure lasting 5 minutes or more, or repeated seizures without recovery. It is a medical emergency (airway, glucose, IV access). Typical adult doses for study, to be checked against current references before any clinical use:

1. **Benzodiazepine first** (within minutes): IV lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg to a maximum of 4 mg per dose (may repeat once), or IM midazolam 10 mg if over 40 kg when no IV access, or IV diazepam.
2. If seizures continue, a **second-line** drug: levetiracetam 60 mg/kg (maximum 4500 mg), fosphenytoin 20 mg phenytoin equivalents (PE)/kg (maximum 1500 mg PE), or valproate 40 mg/kg (maximum 3000 mg). The ESETT trial found these similarly effective.
3. Refractory status: continuous infusion of midazolam, propofol or pentobarbital with EEG monitoring in the ICU.

Give **thiamine** with glucose in patients at risk of alcohol-related deficiency. After a seizure, neuroimaging and EEG guide whether to start long-term therapy; most drivers must refrain from driving for a period set by local law.

@example
title: Dosing second-line therapy in status epilepticus
problem: An 80-kg man in status epilepticus continues seizing after lorazepam. Calculate the fosphenytoin load at 20 mg PE/kg with a maximum of 1500 mg PE, and the levetiracetam load at 60 mg/kg with a maximum of 4500 mg.
step: Fosphenytoin: $20 \times 80 = 1600$ mg PE exceeds the cap, so give 1500 mg PE.
step: Levetiracetam: $60 \times 80 = 4800$ mg exceeds the cap, so give 4500 mg.
step: Only one second-line drug is chosen; the two calculations show how the caps limit large patients.

@mistakes
- Calling every convulsion epilepsy; a single provoked seizure is not epilepsy.
- Using phenytoin or carbamazepine for absence or myoclonic seizures, which they can worsen.
- Reading a low total phenytoin level in hypoalbuminemia as subtherapeutic without correcting it.
- Giving a second-line antiseizure drug before a benzodiazepine in status epilepticus.

@practice self
prompt: A 60-year-old woman has a generalized convulsion followed by right arm weakness lasting 2 hours. Outline how to distinguish Todd paralysis from stroke and the time windows that matter for stroke assessment.
hint: Ask what must be excluded first, and what time windows apply if the answer is stroke.
solution: Witnessed convulsion, tongue laceration and elevated lactate favor a seizure, and weakness usually improves within hours, but Todd paralysis is a diagnosis of exclusion because a stroke can also cause a seizure. Establish last known well, check glucose, and obtain NIHSS and non-contrast head CT with vascular imaging. If an ischemic stroke or large-vessel occlusion is found, thrombolysis is possible within 4.5 hours of last known well and thrombectomy within 6 hours or up to 24 hours in selected cases. MRI and EEG help; the deficit should recover fully for Todd paralysis.
rubric: Recognizes Todd paralysis as a diagnosis of exclusion and orders imaging.
rubric: Names last known well, glucose, NIHSS and non-contrast CT.
rubric: States 4.5 hours for lysis and thrombectomy windows to 24 hours.

@quiz num
prompt: A patient on phenytoin has an observed total level of 8 mg/L and an albumin of 2.0 g/dL; renal function is normal. Using the Sheiner-Tozer equation $C_{\text{normal}} = C_{\text{obs}} / (0.2 \times \text{albumin} + 0.1)$, calculate the corrected level in mg/L.
answer: 16
check: 8 / (0.2 * 2.0 + 0.1)
unit: mg/L
tol: 0.1
hint: Compute the denominator first.
solution: $0.2 \times 2.0 + 0.1 = 0.5$, so $C_{\text{normal}} = 8/0.5 = 16$ mg/L, within the usual 10 to 20 mg/L range.
