@article
Chomsky (1957) offered a famous pair of word strings. "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously" means nothing sensible, yet English speakers feel it is a possible sentence. "Furiously sleep ideas green colorless" has the same words in reverse, and it feels broken. So speakers judge form separately from meaning. Language works on several levels at once, and each level has its own units and rules.

### Five levels

**Phonetics and phonology** concern speech sounds. A **phoneme** is the smallest sound unit that can distinguish words in a language. Two words that differ in exactly one phoneme in the same position form a **minimal pair**: "pat" and "bat" show that /p/ and /b/ are separate phonemes of English. Phoneme inventories differ across languages, from about a dozen to well over a hundred, and a contrast that matters in one language may be ignored in another.

**Morphology** concerns word structure. A **morpheme** is the smallest unit that carries meaning or grammatical function. "Dogs" has two morphemes, the free morpheme *dog* and the bound plural *-s*. A **free morpheme** can stand alone; a **bound morpheme** (a prefix such as *un-*, a suffix such as *-ness*) cannot. A **lexicon** stores the words and morphemes of a language, and a speaker can build new words such as "unfriend" from known parts.

**Syntax** concerns how words combine into phrases and sentences. **Semantics** concerns meaning: what words refer to and the conditions under which a sentence is true. **Pragmatics** concerns what speakers mean in context. "Can you reach the top shelf?" is syntactically a question about ability, but a speaker usually means a request. Grice's work on conversation described how listeners infer such meanings by assuming speakers are cooperative.

### Two design features

Language shows **duality of patterning**: a few dozen meaningless sounds combine into many thousands of meaningful words. It is also **productive**: speakers produce and understand sentences they have never heard. Productivity depends on **compositionality**, the principle that the meaning of a complex expression is built from the meanings of its parts and how they are combined. Compositionality has known exceptions, such as idioms ("kick the bucket"), which are stored as wholes.

### Hierarchical structure

Sentences are not flat lists. "I saw the man with the telescope" has two readings, because "with the telescope" can attach to the verb (I used a telescope) or to the noun phrase (the man had one). The words are identical, so the difference lies in the grouping. Grouping is shown by brackets: [I [saw [the man] [with the telescope]]] versus [I [saw [the [man with the telescope]]]]. Syntactic rules operate on such groups, and **recursion** (a phrase containing a phrase of the same kind) lets sentences grow without limit: "the dog that chased the cat that ate the rat".

Counting structures shows why grouping matters. The number of ways to bracket $n$ words into pairs (binary branching) is the Catalan number

$$C_{n-1} = \frac{1}{n}\binom{2n-2}{n-1}.$$

| words $n$ | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| bracketings | 1 | 2 | 5 | 14 |

Only some bracketings are grammatical, and a learner or listener must find them.

### Counting what a language can build

If a language has $c$ consonants and $v$ vowels and allows syllables of type CV, a syllable inventory has $c \times v$ members; adding CVC syllables adds $c \times v \times c$. A word of $k$ syllables has up to $s^k$ forms for $s$ syllable types. Real languages use only a small fraction of these combinations, because sound patterns are constrained: English allows "str" at the start of a syllable but not "tsr".

### What is established and what is debated

The five-level description is standard and underlies research in all of the following lessons. Debates concern how separate the levels are in the mind, how much of syntax is a special system for language, and how much of meaning comes from the language and how much from context. Later weeks return to these questions.

@example
title: Morphemes and structure of one sentence
problem: Analyze "The unhappy dogs barked" into morphemes, and say which are free and which are bound.
step: Split the sentence into words: the, unhappy, dogs, barked.
step: Split each word into morphemes: the; un- + happy; dog + -s; bark + -ed.
step: Count: 1 + 2 + 2 + 2 = 7 morphemes.
step: Classify: the, happy, dog and bark are free; un-, -s and -ed are bound. The suffix -s marks plural and -ed marks past tense, which are grammatical functions.
answer: Seven morphemes; four free (the, happy, dog, bark) and three bound (un-, -s, -ed).

@example
title: How many possible syllables and words?
problem: A language has 12 consonants and 4 vowels and allows CV and CVC syllables. How many syllables are possible, and how many two-syllable words if any two syllables may combine?
step: CV syllables: $12 \times 4 = 48$.
step: CVC syllables: $12 \times 4 \times 12 = 576$.
step: Total syllables: $48 + 576 = 624$.
step: Two-syllable words: $624^2 = 389{,}376$.
answer: 624 syllables and 389,376 possible two-syllable words, far more than any language uses.

@mistakes
- Treating letters as phonemes: English "ship" has four letters but three phonemes, and "tough" and "though" end in different sounds despite the same letters.
- Counting syllables as morphemes: "banana" has three syllables but one morpheme, and "dogs" has one syllable but two morphemes.
- Thinking that a grammatical sentence must be meaningful or true: syntax, semantics and pragmatics are separate levels.
