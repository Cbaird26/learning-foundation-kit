@article
You recognize a spoken word in a few hundred milliseconds, choosing among tens of thousands of stored words, and you understand a sentence as it unfolds, before it ends. Read this: "The horse raced past the barn fell." Most readers stumble at "fell". Both observations show **incremental processing**: the mind commits to interpretations before the input is complete.

### Measuring word recognition

In a **lexical decision task** a participant sees or hears a string and presses a key as fast as possible to say whether it is a real word. Reaction times (RT) in ms show robust effects. The **frequency effect**: common words are recognized faster than rare ones, and RT falls roughly linearly with the logarithm of frequency. **Semantic priming** (Meyer and Schvaneveldt, 1971): a word such as "nurse" is recognized faster after the related "doctor" than after the unrelated "bread". The **priming effect** is the mean RT after unrelated primes minus the mean RT after related primes, typically a few tens of ms. A common explanation is that activating one word spreads activation to related words.

### Models of recognizing spoken words

Speech arrives over time, so many words are compatible with its first sounds. In the **cohort model** (Marslen-Wilson), the first sounds activate all words that begin that way, and the cohort shrinks as more sounds arrive, until the **uniqueness point**, the position at which only one word remains. In the **TRACE** model (McClelland and Elman, 1986), features, phonemes and words are layers of units that excite and inhibit one another, and activation flows both upward and downward, so that lexical knowledge can influence phoneme perception. Eye-tracking studies show that listeners look at pictures of words that share an onset with the spoken word, in keeping with competition among candidates.

### Ambiguous words

Many words have several meanings. In cross-modal priming (Swinney, 1979), listeners heard a sentence with an ambiguous word such as "bug" and then saw a related word on a screen. Shortly after the ambiguous word, words related to both meanings were primed, even though the context favored one; a few syllables later, only the context-appropriate meaning was primed. Later studies found that strong context and a dominant meaning can change this pattern, so the extent to which context limits early access remains debated.

### Building sentences

Syntactic structure is built incrementally. In a **garden-path sentence**, the first analysis turns out wrong and the reader must reanalyze. "The horse raced past the barn fell" is grammatical, with "raced past the barn" as a reduced relative clause (the horse that was raced past the barn fell), but readers first take "raced" as the main verb. Two accounts compete. **Syntax-first** (or serial) accounts, such as the garden-path model developed by Frazier and colleagues, say that the parser first builds the simplest structure using only syntactic information and revises it when other information conflicts. **Constraint-based** accounts (for example MacDonald, Pearlmutter and Seidenberg, 1994) say that many sources of information, such as word frequency, plausibility and context, compete from the start. Eye-tracking studies find that context can reduce garden-path effects in some sentences, which favors constraint-based accounts, whereas the syntax-first account holds that such effects reflect fast reanalysis. Both camps have refined their models, and the field has not settled on one.

### Brain signals and probability

Event-related potentials give timing evidence. The **N400**, a negative wave about 400 ms after a word, is larger for words that fit the context poorly (Kutas and Hillyard, 1980, for sentence-final words such as "socks" in "He spread the warm bread with socks"). A later positive wave, the **P600**, is often larger for syntactic anomalies and reanalysis.

Probability gives a unified measure. The **surprisal** of a word is $-\log_2 P(\text{word} \mid \text{context})$ in bits. Reading times grow with surprisal, so unexpected words take longer. Surprisal-based theories hold that processing difficulty reflects how much the word shifts the listener's expectations.

@example
title: Surprisal of two completions
problem: In the context "She stirred her coffee with a", suppose the completion probabilities are spoon 0.80, stick 0.10, finger 0.05 and pen 0.05. Find the surprisal of "spoon" and of "pen".
step: Apply $-\log_2 P$ to "spoon": $-\log_2 0.80 = 0.322$ bits.
step: Apply it to "pen": $-\log_2 0.05 = 4.322$ bits.
step: Compare: "pen" carries $4.322 - 0.322 = 4.0$ bits more, a factor of 16 in probability ($0.80/0.05 = 16$), so readers are predicted to slow down on it.
answer: 0.322 bits for "spoon" and 4.322 bits for "pen".

@example
title: A cohort narrowing to its uniqueness point
problem: A toy lexicon holds trace, track, tram, train, trap and tree. Treating letters as phonemes, how many candidates remain after each prefix of "track", and where is the uniqueness point?
step: After "t" and after "tr", all 6 words remain.
step: After "tra", tree is excluded, leaving trace, track, tram, train and trap: 5.

step: After "track", only track remains, so the uniqueness point is the fifth letter.
answer: 6, 6, 5, 2, 1 candidates; the uniqueness point is the fifth position.

@mistakes
- Treating a garden-path sentence as ungrammatical: it is grammatical but misleads the first analysis.
- Treating priming as a measure of conscious association: the priming effect is the RT difference between conditions, and it can occur without awareness of the prime.
- Reading the N400 as the signature of one mental operation: it is a measured signal whose meaning is still debated.

@quiz self
prompt: Give the strongest case for a syntax-first account of garden-path sentences and the strongest case for a constraint-based account, then say what kind of evidence would favor one over the other.
hint: Think about what each account predicts for the first moments of reading when context or plausibility is strongly misleading.
solution: The syntax-first account says that the parser must build structure quickly, that the simplest structure is the cheapest, and that readers show a reliable slowdown at disambiguating words even when other cues are available, as if they had to revise an initial syntactic commitment. Its strongest evidence is garden-path effects that survive some biasing context. The constraint-based account says that all relevant information, including word frequency, plausibility and the discourse context, is used from the first moment and competes, so garden paths are graded and depend on how strongly cues support the wrong analysis. Its strongest evidence is that effects shrink or disappear when cues are manipulated, for example when the noun is a poor agent of the verb. Evidence that favors one account is a measure of the very first moments of processing with strong, controlled context: an effect of the cue on first-pass eye movements supports the constraint-based view, while a delay in using it despite strong cues supports syntax-first. Each side can appeal to differences in how strong the cues were.
