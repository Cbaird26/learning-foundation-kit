@article
Roughly half of the world's people use more than one language regularly, so bilingualism is common, not exceptional. When a bilingual speaks one language, the other does not simply switch off. Does managing two languages change how the mind works beyond language? The question has produced one of the best known, and most disputed, claims in cognitive science.

### Who is a bilingual?

**Simultaneous bilinguals** learn two languages from early infancy; **sequential bilinguals** learn a second language after the first is established. Proficiency, age of acquisition, the amount of use and the setting (home, school, community) all vary and matter. **Code-switching**, alternating languages within a conversation or sentence, is a skilled and rule-governed behavior of fluent bilinguals, not a sign of confusion.

### Both languages are active

Evidence from eye-tracking and priming indicates that, when a bilingual listens or speaks, words of both languages are partly active. A listener hearing a word in one language briefly considers words from the other that start with similar sounds. Because the other language competes, bilinguals need a mechanism of **language control** to select the intended language. Switching languages is slightly slower than staying in one, a **switch cost**. Brain imaging finds that bilinguals use the language network and also frontal and subcortical regions involved in control.

### Costs and benefits in language

On average, a bilingual knows fewer words in each language than a monolingual of that language knows, because experience is divided, and bilinguals name pictures somewhat more slowly and have more tip-of-the-tongue states. Yet bilingual children's **conceptual vocabulary**, the number of distinct concepts they can name in either language, is typically comparable to monolinguals' or larger, and bilingual children reach language milestones in the same broad range as monolinguals. Children's development of each language depends on the amount and quality of input in it.

### The bilingual advantage claim

The **bilingual advantage** hypothesis says that constant practice at selecting one language and suppressing the other strengthens domain-general **executive function** (inhibition, task switching, monitoring). The strongest case for it: studies by Bialystok and colleagues, in children and older adults, reported that bilinguals were faster than monolinguals on tasks with conflict, such as the Simon and flanker tasks; some studies found bilinguals developing symptoms of dementia on average several years later (Bialystok, Craik and Freedman, 2007); and neuroimaging shows differences in control regions.

The strongest case against: many studies find no difference; meta-analyses suggest that the average effect is small or zero once the apparent **publication bias** is considered (small studies with positive results are published more often than those with null results; de Bruin, Treccani and Della Sala, 2015, found this pattern); different executive-function tasks correlate poorly, so a difference on one task is hard to interpret (Paap and Greenberg, 2013); groups often differ in education, income, immigration history and culture; and a large study of children in the United States found no advantage after controls (Dick and colleagues, 2019). Defenders reply that the advantage may appear only under particular conditions (such as high daily language switching), in certain ages or on particular tasks, and that null results from poorly matched samples are not conclusive. At present the evidence for a general advantage is weak and contested, and the studies of bilingualism and dementia depend on observational data that are open to confounding.

### Effect size and sample size

A standardized effect size, **Cohen's $d$**, is the difference of group means divided by the pooled standard deviation:

$$d = \frac{M_1 - M_2}{s_p}, \qquad s_p = \sqrt{\frac{s_1^2 + s_2^2}{2}} \ \text{(equal group sizes)} .$$

By convention $d \approx 0.2$ is small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large. To detect an effect with a two-sided test at $\alpha = 0.05$ and 80% power, an approximate sample size per group is

$$n \approx \frac{2\,(1.96 + 0.84)^2}{d^2} .$$

For $d = 0.2$, about 392 participants per group. A study with 20 per group can detect only large effects, and if only the significant small studies are published, the published effects overestimate the truth.

@example
title: A standardized effect on a conflict task
problem: On a flanker task, the mean interference cost (ms) is 52 (SD 18) for bilinguals and 60 (SD 22) for monolinguals, with equal group sizes. Find Cohen's $d$.
step: Find the pooled SD: $s_p = \sqrt{(18^2 + 22^2)/2} = \sqrt{(324 + 484)/2} = \sqrt{404} = 20.10$ ms.
step: Find the mean difference: $60 - 52 = 8$ ms.
step: $d = 8/20.10 = 0.40$.
step: Interpret: by convention a small to medium effect, but such an effect would need about $2(2.8)^2/0.16 = 98$ participants per group to detect with 80% power.
answer: $d \approx 0.40$ in favor of smaller interference for bilinguals.

@example
title: Conceptual vocabulary
problem: A bilingual child knows 600 words in language A and 400 in language B, and 250 concepts are named in both languages. How many distinct concepts can the child name?
step: Add the words: $600 + 400 = 1000$.
step: Subtract the double count of concepts known in both: $1000 - 250 = 750$.
step: Compare: a monolingual child with 650 words would know fewer concepts, even though the bilingual's vocabulary in each language (600 and 400) is not larger than 650 in either.
answer: 750 distinct concepts.

@mistakes
- Equating a smaller vocabulary in each language with a smaller overall language ability: conceptual vocabulary adds both languages.
- Treating a significant difference in one small study as proof of an advantage: effect sizes, sample sizes and publication bias all matter.
- Treating a difference between groups as caused by bilingualism: the groups may differ in education, income, immigration and culture.

@practice self
prompt: A news story says "Speaking two languages makes children smarter, because bilingual children beat monolingual children on an attention task." Evaluate the claim: say what the evidence would have to show, what alternative explanations exist, and what a better study would do.
hint: Consider confounds, effect size, replication and what "smarter" would mean.
solution: The claim needs evidence that bilingualism causes better executive function, not just a group difference. Alternative explanations include differences in education, income, immigration history, culture and test familiarity between groups, and chance in a small study. The word "smarter" is also vague: executive function tasks measure specific abilities, and different tasks correlate poorly, so an advantage on one task may not generalize. The broader literature is mixed, with some studies and meta-analyses finding small or no effects after publication bias is considered, so a single study cannot settle the question. A better study would match groups carefully on socioeconomic status and ethnicity, use several executive-function tasks, preregister its analysis, have a sample size chosen by power, and report effect sizes with confidence intervals; ideally it would also measure the degree of daily language switching, since proponents argue that the effect depends on it. Defenders of the advantage can fairly say that a null result in poorly matched samples is not conclusive, and so replication across several settings matters.
