@article
An infant cannot tell us what she sees or knows, and cannot reach or point with precision for months. Yet infants look, and where and how long they look is a measurable behavior. Much of what we know about the infant mind comes from looking time: a simple measure that has opened a window into early development, and whose meaning is hotly argued.

### Methods

In **preferential looking** (Fantz, 1961) two stimuli are shown side by side and the observer measures which the infant looks at longer. If infants reliably look longer at one, they can tell the two apart and prefer one. Infants prefer patterned over plain displays, and newborns look longer at face-like patterns than at scrambled ones; whether this reflects a face-specific preference or a general preference for top-heavy patterns is debated.

In **habituation**, one stimulus is shown repeatedly until looking declines to a **criterion**, for example when the mean of three consecutive trials falls below 50% of the mean of the first three. A new stimulus is then shown. If looking recovers (**dishabituation**), the infant has detected the change. Habituation gives a test of discrimination without asking infants to prefer anything.

In **violation of expectation**, infants see a possible and an impossible event. In a classic experiment (Baillargeon, Spelke and Wasserman, 1985), 5-month-old infants saw a screen rotate through a half circle. When the screen stopped at a hidden box, as would be physically required, or continued through the space the box occupied, which should be impossible, infants looked longer at the impossible event. The authors interpreted this as showing that infants represented the hidden box and expected it to block the screen.

### What infants are said to know

Spelke and colleagues proposed **core knowledge** systems: early, limited representations of objects (which are cohesive, continuous in motion and move on contact), of number, of agents with goals, and of space. Wynn (1992) reported that 5-month-olds looked longer at the result of 1 + 1 = 1 or 2 − 1 = 2 than at the correct results, interpreted as a sense of small-number arithmetic. Vision develops over the first year: acuity at birth is low, on the order of one-twentieth of the adult level, and improves over the first year; infants come to use depth cues, and the visual cliff studies (Gibson and Walk, 1960) found that crawling infants avoid a visual drop, though whether this reflects fear or depth perception is debated.

### How to interpret looking

The **rich interpretation** says that longer looking at an impossible event reflects surprise: the infant has expectations about objects, number or agents and notices a violation. The **lean interpretation** says that looking differences can arise from simpler causes: the impossible display may differ in perceptual novelty, in amount of motion, or in familiarity from the possible one, and looking may reflect a preference for novel or complex displays, not knowledge. Careful controls address this, and many violation-of-expectation findings survive them, but some do not. The record is mixed: a coordinated, multi-laboratory project, ManyBabies (ManyBabies Consortium, 2020), found that infants on average preferred infant-directed speech, showing that large collaborative studies can detect robust effects, while replications of some single-laboratory looking-time findings have been inconsistent. A related puzzle is why infants may look as if they understand hidden objects months before they reach for them, as Piaget's tasks had suggested; whether looking and reaching tap different knowledge or the same knowledge with different demands is unresolved.

### Statistics for looking times

Looking times are skewed and noisy, and infants are lost to fussiness, so designs are within-subject, counterbalanced and coded by observers who do not know the condition. A simple test compares each infant's looking at two events with the **paired $t$ statistic**:

$$t = \frac{\bar{d}}{s_d/\sqrt{n}},$$

where $\bar{d}$ is the mean of the differences, $s_d$ their standard deviation and $n$ the number of infants.

@example
title: Habituation criterion
problem: An infant's looking times (s) on seven habituation trials are 24, 21, 18, 12, 9, 8 and 7. The criterion is that the mean of three consecutive trials falls below 50% of the mean of the first three. On which trial is the criterion met?
step: Baseline: $(24 + 21 + 18)/3 = 21$ s, so the criterion level is $10.5$ s.
step: Trials 2 to 4: $(21 + 18 + 12)/3 = 17$; trials 3 to 5: $(18 + 12 + 9)/3 = 13$; trials 4 to 6: $(12 + 9 + 8)/3 = 9.67$.
step: Trials 4 to 6 is the first window below 10.5, so the criterion is met on trial 6.
answer: Trial 6; the test stimulus is shown after it.

@example
title: A paired test of looking times
problem: Sixteen infants looked at an impossible event for a mean of 9.2 s and at a possible event for a mean of 6.4 s. The standard deviation of the individual differences is 4.0 s. Find $t$.
step: The mean difference is $\bar{d} = 9.2 - 6.4 = 2.8$ s.
step: The standard error is $4.0/\sqrt{16} = 1.0$ s.
step: $t = 2.8/1.0 = 2.8$ with 15 degrees of freedom, which exceeds the two-sided 5% critical value of 2.13.
step: Interpret: infants look longer at the impossible event. What that shows depends on the controls: it fits the rich interpretation, and the lean one unless perceptual differences were ruled out.
answer: $t = 2.8$.

@mistakes
- Treating longer looking as proof that an infant "knows" something: it shows that the infant discriminates or is surprised, and the reason needs argument and controls.
- Forgetting counterbalancing: if the impossible event is always shown second, order effects can mimic a difference.
- Comparing the first and last habituation trials only: the criterion is based on windows of trials so that one odd trial does not decide.

@practice self
prompt: A headline says: "Five-month-old babies can do arithmetic, because they look longer when 1 + 1 gives 1." Evaluate the claim: what does looking time show, what alternative explanations exist, and what evidence would strengthen the claim?
hint: Consider the rich and lean interpretations and replication.
solution: Looking longer at an unexpected outcome shows that infants distinguish the outcome from an expected one, and a rich interpretation takes this as a sign of a representation of small numbers and of surprise at a violation. Lean alternatives are that the outcomes differ in perceptual features, such as the number of objects visible, the arrangement or the amount of motion, so that longer looking reflects novelty, and that infants may be responding to a familiarity difference with no arithmetic knowledge. Evidence that would strengthen the claim includes control conditions that match the perceptual features, results that generalize across objects and procedures, and replication in several laboratories, which has been inconsistent for some number findings. It would also help to test with a second measure such as reaching or pupil dilation. A balanced conclusion is that the findings suggest that infants are sensitive to number and to changes in it, while whether they perform arithmetic, as adults do, is contested.
