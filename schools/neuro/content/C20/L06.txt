@article
Children learn language from limited, messy input, and every child of a community ends up with much the same grammar. Three families of theory explain this. They differ on what the child brings to the task, and the debate remains active; each view has strong evidence.

### The nativist view

The **nativist** view, associated with Chomsky (1957, and his 1959 review of Skinner), holds that humans have an inborn faculty of language, **universal grammar** (UG), that constrains the grammars a child considers. The core is the **poverty-of-stimulus argument**: the input does not contain enough information to determine the grammar, so the child must bring constraints. A standard example is **structure dependence**. To form a question from "The boy who is watching Mickey Mouse is happy", a learner using a linear rule (move the first auxiliary) would produce the ungrammatical "Is the boy who watching Mickey Mouse is happy?"; the correct rule moves the auxiliary of the main clause. Crain and Nakayama (1987) elicited such questions from 3- to 5-year-old children, who did not make errors of the linear kind. In the **principles and parameters** version, UG has fixed principles and open parameters set by experience. Further support comes from universal properties of languages, rapid acquisition, and cases in which children create structure: the sign language that arose among deaf children brought together in Nicaraguan schools became more grammatical as new cohorts of children learned it. Challenges: the content of UG has been revised many times, and critics argue that the input holds more information than the argument assumes, and that general learning mechanisms can find structure.

### The statistical-learning view

The **statistical-learning** (or connectionist, emergentist) view holds that much of language is learned by detecting patterns in the input with general-purpose mechanisms. Evidence includes the segmentation work of Saffran, Aslin and Newport (1996). Computational models support it: Elman's (1990) simple recurrent network, trained to predict the next word, developed internal states that grouped words by category, and the Rumelhart and McClelland (1986) network learned past tenses and showed U-shaped development without an explicit rule. Challenges: Pinker and Prince (1988) argued that such a past-tense model failed on important facts and that regular and irregular forms may need different mechanisms (a "words and rules" account); statistical learners must also explain how learners reach hierarchical structure, and many models receive much more input than children do. Recent neural networks trained on large amounts of text produce fluent sentences, and what this implies for children, who learn from far less input and from interaction, is debated.

### The usage-based view

The **usage-based** view (Tomasello, 2003) says that children begin with concrete, item-specific constructions, such as a frame built around a particular verb, and gradually generalize them. Abstract grammar emerges from the child's experience, pattern-finding and reading of others' intentions, with sharing attention as a key social ingredient. Evidence: early productions are tied to particular verbs and rarely extended to others, the use of a construction is predicted by how often the child hears it, and children learn better in interaction than from passive exposure. Challenges: it must explain how abstract knowledge comes about, why some structures are acquired quickly and consistently despite sparse input, and whether the item-based pattern reflects limited knowledge or limited sampling.

### What the views share, and where they split

All three accept that children learn from input and that cognitive capacities are needed; they differ on how rich the built-in structure is and whether any of it is specific to language. These are empirical questions: tests include whether children produce structures not in their input, whether artificial-language learners generalize to new structures, and how models trained on realistic child-directed input behave.

### Computing transitional probabilities

Place boundaries where the transitional probability **dips**: within a word it is high, and between words it is low. Counting pairs makes this precise.

@example
title: Transitional probabilities in a stream
problem: Three words M = mago, D = dobu and T = tepi appear in the order M D T D M T M D, giving the syllable stream ma go do bu te pi do bu ma go te pi ma go do bu. Find $TP(\text{go} \mid \text{ma})$, $TP(\text{do} \mid \text{go})$ and $TP(\text{ma} \mid \text{pi})$.
step: "ma" occurs 3 times, each followed by "go": $TP = 3/3 = 1$.
step: "go" occurs 3 times, followed by "do" twice (before D at positions 2 and 8) and by "te" once: $TP(\text{do} \mid \text{go}) = 2/3 = 0.667$.
step: "pi" occurs 2 times, followed by "do" once and by "ma" once: $TP(\text{ma} \mid \text{pi}) = 1/2 = 0.5$.
step: The within-word TP of 1 is higher than both boundary TPs, so a learner would place boundaries after "go" and "pi".
answer: 1, 0.667 and 0.5.

@example
title: Segmenting at dips
problem: A learner has estimated these transitional probabilities along the stream ma go do bu te pi do bu: ma→go 1.0, go→do 0.67, do→bu 1.0, bu→te 0.5, te→pi 1.0, pi→do 0.5, do→bu 1.0. Where does the learner place word boundaries?
step: Identify the high values: ma→go, do→bu and te→pi are 1.0 and lie inside words.
step: Identify local dips: go→do (0.67), bu→te (0.5) and pi→do (0.5) are lower than their neighbors.
step: Put a boundary at each dip: ma go | do bu | te pi | do bu.
answer: The words ma-go, do-bu, te-pi and do-bu.

@mistakes
- Treating one account as the default and the others as extras: each account is a family of proposals with its own best evidence and open problems.
- Reading the poverty-of-stimulus argument as saying that children get no input: it says that the input underdetermines the grammar.
- Computing a transitional probability with the wrong denominator: divide by the count of the first syllable, not by the total number of syllables.

@practice self
prompt: A child says "goed" at age 3 after saying "went" at age 2. Explain how the nativist (words and rules), the statistical (connectionist) and the usage-based views would each account for this, and say what evidence could distinguish them.
hint: Ask for each view what is stored, what is computed, and what in the input predicts the error.
solution: A words-and-rules nativist account says that the child has acquired a general rule of adding -ed and applies it when the irregular form is not retrieved from memory, so errors reveal the rule. A connectionist account says that a single network learns from many verbs, so regular patterns dominate for a time and the irregular form is overridden, giving a U-shaped curve without an explicit rule. A usage-based account says that "went" was stored as a form, and that "goed" arises from analogy to many regular verbs, with errors depending on how often the child heard each form and on how similar the verb is to regular verbs. Evidence that could distinguish them would be how error rates depend on the frequency of the irregular form, the similarity of the verb to others and the amount of regular input, and whether similar patterns appear for words that have no stored form, such as novel verbs. Each account can adjust to many findings, so the contrasts lie in predictions about novel words and in fine-grained dependence on input.
