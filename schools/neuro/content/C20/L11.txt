@article
Sally puts a marble in her basket and leaves the room. While she is away, Anne moves the marble to a box. Sally returns. Where will she look first? Adults say the basket without a pause: Sally believes the marble is where she left it, though we know it is not. Many 3-year-olds say the box. By 4 or 5, most say the basket. What changed?

### Theory of mind

**Theory of mind** is the ability to attribute mental states (beliefs, desires, intentions, knowledge) to oneself and others and to use them to explain and predict behavior. The term was introduced by Premack and Woodruff (1978) in a study of chimpanzees, and whether nonhuman animals have any theory of mind is still debated. The key test is the **false-belief task**: a person's belief differs from reality, and the child must predict action from the belief. Wimmer and Perner (1983) tested children with a story of a boy, Maxi, whose chocolate is moved while he is away, and found that children of about 4 to 6 years began to answer correctly, while younger ones did not. Baron-Cohen, Leslie and Frith (1985) used the Sally–Anne version with typical children, children with Down syndrome and autistic children; most typical and Down syndrome children passed, while most autistic children failed.

Passing requires representing a belief that differs from reality and inhibiting the child's own knowledge. A meta-analysis of many studies (Wellman, Cross and Watson, 2001) found that, across many task variants, children typically fail at about age 3, are at a transition around 4, and pass by 5, with the same order of development across cultures studied but differing in timing.

### Earlier signs

Well before 4, children show **joint attention** (following gaze and pointing, by about 9 to 12 months), engage in pretend play, and talk about wanting and knowing. Onishi and Baillargeon (2005) reported that 15-month-old infants looked longer when an actor searched for an object in a place inconsistent with her false belief than where she would be expected to search, a result interpreted as implicit understanding of false belief. Replications have been mixed, and large collaborative replication efforts are under way.

### Why the disagreement?

**Early-competence** accounts (some nativist, such as Leslie's modular view, and some in the rich interpretation of looking times) hold that understanding of beliefs is present in infancy and that 3-year-olds fail the standard task because it asks for a verbal answer with high demands on inhibition and language. **Late-development** (constructivist) accounts, such as theory theory, hold that children revise their concept of mind through experience, so infants' looking reflects lower-level processes (associations, behavior rules) and explicit understanding really emerges at about 4. Evidence for the first: simpler or non-verbal versions give earlier success. Evidence for the second: the poor replication record of some infant results, and the robust change at age 4 across tasks and cultures. A third idea is **simulation**: children use their own mind as a model of another's, so understanding grows as they gain self-awareness. These are not all mutually exclusive.

### What predicts progress

Children's performance is related to their language ability, executive function (inhibition, working memory), conversations about feelings and thoughts at home, and interaction with siblings and peers. Deaf children with hearing parents who sign little often show delays in false-belief tasks, while deaf children of signing parents do not, which points to the role of rich conversation about minds.

### Brain and autism

Imaging finds that thinking about others' beliefs engages the **temporoparietal junction** and the **medial prefrontal cortex** (for example Saxe and Kanwisher, 2003), regions that are also involved in other social tasks. Many autistic children and adults find it harder to infer unspoken social cues, and the early finding of difficulty on false-belief tasks inspired the theory-of-mind hypothesis of autism. Researchers now note that many autistic people pass such tasks, especially with higher language ability, that autism involves many features not captured by one deficit, and that difficulties lie in everyday social interaction. Some researchers propose a **double empathy** view: mutual misunderstanding arises between people with different ways of thinking and communicating, so the difficulty is a two-way problem and not a single deficit in one group. Diagnosis and support belong to clinicians and to the autistic person's own goals, and community-preferred language varies.

### Comparing pass rates

To test whether two groups differ in the proportion passing, a **two-proportion $z$ statistic** is

$$z = \frac{\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2}{\sqrt{\hat{p}(1 - \hat{p})\left(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}\right)}}, \qquad \hat{p} = \frac{k_1 + k_2}{n_1 + n_2}.$$

@example
title: Pass rates by age
problem: In a false-belief task, 9 of 30 three-year-olds and 21 of 30 four-year-olds pass. Find the two pass rates and the $z$ statistic for the difference.
step: Rates: $9/30 = 0.30$ and $21/30 = 0.70$, a difference of 0.40.
step: Pooled proportion: $(9 + 21)/60 = 0.50$.
step: Standard error: $\sqrt{0.5 \times 0.5 \times (1/30 + 1/30)} = \sqrt{0.016667} = 0.1291$.
step: $z = 0.40/0.1291 = 3.10$, larger than 1.96, so the difference is unlikely to be chance.
answer: 30% and 70%; $z \approx 3.10$.

@example
title: Reading what a result shows
problem: Fourteen-month-olds look longer when an actor searches where her belief is false than where it is true. What can be concluded?
step: State the observation: looking is longer in the false-belief-inconsistent condition.
step: List interpretations: the infant may represent the actor's belief (rich), or respond to a lower-level feature such as where the actor last saw the object (lean).
step: Check the controls: matched displays, counterbalancing, independent replication.
step: Conclude: the result is compatible with early sensitivity to beliefs, and it does not settle the matter without such controls.
answer: A pattern in looking that is open to rich and lean readings, to be decided by controls and replication.

@mistakes
- Equating passing a false-belief task with all social understanding: it measures one aspect, and social cognition begins before and continues long after age 4.
- Treating autistic children's difficulty on early tasks as a lack of empathy or a single deficit: the picture is varied, and difficulties are often mutual.
- Treating infant looking results as settled evidence of belief understanding: their interpretation and replication are debated.
