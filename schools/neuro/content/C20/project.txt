@project
title: Development essay and study plan
overview: Choose one question in language or cognitive development, write a 2,500-word essay that presents the strongest evidence and arguments on each side, and then design one low-risk study that could move the debate forward. You will also compute the numbers your design depends on, such as an effect size, a sample size and a transitional probability, from scratch.
goal: Present both sides of a contested developmental question at their strongest, with accurate attributions, without telling the reader which side is right.
goal: Separate what is established, what is debated and what is unknown, and say what each kind of evidence can and cannot show.
goal: Design a study with a clear prediction, controls, a power calculation and an ethical plan, and say what each possible result would mean.
step: Pick a question from the supplied list or one of your own approved by your instructor, and write it as a question with two or more defensible answers. State in one paragraph why informed researchers disagree.
step: Collect at least six sources: the Noba Project modules for background, plus original papers or reviews for each side. For each source, note the sample, the method, the main result and its limits, in your own words, without quotations.
step: Build an evidence table (see the supplied template): for each side, list the three strongest findings, what they show, the best objection and what a defender would reply.
step: Write the essay in about 2,500 words: the question and why it matters; the first view at its strongest; the second view at its strongest; the evidence that is most contested, including replication and measurement problems; what is established and what remains open. Do not declare a winner; show what would change your mind.
step: Design one study that could move the debate forward. Give the research question, the participants, the task or measure (low risk only), the conditions, the prediction of each side, the controls, the analysis, and a sample size computed with $n \approx 2(1.96 + 0.84)^2/d^2$ for a stated effect size. Say what a positive result and a null result would each show.
step: Write the ethics plan: how participants, and for children their parents, give informed consent, how data are kept anonymous, how participants can stop at any time, and why the methods are low risk. Do not collect data; the study is a plan.
step: Write from scratch, without libraries, a short program or hand calculation for any computation your design needs, for example transitional probabilities in a supplied stream, mean length of utterance, Cohen's $d$ from the supplied pilot table, or the pass-rate $z$ statistic; show the code or the working in an appendix.
material: **Candidate questions.** (1) Is there a critical period for language acquisition? (2) Is language acquisition best explained by innate constraints or by general learning from input? (3) Do bilinguals have an executive-function advantage? (4) Do infants understand false belief? (5) Is dyslexia best explained by a phonological deficit or by multiple deficits? (6) Are Piaget's stages a good description of cognitive development? (7) How should we interpret the heritability of language and reading skills?

deliverable: The essay of about 2,500 words with a reference list of at least six sources, written in your own words with no quotations beyond a defined term.

rubric: Each side is presented at its strongest, with accurate attributions and its best evidence, and the essay does not declare a winner.

@review
**Week 1: levels of language.** Phonemes, morphemes, syntax, semantics and pragmatics; minimal pairs; productivity, compositionality and hierarchical structure; Catalan counts of bracketings; counting syllables and words from inventories.

**Week 2: speech.** Voice onset time and categorical perception; lack of invariance; $f_0 = 1000/T$; tube resonances $F_k = (2k-1)c/(4L)$; the McGurk effect; Levelt's stages and speech errors; ABX prediction $0.5 + 0.5(p_1 - p_2)^2$.

**Week 3: words and sentences.** Lexical decision, frequency and priming effects, the cohort and uniqueness point, TRACE; Swinney's ambiguity result; garden-path sentences with syntax-first and constraint-based accounts; the N400; surprisal $-\log_2 P$.

**Week 4: brain and aphasia.** Broca and Wernicke; the syndrome table of fluency, comprehension and repetition; double dissociation; dual-stream model; laterality index $(L - R)/(L + R)$.

**Weeks 5 and 6: acquisition.** Milestones; perceptual narrowing; transitional probability $\text{count}(AB)/\text{count}(A)$ and segmentation at dips; MLU; critical-period evidence on both sides; nativist, statistical and usage-based accounts at their strongest.

**Weeks 7 and 8: bilingualism and reading.** Both languages active; conceptual vocabulary; Cohen's $d$ and $n \approx 2(1.96+0.84)^2/d^2$; publication bias; the debate about a bilingual advantage; dual-route and connectionist models; the simple view $R = D \times L$; dyslexia; $z$-scores and PPV.

**Weeks 9 and 10: infants and stages.** Habituation criteria, preferential looking, violation of expectation, rich and lean interpretations, paired $t$; Piaget, Vygotsky, theory theory, core knowledge; standard error of a proportion.

**Weeks 11 and 12: minds and nature–nurture.** False-belief tasks and the debate on early understanding; two-proportion $z$; autism discussed respectfully; Falconer's formulas $h^2 = 2(r_{MZ} - r_{DZ})$, $c^2$, $e^2$; heritability as a population statistic; critical periods.

**Self-check.** Can you define each term above and give an example? Can you compute each quantity without notes? Can you state the strongest case for each side of each debate, and say what evidence would change your mind?

**Common errors.** Mixing letters and phonemes; dividing a transitional probability by the wrong count; reading a looking-time difference as direct knowledge; reading heritability as an individual share; treating a contested question as settled; forgetting that a group difference does not show a cause.

@exam num
prompt: A syllable stream is built from 4 words of equal frequency, presented in random order with no word immediately repeated. Using the transitional probability from the last syllable of a word to the first syllable of one particular other word, what is the surprisal in bits of that first syllable at a word boundary, to two decimal places?
answer: 1.58
tol: 0.01
unit: bits
check: -log2(1 / 3)
hint: After a word, one of the 3 other words follows with equal probability.
solution: The boundary probability is $1/3$, and the surprisal is $-\log_2(1/3) = 1.585$ bits, about 1.58.

@exam self
prompt: Choose one of these debates: (a) whether there is a critical period for language, or (b) whether children understand false belief in infancy. Write a balanced essay (300 to 450 words) that presents the strongest evidence and arguments on each side, says what is established and what is open, and describes one low-risk study with parental consent that could move the debate forward.
hint: Give each side its best evidence, then its best objection, then the study.
solution: On the critical period. The case for it: adults who arrive in a new country after childhood tend to have less native-like grammar, with scores falling as the age of arrival rises (Johnson and Newport, 1989); people who learn a first language late, such as deaf people without early access to sign, often show lasting grammatical difficulties; and early deprivation has lasting effects. The case against a sharp period: the decline with age is gradual, not a sudden end, and is confounded by years of residence, first-language use, and quantity and quality of input; a large online study estimated that the ability to learn grammar remains high until the late teens; and different aspects of language, such as accent and grammar, may follow different timetables. Established: learning is easiest in childhood, and early access to language matters. Open: whether there is a sharp maturational limit, what ends it and which aspects it affects. A study: recruit volunteer families (parents consenting for children aged 5 to 17, children agreeing to take part) from the same community, and test with a short grammaticality judgment and picture-vocabulary task on a tablet at one visit, anonymously. Compare learners of the same second language with many ages of arrival, measuring years of exposure, first-language use and amount of daily input. A sharp break at one age, with input matched, would support a critical period, while a smooth decline that input predicts would support the gradual view. Power: for $d = 0.5$ between adjacent age bands, about 63 per band.
rubric: Presents the strongest evidence and objections on both sides accurately, without declaring a winner.
rubric: Distinguishes established findings from open questions and names confounds.
rubric: Describes a low-risk study with parental consent and says what it would show.
