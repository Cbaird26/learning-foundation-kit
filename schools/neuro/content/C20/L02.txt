@article
Speech comes as a continuous stream of sound with few silences between words, yet you hear separate words made of separate sounds. And the same phoneme sounds different depending on its neighbors: the acoustic form of /d/ in "dee" differs from that in "doo". This is the **lack of invariance** problem: there is no simple one-to-one match between acoustic patterns and phonemes. How do listeners cope?

### The acoustic signal

Voiced speech has a **fundamental frequency** $f_0$ set by the rate of vocal fold vibration, which we hear as pitch: $f_0 = 1000 / T$ Hz, where $T$ is the period in ms. The vocal tract then acts as a filter that boosts certain frequencies, the **formants**. A tube of length $L$, closed at one end (the glottis) and open at the other (the lips), resonates at

$$F_k = \frac{(2k-1)\,c}{4L}, \qquad k = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$$

where $c \approx 35{,}000$ cm/s is the speed of sound in warm air. For $L = 17.5$ cm this gives $F_1 = 500$, $F_2 = 1500$ and $F_3 = 2500$ Hz, near the formants of a neutral vowel. Changing the tongue and lips changes the formants, so vowels differ by their formant patterns. This is the **source–filter** account of voiced speech.

### Voice onset time and categorical perception

Stop consonants such as /b/ and /p/ differ mainly in **voice onset time** (VOT): the delay in ms between the release of the lips and the start of vocal fold vibration. English /b/ has a short VOT and /p/ a long one. If a synthesizer varies VOT in equal steps, listeners do not report a gradual change. An illustrative identification table:

| VOT (ms) | 0 | 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 | 50 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| proportion heard as /p/ | 0.02 | 0.05 | 0.20 | 0.80 | 0.95 | 0.98 |

Listeners hear /b/ up to about 20 ms and /p/ from about 30 ms, with a steep **category boundary**. This is **categorical perception**, studied by Liberman and colleagues in the 1950s. Discrimination of pairs two steps apart is best across the boundary. A classic prediction from the idea that listeners discriminate only through category labels is, for ABX discrimination with identification probabilities $p_1$ and $p_2$ of the two sounds,

$$P(\text{correct}) = 0.5 + 0.5\,(p_1 - p_2)^2 .$$

Two sounds within one category ($p_1 \approx p_2$) are predicted to be at chance, 0.5. Observed discrimination is usually somewhat better than this, and listeners can rate within-category differences, so categorization is strong but not absolute. Infants a few months old discriminate VOT differences across a category boundary (Eimas and colleagues, 1971), which suggests that the perceptual system is prepared for such contrasts before language experience; whether the boundary comes from the auditory system's properties, from speech-specific mechanisms or from both has been debated.

### Coping with variability

Listeners use context: the vowel that follows a consonant, the speaking rate, the speaker's voice. Vision also matters. In the McGurk effect (McGurk and MacDonald, 1976), an audio syllable /ba/ played with a video of a mouth saying /ga/ is often heard as /da/. The brain combines auditory and visual evidence, even when the observer knows what is happening.

### Producing speech

Levelt (1989) described production in three broad stages. **Conceptualization** selects the message. **Formulation** picks words from the lexicon (lexical selection), retrieves their sound forms and builds a sequence of phonemes with a stress pattern (phonological encoding). **Articulation** turns the plan into coordinated movements of the lungs, larynx, tongue, jaw and lips, at typically 4 to 6 syllables per second. Speech errors give evidence on the units. Spoonerisms swap phonemes ("a lack of pies" for "a pack of lies"), and word exchanges swap whole words of the same grammatical class. Errors rarely produce impossible English sounds, which suggests that sound rules apply when the plan is made.

### Established and debated

Acoustic phonetics and the source–filter account are well established. Models of perception differ: some say listeners recover the speaker's articulatory gestures, others that they match acoustic patterns to stored categories, and others that they use statistical learning of cues. The evidence does not yet pick one winner.

@example
title: Formants of a short vocal tract
problem: A vocal tract is modeled as a tube 16 cm long, closed at one end and open at the other. Take $c = 35{,}000$ cm/s. Find the first three resonances.
step: Write the formula $F_k = (2k-1)c/(4L)$ and compute the denominator: $4L = 64$ cm.
step: $F_1 = 1 \times 35{,}000 / 64 = 546.9$ Hz.
step: $F_2 = 3 \times 35{,}000 / 64 = 1640.6$ Hz and $F_3 = 5 \times 35{,}000 / 64 = 2734.4$ Hz.
step: Compare with the 17.5 cm tube: the shorter tube has formants higher by the factor $17.5/16 = 1.094$.
answer: About 547, 1641 and 2734 Hz.

@example
title: Discrimination predicted from identification
problem: Two stimuli are labeled /p/ with probabilities $p_1 = 0.30$ and $p_2 = 0.90$. What ABX accuracy does the label-only prediction give?
step: Find the difference: $p_1 - p_2 = -0.60$, and square it: $0.36$.
step: Apply $0.5 + 0.5 \times 0.36 = 0.68$.
step: Interpret: the pair straddles the boundary, so the label-only prediction is 68% correct. A pair with $p_1 = 0.02$ and $p_2 = 0.05$ gives $0.5 + 0.5 \times 0.0009 = 0.50045$, essentially chance.
answer: 0.68 (68%).

@mistakes
- Confusing the fundamental frequency (one value per voice, heard as pitch) with the formants (resonances of the tract that distinguish vowels).
- Saying that categorical perception means listeners cannot hear within-category differences: they can detect them in some tasks, although less well than between-category differences.
- Assuming a letter or a phoneme has one fixed acoustic pattern: coarticulation makes the same phoneme acoustically variable.
