@article
In 1861 Broca described a patient (Leborgne) who could understand speech but could produce almost only one syllable. After the patient died, Broca found damage to the left frontal lobe. In 1874 Wernicke described patients with fluent speech that made little sense and who also seemed not to understand. Damage in these cases lay in the left temporal lobe. Two kinds of language problem, two different locations: the beginning of the study of language in the brain.

### The classical model

**Broca's area** lies in the left posterior inferior frontal gyrus (roughly Brodmann areas 44 and 45); **Wernicke's area** lies in the posterior superior temporal region. The **arcuate fasciculus** is a fiber bundle that arches around the lateral fissure and links the temporal and frontal regions. The classical (Wernicke–Geschwind) model said that a heard word is processed in the auditory and Wernicke areas, passed forward via the arcuate fasciculus to Broca's area for articulation, and spoken through the motor cortex.

**Aphasia** is an acquired impairment of language caused by brain damage, most often a stroke in the territory of the left middle cerebral artery, and sometimes injury, tumor or a neurodegenerative disease (primary progressive aphasia). Aphasia is a language impairment and not a loss of intelligence. Syndromes are described by three features: **fluency** (rate and effort of speech), **comprehension** and **repetition**.

| syndrome | fluency | comprehension | repetition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Broca's | non-fluent, effortful | relatively good | poor |
| Wernicke's | fluent, often empty or jargon | poor | poor |
| conduction | fluent | relatively good | poor |
| global | non-fluent | poor | poor |
| anomic | fluent, word-finding pauses | good | good |
| transcortical motor | non-fluent | good | good |
| transcortical sensory | fluent | poor | good |

Patients rarely fit a category perfectly, and clinicians use standardized test batteries; diagnosis and treatment are done by neurologists and speech-language pathologists. In Broca's aphasia speech often omits small grammatical words ("agrammatism"), and some patients also have trouble understanding sentences whose meaning depends on word order, such as reversible passives. In conduction aphasia, repeating phrases is disproportionately poor, with sound substitutions, and the disconnection of the arcuate fasciculus was long offered as the cause.

### Double dissociation

If patient A has impaired comprehension with intact production and patient B shows the reverse, the two form a **double dissociation**. It suggests that the two abilities rely on partly separable processes, which a single dissociation cannot show because the impaired task may simply be harder. It does not show that the damaged region is the sole location of the function, since lesions often damage fibers and networks.

### Modern revisions

Lesion studies and imaging show that the classical picture is incomplete. The lesions that cause lasting Broca's aphasia typically extend beyond Broca's area, and damage confined to it often produces milder, more transient problems. Language involves wider networks across the temporal, parietal and frontal lobes. The **dual-stream model** (Hickok and Poeppel, 2007) proposes a ventral pathway through the temporal lobe that maps sound to meaning and a dorsal pathway linking temporal, parietal and frontal regions that maps sound to articulation. Whether Broca's area is mainly for syntax, for sequencing and production, for working memory or for the combining of information is still debated.

**Lateralization.** In most right-handed people language depends mainly on the left hemisphere, and most left-handed people also have left-lateralized language, though more of them have bilateral or right-lateralized language. Lateralization is measured by the Wada test (anesthetizing one hemisphere, used before surgery) and by imaging. A **laterality index** is

$$LI = \frac{L - R}{L + R},$$

where $L$ and $R$ are activation measures (for example the number of active voxels) in the left and right hemispheres. $LI = +1$ means fully left, $0$ symmetric, $-1$ fully right.

### Recovery

Language often improves most in the first months after a stroke, through reduced swelling, restored blood flow and reorganization, and speech and language therapy can help later. Whether right-hemisphere activity after left damage helps recovery or hinders it is debated, with evidence for both roles in different patients and times.

title: Classifying a case
problem: A person speaks slowly, in short effortful phrases ("walk... dog... park"). They follow spoken instructions well but cannot repeat the sentence "The cat sat on the mat". Which syndrome fits best?
step: Fluency: slow, effortful, so non-fluent.
step: Comprehension: relatively good.
step: Repetition: poor. Non-fluent with good comprehension and poor repetition matches Broca's aphasia (transcortical motor would have good repetition).
answer: Broca's aphasia, with a left frontal lesion the typical expectation.

@example
title: A laterality index
problem: In a word-generation fMRI task, 300 voxels are active in the left inferior frontal region and 100 in the right. Find the laterality index.
step: $L - R = 300 - 100 = 200$.
step: $L + R = 400$.
step: $LI = 200/400 = 0.5$, a moderate to clear left lateralization.
answer: $LI = 0.5$.

@mistakes
- Equating non-fluent aphasia with a loss of intelligence or understanding: comprehension is relatively preserved in Broca's aphasia and thinking is largely intact.
- Assuming that a lesion's effect shows the function of the damaged region: lesions can interrupt fibers and wider networks.
- Treating the syndrome table as strict categories: many patients have mixed or changing profiles.
