@article
Identical twins reared apart are often strikingly alike. A child who is moved from a severely deprived setting into a loving family can make large gains. Both facts are true, and each is used to argue that nature or nurture is what matters. The better question is how the two work together, and what such an argument actually shows.

### Heritability

**Heritability** ($h^2$) is the proportion of the variance in a trait among individuals in a particular population, in a particular range of environments, that is associated with genetic differences:

$$h^2 = \frac{V_G}{V_P}, \qquad V_P = V_G + V_E .$$

It is a statistic about differences in a population. It is not the share of any one person's trait that is genetic, and it does not say how changeable the trait is. Height is highly heritable within well-nourished populations, yet average height has risen over generations as nutrition improved. A genetic disease such as phenylketonuria can be prevented from affecting development by diet. If environments become more uniform, heritability rises, because less of the variation comes from the environment.

### The twin method

**Monozygotic** (MZ, identical) twins share nearly all their genetic variants, and **dizygotic** (DZ, fraternal) twins on average half of their variable ones. If both kinds of pair share their family environment to a similar degree (the equal-environments assumption) and genes act additively, then with correlations $r_{MZ}$ and $r_{DZ}$ for a trait (Falconer's formulas):

$$h^2 = 2\,(r_{MZ} - r_{DZ}), \qquad c^2 = r_{MZ} - h^2, \qquad e^2 = 1 - r_{MZ},$$

where $c^2$ is **shared environment** (what makes siblings alike) and $e^2$ is **non-shared environment** plus measurement error. Adoption studies add evidence by comparing adopted children with their biological and adoptive relatives. Findings from such designs: language and reading skills are moderately to substantially heritable, with estimates varying by measure and age; shared environment matters more for vocabulary in early childhood than later. Limits of the method: assumptions about twins' environments may fail, estimates vary across populations and measures, and heritability from twins does not identify which genes or which mechanisms are involved.

### Interplay

Genes and environments are not independent. In **gene–environment interaction** the effect of an environment depends on genotype (a **reaction norm** describes how a genotype's outcome varies across environments). In **gene–environment correlation**, genotypes influence the environments people experience: a child with strong early language skills may be read to more. Therefore a trait can be both heritable and strongly shaped by experience.

### Critical and sensitive periods

In the visual system, kittens deprived of vision in one eye in early weeks lose lasting responses from that eye in visual cortex, but deprivation of an adult cat has little effect (Hubel and Wiesel, 1960s), a classic **critical period**. For language, evidence from late first-language learners and from children adopted from severely deprived institutions suggests that early experience is important, though the debate about a sharp critical period remains open (week 5).

### Three views and a debate

On language, the **nativist** view says that humans are born with language-specific constraints, supported by the universality of grammar, rapid acquisition and the emergence of new sign languages. The **empiricist** (or usage-based and statistical) view says that general learning from rich input, with social cognition, suffices, supported by the dependence of vocabulary on input, by statistical learning by infants and by computational models. The **interactionist** view says that the question of how much is innate is badly posed, because development always involves genes in environments, and that what matters is how biases and experience combine. Each view has strong evidence and unresolved problems: the nativist view must specify what is built in, the empiricist view must explain rapid and uniform acquisition, and the interactionist view needs concrete, testable mechanisms.

@example
title: Falconer's estimates
problem: A trait has twin correlations $r_{MZ} = 0.80$ and $r_{DZ} = 0.50$. Estimate $h^2$, $c^2$ and $e^2$.
step: $h^2 = 2(0.80 - 0.50) = 0.60$.
step: $c^2 = r_{MZ} - h^2 = 0.80 - 0.60 = 0.20$.
step: $e^2 = 1 - r_{MZ} = 0.20$.
step: Check that the parts sum to 1: $0.60 + 0.20 + 0.20 = 1$. In this population, about 60% of the variance is associated with genetic differences. This does not mean that 60% of any individual's trait is genetic.
answer: $h^2 = 0.60$, $c^2 = 0.20$, $e^2 = 0.20$.

@example
title: Heritability changes with the environment
problem: In a population, $V_G = 30$ and $V_E = 20$ (in squared units of the trait). A program makes environments more uniform, reducing $V_E$ to 10 with $V_G$ unchanged. Find $h^2$ before and after.
step: Before: $h^2 = 30/(30 + 20) = 0.60$.
step: After: $h^2 = 30/(30 + 10) = 0.75$.
step: Interpret: the genes did not change, yet heritability rose because there was less environmental variation.
answer: 0.60 before and 0.75 after.

@mistakes
- Reading $h^2 = 0.6$ as "60% of this person's trait is genetic": heritability describes variance among people in a population.
- Treating high heritability as meaning that a trait cannot be changed by environment: it describes variation within current environments.
- Treating heritability within a group as explaining differences between groups: within-group and between-group differences are different questions.

@practice self
prompt: Write a balanced analysis (about 200 to 300 words) of one nature–nurture debate: whether language acquisition depends on innate knowledge specific to language. Give the strongest evidence for the nativist and for the empiricist views, and say what the interactionist adds and what evidence would help to settle it.
hint: Use the poverty-of-stimulus argument, sign-language emergence, statistical learning, input effects and interaction.
solution: The nativist view holds that the input underdetermines grammar and that children need built-in constraints: children acquire complex grammar rapidly and uniformly, avoid errors of the wrong structural kind in question formation, and in new sign languages deaf children impose grammatical structure beyond their input. The empiricist view replies that the input is richer than the argument supposes, that infants detect statistical patterns, that vocabulary and grammar depend on the amount and variety of speech heard, and that models learning from prediction acquire structure without language-specific modules; early speech is also item-specific. The interactionist view holds that the opposition is badly posed: genes shape the learning mechanisms and social motivation, which in turn depend on experience, and heritability estimates for language skills are moderate and vary across ages and environments. Evidence that would help: learners tested on structures absent from their input, with models given realistic child-sized input, and studies of when and how new sign languages acquire structure. Each camp can reinterpret the others' evidence, so the debate turns on which unlearned biases are needed and whether they are specific to language.
rubric: Presents the strongest evidence for both the nativist and empiricist views.
rubric: Explains the interactionist position accurately.
rubric: Names evidence that could help settle the matter and does not declare a winner.
