@article
A newborn cannot say a word, yet in three years a typical child speaks in sentences, with a vocabulary of hundreds of words and with most of the grammar of the community's language. The adult speakers around them never gave a lesson on grammar. What does the evidence show about how this happens?

### Milestones

Timing varies a great deal between typical children; the ages below are rough.

| age | milestone |
|---|---|
| birth | prefers the mother's voice and the rhythm of the native language (DeCasper and Fifer, 1980, for the voice) |
| 6 to 8 months | discriminates speech contrasts of languages not heard before |
| 10 to 12 months | discrimination narrows to native contrasts; babbling is canonical (repeated syllables such as "bababa") from about 6 to 10 months |
| about 12 months | first words |
| 18 to 24 months | rapid growth of vocabulary; first two-word combinations |
| 2 to 4 years | inflections, questions, negatives and complex sentences emerge |

**Perceptual narrowing.** Werker and Tees (1984) tested infants on consonant contrasts from Hindi and Salish, languages not spoken at home. English-learning infants of 6 to 8 months discriminated them, while most English-learning infants of 10 to 12 months did not. Adults who knew these languages, and infants growing up with them, kept the ability. Experience tunes perception to the native language.

### Statistical learning and word segmentation

How do infants find words in a continuous stream? One cue is the **transitional probability** (TP): the probability of syllable B immediately after syllable A,

$$TP(B \mid A) = \frac{\text{count}(AB)}{\text{count}(A)} .$$

Within a word, the TP between syllables is high, and across a word boundary it is lower. Saffran, Aslin and Newport (1996) played 8-month-old infants about two minutes of a continuous synthetic stream built from four nonsense words. The only cue to word boundaries was the TP: within words it was 1.0, across boundaries it was lower (0.33). After exposure, infants listened longer to part-words, which spanned a boundary, than to words, which indicates that they had registered the difference. The result shows that very young infants can track statistics; it does not show that this is the only cue to word boundaries (stress, phonotactics and known words also help) or that the same mechanism builds grammar.

### Vocabulary and meaning

By about 18 months many children learn new words rapidly, sometimes after one exposure, called **fast mapping** (Carey and Bartlett, 1978). Children use constraints and social cues such as the speaker's gaze. Children's vocabularies are measured by parent checklists, and vocabulary size varies widely among typical children and relates to the amount and variety of speech they hear.

### Grammar and errors

A child may say "went" correctly and later "goed", then return to "went". This **overregularization**, applying the regular past-tense rule to an irregular verb, shows that children extract patterns and do not simply copy. A classic measure of grammatical growth is the **mean length of utterance** (MLU), in morphemes (Brown, 1973):

$$\text{MLU} = \frac{\text{total morphemes in the sample}}{\text{number of utterances}} .$$

MLU rises from about 1 at the one-word stage to about 4 in many children by age 3 or 4. Its limits: it depends on the sample and context, and it stops tracking complexity beyond about 4.

### Sensitive periods

Is there a **critical period** for language, a window in which acquisition must happen? Johnson and Newport (1989) tested immigrants who had arrived in the United States at ages from early childhood to adulthood; the earlier the age of arrival, the higher the average score on English grammar judgments, with scores near native levels for early arrivals and declining with age. People who learn a first language late, such as deaf people without access to sign language in childhood, often show lasting difficulties with grammar. The evidence is debated. Critics note that the decline with age may be gradual and not a sharp cutoff, that it may reflect experience, the first language's interference and the amount and quality of input, and that a large online study (Hartshorne, Tenenbaum and Pinker, 2018) estimated that the ability to learn grammar stays high until the late teens. Supporters respond that maturational limits can be graded and differ by aspect of language: accent and grammar may follow different patterns. The question of which aspects have a critical period, and when it ends, is open.

@example
title: Mean length of utterance
problem: A child produces these five utterances: "want juice" (2 morphemes), "doggie running" (doggie, run, -ing: 3), "Mommy's hat" (Mommy, 's, hat: 3), "no" (1), "I want more juice" (4). Find the MLU.
step: Add the morphemes: $2 + 3 + 3 + 1 + 4 = 13$.
step: Divide by the number of utterances: $13/5 = 2.6$.
step: Interpret: an MLU of 2.6 is typical of many children at about two and a half years, with short phrases and a few inflections (children vary widely).
answer: MLU = 2.6 morphemes.

@example
title: A transitional probability in a stream
problem: A stream is built from three words A = kofa, B = lume and C = ripo in the order A B A C B C A B (syllables ko fa lu me ko fa ri po lu me ri po ko fa lu me). Find $TP(\text{fa} \mid \text{ko})$ and $TP(\text{lu} \mid \text{fa})$.
step: "ko" occurs 3 times and each time is followed by "fa", so $TP(\text{fa} \mid \text{ko}) = 3/3 = 1$.
step: "fa" occurs 3 times, at the end of A. It is followed by "lu" after the first A and the third A, and by "ri" after the second A.
step: $TP(\text{lu} \mid \text{fa}) = 2/3 = 0.667$.
step: Compare: within-word TP is 1 and the boundary TP is lower, which is the cue to where words begin and end.
answer: 1 within the word and 0.667 across the boundary.

@mistakes
- Reading Saffran and colleagues as showing that grammar is learned by statistics: the study showed that infants can use TP to segment an artificial stream.
- Counting words and not morphemes in MLU: "running" counts as two morphemes in Brown's system, and a possessive 's counts as one.
- Treating the critical period as settled: the evidence for it and the evidence for gradual decline are both substantial.

@practice self
prompt: Immigrants are grouped by their age of arrival, and their mean score on a grammar test is 95% for ages 3 to 7, 88% for 8 to 10, 80% for 11 to 15 and 72% for 17 to 39 (invented numbers). Explain how a supporter of a sharp critical period and a supporter of a gradual decline could each read these data, and what extra evidence would help.
hint: Think about whether the decline is abrupt, and about other things that change with age of arrival.
solution: A supporter of a critical period would point to the highest scores in the youngest group and to the steady fall as arrival age passes childhood, reading this as a maturational limit. A supporter of gradual decline would note that the scores fall steadily across all groups, with no sharp break, and that older arrivals differ in schooling, use of the first language, amount of English input and motivation, any of which could lower scores. Additional evidence would be data on the precise age profile with many age groups, controls for years of residence, amount and quality of input and first-language use, and comparison of different aspects such as accent, vocabulary and grammar. A sharp break at one age across many aspects would favor a critical period, and a smooth decline that is explained by input measures would favor the gradual view.
