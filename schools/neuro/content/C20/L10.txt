@article
Show a 5-year-old two equal rows of coins, spread one row out, and ask which has more. Many 5-year-olds say the longer row, though nothing was added or removed. Most 8-year-olds say they are the same. Something changes between 5 and 8: what, and how? Piaget and Vygotsky gave the two most influential answers.

### Piaget

Piaget proposed that children build knowledge by acting on the world. A **schema** is an organized way of acting or thinking. **Assimilation** fits new experience into existing schemas; **accommodation** changes schemas when experience does not fit; **equilibration** is the process that balances the two. He described four stages, with approximate ages:

| stage | ages | key achievements |
|---|---|---|
| sensorimotor | 0 to 2 years | acting on objects; object permanence |
| preoperational | 2 to 7 years | symbols and language; judgments dominated by appearance; egocentrism |
| concrete operational | 7 to 11 years | **conservation**, reversibility, classification |
| formal operational | 11 years on | abstract and hypothetical reasoning |

**Conservation** is the understanding that quantity does not change when appearance changes. Piaget's A-not-B task shows an infant who has found a toy at location A search at A again after seeing it hidden at B, which he took to show an incomplete concept of objects.

**Critiques.** Later work found that tasks can hide competence: in one study, children as young as 3 and a half to 5 years placed a doll to hide from two police officers (Hughes, 1975) that Piaget's three-mountains task would have predicted they could not, because the task was framed in a way they understood. Looking-time studies suggested earlier understanding of objects (week 9). The stages are less abrupt than Piaget implied: a child can succeed in one domain and fail in another, and formal operations are not reached by all adults in all domains, and vary across cultures and education. Piaget's emphasis on the child's active construction of knowledge is widely accepted, but the stage structure is not.

### Vygotsky

Vygotsky stressed that thinking develops in social and cultural activity, with language as a central tool. The **zone of proximal development** (ZPD) is the range of tasks a learner cannot yet do alone but can do with help from a more skilled partner. **Scaffolding** (Wood, Bruner and Ross, 1976) describes the support a tutor gives and then withdraws. **Private speech**, children talking to themselves while working, is said to become inner speech that guides thought. Evidence: children's private speech increases on difficult tasks, and instruction within the ZPD can help children learn. Critiques: the ZPD is hard to measure precisely, and the account is less specific than Piaget's about mechanisms.

### Modern views

Modern accounts mix elements. **Core knowledge** theorists (Spelke) hold that some systems are present early and shape later learning, a more nativist view than Piaget's. **Theory theory** (Gopnik and Meltzoff, 1997; Carey) holds that children, like scientists, form and revise intuitive theories with evidence, a constructivist view. **Information-processing** accounts explain change through growth in working memory, speed and executive function, which are linked to maturation of the prefrontal cortex through adolescence. Siegler (1996) argued that children use many strategies at once, with the mix shifting over time (**overlapping waves**), not moving through stages. **Dynamic systems** accounts treat development as emerging from many interacting parts.

The main debates are whether development is continuous or stage-like, whether change comes from domain-general or domain-specific mechanisms, and how much is built in. Each view explains some findings well.

### Numbers from developmental studies

A **proportion** of children who pass a task is $\hat{p} = k/n$, with **standard error** $\sqrt{\hat{p}(1 - \hat{p})/n}$. Comparing percentages between ages gives the change in percentage points.

@example
title: Conservation by age
problem: In a study, 12 of 40 five-year-olds, 28 of 40 seven-year-olds and 38 of 40 nine-year-olds pass a number conservation task. Compute the percent passing at each age and the change from age 5 to age 9.
step: Age 5: $12/40 = 30\%$.
step: Age 7: $28/40 = 70\%$.
step: Age 9: $38/40 = 95\%$.
step: Change from 5 to 9: $95 - 30 = 65$ percentage points. Note that the rise is gradual, with a majority at age 7, in keeping with a transition and not a single jump.
answer: 30%, 70%, 95%; a rise of 65 percentage points.

@example
title: A scaffolded gain
problem: A child solves 4 of 10 puzzle problems alone and 8 of 10 when a tutor gives hints. What is the scaffolded gain, and what does it suggest?
step: Subtract: $8 - 4 = 4$ problems.
step: Interpret: the child's independent performance is 4 and the performance with support is 8, so the problems in between lie within the zone of proximal development.
step: A child who scores 4 alone and 5 with hints has a gain of 1 and may be less ready for such tasks.
answer: A gain of 4 problems.

@mistakes
- Treating Piaget's ages as fixed laws: they are approximate and vary with task, domain and culture.
- Treating a failure on a task as proof that the ability is missing: the task may place demands (language, memory, framing) unrelated to the ability.
- Equating the ZPD with a trait: it describes performance with support on a task, not a fixed property of the child.

@practice self
prompt: Design a looking-time experiment to test whether 5-month-old infants discriminate two objects from three objects, and say what the result could and could not show. Use only low-risk methods with parental consent.
hint: Choose a method, the stimuli, the controls for non-numerical features, the measure and the counterbalancing.
solution: Use habituation. Infants sit on a parent's lap, and the parent has given informed consent and may stop at any time. Show arrays of 2 objects across trials, varying object size, spacing and position so that total area and arrangement do not predict number, until looking falls to a criterion (mean of three trials below 50% of the first three). At test, show alternating 3-object arrays (new number) and 2-object arrays (old number, new arrangement), counterbalancing order, with a coder who does not know which array is on the screen. The prediction is longer looking at 3-object arrays if infants discriminate number. Analyze with a paired comparison and report effect sizes. A positive result would show that infants can distinguish 2 from 3 under these conditions. It would not show that they count or do arithmetic, and a lean explanation, that infants respond to total contour length or another continuous cue, is addressed by varying and controlling those cues. A null result would not show that infants cannot discriminate, since infants may fail to attend or the test may lack power.

rubric: States a prediction and the measure.
rubric: Says what a positive and a null result can and cannot show, and notes consent.
