@article
Speaking is learned without lessons in every community. Reading is not: writing was invented only a few thousand years ago, and children generally need years of instruction. The brain has no reading organ; reading recruits networks for vision and spoken language. Why do most children learn to read, and why do some find it much harder?

### Writing systems

Writing systems map written symbols onto language at different levels. **Alphabetic** systems map letters roughly to phonemes, **syllabic** systems (Japanese kana) map symbols to syllables, and **logographic** systems (Chinese characters) map characters to morphemes or words, many with a part that hints at sound. **Orthographic depth** describes how consistently spelling maps onto sound: Finnish and Italian are **shallow**, and English, with "tough", "though" and "through", is **deep**. Children generally learn to decode accurately sooner in shallow orthographies.

### Components of reading

**Phonological awareness**, the ability to notice and manipulate the sounds of speech (for example saying "cat" without the /k/), is among the best early predictors of reading. The **simple view of reading** (Gough and Tunmer, 1986) says that reading comprehension is the product of decoding and linguistic comprehension:

$$R = D \times L,$$

each scaled from 0 to 1. A child who decodes well but understands spoken language poorly, or the reverse, reads with poor comprehension, and if either is 0, so is comprehension. The model is a useful first description, though some researchers argue that other factors (fluency, vocabulary, background knowledge) also play roles.

### How skilled readers read words

The **dual-route** model (Coltheart and colleagues, 2001) proposes two routes from print to speech: a **lexical** route that looks up whole words in a stored lexicon (needed for irregular words such as "yacht") and a **sublexical** route that converts letters to sounds by rules (needed for nonwords such as "blint"). Evidence comes from patterns in acquired reading disorders: some patients read regular words and nonwords but misread irregular words (surface dyslexia) and others read real words but not nonwords (phonological dyslexia). The **connectionist** "triangle" model (Seidenberg and McClelland, 1989; Plaut and colleagues, 1996) proposes a single network of orthographic, phonological and semantic units whose weights are learned, without separate routes. Both models fit much data, and their relative merits remain debated.

### Developmental dyslexia

**Developmental dyslexia** is a persistent difficulty with accurate or fluent word reading and spelling that is unexpected given a person's other abilities and educational opportunity. Estimates of how common it is vary with the criteria, commonly around 5 to 10 percent of people. It runs in families and is found in many languages, though its profile differs, with slow reading rather than inaccuracy common in shallow orthographies. People with dyslexia are diverse; difficulty with reading says nothing about intelligence, and it has nothing to do with seeing letters backwards.

The **phonological deficit** account holds that the core problem is in representing and manipulating speech sounds, which interferes with learning letter–sound links. Brain imaging finds, on average, reduced activity in left temporoparietal and occipitotemporal regions, including the "visual word form area" (a region that responds to written words; Cohen and colleagues, 2000). It is debated whether such differences are causes or consequences of different reading experience: studies of pre-reading children at family risk, and studies in which adults who learn to read are compared with those who do not, are used to address this. Other proposals are auditory processing difficulties, magnocellular visual deficits and cerebellar involvement; evidence for them is more mixed. The **multiple-deficit** model (Pennington, 2006) proposes several risk factors that combine probabilistically.

Structured, explicit teaching of letter–sound correspondences helps many children with reading difficulties, and earlier support tends to work better. Diagnosis, and decisions about support, belong to qualified professionals who assess each person.

### Standard scores and screening

Reading tests report **standard scores**, with population mean 100 and SD 15, and a $z$-score $z = (x - \mu)/\sigma$. A **screening test** flags children who may need assessment. Its **sensitivity** is the probability of a positive result among children who have the difficulty, its **specificity** the probability of a negative among those without it, and the **positive predictive value** (PPV), by Bayes' rule, is

$$\text{PPV} = \frac{\text{sens} \times \text{prev}}{\text{sens} \times \text{prev} + (1 - \text{spec})(1 - \text{prev})}.$$

Because prevalence is low, many positives are false even with a good test.

@example
title: The simple view
problem: A child decodes at 0.8 of the maximum and has linguistic comprehension of 0.5. What reading comprehension does the simple view predict, and what would it predict if comprehension were raised to 0.8?
step: Multiply: $0.8 \times 0.5 = 0.40$.
step: Raise comprehension: $0.8 \times 0.8 = 0.64$.
step: Interpret: raising the weaker component by 0.3 raised the product by 0.24, and if either component were 0 the product would be 0.
answer: 0.40, rising to 0.64.

@example
title: A screening test's predictive value
problem: A screening test has sensitivity 0.80 and specificity 0.90, and the difficulty has a prevalence of 5%. Among children with a positive result, what fraction truly have the difficulty?
step: Take 1000 children: 50 have it and 950 do not.
step: True positives: $0.80 \times 50 = 40$. False positives: $0.10 \times 950 = 95$.
step: PPV $= 40/(40 + 95) = 0.296$.
step: So about 70% of positives are false alarms, which is why screening is followed by a full assessment.
answer: PPV $\approx 0.30$.

@mistakes
- Describing dyslexia as seeing letters or words backward or as low intelligence: it is a difficulty with word reading and spelling, linked in most cases to speech-sound processing.
- Treating differences in brain activity as proof of cause: they may result from differences in reading experience.
- Forgetting prevalence when judging a test: a test with high sensitivity and specificity can still have a low positive predictive value when the condition is uncommon.

@practice self
prompt: Brain imaging finds, on average, less activity in a left occipitotemporal region in adults with dyslexia during word reading. Explain why this does not by itself show that the difference causes dyslexia, and describe a study design that could distinguish a cause from a consequence.
hint: Think about the effect of reading experience on the brain and about studying children before they read.
solution: Reading itself changes the brain: the region may respond to words more strongly because a person has read a great deal, so a difference in adults could result from less reading practice, not cause it. A design to distinguish the two would follow children from before they learn to read, scan them with a task that does not need reading, and test whether the difference exists already and predicts later reading outcomes; comparing children at family risk with others is also useful. A second design would compare dyslexic children with younger typically developing children matched on reading level, since a difference remaining in the first comparison but not in the second suggests an effect of reading experience. Findings from such designs are mixed, with some differences present early and others appearing to depend on experience, so both causes and consequences are likely.
