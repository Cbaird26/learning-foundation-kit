@article
Most chemistry in the body, and in the laboratory, happens in water. A **solution** is a homogeneous mixture: the **solute** is dissolved in the **solvent**. In an aqueous solution the solvent is water.

### Electrolytes

Substances that dissolve to give ions are **electrolytes**. Soluble ionic compounds such as NaCl are **strong electrolytes**: they dissociate completely, $\text{NaCl}(aq) \to \text{Na}^+(aq) + \text{Cl}^-(aq)$. Strong acids (HCl, $\text{HNO}_3$, $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$) and strong bases (NaOH, KOH) also ionize completely. **Weak electrolytes** such as acetic acid ionize only slightly, and **nonelectrolytes** such as glucose dissolve as intact molecules.

### Precipitation and net ionic equations

When two solutions mix, ions can swap partners. If one of the new combinations is insoluble, it forms a solid **precipitate**. Useful solubility rules: nitrates, alkali-metal and ammonium salts are soluble; chlorides, bromides and iodides are soluble except with $\text{Ag}^+$, $\text{Pb}^{2+}$ and $\text{Hg}_2^{2+}$; sulfates are soluble except with $\text{Ba}^{2+}$, $\text{Pb}^{2+}$ and $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ (slightly); carbonates, phosphates and hydroxides are mostly insoluble apart from alkali-metal and ammonium salts.

To write a **net ionic equation**, write the molecular equation, split strong electrolytes into ions, and cancel the **spectator ions** that appear unchanged on both sides. What remains is the real chemistry.

### Acid-base reactions

In a neutralization, $\text{H}^+$ from an acid reacts with $\text{OH}^-$ from a base to give water. For a strong acid with a strong base the net ionic equation is $\text{H}^+(aq) + \text{OH}^-(aq) \to \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$. At the **equivalence point**, moles of acid times the number of acidic protons per molecule equal moles of base times hydroxides per formula unit.

### Redox reactions

**Oxidation** is loss of electrons and **reduction** is gain (OIL RIG). Track electrons with **oxidation numbers**: free elements are $0$; a monatomic ion equals its charge; oxygen is usually $-2$ and hydrogen usually $+1$; the sum over a species equals its charge. The species that is oxidized is the **reducing agent**, and the species that is reduced is the **oxidizing agent**. In $\text{Zn} + \text{Cu}^{2+} \to \text{Zn}^{2+} + \text{Cu}$, zinc goes from $0$ to $+2$ (oxidized, reducing agent), and copper goes from $+2$ to $0$ (reduced, oxidizing agent).

### Molarity and dilution

**Molarity** is moles of solute per liter of solution:

$$M = \frac{n_{\text{solute}}}{V_{\text{solution}}}.$$

Notice that the denominator is the volume of the whole solution, not the solvent. For a salt that dissociates, the ion concentrations follow the formula: a $0.10$ M solution of $\text{CaCl}_2$ has $[\text{Ca}^{2+}] = 0.10$ M and $[\text{Cl}^-] = 0.20$ M.

When you add solvent, the moles of solute do not change, so
$$M_1 V_1 = M_2 V_2.$$
This is the **dilution equation**, and it works with any consistent volume unit.

### Titration

In a **titration**, a solution of known concentration (the titrant) is added from a buret to a measured volume of analyte until the equivalence point, detected with an indicator. Moles of titrant delivered, converted through the balanced equation, give the moles of analyte, and dividing by the analyte volume gives its molarity.

@example
title: A precipitation reaction
problem: Solutions of lead(II) nitrate and potassium iodide are mixed. Write the net ionic equation.
step: Molecular equation: $\text{Pb(NO}_3)_2(aq) + 2\,\text{KI}(aq) \to \text{PbI}_2(s) + 2\,\text{KNO}_3(aq)$. Lead(II) iodide is an exception to the iodide rule, so it is the precipitate.
step: Split the soluble strong electrolytes: $\text{Pb}^{2+} + 2\text{NO}_3^- + 2\text{K}^+ + 2\text{I}^- \to \text{PbI}_2(s) + 2\text{K}^+ + 2\text{NO}_3^-$.
step: Cancel the spectators $\text{K}^+$ and $\text{NO}_3^-$.
step: Net ionic equation: $\text{Pb}^{2+}(aq) + 2\,\text{I}^-(aq) \to \text{PbI}_2(s)$.

@example
title: Finding an acid concentration by titration
problem: A $25.00$ mL sample of HCl is neutralized by $20.00$ mL of $0.100$ M NaOH. What is the molarity of the HCl?
step: The reaction is $\text{HCl} + \text{NaOH} \to \text{NaCl} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$, a $1:1$ ratio.
step: Moles of NaOH: $0.100\ \text{mol/L} \times 0.02000\ \text{L} = 2.00\times10^{-3}$ mol.
step: Moles of HCl equal moles of NaOH: $2.00\times10^{-3}$ mol.
step: Molarity: $2.00\times10^{-3}\ \text{mol} / 0.02500\ \text{L} = 0.0800$ M.

@mistakes
- Dividing by the volume of the solvent instead of the final volume of the solution when computing molarity.
- Forgetting to split a strong electrolyte into all of its ions (for example $\text{CaCl}_2$ gives $2$ chloride ions per formula unit).
- Ignoring stoichiometry in titration, treating a diprotic acid as if it had only one acidic proton.
- Mixing mL and L in $M_1V_1 = M_2V_2$ without matching units or in $n = MV$.

@practice num
prompt: A solution is made by dissolving $5.85$ g of NaCl ($M = 58.44$ g/mol) in enough water to make $500.$ mL of solution. What is its molarity? Give your answer to three decimal places.
answer: 0.200
check: 5.85 / 58.44 / 0.500
tol: 1%
unit: M
hint: Convert grams to moles, then convert mL to liters.
hint: $n = 5.85/58.44$ and $V = 0.500$ L.
solution: $n = 5.85/58.44 = 0.1001$ mol. $M = 0.1001/0.500 = 0.200$ M.

@practice num
prompt: A $10.0$ mL portion of $6.00$ M HCl is diluted with water to a final volume of $250.$ mL. What is the new molarity?
answer: 0.240
check: 6.00 * 10.0 / 250.
tol: 1%
unit: M
hint: Moles of HCl are unchanged by dilution.
hint: Use $M_1V_1 = M_2V_2$ and solve for $M_2$.
solution: $M_2 = M_1V_1/V_2 = (6.00)(10.0)/250. = 0.240$ M.

@practice num
prompt: A $20.00$ mL sample of sulfuric acid, $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$, is titrated to the equivalence point with $30.00$ mL of $0.100$ M NaOH. What is the molarity of the acid? Assume both protons react. Give three significant figures.
answer: 0.0750
check: 0.100 * 30.00 / 1000 / 2 / (20.00/1000)
tol: 1%
unit: M
hint: Find moles of NaOH delivered.
hint: The balanced equation is $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 + 2\,\text{NaOH} \to \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4 + 2\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$, so acid moles are half the base moles.
solution: $n(\text{NaOH}) = 0.100 \times 0.03000 = 3.00\times10^{-3}$ mol. $n(\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4) = 1.50\times10^{-3}$ mol. Divide by $0.02000$ L to get $0.0750$ M.

@quiz num
prompt: You mix $100.0$ mL of $0.200$ M NaCl with $100.0$ mL of $0.300$ M $\text{CaCl}_2$. Assuming volumes are additive, what is the final chloride ion concentration?
answer: 0.400
check: (0.100*0.200 + 0.100*0.300*2) / 0.200
tol: 1%
unit: M
hint: Count moles of chloride from each solution separately; $\text{CaCl}_2$ supplies two per formula unit.
hint: Total chloride is $0.0200 + 0.0600$ mol. Divide by the total volume in liters.
solution: Chloride from NaCl: $0.0200$ mol. From $\text{CaCl}_2$: $2 \times 0.0300 = 0.0600$ mol. Total $0.0800$ mol in $0.2000$ L gives $0.400$ M.

@quiz mc
prompt: In the reaction $\text{Mg}(s) + \text{Fe}^{2+}(aq) \to \text{Mg}^{2+}(aq) + \text{Fe}(s)$, which species is the oxidizing agent?
choice: $\text{Mg}(s)$
choice*: $\text{Fe}^{2+}(aq)$
choice: $\text{Mg}^{2+}(aq)$
choice: $\text{Fe}(s)$
hint: The oxidizing agent is the species that is itself reduced.
solution: Iron goes from $+2$ to $0$, gaining electrons, so $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ is reduced and acts as the oxidizing agent. Magnesium is oxidized and is the reducing agent.
