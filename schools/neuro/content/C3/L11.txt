@article
Covalent bonds hold atoms together inside molecules, but what holds one molecule to the next? **Intermolecular forces (IMFs)** are attractions between separate molecules or ions. They are much weaker than covalent bonds, typically $0.05$ to $40$ kJ/mol versus hundreds of kJ/mol for bonds, but they decide whether a substance is a gas, liquid or solid at body temperature, how drugs dissolve, and how proteins fold.

### London dispersion forces

Every molecule, polar or not, has electrons that move. At any instant the electron cloud may be lopsided, creating a momentary dipole that induces a dipole in a neighbor. The resulting attraction is the **London dispersion force**. It grows with the number of electrons and with the molecule's **polarizability**, the ease with which its electron cloud is distorted, and with the surface area available for contact. That is why boiling points rise down the noble gases and the halogens, and why long straight-chain alkanes boil higher than their branched, more compact isomers.

### Dipole-dipole forces

Polar molecules have permanent dipoles, and they line up positive end to negative end. These **dipole-dipole forces** add to dispersion forces. For molecules of similar size, a more polar molecule usually has the higher boiling point.

### Hydrogen bonding

A **hydrogen bond** is an especially strong dipole-dipole attraction between a hydrogen atom covalently bonded to N, O or F (the donor) and a lone pair on another N, O or F atom (the acceptor). Hydrogen bonds explain why water ($100\,^\circ$C) boils far above $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ ($-60\,^\circ$C), why ice floats, and why DNA base pairs hold together. A molecule can donate only if it has an H on N, O or F. Dimethyl ether can accept hydrogen bonds but cannot donate them, so it boils far lower than its isomer ethanol.

### Ion-dipole forces

When an ionic compound dissolves in water, each ion is surrounded by water molecules oriented with their opposite charge toward the ion: **ion-dipole forces**. These are the strongest of the intermolecular forces and drive the dissolution of salts.

### Ranking boiling points

To rank substances:

1. Identify the forces. Hydrogen bonding beats dipole-dipole, which beats dispersion, **for molecules of similar size**.
2. If the dominant forces match, the larger or less branched molecule has more dispersion and boils higher.
3. Large molecules can outweigh weaker special forces: dispersion in a large molecule can exceed the dipole-dipole forces in a small one.

### Vapor pressure and the Clausius-Clapeyron equation

**Vapor pressure** is the pressure of vapor in equilibrium with its liquid at a given temperature. Stronger IMFs mean lower vapor pressure and a higher **normal boiling point**, where vapor pressure equals $1$ atm. Vapor pressure rises exponentially with temperature:
$$\ln\frac{P_2}{P_1} = \frac{\Delta H_{\text{vap}}}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_1} - \frac{1}{T_2}\right),$$
with $R = 8.314$ J mol$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$ and $T$ in kelvin. A large $\Delta H_{\text{vap}}$, the energy needed to vaporize one mole of liquid, reflects strong IMFs. Evaporative cooling of sweat works because the molecules that escape carry away heat, an effect large for water because of its high $\Delta H_{\text{vap}}$ (about $40.7$ kJ/mol at $100\,^\circ$C).

@example
title: Ranking boiling points of three isomers and relatives
problem: Rank propane ($\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{CH}_3$), dimethyl ether ($\text{CH}_3\text{OCH}_3$) and ethanol ($\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{OH}$) by boiling point.
step: The three have similar molar masses ($44$, $46$ and $46$ g/mol), so dispersion forces are similar and the other forces decide.
step: Propane is nonpolar, so it has only dispersion forces.
step: Dimethyl ether is polar but cannot donate hydrogen bonds, so it has dipole-dipole forces plus dispersion.
step: Ethanol has an O–H group and forms hydrogen bonds, so the order is propane $<$ dimethyl ether $<$ ethanol (about $-42$, $-24$ and $78\,^\circ$C).

@example
title: Vapor pressure at a new temperature
problem: Water has $\Delta H_{\text{vap}} = 40.7$ kJ/mol and a vapor pressure of $1.00$ atm at $373.15$ K. Estimate its vapor pressure at $353$ K.
step: Rearrange the equation: $\ln(P_2/P_1) = \frac{\Delta H_{\text{vap}}}{R}\left(\frac1{T_1} - \frac1{T_2}\right)$ with $T_1 = 373.15$ K and $T_2 = 353$ K.
step: $\frac{1}{373.15} - \frac{1}{353} = -1.530\times10^{-4}\ \text{K}^{-1}$, and $\Delta H_{\text{vap}}/R = 40700/8.314 = 4895$ K.
step: $\ln(P_2/P_1) = (4895)(-1.530\times10^{-4}) = -0.749$.
step: $P_2 = 1.00 \times e^{-0.749} = 0.473$ atm.

@mistakes
- Treating hydrogen bonds as true covalent bonds, or thinking they occur whenever H is present (H must be on N, O or F).
- Ignoring dispersion forces in large molecules and ranking only by polarity.
- Using Celsius temperatures in the Clausius-Clapeyron equation.
- Confusing intermolecular forces (which change when a substance boils) with intramolecular bonds (which do not).

@quiz num
prompt: A liquid has a vapor pressure of $100.$ torr at $300.$ K and $400.$ torr at $330.$ K. Calculate its enthalpy of vaporization in kJ/mol. Use $R = 8.314$ J/(mol K).
answer: 38.0
check: 8.314 * math.log(400/100) / (1/300 - 1/330) / 1000
tol: 1%
unit: kJ/mol
hint: Solve the Clausius-Clapeyron equation for $\Delta H_{\text{vap}}$.
hint: $\Delta H_{\text{vap}} = R\ln(P_2/P_1)/(1/T_1 - 1/T_2)$.
solution: $\ln(400/100) = 1.386$ and $1/300 - 1/330 = 3.030\times10^{-4}$ K$^{-1}$. $\Delta H_{\text{vap}} = (8.314)(1.386)/(3.030\times10^{-4}) = 3.80\times10^4$ J/mol $= 38.0$ kJ/mol.
