@article
A **chemical bond** is an attraction that holds atoms together. Atoms bond to reach lower energy, often by attaining a noble-gas valence configuration. For main-group elements this is usually eight valence electrons, the **octet rule** (two for hydrogen).

### Bond types

In an **ionic bond**, electrons are transferred from a metal to a nonmetal, giving oppositely charged ions held by electrostatic attraction (NaCl). In a **covalent bond**, atoms share electron pairs (H$_2$, CH$_4$). Between these extremes, unequal sharing produces a **polar covalent bond**. As a rule of thumb, an electronegativity difference $\Delta\text{EN} \lesssim 0.4$ is nonpolar covalent, roughly $0.4$ to $1.7$ is polar covalent, and above about $1.7$ is ionic. The more electronegative atom carries a partial negative charge $\delta^-$.

### Drawing a Lewis structure

1. Count total **valence electrons**: sum over atoms, add one for each negative charge, subtract one for each positive charge.
2. Place the least electronegative atom (not H) in the center and connect atoms with single bonds.
3. Complete octets of outer atoms with lone pairs.
4. Put leftover electrons on the central atom.
5. If the central atom lacks an octet, convert lone pairs from outer atoms into double or triple bonds.

For $\text{CO}_3^{2-}$: $4 + 3(6) + 2 = 24$ valence electrons. Carbon is central with three oxygens; after satisfying octets, carbon needs a double bond to one oxygen.

### Formal charge

**Formal charge** tracks electron bookkeeping assuming every bond is shared equally:
$$\text{FC} = (\text{valence electrons}) - (\text{nonbonding electrons}) - \tfrac12(\text{bonding electrons}).$$
Formal charges sum to the overall charge of the species. The best Lewis structure usually has formal charges closest to zero, with any negative charge on the more electronegative atom.

### Resonance

When more than one valid Lewis structure exists that differ only in placement of electrons, the molecule is a **resonance hybrid**: the real structure is an average of the contributors. In carbonate, the double bond could be on any of the three oxygens, so each C–O bond has the same length, intermediate between single and double, with a bond order of $4/3$. Resonance stabilizes ozone, nitrate, carboxylates and the peptide bond.

### Exceptions to the octet rule

- **Incomplete octets:** boron compounds such as $\text{BF}_3$ have six electrons around B.
- **Odd-electron species** (radicals): NO has $11$ valence electrons, so one atom cannot have an octet.
- **Expanded octets:** central atoms in period 3 or below (P, S, Xe) may have more than eight electrons, as in $\text{PCl}_5$, $\text{SF}_6$ and $\text{XeF}_2$.

### Bond energies

Breaking bonds requires energy; forming bonds releases it. Using average **bond enthalpies**,
$$\Delta H \approx \sum (\text{bonds broken}) - \sum (\text{bonds formed}).$$
This is only an estimate because bond energies are averaged over many compounds. Typical values (kJ/mol): H–H $436$, Cl–Cl $243$, H–Cl $431$, C–C $347$, C=C $614$, C–H $413$.

@example
title: Lewis structure of carbonate
problem: Draw the Lewis structure of $\text{CO}_3^{2-}$ and assign formal charges.
step: Count valence electrons: $4 + 18 + 2 = 24$.
step: Place C in the center with single bonds to three O atoms (6 electrons), then give each O three lone pairs (18 electrons). All $24$ electrons are used, but carbon has only six.
step: Move a lone pair from one O to form a C=O double bond, giving carbon an octet.
step: Formal charges: C $= 4 - 0 - 4 = 0$; double-bonded O $= 6 - 4 - 2 = 0$; each single-bonded O $= 6 - 6 - 1 = -1$. Sum $= -2$, matching the ion.
step: The double bond can sit on any oxygen, so there are three equivalent resonance structures.

@example
title: Estimating a reaction enthalpy
problem: Estimate $\Delta H$ for $\text{H}_2 + \text{Cl}_2 \to 2\,\text{HCl}$.
step: Bonds broken: one H–H ($436$) and one Cl–Cl ($243$), total $679$ kJ.
step: Bonds formed: two H–Cl at $431$ each, total $862$ kJ.
step: $\Delta H \approx 679 - 862 = -183$ kJ, so the reaction is exothermic.

@mistakes
- Forgetting to add or remove electrons for the ion's charge when counting valence electrons.
- Treating resonance structures as rapidly interconverting molecules rather than as contributors to one hybrid.
- Reversing the bond-energy formula (using formed minus broken).
- Placing hydrogen as a central atom, though it can form only one bond.

@practice num
prompt: Use average bond enthalpies (kJ/mol): H–H $436$, Cl–Cl $243$, H–Cl $431$. What is $\Delta H$ in kJ for $\text{H}_2 + \text{Cl}_2 \to 2\,\text{HCl}$ per mole of reaction as written?
answer: -183
check: (436 + 243) - 2*431
hint: Add energy for the bonds broken and subtract energy for the bonds formed.
hint: Broken: $436 + 243$. Formed: $2 \times 431$.
solution: $\Delta H \approx (436 + 243) - 2(431) = 679 - 862 = -183$ kJ.

@practice num
prompt: A Lewis structure of the sulfate ion, $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$, uses two S=O double bonds and two S–O$^-$ single bonds, with an expanded octet on sulfur and no lone pairs on sulfur. What is the formal charge on sulfur?
answer: 0
check: 6 - 0 - (2*4 + 2*2)/2
hint: Count the bonding electrons around sulfur: each double bond contributes $4$ and each single bond contributes $2$.
hint: Sulfur has $12$ bonding electrons and $6$ valence electrons.
solution: $\text{FC} = 6 - 0 - 12/2 = 0$. The two $-1$ charges are on the single-bonded oxygens, so the total is $-2$.

@quiz num
prompt: Estimate $\Delta H$ in kJ for $\text{C}_2\text{H}_4 + \text{H}_2 \to \text{C}_2\text{H}_6$ using bond enthalpies (kJ/mol): C=C $614$, C–C $347$, H–H $436$, C–H $413$.
answer: -123
check: (614 + 436) - (347 + 2*413)
hint: Do not count C–H bonds that are unchanged, or count them on both sides.
hint: Broken: C=C and H–H. Formed: one C–C and two new C–H bonds.
solution: Broken: $614 + 436 = 1050$ kJ. Formed: $347 + 2(413) = 1173$ kJ. $\Delta H \approx 1050 - 1173 = -123$ kJ.
