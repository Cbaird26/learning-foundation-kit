@article
A network trains for an hour and the loss is stuck at 2.30. Or it falls beautifully for three epochs and then becomes `nan`. Or the training loss is excellent and the validation loss is awful. None of these is a mystery once you know which numbers to look at: the **learning curves** (loss against epoch, for training and validation data) and the **gradient magnitudes** by layer. This lesson is a field guide to reading both.

### The four common learning curves

Because the site shows no images, think of each curve as a table of epochs and losses.

| symptom | training loss | validation loss | usual causes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **underfitting or too slow** | high and falling slowly or flat | high, close to training | learning rate too small, model too small, bad initialization, too little training |
| **overfitting** | keeps falling | falls, then rises | too many parameters for the data, too little regularization or data |
| **divergence** | rises, spikes, or becomes `nan` or `inf` | follows training | learning rate too high, exploding gradients, a numerical bug (log of 0) |
| **stuck at chance** | flat at about $\log K$ for $K$ classes | same | dead units, vanishing gradients, learning rate far too small, labels not connected to inputs |

An example for a 10-class problem: a curve that starts at 2.30 ($\approx \log 10$) and stays at 2.30 for ten epochs says that the network outputs a near-uniform distribution and nothing is being learned. A curve that starts at 2.30, falls to 0.40 and then jumps to 14.8 and `nan` says that a step overshot. Always check that the loss at step 0 is near $\log K$ (with sensible initialization) and that the network can overfit a single batch of 10 examples; a network that cannot memorize ten examples has a bug, not a hyperparameter problem.

### Vanishing and exploding gradients

By the chain rule, the gradient reaching layer $l$ of a deep network is a product of one factor per layer above it. Take a chain of one-unit layers $a_l = \sigma(w a_{l-1})$ with a shared weight $w$. Since $\partial a_l/\partial a_{l-1} = w\,a_l(1 - a_l)$ and $\sigma'(z) \le 1/4$, each factor has magnitude at most $|w|/4$. With $|w| = 1$ the gradient shrinks by at least a factor of 4 per layer: after 8 layers it is at most $4^{-8} \approx 1.5 \times 10^{-5}$. This is the **vanishing gradient problem**. The magnitudes of $\partial L/\partial a_l$ for a 6-layer chain with $w = 1$, $a_0 = 0.5$ and $\partial L/\partial a_6 = 1$, from layer 6 down to layer 0:

| layer | 6 | 5 | 4 | 3 | 2 | 1 | 0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $|\partial L/\partial a_l|$ | 1 | 0.225 | 0.0505 | 0.0114 | 0.00256 | 0.000581 | 0.000137 |

The early layers receive almost no learning signal and train extremely slowly. Conversely, if the per-layer factor is larger than 1 (for a linear chain with $|w| = 1.5$, the gradients are $1, 1.5, 2.25, 3.375, \dots$) the gradient grows geometrically: the **exploding gradient problem**, which shows up as huge parameter updates, loss spikes and `nan`.

Remedies, each of which you have met or will meet: ReLU-type activations, whose derivative is 1 for positive inputs; careful initialization (Week 6); normalization layers (Week 9); residual connections (Week 11); a smaller learning rate; and, for exploding gradients, **gradient clipping**.

### Gradient clipping

Clipping by norm rescales the gradient vector $\mathbf{g}$ when its norm exceeds a threshold $c$:

$$\mathbf{g} \leftarrow \mathbf{g}\,\min\!\Big(1, \frac{c}{\lVert \mathbf{g} \rVert_2}\Big).$$

It keeps the direction and limits the step size. For $\mathbf{g} = (3, 4)$ (norm 5) and $c = 2$ the result is $(1.2, 1.6)$. It is standard when training recurrent networks and transformers, where occasional enormous gradients would otherwise wreck a run.

### A checklist

1. Check the initial loss against $\log K$ (or against the variance of the targets for regression).
2. Overfit one tiny batch. If it fails, look for bugs: a wrong sign, forgotten `zero_grad`, mismatched labels, a missing activation.
3. Watch the training and validation curves together.
4. Log the gradient norm of each layer (or the ratio of update size to weight size, which is roughly $10^{-3}$ when things are healthy).
5. Change one thing at a time.

### In code

```python
import math

def clip_by_norm(g, max_norm):
    norm = math.sqrt(sum(v * v for v in g))
    scale = min(1.0, max_norm / norm) if norm > 0 else 1.0
    return [v * scale for v in g]

print(clip_by_norm([3.0, 4.0], 2.0))
```

This prints `[1.2, 1.6]`. In PyTorch the same operation is `torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(parameters, max_norm)`, called between `loss.backward()` and `optimizer.step()`.

@example
title: Diagnosing a learning curve
problem: A 10-class classifier gives these mean losses at epochs 1, 2, 3, 4, 5: training $2.31, 2.30, 2.30, 2.30, 2.30$ and validation $2.31, 2.30, 2.31, 2.30, 2.30$. What is happening and what would you check?
step: $\log 10 = 2.3026$, so the loss is exactly the loss of a uniform guess: the network has learned nothing.
step: Training and validation agree, so this is not overfitting, and the loss is flat, so it is not diverging.
step: The candidates are a learning rate far too small, vanishing gradients in a deep sigmoid network, dead ReLUs, or labels disconnected from inputs. The checks: print gradient norms per layer, try overfitting one batch, and try a larger learning rate.
answer: Stuck at chance (about $\log K$): look at gradient norms, initialization and the learning rate.

@example
title: Vanishing gradient through two sigmoid layers
problem: A chain has $a_l = \sigma(w a_{l-1})$ with $w = 2$ for $l = 1, 2$, and $a_0 = 0$. With $\partial L/\partial a_2 = 1$, compute $|\partial L/\partial a_1|$ and $|\partial L/\partial a_0|$.
step: Forward: $a_1 = \sigma(0) = 0.5$ and $a_2 = \sigma(2 \cdot 0.5) = \sigma(1) = 0.7311$.
step: Layer 2's factor is $w\,a_2(1 - a_2) = 2 \cdot 0.7311 \cdot 0.2689 = 0.3932$, so $|\partial L/\partial a_1| = 0.3932$.
step: Layer 1's factor is $w\,a_1(1 - a_1) = 2 \cdot 0.5 \cdot 0.5 = 0.5$, so $|\partial L/\partial a_0| = 0.3932 \cdot 0.5 = 0.1966$.
answer: $0.3932$ and $0.1966$.

@mistakes
- Judging a run by the training loss alone: only the validation curve distinguishes a model that generalizes from one that has memorized.
- Treating `nan` as a mystery of the optimizer: it is nearly always an overflow, a log of zero or a learning rate that is too high; find the first step where it appears.
- Clipping each gradient component separately (changing the direction) when the intention is to clip the whole gradient vector by its norm.
- Concluding "the learning rate is too small" from a flat loss without checking for bugs: first confirm that the network can overfit one small batch.

model:
```python
import math

def clip_by_global_norm(grads, max_norm):
    """Rescale all gradient lists together so the global norm is at most max_norm."""
    norm = math.sqrt(sum(v * v for layer in grads for v in layer))
    if norm <= max_norm or norm == 0:
        return [list(layer) for layer in grads]
    scale = max_norm / norm
    return [[v * scale for v in layer] for layer in grads]
```
hint: Compute one norm over every entry of every list, then multiply every entry by `max_norm / norm` if that norm is too large.
solution: Clipping by the global norm keeps the direction of the whole gradient, so the same factor must be applied to every layer; clipping each list to its own norm would change the relative sizes of the layers, which the first test (norms 6 and 8 individually, 10 together) detects. Vectors within the limit and zero vectors come back unchanged, and the inputs are not modified.

model:
```python
import math

def backward_scales(w, x0, depth, act):
    """Gradient magnitudes at each layer of a chain of one-unit layers."""
    a = [x0]
    for _ in range(depth):
        z = w * a[-1]
        a.append(1 / (1 + math.exp(-z)) if act == 'sigmoid' else z)
    g = 1.0
    out = [1.0]
    for l in range(depth, 0, -1):
        local = w * a[l] * (1 - a[l]) if act == 'sigmoid' else w
        g *= local
        out.append(abs(g))
    return out
```
hint: Run the forward pass storing every `a[l]`, then walk backward from layer `depth` to 1, multiplying a running gradient by each layer's local derivative.
solution: The forward values are needed because the sigmoid's local derivative depends on the layer's output. The gradient entering layer $l$ is the product of the local derivatives of layers $l+1, \dots, \text{depth}$; the lists show geometric growth for $w = 1.5$, geometric decay for $w = 0.5$, and the characteristic fast decay of sigmoid chains.

model:
```python
import math

def diagnose(train, val):
    """Rule-based diagnosis of a training run from its learning curves."""
    if any(math.isnan(v) or math.isinf(v) for v in train) or train[-1] > 10 * train[0]:
        return 'diverged'
    if train[-1] > 0.9 * train[0]:
        return 'underfitting'
    if val[-1] > 1.2 * min(val):
        return 'overfitting'
    return 'healthy'
```
hint: Check the rules in the stated order with `if` statements and return as soon as one matches; use `math.isnan` and `math.isinf` for the first rule.
solution: The order matters: a run that blows up must be called diverged before the other rules look at its numbers. `nan` compares false with everything, so it needs an explicit `isnan` test. The last two tests sit on either side of the 1.2 threshold: a final validation loss of 0.6 is 1.33 times the minimum 0.45 (overfitting), while 0.55 is only 1.1 times the minimum 0.5 (healthy).
