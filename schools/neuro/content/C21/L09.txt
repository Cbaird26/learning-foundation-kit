@article
Ana bites into a lemon. Her brain registers the chemicals, her face puckers, she says "sour," and she learns not to do it again. Science can in principle explain all of that. But there is also something it is like for Ana to taste the lemon: a sour experience. Why should the processing of chemical signals be accompanied by that? The question is among the most debated in philosophy of mind.

### Kinds of consciousness

Ned Block distinguished **access consciousness**, a state's availability for reasoning, report and the control of action, from **phenomenal consciousness**, the way experience feels from the inside. Thomas Nagel (1974) gave a formulation: an organism has conscious mental states if and only if there is something it is like to be that organism. The states with this feature are said to have **qualia** or a phenomenal character. Whether access and phenomenal consciousness can come apart is disputed.

### Easy problems and the hard problem

David Chalmers (1995) divided the problems of consciousness. The **easy problems** concern explaining cognitive functions: discrimination, integration of information, report, attention, the control of behavior. They are "easy" only in that we know what kind of explanation would solve them: a mechanism that performs the function. The **hard problem** is why the performance of these functions is accompanied by experience at all.

### The explanatory gap

Joseph Levine (1983) argued that there is an **explanatory gap**. Once we know that heat is the motion of molecules, the physical story leaves nothing about heat unexplained. But a statement such as "pain is C-fiber firing" leaves a question open: why should that physical state feel like this, rather than like something else or like nothing? The argument runs:

| P1 | A physical (structural or functional) explanation explains a fact only by showing that it follows from facts about physical structure and function. |
| P2 | Facts about experience, such as why a physical state feels the way it does or feels like anything at all, do not follow from facts about physical structure and function. |
| C | So physical explanation cannot fully explain facts about experience: there is an explanatory gap. |

The conclusion is about explanation, an **epistemic** gap. Whether it shows an **ontological** gap, that consciousness is not physical, is the question. Dualists infer that it is; others deny that the inference is valid.

### Responses

Chalmers classified responses in a way that has become standard.

**Type-A materialism** denies that there is a hard problem beyond the easy ones. Once the functions are explained, nothing is left to explain; the sense that something is left over is a mistake. Daniel Dennett is the best-known proponent.

**Type-B materialism** accepts the epistemic gap but denies that it shows an ontological gap. Phenomenal states are physical states, and the identity is not derivable a priori, just as "water is H$_2$O" is a posteriori. The **phenomenal concept strategy**, developed by Brian Loar and others, says that we have special concepts of our experiences, which pick out brain states in a way that seems to leave a gap, though the states are the same. Critics ask whether the strategy explains the gap without returning the problem to the concepts.

Other responses go beyond physicalism: interactionist dualism, epiphenomenalism and views on which consciousness, or its building blocks, is fundamental, such as Russellian monism and panpsychism (week 11).

### Scientific theories

Scientific theories of consciousness include **global workspace theories** (a state is conscious when it is broadcast widely to the brain's systems), **higher-order theories** (a state is conscious when one is aware of being in it), **recurrent processing theories** and **integrated information theory**, which identifies consciousness with a measure of integrated information. They differ on what is necessary, and so they give different verdicts about animals and artificial systems. Many are aimed at the easy problems or at correlates of consciousness; whether they address the hard problem is disputed. In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey, functionalism was the most accepted view on consciousness (33%), then dualism (22%) and identity theory (13%), with panpsychism at 8% and eliminativism at 5%; those figures do not show which view is correct.

### Why it matters

Whether any artificial system is conscious is an open question. As of 2026 there is no consensus about whether any AI system is conscious or what would settle it. The hard problem is one reason: if experience is not entailed by function, then information about behavior or function leaves the question open.

title: Easy or hard?
problem: Classify each as an easy problem or as the hard problem: (a) how the brain integrates information from the senses; (b) why integration of information is accompanied by experience; (c) how attention selects some stimuli for report.
step: (a) Integration of information is a function, and the explanation is a mechanism: an easy problem.
step: (b) The question is why the function is accompanied by experience: the hard problem.
step: (c) Selection of stimuli for report is a function: an easy problem.
step: "Easy" does not mean simple. It means that we know what kind of explanation would solve them.
answer: (a) easy; (b) hard; (c) easy.

@example
title: Epistemic gap versus ontological gap
problem: A student argues: "There is an explanatory gap between brain states and experience. So experience is not physical." What is missing?
step: The stated premise is that physical explanation leaves a gap in what we can explain.
step: The conclusion is about what exists: that experience is not physical. This is an ontological claim.
step: The step needs a bridge: if there is an explanatory gap, there is a gap in the nature of things.
step: Type-B materialists deny the bridge: identities known a posteriori may leave us without an explanation of why, as with the apparent gap between water and H$_2$O before chemistry.
answer: The missing premise is the bridge from the epistemic gap to the ontological gap, which type-B materialists reject.

@mistakes
- Treating the hard problem as the claim that consciousness is not physical. It is a problem about explanation; dualism is one response to it.
- Confusing phenomenal with access consciousness.
- Saying the easy problems are easy to solve. They are tractable in the sense that mechanisms would explain them.
- Taking the share of philosophers who accept a view to settle which view is true.

@practice self
prompt: In four or five sentences, explain the difference between an epistemic gap and an ontological gap, and why the difference matters for the hard problem.
hint: Compare water and H$_2$O.
solution: An epistemic gap is a gap in what we can explain or know: physical facts do not let us see why experience accompanies them. An ontological gap is a gap in what exists: experience is something over and above the physical. The difference matters because the argument from the explanatory gap supports a conclusion about explanation, and dualists need an extra premise to get a conclusion about being. Type-B materialists deny that bridge, saying that a posteriori identities may leave an explanatory gap without an ontological one. The question is whether the gap, unlike earlier ones, can be closed.
