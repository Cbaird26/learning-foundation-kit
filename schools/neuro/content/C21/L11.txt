@article
Most answers to the mind-body problem try to keep the mind in some form: as a separate substance, as a brain state, as a functional role. Three views take more radical lines. One says our ordinary mental concepts are mistaken and may be discarded. One says that what we take to be consciousness is a kind of illusion. One says that mind is more widespread than we think, perhaps a feature of matter itself.

### Eliminative materialism

Paul and Patricia Churchland argued that **folk psychology**, our everyday framework of beliefs, desires and intentions, is a theory about the causes of behavior, and may be a false one. In "Eliminative Materialism and the Propositional Attitudes" (1981), Paul Churchland argued roughly as follows:

| P1 | Folk psychology is an empirical theory of the causes of behavior. |
| P2 | Folk psychology is a poor theory: it has made little progress, fails to explain many phenomena such as sleep, learning and mental illness, and does not fit with what neuroscience is finding. |
| P3 | If a theory is a poor one and does not fit mature science, it is likely to be replaced, and the entities it posits will turn out not to exist. |
| C | So the beliefs and desires of folk psychology probably do not exist. |

Compare the replacement of talk of caloric or witches by better theories. Objections: against P1, some argue that folk psychology is not a theory but a skill of interpreting others (as simulation theorists hold) or a normative practice. Against P2, defenders say that folk psychology is very successful at predicting behavior in daily life. Against P3, a theory may be revised without its entities being eliminated. A further objection is that the thesis is **self-undermining**, since asserting it expresses a belief. Eliminativists reply that this assumes the very framework in dispute. In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey about 5% accepted or leaned toward eliminativism about consciousness.

### Illusionism

**Illusionism** is a view about phenomenal consciousness. Keith Frankish, who named the position in 2016, and Daniel Dennett (in earlier work on "quining qualia") argue that phenomenal consciousness as the dualist or the realist conceives it, with intrinsic, ineffable, private qualities, does not exist; there is an introspective illusion that we have it. The task for a science of consciousness is then to explain why we are so strongly inclined to believe in it, the **illusion problem**. Illusionism is thus a more specific position than eliminativism about beliefs: it does not deny that people have beliefs and attitudes, only that experiences have the properties philosophers attribute to them.

The leading objection is that the very appearance of experience seems to be experience: if it seems to me that I am in pain, then there is something it is like to seem that way, and so the illusion would itself be phenomenal. Illusionists reply that the seeming is a matter of a representation or judgment, not of phenomenal feel. Whether that reply works is the main dispute.

### Panpsychism

**Panpsychism** says that mind or consciousness is a fundamental and ubiquitous feature of the physical world. Its modern defenders (including Galen Strawson and Philip Goff) begin from the observation, drawn from Russell and Eddington, that physics describes what matter does (its dispositions, relations and structure) but not what it is intrinsically. Consciousness may be the intrinsic nature that fills in the structure. This is a version of **Russellian monism**. Its supporters say it avoids both emergence from nothing and a gap between mind and matter.

The central difficulty is the **combination problem**: how do the simple experiences of micro-level entities combine to form the unified experience of a person? William James already raised a form of the problem. Critics also say that panpsychism is unfalsifiable or that it adds a mystery without explanatory gain. In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey about 8% accepted or leaned toward panpsychism.

### Evaluating the three

Each of these views is a response to the same pressure: the difficulty of fitting experience into the physical picture. Eliminativism and illusionism reduce the explanandum; panpsychism expands the base. None is accepted as established, and the survey figures say nothing about their truth.

@example
title: Eliminativism or illusionism?
problem: Classify: (a) "There are no beliefs, only neural states." (b) "People believe things, but experiences lack the intrinsic qualities philosophers attribute to them; the belief that they have them is an illusion." (c) "Consciousness is a feature of the intrinsic nature of matter."
step: (a) Denies that beliefs exist: eliminativism about the propositional attitudes.
step: (b) Accepts beliefs but denies phenomenal properties: illusionism.
step: (c) Locates consciousness in the nature of matter: a panpsychist or Russellian monist view.
step: Each is a distinct position and none should be run together with the others.
answer: (a) eliminativism; (b) illusionism; (c) panpsychism or Russellian monism.

@example
title: Which premise do the objections attack?
problem: Say which premise of the eliminativist argument each of these attacks: (a) "Folk psychology is a skill, not a theory." (b) "Folk psychology predicts behavior very well." (c) "Theories get revised without eliminating their entities."
step: (a) Attacks P1, that folk psychology is an empirical theory.
step: (b) Attacks P2, that it is a poor theory.
step: (c) Attacks P3, the claim that a poor theory's entities will be eliminated.
step: Each leaves the argument valid and denies one premise.
answer: (a) P1; (b) P2; (c) P3.

@mistakes
- Treating illusionism as eliminativism about the mind in general. Illusionists accept beliefs and attitudes.
- Treating panpsychism as the claim that rocks think. It is the claim that fundamental entities have some experiential feature.
- Saying eliminativism is self-refuting beyond question. Eliminativists dispute that the objection assumes the framework in dispute.
- Confusing the survey percentages with evidence of truth.

prompt: In four or five sentences, explain the combination problem for panpsychism and say how a panpsychist might respond.
hint: Think of how many tiny experiences make up one subject.
solution: Panpsychists hold that fundamental entities have simple experiential features. A person has a unified experience, so we need an account of how many micro-experiences combine into a subject. The problem is that experiences of different subjects do not seem to add up to a single experience, as the experiences of different people do not combine into one. A panpsychist might respond that some physical relations, such as those found in integrated systems, ground combination, or that a combination is brute but not mysterious. The reply must say why combination occurs here and not between people.
