@article
A thermostat, a mercury thermometer and a digital sensor are made of different stuff, yet each can be a temperature sensor. What makes something a temperature sensor is what it does: it responds to temperature and produces a signal that controls other things. **Functionalism** applies the same idea to the mind. What makes a state a pain, a belief or a desire is not what it is made of but the role it plays: its causal relations to stimuli, to behavior and to other mental states.

### The theory

Functionalism, developed from the 1960s by Hilary Putnam, and in different forms by David Lewis and D. M. Armstrong, defines mental states by their **functional roles**. Pain, for instance, is roughly the state typically caused by bodily damage that tends to cause the desire to be rid of it, the belief that something is wrong, wincing and avoidance. The state that actually plays this role in a given creature is the **realizer** of the role. Functionalism differs from behaviorism because the roles are specified in terms of inner states and their relations to each other, not just stimuli and behavior. It differs from the identity theory because it does not identify pain with a particular physical type.

Putnam's early version, **machine-state functionalism**, modeled the mind on a computing machine: mental states are like the states of a program, defined by how they take inputs and previous states to outputs and next states. Lewis and Armstrong gave **causal-role** versions, which define mental states by the causal roles that common sense associates with them.

### Multiple realizability

The key argument against the type-identity theory is **multiple realizability**. If the same mental state can be realized by physically different states in different creatures or machines, then the mental type cannot be identical to any one physical type.

| P1 | If pain were identical to a single type of brain state, then every possible creature in pain would be in that brain state. |
| P2 | Some possible creatures in pain, with physically different nervous systems or even non-biological systems, would not be in that brain state. |
| C | So pain is not identical to a single type of physical state. |

Three illustrations: first, a mousetrap can be a spring trap, a cage or a glue board, so "mousetrap" is a functional kind that no single mechanism fits; second, a computer program such as a sorting routine can run on different hardware; third, vision has evolved separately in different animal groups with physically different eyes. For pain, Putnam's thought was that a creature with a very different brain, or an alien or a machine, could be in pain. Premise P2 is an empirical and conceptual claim, not a settled fact: how widely pain is realized is a matter of dispute, and the whole argument depends on it.

### Replies to multiple realizability

Identity theorists respond that the argument shows only that there are different **local** identities: human pain is one brain state, octopus pain another, and so on (Lewis defended this kind of species-relative identity). Jaegwon Kim argued that if pain is really multiply realized, the physical kinds that realize it have so little in common that pain is not a single scientific kind at all, which would make it unfit for laws. Defenders of functionalism reply that special sciences such as psychology have their own legitimate kinds.

### Objections to functionalism

**Absent qualia.** Ned Block imagined the "China brain": the population of China is organized so that each person plays the role of a neuron, with radio links, producing the functional organization of a brain for a while. If functionalism is right, this system has mental states, including pain. Block thought it implausible that the whole system feels anything.

| P1 | If functionalism is true, then necessarily any system with the right functional organization has the corresponding mental states, including conscious states. |
| P2 | It is possible for a system to have the functional organization of a mind without any conscious experience (absent qualia). |
| C | So functionalism is not true as a theory of conscious states. |

Functionalists reply by denying P2: the intuition rests on a failure to imagine the scale and speed of the system, or on a prejudice against nonbiological systems. Others accept a restriction of functionalism to cognitive but not to phenomenal states. The **inverted spectrum** raises a related worry: two people might have functionally identical color experiences with different qualities (what you call red looks as green does to me), which functionalism would count as the same state. No such case is established, and whether such a case is possible is itself a contested matter.

### Where things stand

In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey, functionalism was the most popular response on consciousness, at about 33%. That share says nothing about whether the view is true; the objections above still have to be answered.

@example
title: Three examples of multiple realizability
problem: Give three examples of a kind that is multiply realizable and say what makes each one a functional kind.
step: A clock: pendulum clocks, quartz clocks and sundials differ in mechanism. What they have in common is the function of indicating time.
step: A currency: coins, notes and card payments are all money because of their role in exchange, not their material.
step: A computer program, such as one that sorts names, can run on different hardware with different physical states, though it performs the same function.
step: In each case the kind is picked out by a role, so no single physical type is identical to it. Functionalists say mental kinds are like this.
answer: Clocks, money and programs are multiply realizable: each is defined by a role, not by a material type.

@example
title: Role and realizer
problem: A philosopher says: "Pain is the state that is typically caused by tissue damage and causes avoidance behavior and the desire for relief." In a human, this role is played by a certain pattern of neural firing. What is the role, what is the realizer, and does the functionalist say pain is the role or the realizer?
step: The role is the causal profile: caused by tissue damage, causing avoidance and the desire for relief.
step: The realizer is the physical state that plays the role in a given creature, the neural pattern in the human.
step: Role functionalism says that pain is the role itself, a higher-level state; realizer functionalism says pain is the state that plays the role. Both are called functionalist, and they differ on this point.
step: Either way, the role-based definition is what allows a creature with a different realizer to be in pain too.
answer: The role is the causal profile and the realizer is the neural pattern. Functionalists divide on whether pain is the role or the realizer.

@mistakes
- Thinking multiple realizability shows that the mind is not physical. Functionalism is compatible with physicalism: each realizer may be physical.
- Confusing role and realizer.
- Treating functionalism as behaviorism: functional roles include relations to other inner states.
- Taking the China brain to prove functionalism false. It is an intuition-based objection that functionalists can contest.

hint: Say what would be true of every creature in pain if type identity held.
solution: If pain were identical to one type of brain state, every creature in pain would be in that state. But a creature with a physically different nervous system, or a machine with the right organization, might also feel pain, so pain cannot be identical to a single type. One way to resist is to adopt local identities: human pain is one brain state and octopus pain another, so type identity holds within species. The cost is that pain is then not a single kind across species, and the explanation of what unites them seems to require a functional description.
