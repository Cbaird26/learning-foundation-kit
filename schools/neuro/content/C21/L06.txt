@article
Imagine you are locked in a room. Through a slot come cards with Chinese characters, which you cannot read. You have a thick rulebook, written in English, that tells you which cards to pass back out in response to which. Outside, a native speaker is passing in questions and receiving fluent answers. Do you understand Chinese? John Searle argued in 1980 that you do not, and that this shows something about computers.

### The target: strong AI

Searle distinguished **weak AI**, the view that computers are useful tools for studying the mind, from **strong AI**, the view that an appropriately programmed computer literally has a mind and understands. His Chinese room argument targets strong AI, in particular the claim that running the right program is sufficient for understanding.

### The thought experiment

In the room, the person follows the rulebook, which is a program. By hypothesis, the room passes a Turing test in Chinese. But the person understands no Chinese: he is manipulating symbols according to their shapes. Since a computer running a program does only the same, it too understands nothing. Searle later set the argument out in a more explicit form:

| P1 | Programs are formal (syntactic): they are defined by the manipulation of symbols according to their form. |
| P2 | Minds have mental contents (semantics): thoughts are about things and have meanings. |
| P3 | Syntax by itself is neither constitutive of nor sufficient for semantics. |
| C | So programs by themselves are neither constitutive of nor sufficient for minds. |

The conclusion follows, and the debate is about P3, which the room is supposed to support by example. Searle did not say that machines cannot think: he said brains are machines that do, and that what matters is the **causal powers** of the brain, not the program. This position is often called **biological naturalism**. His claim is about programs, not about all artificial systems.

### The replies

**The systems reply.** The person does not understand Chinese, but the whole system (person, rulebook, scratch paper, the room) does. Understanding belongs to the system, as one neuron does not understand English although a brain does. Searle's response: let the person memorize the rulebook and do all the work in his head, so that he is the whole system. He still does not understand. Defenders reply that a distinct "virtual mind" might be realized by the person's activity, as a program can run on a computer that does not itself "know" the program's contents. Searle's critics say his reply begs the question by assuming that if the person lacks understanding, nothing in him has it.

**The robot reply.** Put the computer in a robot with cameras and limbs so that the symbols are connected to the world by causal links. Searle responds that the robot's inputs are still just more symbols, and the person in the robot would still not understand. Critics say that grounding in the world might be what supplies meaning, and that the connection, not the symbols, is the missing ingredient.

**The brain simulator reply.** Suppose the program simulates the neurons of a native speaker. Searle replies that a simulation of a storm does not get anything wet; simulating the neurons' formal structure is not duplicating their causal powers.

**The other-minds reply.** We attribute understanding to other people only on behavioral grounds, so if the room behaves the same way, we should do the same. Searle says the issue is not how we know but what understanding is.

### Evaluating the argument

The argument has force against a view on which running any program that produces the right behavior suffices for understanding. Three questions remain. First, does the thought experiment establish P3, or does it merely rely on an intuition that the replies challenge? Second, what is it about brains that supplies semantics, and why could not something else supply it? Third, what is the status of the intuition that the system understands nothing? As of 2026 there is no consensus on whether any AI system understands, and the Chinese room remains an influential argument that bears on the question without settling it.

title: Locating the target of the argument
problem: Which claim does the Chinese room argument attack: that the room passes the Turing test, that machines can think, or that running the right program suffices for understanding?
step: The room is stipulated to pass the Turing test, so the argument does not deny that.
step: Searle says that brains are machines that think, so he does not argue that no machine could think.
step: The target is the claim that implementing a program is sufficient for understanding, that is, strong AI.
step: So the conclusion is limited: programs by themselves are not sufficient for minds.
answer: The argument attacks the sufficiency of running the right program, not the possibility of machine thought.

@example
title: Evaluating the systems reply
problem: Evaluate the systems reply and Searle's internalization response in one paragraph.
step: The systems reply says the understanding is a property of the whole system, not the person, as no neuron understands English although a brain does.
step: Searle's response: the person memorizes the rules and does everything himself. The system is now inside him, and he still does not understand Chinese.
step: The defender can answer that the person and the system are distinct subjects of understanding: the person's lack of Chinese does not show that the symbol-manipulating process lacks it, just as the hardware does not need to know what the software does.
step: Assessment: the reply needs an account of why the system understands, not just a denial that the person's failure settles the matter. Searle's response needs an argument that a subject cannot host another subject.
answer: The reply is strong if one can independently motivate understanding at the level of the system; the internalization response presupposes that the person is the only candidate subject.

@mistakes
- Saying Searle claims machines cannot think. He says that brains are machines that think and that programs are not sufficient.
- Treating the systems reply as a proof that the room understands. It is a reply that shows that the person's ignorance does not settle the question.
- Confusing the Chinese room with the Turing test. The room grants that the test is passed.
- Treating a simulation as a duplication, as the brain simulator reply may do.
