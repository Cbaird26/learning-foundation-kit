@article
Ana stubs her toe. The stub is a physical event: tissue is damaged and nerves fire. A moment later she feels a sharp pain, decides to swear, and swears. Pain, decision and swearing seem to belong to two different families of things: the mental (feelings, thoughts, intentions) and the physical (tissue, neurons, muscle movements). Yet the families seem to interact in both directions. The **mind-body problem** is the problem of saying what the relation between them is. Are they two kinds of thing, one kind, or something else, and how can they affect each other?

### A map of answers

- **Substance dualism**: minds (or selves) and bodies are two different kinds of substance, each able in principle to exist without the other.
- **Property dualism**: there is only one kind of substance, but it has two fundamentally different kinds of properties, physical and mental, and the mental ones are not physical properties.
- **Physicalism**: everything is physical, or depends on and is determined by the physical. Its versions (behaviorism, identity theory, functionalism, eliminativism) are the subject of the next weeks.
- **Idealism**: everything is mental, or depends on minds.

The 2020 PhilPapers Survey found about 52% of respondents accepting or leaning toward physicalism and about 32% non-physicalism. The share of philosophers who hold a view is not evidence that it is true, and the arguments below must be judged on their merits.

### Descartes's real distinction

In Meditation VI, Descartes argues that mind and body are **really distinct**: two substances, each capable of existing without the other. Two arguments are standard.

**The conceivability argument.** Descartes holds that whatever he understands clearly and distinctly can be brought about by God just as he understands it. In Meditation II he has already argued that he can doubt that bodies exist but cannot doubt that he is thinking. He concludes that he can clearly and distinctly understand himself as a thinking, non-extended thing, and body as an extended, non-thinking thing.

| P1 | I can clearly and distinctly conceive of myself existing as a thinking thing without a body. |
| P2 | Whatever I can clearly and distinctly conceive could exist just as I conceive it. |
| C1 | So it is possible that I exist without a body. |
| P3 | If it is possible for one thing to exist without another, they are not identical. |
| C | So I am not identical to my body: mind and body are distinct. |

The argument is valid. P2 is the contested step from conceivability to possibility, and Descartes underwrites it by appeal to God's power, so the argument inherits any doubt about how that guarantee works. P1 is also contested: does his doubting show that he can conceive himself without a body, or only that he cannot tell from the inside whether he has one?

**The divisibility argument.** Descartes also says that a body, being extended, can always be divided into parts, while the mind is entirely one: he cannot distinguish parts of it.

| P1 | Every body is divisible. |
| P2 | The mind is not divisible. |
| P3 | If $x$ has a property that $y$ lacks, then $x \neq y$ (Leibniz's Law). |
| C | So the mind is not a body. |

It is valid. Critics deny P2, noting that minds can be fragmented or divided in some pathological cases, or say that indivisibility is not clearly a property of the mind itself.

### Objections

**Arnauld's objection** (the Fourth Set of Objections) says that Descartes's reasoning proves too much. Someone can clearly conceive a triangle as right-angled while not knowing that the Pythagorean theorem holds of it, and so conceive it as lacking a property it necessarily has. Likewise, Descartes may conceive the mind without the body only because his knowledge of the mind's nature is incomplete. Descartes replies that he needs only an understanding that is complete enough to be of a whole thing, and that the mind as a thinking thing is such a thing. This objection targets P1, or the move from P1 to C1.

**Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia** asked Descartes in correspondence (1643) how an unextended thinking substance can move an extended body, since motion seems to require contact and pushing. This is the **interaction problem**. It does not refute the real distinction directly, but it threatens the dualist's account of the obvious facts of mental causation. Descartes's replies appeal to the primitive notion of the union of mind and body, and he located the point of interaction in the pineal gland; most readers judge that this does not solve the problem. Later critics add that interaction seems to conflict with the causal closure of the physical, an issue taken up in week 8.

### Where things stand

Substance dualism is a minority view in contemporary philosophy, but the conceivability argument has descendants that are very much alive: the zombie argument of week 10 is one. When you evaluate the argument, ask which premise carries the weight, what the objector denies, and whether the reply restores the premise or merely postpones the problem.

@example
title: Finding the hidden premise
problem: Ana argues: "I can imagine my mind surviving without my body. So my mind is not my body." Put this in standard form, find what is missing, and say what the missing premise shares with Descartes's argument.
step: The stated premise is P1: I can imagine my mind existing without my body. The stated conclusion is C: my mind is not my body.
step: The jump needs a bridge from imagining to possibility: P2, if I can conceive of a thing existing without another, then it is possible for the first to exist without the second.
step: It also needs P3, the principle that things that can exist apart are not identical. With P1, P2 and P3 the argument is valid.
step: Descartes adds a restriction: the conception must be clear and distinct. Ana's premise is vulnerable because imagining can be incomplete: she may simply not be considering what her mind depends on.
answer: The missing premises are the conceivability-possibility bridge and the principle that separability implies distinctness. Descartes's version adds the clear and distinct restriction, which is where Arnauld presses.

@example
title: Which premise does the objector deny?
problem: A critic says: "Minds are not indivisible. When the two halves of the brain are surgically disconnected, a person can behave as two partly separate centers of control." Which premise of the divisibility argument does this target, and what does Descartes need to reply?
step: Recall the argument: P1 every body is divisible; P2 the mind is not divisible; P3 Leibniz's Law; C the mind is not a body.
step: The critic denies P2: if a mind can come apart, it has a property the divisibility argument says only bodies have.
step: Descartes needs to say that what is divided in such a case is not the mind itself, or that the unity he means is the unity of a single point of view, which such cases may not undo. This is a way of restating P2, not an independent proof of it.
answer: The objection targets P2. The argument remains valid; the dispute is over whether P2 is true.

@mistakes
- Saying that Descartes thinks the mind has no connection to the body. He holds that mind and body are really distinct but intimately united in a human being.
- Treating the interaction problem as a proof that dualism is false. It is an objection to the dualist's account of causation, and dualists have responded to it.
- Confusing substance dualism with property dualism: the second keeps one substance with two kinds of properties.
- Reading Arnauld as saying that the mind is a body. His objection concerns whether conceiving one thing without another shows they are distinct.

prompt: In four or five sentences, explain Princess Elisabeth's interaction problem for Descartes and give one way a dualist might reply.
hint: Say what seems to be required for one thing to move another and what the mind lacks.
solution: Elisabeth asked how a thinking substance that has no extension can move a body. Ordinary pushing seems to require contact between extended things, and a mind has no size, shape or location to make contact with. The problem threatens the dualist's explanation of why decisions lead to action. One reply, in the spirit of Descartes, is that the union of mind and body is a primitive fact not to be explained by the model of pushing. Another is to say that causation need not require contact. The cost of either reply is that it explains little about how the interaction happens, and that it leaves the question of conflict with the physical sciences open.
