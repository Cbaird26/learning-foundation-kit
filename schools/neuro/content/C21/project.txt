@project
title: A philosophy of mind paper
overview: Write a 2,500-word paper that defends an answer to one question: can physicalism explain consciousness, could a machine think, or is mental causation a problem for nonreductive physicalism. The paper must reconstruct the strongest opposing argument in standard form and reply to it.
goal: Defend a clear thesis on one of the three questions with arguments, not assertion.
goal: Reconstruct the strongest opposing argument fairly, in standard form, and say which premise your reply targets.
goal: Anticipate the best objection to your reply and respond to it.
step: Choose one question and write your thesis in a single sentence. Decide in advance what would count against it.
step: Read the relevant course lessons (week 9 and 10 for consciousness, weeks 5 and 6 for machines, weeks 4 and 8 for mental causation) and the course reading, and find at least three sources through the encyclopedias and PhilPapers. Cite everything you use.
step: Reconstruct the strongest opposing argument in standard form, with numbered premises and a conclusion. Use the supplied material if you wish, but state it in your own words and check it against your sources.
step: Decide which premise your reply rejects, or which step you say is invalid, and write the reply. Give a reason that a defender of the opposing argument would have to take seriously.
step: Write the best objection to your reply, state it fairly, and respond. Say what your view costs.
step: Draft the paper: introduction with thesis, the opposing argument, your reply, objection and response, conclusion. Aim for about 2,500 words.
step: Revise: check each attribution and each claim about a text, remove anything you cannot support, and confirm that the premise numbers in your reply match the argument.
deliverable: A paper of about 2,500 words with a reference list.
deliverable: A one-paragraph note naming the premise you reject and the strongest objection to your reply.
rubric: The thesis is clear and defended by argument, not by appeal to a survey or authority.
rubric: The opposing argument is reconstructed in valid standard form and is the strongest available, not a straw man.
rubric: The reply targets a specific premise or inference and gives a reason.
rubric: The best objection to the reply is stated fairly and answered.
rubric: Attributions and claims about texts are accurate, and sources are cited.
safety: If you write on machines, do not claim as settled that any actual AI system does or does not understand or is or is not conscious; as of 2026 there is no consensus, and your paper should argue from premises.
tutor: Is my opposing argument valid as I have written it, and which premise is really doing the work?
tutor: What would a defender of the view I am rejecting say in reply to my objection?
tutor: Have I attributed these arguments to the right philosophers, and where should I check?
material: **Argument A (consciousness).** P1: A physical (structural or functional) explanation explains a fact only by showing that it follows from facts about physical structure and function. P2: Facts about experience, such as why a physical state feels the way it does or feels like anything at all, do not follow from facts about physical structure and function. C: So physical explanation cannot fully explain facts about experience: there is an explanatory gap.
material: **Argument B (machines).** P1: Programs are formal: defined by the manipulation of symbols according to their form. P2: Minds have mental contents. P3: Syntax by itself is neither constitutive of nor sufficient for semantics. C: So programs by themselves are neither constitutive of nor sufficient for minds.
material: **Argument C (mental causation).** P1: A mental event M causes a physical event P*. P2: P* has a sufficient physical cause P, the realizer of M. P3: M is distinct from P. P4: An effect with a sufficient cause has no other distinct cause unless it is overdetermined. P5: P* is not overdetermined by M and P. C: So M is identical to P, or M does not cause P*.

@review
### Key ideas, week by week

1. **Dualism.** Descartes's conceivability argument (clear and distinct conception, then possibility, then distinctness) and his divisibility argument (Leibniz's Law). Objections: Arnauld's incomplete-knowledge point and Elisabeth's interaction problem.
2. **Behaviorism.** Methodological versus logical; dispositions; Ryle's category mistake; the circularity objection and the super-Spartans.
3. **Identity theory.** Type versus token identity; contingent identities; Smart's topic-neutral analysis; Kripke's modal argument.
4. **Functionalism.** Role versus realizer; multiple realizability; Block's absent-qualia objection.
5. **Turing test.** The imitation game; sufficiency versus evidence; Blockhead.
6. **Chinese room.** Strong AI; the syntax-semantics argument; systems, robot and brain-simulator replies.
7. **Intentionality.** Brentano; opacity; narrow and wide content; Twin Earth; the disjunction problem.
8. **Mental causation.** Closure, exclusion, Kim's argument, and the responses.
9. **Consciousness.** Phenomenal versus access; easy and hard problems; the explanatory gap; type-A and type-B.
10. **Knowledge and zombie arguments.** Which premise each reply denies.
11. **Radical views.** Eliminativism, illusionism, panpsychism and their objections.
12. **Personal identity.** Locke, Reid, fission, animalism.

### Key distinctions

- Epistemic gap versus ontological gap.
- Syntax versus semantics; programs versus causal powers.
- Narrow versus wide content.
- Reduction, overdetermination and epiphenomenalism as the options in the exclusion argument.
- Conceivability versus possibility.

### Self-check

- Can you state each argument in standard form and name its weakest premise?
- Can you say which premise each reply rejects?
- Can you state each view without confusing it with a neighbor?
- Can you report the state of the field without treating survey figures as evidence?

### Common errors

- Confusing validity with truth of the premises.
- Saying Searle denies that machines can think.
- Treating the explanatory gap as a proof of dualism.
- Treating the claim that no consensus exists as a claim that all views are equally good.

choice*: From what is conceivable, or what is not explained, to what is metaphysically the case
choice: From a mental event to its physical realizer
choice: From a program to its hardware
choice: From a memory to a person
hint: Type-B materialists reject this move.
solution: Both arguments need a bridge from an epistemic or conceptual claim to a metaphysical one. Type-B materialists reject that bridge by appeal to a posteriori identities.

@exam self
prompt: Pick two of the following arguments: Descartes's conceivability argument, the multiple realizability argument against type identity, the Chinese room, and the exclusion argument. State each in standard form and explain what they have in common in how they use a possibility or a principle about causation.
hint: Compare the form of the premises and the role of the key bridging principle.
solution: Take the conceivability argument (P1: I can clearly and distinctly conceive of myself without a body. P2: What can be so conceived could exist so. P3: If one thing could exist without another, they are not identical. C: Mind is not body) and multiple realizability (P1: If pain were identical to a single physical type, every possible creature in pain would be in it. P2: Some possible creatures in pain, with different physical makeup, would not be in it. C: Pain is not identical to a single physical type). Both move from a claim about what is possible (separate existence, or realization in different physical systems) to a conclusion about what things are. Each is valid, and each depends on a possibility premise that is contested: Arnauld questions whether the conception is adequate, and critics of multiple realizability question how wide the range of realizers is. The shared lesson is that such arguments are only as strong as the case for their possibility premise.
rubric: States two arguments accurately in standard form.
rubric: Identifies a genuine shared feature.
rubric: Notes the contested premise in each.
