@article
Ana decides to raise her hand, and her arm goes up. We take it that her decision caused her arm to rise. But her arm's rising also has a complete physical explanation in terms of neural signals and muscle contractions. If the physical story is complete, what is left for the decision to do? This is the problem of **mental causation**.

### Three principles

- **Mental causation**: mental events cause physical events (and the reverse). Pain makes us wince; decisions move arms.
- **Causal closure of the physical**: every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause. Physical effects do not need a nonphysical cause.
- **Causal exclusion**: an event does not have two distinct sufficient causes at once, unless it is a case of overdetermination, as when two bullets simultaneously strike and each is enough to kill.

Add **nonreductive physicalism**: mental properties depend on physical properties (they supervene on or are realized by them) but are not identical to them. This is the view that multiple realizability seems to favor. Jaegwon Kim argued that nonreductive physicalism is unstable.

### The exclusion argument

Jaegwon Kim's argument (developed in work such as *Mind in a Physical World*, 1998) can be put as follows. Let $M$ be a mental event that causes a physical event $P^*$, and let $P$ be the physical event that realizes $M$.

| P1 | $M$ causes the physical event $P^*$ (mental causation). |
| P2 | $P^*$ has a sufficient physical cause $P$ (causal closure), and $P$ is the physical realizer of $M$. |
| P3 | $M$ is distinct from $P$ (nonreductive physicalism). |
| P4 | If an effect has a sufficient cause, it has no other distinct cause, unless it is overdetermined (exclusion). |
| P5 | The effect $P^*$ is not overdetermined by $M$ and $P$ (it would have to be so in every case of mental causation, which seems implausible). |
| C | So $M$ is not distinct from $P$ (reduction), or $M$ is not a cause of $P^*$ (epiphenomenalism). |

The argument is valid given its premises. Kim concluded that mental properties must be reduced to physical ones if they are to be causally efficacious, or else be epiphenomenal. For multiply realizable properties, he suggested a **local reduction**: the property of being in pain-in-humans is a physical property, though pain in general is not a genuine causal kind.

### Responses

**Accept overdetermination.** Deny P5: mental and physical events are both causes, but this is not a coincidence, since the mental event depends on the physical one. Critics say that the dependence makes this unlike ordinary overdetermination, and that it would be a systematic double causation.

**Deny causal closure.** Reject P2: the physical world is not causally closed, and mental events add causal contributions. This is the interactionist dualist's response. It faces the question of how a nonphysical cause fits with physical conservation laws; defenders argue that conservation laws are compatible with mental interventions in some ways, a controversial matter.

**Deny exclusion.** Reject P4: causes at different levels do not compete. The **proportionality** view of Stephen Yablo says that the mental property may be the better cause because it is proportional to the effect: it is neither too specific nor too general. Counterfactual theories of causation say that if the mental event had not occurred, the effect would not have occurred, which suffices for causation. Critics say that counterfactual dependence is cheap and does not establish production.

**Accept reduction.** Accept that $M$ is identical to $P$: this is reductive physicalism (week 3), with the cost of multiple realizability.

**Accept epiphenomenalism.** Say that mental events have no effects. This is the view that mental events are by-products of physical processes, much as a steam whistle accompanies an engine without driving it. Most philosophers find it hard to accept because it seems to conflict with the sense that our decisions matter.

Donald Davidson's **anomalous monism** (1970) holds that each mental event is identical to a physical event (token identity) but that there are no strict psychophysical laws. Critics, including Kim, argued that on this view mental events cause only in virtue of their physical properties, so mental properties are inert.

### Where things stand

There is no consensus. The argument shows that the claim that mental properties are both distinct from physical properties and causally relevant requires a choice among its horns.

@example
title: Mapping the horns
problem: Ana says: "My decision to raise my hand caused my hand to rise, but the neural events fully explained the rising." Which premise of Kim's argument does each of these answers reject? (a) "Both are causes; it is overdetermination." (b) "The physical world is not closed." (c) "Causes at different levels do not compete."
step: (a) Overdetermination accepted: this rejects P5, the claim that the effect is not overdetermined by the mental and physical causes.
step: (b) Denying closure rejects the claim in P2 that the effect has a sufficient physical cause.
step: (c) Denying that levels compete rejects P4, the exclusion principle.
answer: (a) P5; (b) P2; (c) P4.

@example
title: Why the conclusion is a dilemma
problem: Explain why Kim's conclusion is a choice between reduction and epiphenomenalism.
step: Suppose $M$ is distinct from $P$ and $P$ already suffices for $P^*$.
step: If $M$ also caused $P^*$, there would be two sufficient causes, which exclusion forbids unless overdetermination, which the argument rules out.
step: So either $M$ is not distinct from $P$, which is reduction, or $M$ does not cause $P^*$, which is epiphenomenalism.
step: The nonreductive physicalist must reject a premise to avoid both.
answer: Given the premises, distinctness and efficacy cannot both hold, so one must go.

@mistakes
- Treating causal closure as the claim that there are no nonphysical things. It says that physical effects have sufficient physical causes.
- Confusing overdetermination with causal exclusion. Overdetermination is the exception that exclusion allows.
- Saying epiphenomenalism denies that mental events exist. It denies that they have effects.
- Taking Kim's argument to prove reductive physicalism. It forces a choice, and the responses reject premises.

@practice self
prompt: In four or five sentences, explain Yablo's proportionality response to exclusion.
hint: Think of what makes a cause neither too specific nor too general.
solution: Yablo's response says that causes can be better or worse relative to an effect, and that a cause is proportional when it is neither too specific nor too general for the effect. A mental property may be a proportional cause of an action, while a very specific physical realizer includes details that do not matter to the effect. So the mental property competes successfully with, or at least does not compete with, the realizer, and exclusion does not remove it. An objection is that proportionality is a condition on explanation or on selecting causes, not on causal production, so it leaves the production by the physical realizer untouched.
