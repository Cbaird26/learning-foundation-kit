@article
If you want to know whether Ana believes that it will rain, what do you check? One answer: you check what she does. She takes an umbrella, cancels the picnic, says "bring a coat." Behaviorism takes this answer very seriously. It says that mental words are about behavior, so that the mind is not a hidden inner theater but something shown in what people do and would do.

### Two behaviorisms

**Methodological behaviorism**, associated with psychologists such as John B. Watson, is a thesis about scientific method: psychology should study observable behavior and stimuli, not introspective reports of inner states. It is silent on whether inner states exist, so it is compatible with dualism.

**Logical (or analytical) behaviorism** is a thesis in philosophy about the meaning of mental language. Carl Hempel, in the 1930s, argued in the tradition of the logical positivists that psychological statements can be translated into statements about behavior. Gilbert Ryle, in *The Concept of Mind* (1949), gave the best-known philosophical version. A sentence such as "Ana believes that it will rain" does not describe a private inner event. It says that Ana is **disposed** to behave in certain ways: to take an umbrella, to say "it will rain" when asked, and so on.

A **disposition** is a tendency that may never be manifested: a glass is fragile even when it is not breaking. So a behaviorist can say that someone in pain who stays silent still has pain, because the disposition to wince, complain and avoid the cause is present.

### Ryle's category mistake

Ryle's main target was the picture he called the "ghost in the machine": the Cartesian view that each person has a body and, in addition, a private inner mind. He said the picture rests on a **category mistake**: treating something as belonging to a category it does not belong to. A visitor who sees every college building and then asks "but where is the university?" has misunderstood how "university" works. Ryle held that "mind" is similar: it is not another thing alongside the body but a way of describing the intelligent behavior of people, and treating it as an additional object produces the problem of how the two interact. Whether Ryle's logical behaviorism is exactly the view just described is disputed among interpreters, but the textbook version is a good target for the objections below.

### The circularity objection

Philosophers, including Roderick Chisholm, pressed an objection to the translation project.

| P1 | If logical behaviorism is true, each mental-state sentence can be translated into a sentence using only behavioral and dispositional vocabulary. |
| P2 | Whether a belief disposes someone to act in a given way depends on what else the person believes and wants. |
| C1 | So any adequate translation of a belief sentence must mention other mental states. |
| P3 | A translation that mentions mental states is not a translation into purely behavioral and dispositional vocabulary. |
| C | So mental-state sentences cannot be translated into purely behavioral vocabulary, and logical behaviorism is false. |

Ana's belief that it will rain leads her to take an umbrella only if she wants to stay dry and believes the umbrella will keep her dry. If she wants to get wet, the same belief produces different behavior. So the analysis of one belief needs further beliefs and desires, whose analysis needs further beliefs and desires, and so on. The behaviorist can reply that the analysis may be a vast holistic web in which all mental states are analyzed together, but it is hard to see that the web ever reaches purely behavioral terms.

### The super-Spartans

Hilary Putnam's "Brains and Behavior" (1963) describes a community of **super-Spartans** who feel pain but have trained themselves never to show it: no winces, no groans, no disposition to say "ouch," even when asked. If this is possible, pain does not consist in dispositions to behave. Putnam paired this with **perfect actors**, beings who show every sign of pain without feeling any. The behaviorist can reply that the super-Spartans are not really possible, or that they retain some subtle disposition (for example, to show pain under hypnosis), but the reply looks like a promissory note.

### Other problems

Behaviorism seems to leave out the **first-person** point of view: you know you are in pain without observing your own behavior. And it has trouble with **qualia**, how experience feels. Behaviorism's lasting contribution is the emphasis on public evidence and on dispositional analysis, which functionalism (week 4) keeps while allowing inner states.

@example
title: Analyzing a mental sentence
problem: Give a logical behaviorist analysis of "Leo is thirsty" and then show how the circularity objection applies.
step: A first analysis: Leo is disposed to seek water, to say "I'd like a drink" when asked, and to drink eagerly when water is available.
step: Test the analysis. If Leo is thirsty but believes the only water is poisoned, he will not drink; if he is thirsty but wants to complete a fast, he will refuse. The disposition depends on his beliefs and desires.
step: So the analysis must be revised: Leo is disposed to drink, provided he does not believe the water is poisoned, does not want to fast, and so on. The added clauses mention beliefs and desires.
step: This is the circularity objection: the analysis of one mental state uses others, so the project of translating mental talk into purely behavioral talk seems not to succeed.
answer: Any behavioral analysis of thirst needs clauses about other beliefs and desires, which is why Chisholm-style circularity is taken to be a serious objection.

@example
title: Super-Spartans and what they show
problem: Say what the super-Spartan case is supposed to show, and what a behaviorist might reply.
step: The super-Spartans feel pain but have no disposition to behave as pained people do. If they are possible, then having pain is not the same as having these dispositions.
step: The behaviorist can first deny that the case is possible: perhaps feeling pain without any pain behavior, even a disposition to show it in some circumstance, is not a coherent description.
step: A second reply: the case is possible but not a counterexample to a behaviorism that allows the disposition to appear under some conditions, such as extreme duress.
step: A critic answers that these replies make behaviorism hard to test and look ad hoc.
answer: The case is meant to show that pain is not just a disposition to behave. Replies deny possibility or retreat to weak dispositions, with the cost of seeming ad hoc.

@mistakes
- Treating methodological behaviorism as the claim that there are no inner states. It is a claim about what psychology should study.
- Thinking a disposition requires actual behavior. A disposition may never be manifested.
- Describing Ryle as a skeptic about the existence of mental life. He rejected the Cartesian picture of what mental life is.
- Treating the super-Spartan objection as showing that behavior is irrelevant to the mind. It shows only that mental states are not identical to behavioral dispositions.

@quiz self
prompt: Reconstruct the circularity objection to logical behaviorism in standard form and evaluate the reply that the analysis can be holistic.
hint: Include the premise that behavioral effects of a belief depend on other mental states.
solution: P1: If logical behaviorism is true, mental-state sentences can be translated into purely behavioral and dispositional sentences. P2: Whether a mental state disposes someone to behave in a given way depends on the person's other mental states. C1: So any translation of a mental-state sentence mentions other mental states. P3: A translation that mentions mental states is not purely behavioral. C: So logical behaviorism is false. The holistic reply says that all mental states are analyzed together as one web of dispositions, so no single translation is needed. This avoids a regress but not the problem: the whole web still has to be expressed in behavioral terms, and in the dependencies between states it is hard to see where purely behavioral vocabulary enters. The reply is better seen as a step toward functionalism, which allows the web to be defined by causal relations among inner states.
