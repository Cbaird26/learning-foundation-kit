@article
Neuroscientists find that when Ana reports a sharp pain, a certain pattern of neural activity occurs. What is the relation between the pain and the pattern? Dualists say they are distinct things that go together. The **identity theory** says something bolder: the pain just is the neural activity, in the way that lightning just is an electrical discharge.

### The theory

The identity theory was developed in the 1950s by U. T. Place and J. J. C. Smart, with Herbert Feigl as an important early voice. Its core is the thesis that **mental states and processes are identical to states and processes in the brain**. Its most common form is **type identity**: each type of mental state (being in pain) is the same type as a type of physical state (a pattern of neural firing). Contrast **token identity**, which says only that each particular mental event (this pain of Ana's, now) is a particular physical event, without requiring that pains of one type always be the same type of physical event.

Place and Smart argued that the identity is a **contingent, empirically discovered identity**, like "lightning is an electrical discharge" or "water is H$_2$O". These identities are not known by analyzing the meanings of the words; they were found by science. In this respect the theory differs from logical behaviorism, which claimed that mental words are definable in behavioral terms. The identity theorist agrees that the meaning of "pain" does not mention neurons; what is discovered is that the thing "pain" picks out is a brain process.

Smart added that we can analyze reports of experience in **topic-neutral** terms: "I am having an afterimage" says that something is going on in me that is like what goes on when I see an orange, without saying what kind of thing that is. Neutral on whether it is mental or physical, the report leaves it to science to say.

### Identity versus correlation

A correlation is weaker than an identity. Dualists can accept that neural states and mental states are perfectly correlated and still insist that they are distinct, as two things can regularly accompany each other. The identity theorist says that the simplest explanation of the correlation is identity: positing two things where one will do multiplies entities. This is an argument from **parsimony**, and it is the main positive argument for the theory.

### Objections

**The Leibniz's Law objection.** An afterimage may be orange and located in the visual field, while a brain process is neither orange nor located in the visual field. Leibniz's Law says that identical things share all properties. Smart replied that what is orange is the thing that the experience represents, or what the report is about, and that the report of an orange afterimage is a report of an experience that is like seeing something orange.

**Kripke's modal argument.** In *Naming and Necessity* (1972), Saul Kripke argued that the identity theory faces a problem about necessity. If both terms of an identity are **rigid designators**, which pick out the same thing in every possible world where it exists, then a true identity statement between them is necessarily true.

| P1 | "Pain" and "$B$" are both rigid designators. |
| P2 | If "pain" and "$B$" are both rigid designators and pain is identical to $B$, then pain is necessarily identical to $B$. |
| P3 | It is not necessary that pain is identical to $B$: it seems possible for there to be pain without $B$, and $B$ without pain. |
| C | So pain is not identical to $B$. |

Kripke explains why the identity of heat with molecular motion seems contingent although it is necessary: we pick out heat by the sensation it produces, and we mistake the possibility of a world where something produces the heat sensation without being molecular motion for a world where heat is not molecular motion. For pain, the proposed explanation fails, he argued, because what picks out pain is the feeling itself: anything with that feeling is pain, so the feeling cannot occur without pain. Replies: some identity theorists deny that mental terms are rigid or defend the claim that the apparent possibility of pain without $B$ rests on a different illusion; others accept the argument against type identity but keep a token version.

**Multiple realizability.** Putnam argued that pain could be realized in physically different ways in different creatures, so pain cannot be identical to a single type of brain state. This objection is taken up in week 4.

### Where things stand

The identity theory in its simplest form is no longer a leading position, but its core idea survives in weaker forms: **local** identities (human pain is a human brain state) and the thought that mind depends on brain. The modal argument also lives on in the zombie argument of week 10.

@example
title: Spotting contingent identity
problem: The identity theorist claims "pain is the firing of C-fibers" is like "lightning is an electrical discharge". In what respects is the comparison supposed to help?
step: The lightning identity was discovered by empirical science, and no one could find it by analyzing the word "lightning".
step: The identity does not make "lightning" mean "electrical discharge". Someone could understand "lightning" perfectly well without knowing the identity.
step: Likewise, the identity theorist says someone can understand "pain" without knowing it is a brain process.
step: So the comparison is meant to show that the identity theory is not refuted merely because the words differ in meaning or because people did not know the identity.
answer: The comparison shows that an identity can be a posteriori and can hold even though the terms differ in meaning, which answers the objection that pain and brain states are obviously different things.

@example
title: Which premise does the identity theorist reject?
problem: Kripke's argument says: P1 "pain" and "$B$" are both rigid designators; P2 if they are both rigid and pain is identical to $B$, the identity is necessary; P3 the identity is not necessary; C pain is not identical to $B$. What are the options for the identity theorist?
step: The argument is valid: if pain were identical to $B$, P1 and P2 would make the identity necessary, which contradicts P3.
step: Option one: reject P1 by arguing that one of the terms is not rigid. For instance, someone might say "pain" refers to whatever plays a certain role, which differs across creatures.
step: Option two: reject P3 by explaining why the possibility of pain without $B$ is merely apparent, as in the case of heat.
step: Option three: give up type identity and keep a weaker thesis, such as token identity.
answer: The identity theorist can reject P1, reject P3 or weaken the identity thesis, and each option has costs.

@mistakes
- Treating the identity theory as the claim that mental words mean the same as brain words. It is a claim about what the words pick out.
- Confusing identity with correlation: dualists can accept perfect correlation.
- Treating type identity and token identity as the same thesis: token identity does not require a single physical type for each mental type.
- Saying that Kripke proved that the mind is not the brain. His argument is a challenge that the identity theorist can contest by rejecting a premise.

@practice self
prompt: Explain in four or five sentences why parsimony is the main positive argument for the identity theory, and state one objection.
hint: Say what the dualist can accept and why that leaves the identity theorist an argument.
solution: Dualists accept that mental and neural states are correlated. The identity theorist says that identity explains the correlation without positing two things that happen to go together, and that when the two stories explain the same facts we should prefer the simpler one. One objection is that parsimony is a weak consideration if identity conflicts with other claims: for instance, Kripke's modal argument says identity would make the connection necessary, but pain without the brain state seems possible. Another is that the simplicity of a theory does not show it is true unless the identities can survive objections such as multiple realizability.
