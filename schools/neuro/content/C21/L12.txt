@article
Ana wakes tomorrow morning. What makes the person in that bed Ana? The same body? The same brain? Her memories and character? Something else? This is the problem of **personal identity**: what makes a person at one time the same person as a person at another? It is related to the mind-body problem, since the answer depends on what a person is.

### Locke and the memory criterion

John Locke, in the *Essay* (Book II, chapter 27, added in the second edition), distinguished a person from a human being or a substance. A **person** is a thinking intelligent being that can consider itself as itself in different times and places. He held that personal identity consists in **consciousness** extending backward to past actions, which later philosophers have interpreted as a **memory criterion**: a person at a later time is the same as a person earlier if the later one can remember the earlier one's experiences. This is a version of the **psychological view**.

Thomas Reid objected, in *Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man* (1785), with the case of a brave officer who was flogged as a boy, took a standard in battle, and became a general. The general remembers the standard but not the flogging, and the officer remembered the flogging. If identity is memory, then the general is the officer, the officer is the boy, but the general is not the boy: a violation of the transitivity of identity. Defenders revise the criterion to **overlapping chains** of memory, or to broader psychological continuity. Joseph Butler's objection says that memory presupposes identity, so it cannot constitute it: that is, you can remember only your own experiences. A reply is to define **quasi-memory**, which does not presuppose identity.

### The main views

- **Psychological continuity views**: you are the same person as anyone to whom you are connected by overlapping chains of memory, intention, character and belief, with the right kind of cause.
- **Biological (animalist) views** (Eric Olson, Paul Snowdon): you are a human animal, and you persist as long as the animal does. On this view, if your brain were transplanted into another body, the animal would remain behind and you would too.
- **Further-fact views**: personal identity involves something over and above physical or psychological continuity, such as a soul or a simple subject.

In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey about 44% accepted or leaned toward the psychological view, 19% the biological view and 15% the further-fact view. These figures do not show which is correct.

### Fission

Derek Parfit, in *Reasons and Persons* (1984), used cases of **fission**: suppose that Ana's brain is divided and each half is transplanted into a new body; each resulting person is psychologically continuous with Ana.

| P1 | Ana is psychologically continuous with both resulting people, Left and Right, and equally so. |
| P2 | Left and Right are two distinct people. |
| P3 | Identity is transitive: if Ana is Left and Ana is Right, then Left is Right. |
| P4 | On the psychological view, if Ana is identical to one of them and not the other, her psychological relation to that one must differ from her relation to the other. |
| C | So, on the psychological view, Ana is identical to neither Left nor Right. |

Parfit drew the further conclusion that identity is not what matters in survival: what matters is psychological continuity, which in fission is preserved in both. Other responses say that Ana is identical to both (as a branching person, on a view that treats people as four-dimensional worms), or that one is Ana by a closest-continuer rule, or that the case is impossible. Parfit's view that identity is not what matters is a radical claim that remains controversial.

### The no-self tradition

David Hume (*Treatise* 1.4.6) reported that when he looked inward he found only particular perceptions and no self. This has been read as a **bundle view** of the self. Buddhist traditions develop their own accounts of non-self with different arguments.

### The hard problem essay

The assignment for this block is a three-page essay on the hard problem. A good essay states the problem precisely, picks one response, reconstructs the strongest objection and replies.

@example
title: Reid's objection step by step
problem: Show how Reid's brave officer case gives a problem for the simple memory criterion.
step: The boy is flogged. The officer remembers the flogging. The general remembers taking the standard, but not the flogging.
step: By the memory criterion, the officer is the boy, and the general is the officer.
step: By transitivity of identity, the general is the boy.
step: But the general cannot remember the flogging, so the criterion says he is not the boy. Contradiction.
answer: The simple criterion conflicts with transitivity; the repair is overlapping chains of memory.

@example
title: Outlining an essay on the hard problem
problem: Draft an outline for a three-page essay defending a response to the hard problem.

step: State the problem: Chalmers's easy problems and the hard problem, with the explanatory gap argument.
step: Present the strongest opposing argument: the conceivability argument, or the knowledge argument.
step: Reply: identify the premise you deny and give a reason, for example the phenomenal concept strategy.
step: Address the best objection to your reply, and say what it costs.
answer: The outline is thesis, problem, strongest opposing argument, reply and best objection with a cost.

@mistakes
- Saying that Locke thought personal identity was the same as bodily identity.
- Taking the fission argument to show that personal identity is impossible. It poses a problem for particular theories.
- Confusing the psychological view with the further-fact view.
- Treating survey figures as evidence for which view is true.
