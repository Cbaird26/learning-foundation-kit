@article
A neuron's input-output function is not fixed. A cup of coffee, a threatening noise or the onset of sleep changes how the same neurons respond to the same synapses, within seconds or minutes. These changes are made largely by **neuromodulators** such as acetylcholine, dopamine, serotonin and noradrenaline, which act mainly on receptors that are not channels.

### G-protein signaling

A **metabotropic receptor**, usually a G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR), activates a heterotrimeric **G protein** inside the cell. The activated G protein splits into an $\alpha$ subunit and a $\beta\gamma$ complex, and these act on effectors:

- $G_s$ activates adenylyl cyclase, which makes the second messenger **cAMP** from ATP; cAMP activates protein kinase A (PKA), which phosphorylates channels and other proteins.
- $G_{i/o}$ inhibits adenylyl cyclase, and its $\beta\gamma$ subunits can open G-protein-gated inwardly rectifying K+ (GIRK) channels directly, hyperpolarizing the cell.
- $G_q$ activates phospholipase C, producing IP$_3$ (which releases Ca2+ from internal stores) and diacylglycerol (which activates protein kinase C).

A well-studied example is the muscarinic acetylcholine receptor: activating $G_q$-coupled muscarinic receptors closes a K+ current called the **M current** (carried by Kv7 channels), which depolarizes neurons and makes them more excitable.

Compared with ionotropic synapses, metabotropic signaling is slower (hundreds of milliseconds to minutes), diffuse and amplifying: one active receptor activates many G proteins, and each enzyme makes many messenger molecules. Messengers are removed by degradation, so their concentration is a balance of production and decay.

### A two-stage cascade

A simple model has the fraction of active G protein $G$ relax toward the ligand-set level $L$ (between 0 and 1), and a messenger concentration $c$ (µM) produced in proportion to $G$ and degraded at rate $k_d$:

$$\tau_G\frac{dG}{dt} = L - G, \qquad \frac{dc}{dt} = k_s G - k_d\,c, \qquad c_\infty = \frac{k_s L}{k_d}.$$

With $\tau_G = 0.5$ s, $k_s = 2\ \mu\text{M/s}$ and $k_d = 0.5\ \text{s}^{-1}$ (illustrative values), a full ligand step ($L = 1$) gives $c_\infty = 4\ \mu\text{M}$. Euler steps of 0.01 s give:

| time (s) | 1 | 2 | 5 | 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $G$ | 0.867 | 0.982 | 1.000 | 1.000 |
| $c$ (µM) | 0.96 | 2.08 | 3.57 | 3.97 |

The messenger lags the receptor by seconds, and the lag is set mainly by the slow degradation, $1/k_d = 2$ s. The steady-state response to a ligand concentration $[A]$ follows the Hill equation, $R = [A]^h/(\text{EC}_{50}^h + [A]^h)$; at the $\text{EC}_{50}$ the response is half-maximal.

### Modulating a conductance

How does closing the M current change integration? Treat it as an extra leak conductance $g_M$ in parallel with $g_L$. The input resistance is $R = 1/(g_L + g_M)$ and the time constant $\tau = C/(g_L + g_M)$. With $g_L = 10$ nS, $g_M = 5$ nS and $C = 200$ pF, $R = 66.7$ MΩ and $\tau = 13.3$ ms. If a modulator closes the M current ($g_M \to 0$), $R = 100$ MΩ and $\tau = 20$ ms: a 100 pA current now produces 10 mV instead of 6.7 mV, and the EPSP lasts longer, widening the window for temporal summation (week 9).

### Summation in time and space

Put the two together. A synapse at distance $x$ from the soma produces a local EPSP that attenuates along the dendrite, roughly by $e^{-x/\lambda}$ in steady state (week 4), and then decays with the membrane time constant. A simple first-order model sums the filtered contributions: the soma sees $\sum_i a_i\,e^{-x_i/\lambda}\,s(t - t_i)$, where $a_i$ is the local EPSP amplitude and $s$ is the normalized EPSP shape of week 9. It captures two facts: nearby inputs count more than distant ones, and inputs count together only if they arrive within about $\tau_m$ of each other. It ignores dendritic delay and the broadening of distant EPSPs, active dendritic channels and conductance effects, all of which matter in real neurons.

### In code

```python
def cascade(L, t_end, dt=0.01, tau_g=0.5, k_s=2.0, k_d=0.5):
    """Euler simulation; returns lists of G and c (uM) at each step."""
    G = c = 0.0
    Gs, cs = [G], [c]
    for _ in range(int(round(t_end / dt))):
        G += dt * (L - G) / tau_g
        c += dt * (k_s * G - k_d * c)
        Gs.append(G)
        cs.append(c)
    return Gs, cs

Gs, cs = cascade(1.0, 5.0)
print(round(Gs[-1], 3), round(cs[-1], 2))
```

This prints `1.0 3.57`: the G-protein fraction has saturated by 5 s while the messenger is still approaching its steady state of 4 µM.

@example
title: A dose–response midpoint
problem: A receptor gives normalized responses $0.20, 0.45, 0.80$ at agonist concentrations $1, 3, 10$ µM. Estimate the EC$_{50}$ by linear interpolation of the response against $\log_{10}$ concentration.
step: The response crosses 0.5 between 3 µM (0.45) and 10 µM (0.80).
step: The fraction of the way is $(0.5 - 0.45)/(0.80 - 0.45) = 0.143$.
step: In log units: $\log_{10} 3 = 0.477$ and $\log_{10} 10 = 1$, so the crossing is at $0.477 + 0.143 \times 0.523 = 0.552$.
step: $\text{EC}_{50} = 10^{0.552} = 3.57$ µM.
answer: About $3.6$ µM.

@mistakes
- Confusing a second messenger with the neurotransmitter: the transmitter binds outside; cAMP, IP$_3$ and Ca2+ are made inside by enzymes.
- Calling all G-protein effects excitatory: $G_s$ and $G_q$ often excite, but $G_{i/o}$ opens GIRK channels and hyperpolarizes; the effect depends on the targets in that cell.
- Saying a slower cascade must be a weaker one: amplification gives metabotropic signals large effects even though they start after a delay.
- Averaging concentrations on a linear axis when finding an EC$_{50}$: dose–response curves are close to symmetric on a logarithmic axis.

model:
```python
import math


def reaches_threshold(inputs, lam, tau_m, tau_s, thr):
    """First grid time (ms) at which the summed soma EPSPs reach thr, or None."""
    t_peak = tau_m * tau_s / (tau_m - tau_s) * math.log(tau_m / tau_s)
    peak = math.exp(-t_peak / tau_m) - math.exp(-t_peak / tau_s)
    for k in range(601):
        t = 0.1 * k
        total = 0.0
        for t_i, x_i, a_i in inputs:
            dt = t - t_i
            if dt > 0:
                shape = (math.exp(-dt / tau_m) - math.exp(-dt / tau_s)) / peak
                total += a_i * math.exp(-x_i / lam) * shape
        if total >= thr:
            return t
    return None
```
hint: At each grid time add up the attenuated, time-shifted EPSP of every input already arrived; return as soon as the total reaches the threshold.
solution: Spatial attenuation multiplies each amplitude by $e^{-x/\lambda}$ and temporal filtering spreads it in time, so three 4 mV inputs reach 5 mV at the soma only when they arrive close together and are not too far (the fourth and fifth tests): spaced 30 ms apart or on a dendrite with a short length constant they never sum to threshold.

model:
```python
import math


def ec50_from_curve(concs, responses):
    """EC50 by interpolation of the 0.5 crossing on a log10 concentration axis."""
    for k in range(len(concs) - 1):
        if responses[k] < 0.5 <= responses[k + 1]:
            frac = (0.5 - responses[k]) / (responses[k + 1] - responses[k])
            lo, hi = math.log10(concs[k]), math.log10(concs[k + 1])
            return 10 ** (lo + frac * (hi - lo))
    return None
```
hint: Find the pair that brackets 0.5, compute the fraction of the way in response, and apply that fraction to the logarithms of the two concentrations.
solution: Interpolating in log concentration respects the roughly symmetric shape of a dose–response curve; interpolating the concentrations directly would be biased toward the higher value. A curve that starts above 0.5 or never reaches it has no bracketing pair, so the result is `None` (the last two tests). The estimate 4.64 µM for a true EC$_{50}$ of 5 µM shows the interpolation error with coarse sampling.
