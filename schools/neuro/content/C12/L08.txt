@article
Record from a muscle fiber near its nerve ending and, even with no stimulation, you see tiny, randomly timed depolarizations of a fraction of a millivolt. Stimulate the nerve and the response is a much larger depolarization whose size, in a low-calcium bath, jumps among 0, 1, 2, ... times the size of the tiny events. Fatt and Katz (1952) described the small events and del Castillo and Katz (1954) showed that transmitter is released in discrete packets, **quanta**, each probably one synaptic vesicle.

### From spike to postsynaptic current

At a chemical synapse the presynaptic spike opens voltage-gated Ca2+ channels, and Ca2+ entering the terminal triggers fusion of vesicles with the membrane (through the SNARE protein machinery and calcium sensors such as synaptotagmin). Transmitter diffuses across the cleft (tens of nanometers) in a fraction of a millisecond and binds postsynaptic receptors. **Ionotropic receptors** are themselves channels: AMPA and NMDA receptors for glutamate, GABA$_\text{A}$ and glycine receptors (Cl- channels), and the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor. **Metabotropic receptors** act through G proteins (week 10). The postsynaptic current is $I_\text{syn} = g_\text{syn}(t)\,(V - E_\text{rev})$: for cation-permeable glutamate receptors $E_\text{rev}\approx 0$ mV, so at $-70$ mV the current is inward (excitatory); for GABA$_\text{A}$ receptors $E_\text{rev}$ is near $E_\text{Cl}$. Binding is graded by concentration: with $h$ binding sites the occupied fraction is $c^h/(\text{EC}_{50}^h + c^h)$ (the Hill equation).

Release depends steeply on calcium: the number of quanta released scales roughly as $[\text{Ca}]^{\,3\text{ to }4}$ in many synapses, so small changes in Ca2+ entry change release a lot.

### The binomial model

Suppose a synapse has $n$ independent release sites, each releasing one quantum with probability $p$ per spike, and each quantum produces a postsynaptic response of size $q$. The number released $k$ follows a binomial distribution:

$$P(k) = \binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}.$$

The mean response is $\mu = npq$ and the variance is $\sigma^2 = np(1-p)q^2$, so the squared coefficient of variation is $\text{CV}^2 = (1-p)/(np)$. The probability of a **failure** (no release) is $(1-p)^n$. For $n = 4$ and $p = 0.3$ the probabilities of $k = 0$ to 4 quanta are 0.2401, 0.4116, 0.2646, 0.0756 and 0.0081. When $p$ is small and $n$ large, $P(k)$ approaches the **Poisson** distribution with mean $m = np$ (the **quantal content**): $P(k) = e^{-m}m^k/k!$.

### Estimating the parameters

- **Method of failures.** For a Poisson process, $P(0) = e^{-m}$, so $m = \ln(N/N_0)$, where $N$ is the number of trials and $N_0$ the number of failures. The quantal size is $q = \bar{x}/m$, where $\bar{x}$ is the mean response over all trials.
- **Variance–mean analysis.** Eliminating $p$ from the binomial moments gives $\sigma^2 = q\mu - \mu^2/n$, a parabola. If you vary $p$ (for example with the extracellular Ca2+ concentration) and measure $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$, the ratio $\sigma^2/\mu = q - \mu/n$ is a straight line in $\mu$ with intercept $q$ and slope $-1/n$.

Quantal analysis distinguishes **presynaptic** from **postsynaptic** changes. If $q$ changes (fewer receptors, a receptor antagonist), the mean changes in proportion but the failure rate and CV do not, because neither depends on $q$. If $p$ changes (a different Ca2+ level, a presynaptic modulator), the failure rate and the CV change along with the mean. The model has limits: sites differ in $p$, quantal size varies, and the number of sites can be hard to define, so the estimates are model-dependent.

### In code

```python
import math


def release_pmf(n, p):
    """Probabilities of releasing 0..n quanta from n independent sites."""
    return [math.comb(n, k) * p ** k * (1 - p) ** (n - k) for k in range(n + 1)]

pmf = release_pmf(4, 0.3)
print([round(x, 4) for x in pmf])
print(round(sum(k * x for k, x in enumerate(pmf)), 3))
```

This prints `[0.2401, 0.4116, 0.2646, 0.0756, 0.0081]` and `1.2`, the mean number of quanta $np$.

@example
title: Method of failures
problem: A synapse is stimulated 120 times in low calcium. There are 18 failures, and the mean response over all 120 trials is $0.76$ mV. Estimate the quantal content and the quantal size.
step: Poisson failure probability: $18/120 = 0.15 = e^{-m}$.
step: Quantal content $m = \ln(120/18) = \ln 6.667 = 1.897$.
step: Mean response $= mq$, so $q = 0.76/1.897 = 0.40$ mV.
answer: $m \approx 1.90$ quanta per spike and $q \approx 0.40$ mV.

@example
title: Raising and lowering release probability
problem: A synapse has $n = 10$ sites and $q = 0.3$ mV. Compare $p = 0.5$ with $p = 0.1$: mean response, CV and failure probability.
step: At $p = 0.5$: $\mu = 10 \times 0.5 \times 0.3 = 1.5$ mV; $\text{CV} = \sqrt{(1 - 0.5)/(10 \times 0.5)} = \sqrt{0.1} = 0.316$; failures $0.5^{10} = 0.001$.
step: At $p = 0.1$: $\mu = 10 \times 0.1 \times 0.3 = 0.3$ mV; $\text{CV} = \sqrt{0.9/1} = 0.949$.
step: Failure probability $0.9^{10} = 0.349$.
step: Lowering $p$ fivefold lowered the mean fivefold, tripled the CV and made failures common.
answer: $p=0.5$: mean 1.5 mV, CV 0.32, failures 0.1%. $p=0.1$: mean 0.3 mV, CV 0.95, failures 35%.

@mistakes
- Estimating quantal size as the mean response: $\bar{x} = mq$, so divide by the quantal content, and count failures to find $m$.
- Believing a smaller mean response always means less release: a lower $q$ (postsynaptic) lowers the mean without changing failure rate or CV.
- Using the Poisson formula when $p$ is large: with $p$ near 1 the binomial variance $np(1-p)q^2$ is much smaller than the Poisson value $mq^2$.
- Counting noise as release: failures must be defined by a threshold above the recording noise, such as 0.15 mV in a trace whose noise is a few hundredths of a millivolt.

```python
import math


def failure_analysis(amps, thr):
    """Quantal content and size by the method of failures."""
    n = len(amps)
    fails = sum(1 for a in amps if abs(a) < thr)
    if fails == 0:
        return None, None
    m = math.log(n / fails)
    return m, (sum(amps) / n) / m
```
hint: Count the trials with a response smaller than the threshold, take the log of the ratio of trials to failures, and divide the overall mean by that number.
solution: The mean over all trials (including failures) equals $mq$, so $q$ is that mean divided by $m$. The third test has no failures, where $\ln(N/N_0)$ is undefined. With all amplitudes equal to 0 or 0.9 and half failures, $m = \ln 2$ and $q = 0.45/\ln 2$.

```python
def fit_variance_mean(mus, variances):
    """Number of sites and quantal size from the variance-mean relation."""
    ys = [v / m for v, m in zip(variances, mus)]
    k = len(mus)
    mean_x = sum(mus) / k
    mean_y = sum(ys) / k
    sxy = sum((x - mean_x) * (y - mean_y) for x, y in zip(mus, ys))
    sxx = sum((x - mean_x) ** 2 for x in mus)
    slope = sxy / sxx
    return -1.0 / slope, mean_y - slope * mean_x
```
hint: Compute $y = \sigma^2/\mu$ for each point and fit $y$ against $\mu$ with the usual slope and intercept formulas; the slope is negative.
solution: From $\sigma^2 = q\mu - \mu^2/n$, dividing by $\mu$ gives a line with intercept $q$ and slope $-1/n$. The data in the second test come from $n = 12$, $q = 0.25$ mV at four release probabilities, and the third from two points on a line. With only two points the fit is exact but gives no check on the model.

```python
def ca_scaled_release(p_ref, ca_ref, ca, n_sites, power=4):
    """Release probability, mean quanta and failure probability versus calcium."""
    p = min(1.0, p_ref * (ca / ca_ref) ** power)
    return p, n_sites * p, (1.0 - p) ** n_sites
```
hint: Scale $p$ first, apply the cap at 1, and then compute the mean and the failure probability from the capped $p$.
solution: Halving calcium with a fourth-power law cuts $p$ sixteenfold (the third test), which is why release is so sensitive to calcium entry. The cap handles the saturating case, where every site releases and failures are impossible.
