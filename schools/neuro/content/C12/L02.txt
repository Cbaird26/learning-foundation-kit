@article
Last week gave a clean answer for a membrane that passes only potassium: $E_\text{K} = -89$ mV. But a real neuron at rest sits near −65 to −70 mV, and it never quite reaches $E_\text{K}$. The membrane leaks sodium and chloride as well. How do several leaks combine into one voltage?

### The GHK voltage equation

Goldman (1943) and Hodgkin and Katz (1949) assumed that the electric field inside the membrane is constant, and that each ion crosses independently through its own pathway with **permeability** $P$ (a velocity, in cm/s). Setting the total current to zero gives the **GHK voltage equation**

$$V_m = \frac{RT}{F}\,\ln\frac{P_\text{K}[\text{K}]_\text{out} + P_\text{Na}[\text{Na}]_\text{out} + P_\text{Cl}[\text{Cl}]_\text{in}}{P_\text{K}[\text{K}]_\text{in} + P_\text{Na}[\text{Na}]_\text{in} + P_\text{Cl}[\text{Cl}]_\text{out}} .$$

Cations appear with outside concentration on top, as in the Nernst equation; the anion Cl- is flipped (inside on top) because its charge is negative. With a single ion the equation reduces to the Nernst equation, and only **relative** permeabilities matter.

For the squid axon, Hodgkin and Katz estimated relative permeabilities at rest of $P_\text{K} : P_\text{Na} : P_\text{Cl} = 1 : 0.04 : 0.45$. With the mammalian concentrations of week 1 at $37\,^\circ$C:

| condition | $P_\text{K} : P_\text{Na} : P_\text{Cl}$ | $V_m$ (mV) |
|---|---|---|
| rest | 1 : 0.04 : 0.45 | −67.33 |
| rest, no chloride term | 1 : 0.04 : 0 | −68.57 |
| peak of an action potential | 1 : 20 : 0.45 | +51.13 |

At rest the potential is a compromise close to $E_\text{K}$, because K+ is by far the most permeant ion. During the action potential, sodium permeability rises about 500-fold relative to potassium and the potential swings toward $E_\text{Na} = +66.6$ mV, without reaching it because K+ and Cl- still leak.

### Extracellular potassium

Because K+ dominates, the resting potential follows $[\text{K}]_\text{out}$:

| $[\text{K}]_\text{out}$ (mM) | $V_m$ (mV) |
|---|---|
| 2.5 | −72.09 |
| 5 | −67.33 |
| 10 | −59.77 |
| 20 | −49.06 |

Doubling $[\text{K}]_\text{out}$ from 5 to 10 mM moves $V_m$ by 7.6 mV, less than the 18.5 mV that Nernst predicts for a pure K+ membrane, because the sodium and chloride terms dilute the dependence.

### Conductances and the leak balance

The same idea can be written with currents. Each ion carries $I_i = g_i (V_m - E_i)$, with conductance $g_i$ in nanosiemens (nS). At a steady voltage the net current is zero, $\sum_i g_i (V_m - E_i) = 0$, so

$$V_m = \frac{\sum_i g_i E_i}{\sum_i g_i} .$$

The resting potential is a **conductance-weighted average** of the equilibrium potentials: the ion with the largest conductance pulls hardest. This form is the one used in circuit models. It is not identical to GHK, because GHK conductances depend on concentrations and voltage, but it is the right picture near rest. Outward K+ current and inward Na+ and Cl- leaks balance without any net ion flow being "at equilibrium": ions still leak, and the Na+/K+ pump (3 Na+ out for 2 K+ in per ATP) restores the gradients over time.

### GHK current

The same constant-field assumption gives the current of one ion at any voltage:

$$I = P z^2 \frac{V F^2}{RT}\,\frac{[\text{ion}]_\text{in} - [\text{ion}]_\text{out}\, e^{-zVF/RT}}{1 - e^{-zVF/RT}} .$$

It is zero at the Nernst potential and, unlike $g(V - E)$, curved: it rectifies, giving more current when the ion flows down a steep gradient. At $V = 0$ it reduces to $P z F([\text{ion}]_\text{in} - [\text{ion}]_\text{out})$.

### In code

```python
import math

R, F = 8.314462618, 96485.33212


def ghk_voltage(ions, temp_c=37.0):
    """GHK voltage (mV); ions is a list of (z, P, c_out, c_in), z = +1 or -1."""
    top = bottom = 0.0
    for z, p, c_out, c_in in ions:
        if z > 0:
            top += p * c_out
            bottom += p * c_in
        else:
            top += p * c_in
            bottom += p * c_out
    return 1000.0 * R * (temp_c + 273.15) / F * math.log(top / bottom)

ions = [(1, 1.0, 5, 140), (1, 0.04, 145, 12), (-1, 0.45, 110, 10)]
print(round(ghk_voltage(ions), 2))
```

This prints `-67.33`. The loop is the whole idea: each ion adds a permeability-weighted term to the top and bottom of the logarithm.

@example
title: A potassium–sodium membrane
problem: At $37\,^\circ$C a membrane has $P_\text{Na}/P_\text{K} = 0.02$ and no chloride permeability. Concentrations (mM): K+ 5 out, 140 in; Na+ 145 out, 12 in. Find $V_m$.
step: Take $P_\text{K} = 1$ and $P_\text{Na} = 0.02$. The numerator is $1 \times 5 + 0.02 \times 145 = 7.9$.
step: The denominator is $1 \times 140 + 0.02 \times 12 = 140.24$.
step: The ratio is $7.9/140.24 = 0.05633$, with $\ln 0.05633 = -2.876$.
step: Multiply by $RT/F = 26.73$ mV: $V_m = -76.9$ mV.
answer: $V_m \approx -76.9$ mV, only 12 mV above $E_\text{K}$.

@example
title: A leak balance
problem: A cell has leak conductances $g_\text{K} = 8$ nS, $g_\text{Na} = 1$ nS and $g_\text{Cl} = 4$ nS with $E_\text{K} = -89$ mV, $E_\text{Na} = +66.6$ mV and $E_\text{Cl} = -64.1$ mV. What is the voltage at which the net current is zero?
step: The weighted sum is $8(-89) + 1(66.6) + 4(-64.1) = -712 + 66.6 - 256.4 = -901.8$.
step: The total conductance is $8 + 1 + 4 = 13$ nS.
step: Divide: $V_m = -901.8/13 = -69.4$ mV.
step: Check the currents in picoamperes (nS × mV = pA): K+ $8 \times (-69.4+89) = +156.8$, Na+ $1 \times (-69.4 - 66.6) = -136.0$, Cl- $4 \times (-69.4 + 64.1) = -21.2$; they sum to about zero.
answer: $V_m \approx -69.4$ mV.

@mistakes
- Flipping the wrong ions: in the GHK equation the anion Cl- has the inside concentration in the numerator; putting its outside concentration there gives a wrong sign for the chloride term.
- Using absolute permeabilities as if they set the scale: only the ratios $P_\text{Na}/P_\text{K}$ and $P_\text{Cl}/P_\text{K}$ matter in the voltage equation.
- Believing the resting potential is a Nernst potential: no single ion is at equilibrium at rest, and the cell survives only because pumps remove the leaked ions.
- Averaging equilibrium potentials without weights: the conductance-weighted mean is not the plain mean of the $E_i$.

model:
```python
import math

R, F = 8.314462618, 96485.33212


def ghk_current(V, z, P, c_out, c_in, temp_c=37.0):
    """GHK current density (uA/cm^2) for one ion; V in mV, c in mM, P in cm/s."""
    rt = R * (temp_c + 273.15)
    u = z * (V * 1e-3) * F / rt
    if abs(u) < 1e-9:
        return P * z * F * (c_in - c_out)
    pre = P * z * z * (V * 1e-3) * F * F / rt
    return pre * (c_in - c_out * math.exp(-u)) / (1.0 - math.exp(-u))
```
hint: Compute $u$ first and handle the removable singularity at $u = 0$ separately; everything else is the formula as written.
solution: The formula divides by $1 - e^{-u}$, which vanishes at $V = 0$, so a limit branch is needed. The fifth test checks the Nernst property: at $V = E_\text{K}$ the current is zero, and the fourth shows that a chloride (anion) current at −70 mV is negative, meaning inward, matching the sign convention for $z = -1$.

model:
```python
import math


def solve_p_ratio(target_V, k_out, k_in, na_out, na_in, temp_c=37.0):
    """Bisection for P_Na/P_K that gives the target GHK potential."""
    rt_f = 8.314462618 * (temp_c + 273.15) / 96485.33212 * 1000.0

    def volt(r):
        return rt_f * math.log((k_out + r * na_out) / (k_in + r * na_in))

    lo, hi = 0.0, 100.0
    if target_V < volt(lo) or target_V > volt(hi):
        return None
    for _ in range(60):
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2.0
        if volt(mid) < target_V:
            lo = mid
        else:
            hi = mid
    return (lo + hi) / 2.0
```
hint: Write the voltage as a function of the ratio $r$, check the two ends of the range first, then keep the half-interval that still brackets the target.
solution: The voltage is monotonic in $r$, so bisection converges: if the voltage at the midpoint is below the target, the answer is in the upper half. The `None` cases are the targets outside $[V(0), V(100)]$; $V(0)$ is $E_\text{K}$, so no ratio can give a potential below it.

model:
```python
def leak_balance(gs, Es):
    """Voltage of zero net current for parallel conductances, and the residual."""
    total = sum(gs)
    if total == 0:
        raise ValueError("total conductance is zero")
    V = sum(g * E for g, E in zip(gs, Es)) / total
    I_net = 0.0
    for g, E in zip(gs, Es):
        I_net += g * (V - E)
    return V, I_net
```
hint: The zero-current voltage is the conductance-weighted mean of the $E_i$; then plug it back in to confirm the net current vanishes.
solution: Setting $\sum g_i(V - E_i) = 0$ gives $V = \sum g_i E_i/\sum g_i$. Multiplying all conductances by the same factor leaves $V$ unchanged (the fourth test), because only ratios of conductances matter, just as only relative permeabilities matter in the GHK equation.
