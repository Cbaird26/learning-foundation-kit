@article
A single amino acid change in a sodium channel can cause a child to have seizures, and a toxin from a pufferfish can stop a heart and a diaphragm by binding one site on the same family of channels. Both facts follow from the quantitative picture of the past eleven weeks: excitability is the result of specific conductances and synaptic parameters, so changing one of them changes behavior in predictable ways. This lesson is a textbook-level account, not medical advice; diagnosis and treatment are for clinicians.

### Channelopathies

A **channelopathy** is a disease caused by altered function of an ion channel or receptor, most often through a mutation in its gene. The change can be a **loss of function** (fewer or less active channels) or a **gain of function** (more activity or altered gating). Well-established examples:

| gene and protein | change | consequence |
|---|---|---|
| SCN1A, Nav1.1 | loss of function | severe epilepsy of infancy (Dravet syndrome); evidence from mouse models points to reduced Na+ current in inhibitory interneurons, so inhibition weakens |
| KCNQ2, Kv7.2 (M current) | reduced current | benign familial neonatal seizures; less M current means higher input resistance and more excitability (week 10) |
| CACNA1A, Cav2.1 | altered function | episodic ataxia type 2 and familial hemiplegic migraine; Cav2.1 is a presynaptic Ca2+ channel that controls release |
| SCN9A, Nav1.7 | loss or gain of function | loss gives a congenital insensitivity to pain; some gain-of-function variants cause painful disorders |
| CLCN1, muscle Cl- channel | loss of function | myotonia: with less Cl- conductance the muscle membrane is hyperexcitable |

Not all are mutations. In **myasthenia gravis**, autoantibodies reduce the number of functional nicotinic acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular junction: the quantal size $q$ falls while release is initially normal, so the end-plate potential may not reach threshold and muscles weaken, particularly with repeated use. In **Lambert–Eaton syndrome**, autoantibodies against presynaptic Ca2+ channels reduce release, a fall in quantal content with normal quantal size. Quantal analysis (week 8) distinguishes them. In multiple sclerosis, demyelination lowers the safety factor and causes conduction block (week 7).

### Drugs acting on channels and synapses

| agent | target | main effect |
|---|---|---|
| tetrodotoxin | pore of voltage-gated Na+ channels | blocks Na+ current, abolishing spikes |
| tetraethylammonium | K+ channel pore | blocks K+ current, prolonging spikes |
| local anesthetics and some antiepileptic drugs | voltage-gated Na+ channels | **use-dependent** block of Na+ current |
| benzodiazepines | GABA$_\text{A}$ receptor (an allosteric site) | increase the response to GABA, enhancing inhibition |
| curare | nicotinic receptor | competitive antagonist at the neuromuscular junction |
| serotonin-reuptake inhibitors | serotonin transporter | prolong transmitter in the extracellular space |
| ketamine | NMDA receptor channel | open-channel block |

Block by a drug at concentration $[D]$ with half-blocking concentration $\text{IC}_{50}$ and Hill coefficient $h$ leaves a fraction

$$f_\text{unblocked} = \frac{1}{1 + ([D]/\text{IC}_{50})^h}$$

of the channels working. In the Hodgkin–Huxley model, a Na+ blocker is a scaling of $\bar g_\text{Na}$ by $f$: with $f = 1$ the 1 ms pulse of week 6 needs $6.9$ µA/cm² to fire; at $f = 0.7$ it needs $10.7$, at $f = 0.5$ it needs $14.2$, and a 10 µA/cm² pulse (which fires at $f = 1$ with a peak of $+40$ mV) fails to produce a spike below $f \approx 0.74$.

### Use-dependent block

Local anesthetics and several antiepileptic drugs bind preferentially to open or inactivated channels, so they block more strongly in neurons firing quickly, and spare neurons firing slowly. Let each spike bind the drug to a fraction $b$ of the channels not yet blocked, and let block recover with time constant $\tau_\text{rec}$ between spikes separated by $T$. If $B$ is the blocked fraction just before a spike, the update per spike is $B \to [B + b(1 - B)]\,e^{-T/\tau_\text{rec}}$, with a fixed point

$$B_\infty = \frac{b\,E}{1 - (1 - b)\,E}, \qquad E = e^{-T/\tau_\text{rec}}.$$

For $b = 0.1$ and $\tau_\text{rec} = 1000$ ms (illustrative values), $B_\infty = 0.055$ at 1 Hz, $0.487$ at 10 Hz and $0.832$ at 50 Hz: the high-frequency discharge of an epileptic focus is suppressed far more than normal low-frequency activity, a selectivity that explains why these drugs can be tolerated.

### In code

```python
import math


def fraction_unblocked(conc, ic50, h=1.0):
    """Fraction of channels not blocked at drug concentration conc."""
    return 1.0 / (1.0 + (conc / ic50) ** h)

print([round(fraction_unblocked(c, 2.0), 3) for c in (0.0, 2.0, 6.0, 20.0)])
```

This prints `[1.0, 0.5, 0.25, 0.091]`: no block with no drug, half at the IC$_{50}$, and a steep fall beyond it.

@example
title: Use-dependent block at 20 Hz
problem: A drug has $b = 0.1$ and $\tau_\text{rec} = 1000$ ms. What is the steady-state blocked fraction at 20 Hz?
step: The interspike interval is $T = 1000/20 = 50$ ms, so $E = e^{-50/1000} = 0.9512$.
step: The numerator is $bE = 0.09512$.
step: The denominator is $1 - (1 - b)E = 1 - 0.9 \times 0.9512 = 0.1439$.
step: The ratio is $0.09512/0.1439 = 0.661$.
answer: About 66% of the channels are blocked at 20 Hz, compared with 5.5% at 1 Hz.

@example
title: Potentiating GABA
problem: A cell has $g_L = 10$ nS ($E_L = -70$ mV) and a tonic GABA$_\text{A}$ conductance $g_i = 4$ nS with $E_i = -80$ mV. A benzodiazepine-like drug doubles the GABA conductance. Find the steady voltage before and after, and the total conductance.
step: Before: $V = (10 \times (-70) + 4 \times (-80))/14 = -1020/14 = -72.86$ mV, with total conductance 14 nS.
step: After: $g_i = 8$ nS and $V = (-700 - 640)/18 = -74.44$ mV, with total conductance 18 nS.
step: The cell is 1.6 mV more hyperpolarized and its input resistance is lower by $14/18$, so any excitatory input has a smaller effect (shunting).
answer: $-72.86 \to -74.44$ mV, total conductance $14 \to 18$ nS: hyperpolarization plus shunting.

@mistakes
- Equating a channelopathy with a mutation in a single neuron type: the same channel can have different roles in excitatory and inhibitory cells, which is why loss of Nav1.1 can increase network excitability.
- Treating all channel blockers as equal: use-dependent drugs act more at high firing rates, while a pore blocker such as tetrodotoxin blocks regardless of activity.
- Reading a weaker response as reduced release: lowered receptor number (postsynaptic) and reduced release (presynaptic) have different quantal signatures.
- Taking an illustrative number for a measured one: the parameters in these models are chosen to show the logic, not to describe a particular drug.

model:
```python
import math


def use_dependent_block(b, tau_rec, rate_hz, n_spikes):
    """Blocked fraction just before each spike of a regular spike train."""
    T = 1000.0 / rate_hz
    decay = math.exp(-T / tau_rec)
    B = 0.0
    values = []
    for _ in range(n_spikes):
        values.append(B)
        B = B + b * (1.0 - B)
        B = B * decay
    return values
```
hint: In each loop pass, record `B` first, then bind, then recover; the rate in hertz gives the interval $T = 1000/\text{rate}$ in ms.
solution: The first recorded value is 0 because the channels start unblocked. Over many spikes the recorded values converge to the fixed point $B_\infty = bE/(1 - (1 - b)E)$ (the second and third tests), which grows with firing rate because less time is available for recovery: that is the use dependence.

```python
import math


def rates(V):
    x, y = V + 55.0, V + 40.0
    a_n = 0.1 if abs(x) < 1e-7 else 0.01 * x / (1.0 - math.exp(-x / 10.0))
    a_m = 1.0 if abs(y) < 1e-7 else 0.1 * y / (1.0 - math.exp(-y / 10.0))
    return (a_n, 0.125 * math.exp(-(V + 65.0) / 80.0),
            a_m, 4.0 * math.exp(-(V + 65.0) / 18.0),
            0.07 * math.exp(-(V + 65.0) / 20.0),
            1.0 / (1.0 + math.exp(-(V + 35.0) / 10.0)))


def hh_simulate(amp, scale):
    V, dt = -65.0, 0.05
    a_n, b_n, a_m, b_m, a_h, b_h = rates(V)
    n, m, h = a_n / (a_n + b_n), a_m / (a_m + b_m), a_h / (a_h + b_h)
    peak = V
    for k in range(100):
        I = amp if k * dt < 1.0 else 0.0
        a_n, b_n, a_m, b_m, a_h, b_h = rates(V)
        i_ion = (scale * 120.0 * m ** 3 * h * (V - 50.0)
                 + 36.0 * n ** 4 * (V + 77.0) + 0.3 * (V + 54.387))
        dn = a_n * (1 - n) - b_n * n
        dm = a_m * (1 - m) - b_m * m
        dh = a_h * (1 - h) - b_h * h
        V, n, m, h = V + dt * (I - i_ion), n + dt * dn, m + dt * dm, h + dt * dh
        peak = max(peak, V)
    return peak


def critical_na_fraction(amp):
    """Smallest Na+ conductance fraction that still gives a spike, by bisection."""
    lo, hi = 0.0, 1.0
    for _ in range(8):
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2.0
        if hh_simulate(amp, mid) > 0.0:
            hi = mid
        else:
            lo = mid
    return (lo + hi) / 2.0
```
hint: Keep `lo` (no spike) and `hi` (spike); test the midpoint with `hh_simulate` and move the end that agrees with the outcome.
solution: A stronger stimulus needs less sodium conductance to fire, so the critical fraction falls with amplitude (the third test). The bracket tests check that a fraction a little above the result fires and one a little below does not; eight halvings locate it to within about 0.004. This is a model of how a Na+ channel blocker raises threshold until the neuron cannot spike.

model:
```python
def gaba_potentiation(g_L, E_L, g_i, E_i, factor):
    """Steady voltage before and after scaling an inhibitory conductance."""
    v_before = (g_L * E_L + g_i * E_i) / (g_L + g_i)
    g_after = g_i * factor
    total = g_L + g_after
    v_after = (g_L * E_L + g_after * E_i) / total
    return v_before, v_after, total
```
hint: The steady voltage is the conductance-weighted mean of $E_L$ and $E_i$; recompute it with $g_i$ multiplied by `factor`.
solution: If $E_i$ equals $E_L$ the voltage does not move but the total conductance rises (the second test: pure shunting). If $E_i$ is above $E_L$ (the third test), more GABA conductance depolarizes, which is why the reversal potential, set by chloride regulation, decides whether potentiating GABA inhibits by hyperpolarization, by shunting, or can even excite.
