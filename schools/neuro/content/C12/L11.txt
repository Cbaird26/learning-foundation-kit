@article
Last week's receptors and the Hodgkin–Huxley conductances were treated as smooth, continuous quantities. At the molecular scale they are not: each channel is a single protein that flips between a few states, and a conductance is the average over thousands of such flips. This lesson moves between the scales: from one molecule to one current, and from one gene to one density of channels.

### Single channels

The patch clamp (Neher and Sakmann, 1976) records the current through one channel. The current jumps between zero and a fixed value $i = \gamma\,(V - E)$, where $\gamma$ is the **unitary conductance** (typically 10 to 100 pS for ion channels; 1 pS is $10^{-12}$ S). With $N$ channels, each open with probability $p$, the macroscopic current is

$$I = N\,p\,\gamma\,(V - E).$$

For $N = 500$, $\gamma = 20$ pS, $p = 0.3$, $V = -20$ mV and $E_\text{K} = -90$ mV, each open channel carries $1.4$ pA and the total is $210$ pA. Voltage-gated K+, Na+ and Ca2+ channels share a common architecture: four subunits or domains around a central pore, each with six transmembrane segments; the fourth segment (S4) carries charges that move in the field and acts as the **voltage sensor**.

### Gating

If opening moves an effective gating charge $z_g$ across the membrane, the open probability at steady state follows a **Boltzmann curve**

$$p_\infty(V) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(V - V_{1/2})/k}}, \qquad k = \frac{RT}{z_g F}.$$

$V_{1/2}$ is the voltage of half-activation and $k$ the slope factor in mV. At $37\,^\circ$C, $RT/F = 26.7$ mV, so a gating charge of 4 gives $k = 6.7$ mV; ten millivolts above $V_{1/2}$ the open probability is 0.82. A simple two-state channel $C \rightleftharpoons O$ with opening rate $\alpha$ and closing rate $\beta$ has $p_\text{open} = \alpha/(\alpha + \beta)$, and the durations of open and closed events are exponentially distributed with means $1/\beta$ and $1/\alpha$. Real channels have several closed states, an inactivated state and drug-binding states; this is the simplest model.

### Pumps and transporters

Channels let ions move down their electrochemical gradients; **pumps** and **transporters** maintain those gradients. The **Na+/K+-ATPase** uses one ATP to move 3 Na+ out and 2 K+ in, a net transfer of one positive charge out per cycle, so it is **electrogenic**: a pump cycling 60 times per second moves $60 \times 1.6\times10^{-19}$ C/s $= 9.6\times10^{-18}$ A, and $2 \times 10^{6}$ pumps give 19 pA of outward current. This directly hyperpolarizes the cell by a few millivolts in some conditions, but its main role is to restore the gradients that all other currents rely on.

Chloride is moved by cotransporters. **NKCC1** brings Cl- in (with Na+ and K+), raising $[\text{Cl}^-]_\text{in}$; **KCC2** pumps Cl- out with K+, lowering it. This sets $E_\text{Cl}$ and so whether GABA$_\text{A}$ receptors hyperpolarize or depolarize. With $[\text{Cl}]_\text{out} = 110$ mM at $37\,^\circ$C:

| $[\text{Cl}]_\text{in}$ (mM) | 5 | 10 | 20 | 30 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $E_\text{Cl}$ (mV) | −82.6 | −64.1 | −45.6 | −34.7 |

In many immature neurons KCC2 is expressed at low levels and GABA is depolarizing; as KCC2 rises during development, GABA becomes hyperpolarizing or shunting in most mature neurons. Reduced KCC2 function in a mature neuron weakens inhibition, which has been implicated in several disorders (week 12).

### Genes and channel numbers

Each channel type is encoded by a gene: for example, Nav1.1 by SCN1A, Cav2.1 by CACNA1A, the Kv7.2 subunit of the M channel by KCNQ2, KCC2 by SLC12A5. The number of channels in a membrane depends on transcription, mRNA stability, translation, trafficking and degradation. A minimal model of expression has mRNA $m$ made at rate $k_m$ and degraded at rate $\gamma_m$, and protein $P$ made in proportion to $m$:

$$\frac{dm}{dt} = k_m - \gamma_m m, \qquad \frac{dP}{dt} = k_p m - \gamma_p P, \qquad m_\infty = \frac{k_m}{\gamma_m},\quad P_\infty = \frac{k_p m_\infty}{\gamma_p}.$$

A half-life $t_{1/2}$ corresponds to $\gamma = \ln 2/t_{1/2}$. Steady-state channel number is proportional to the synthesis rate and inversely proportional to the decay rate, and it approaches steady state with the time constant $1/\gamma_p$; for a protein with a 10-hour half-life, changes in expression take days.

@example
title: How many channels in a patch?
problem: A patch at $-10$ mV with $E_\text{K} = -90$ mV carries a macroscopic K+ current of 150 pA. The unitary conductance is 25 pS and the open probability is 0.25. How many channels are in the patch?
step: The driving force is $-10 - (-90) = 80$ mV, so one open channel carries $25 \times 80/1000 = 2.0$ pA (pS × mV = fA).
step: The mean current per channel is $p\,i = 0.25 \times 2.0 = 0.5$ pA.
step: Therefore $N = I/(p\,i) = 150/0.5 = 300$.
answer: About 300 channels.

@example
title: Open probability from the gating charge
problem: A channel has $V_{1/2} = -40$ mV and an effective gating charge $z_g = 4$. At $37\,^\circ$C, what is the open probability at $-30$ mV?
step: The slope factor is $k = RT/(z_g F) = 26.73/4 = 6.68$ mV.
step: The distance from the midpoint is $-30 - (-40) = +10$ mV, so $(V - V_{1/2})/k = 1.497$.
step: Evaluate: $p = 1/(1 + e^{-1.497}) = 1/(1 + 0.224) = 0.817$.
answer: $p \approx 0.82$.

@mistakes
- Mixing units of conductance and current: pS times mV gives fA, so divide by 1000 to get pA.
- Using the macroscopic current as if it were the current of one channel: $I = N p\,i$, and $p$ is the fraction of time (or of channels) open.
- Believing the pump creates the resting potential: the Na+/K+-ATPase maintains the gradients and adds a small electrogenic current, but the resting potential comes mainly from K+ (and other leak) permeabilities.
- Assuming GABA is always hyperpolarizing: its effect depends on $E_\text{Cl}$, which is set by KCC2 and NKCC1 and varies with cell type and age.

```python
def channel_stats(events):
    """Open probability and opening/closing rates from dwell times."""
    open_times = [d for s, d in events if s == "O"]
    closed_times = [d for s, d in events if s == "C"]
    if not open_times or not closed_times:
        raise ValueError("need both open and closed events")
    p_open = sum(open_times) / (sum(open_times) + sum(closed_times))
    alpha = len(closed_times) / sum(closed_times)
    beta = len(open_times) / sum(open_times)
    return p_open, alpha, beta
```
hint: Split the durations by state; the mean duration is the sum divided by the count, and the rate is its reciprocal.
solution: The open probability is a ratio of times, while the rates come from mean dwell times of each kind of event. In the third test there are two open and three closed events, so $p$ is not $\alpha/(\alpha + \beta) = 0.5$: the record is short and the event counts differ, which shows why long recordings are needed for good estimates.

```python
import math


def expression_levels(k_m, k_p, half_life_m, half_life_p, t_end, dt):
    """Euler simulation of mRNA and protein levels."""
    g_m = math.log(2.0) / half_life_m
    g_p = math.log(2.0) / half_life_p
    m = P = 0.0
    for _ in range(int(round(t_end / dt))):
        m, P = m + dt * (k_m - g_m * m), P + dt * (k_p * m - g_p * P)
    return m, P
```
hint: Convert the half-lives to decay rates, then update both variables from the old values; a tuple assignment does this in one line.
solution: Updating `m` first and then using the new `m` for `P` would be a different scheme and give slightly different numbers. After many protein half-lives the levels approach $m_\infty = k_m/\gamma_m$ and $P_\infty = k_p m_\infty/\gamma_p$ (the third test); the protein, with its longer half-life, takes much longer to settle.

model:
```python
import math


def boltzmann_fit(Vs, ps):
    """Half-activation voltage and slope factor from a logit-linear fit."""
    ys = [math.log(p / (1.0 - p)) for p in ps]
    n = len(Vs)
    mean_v = sum(Vs) / n
    mean_y = sum(ys) / n
    sxy = sum((v - mean_v) * (y - mean_y) for v, y in zip(Vs, ys))
    sxx = sum((v - mean_v) ** 2 for v in Vs)
    slope = sxy / sxx
    intercept = mean_y - slope * mean_v
    return -intercept / slope, 1.0 / slope
```
hint: Compute the logit of each probability, fit logit against voltage, then solve logit $= 0$ for the voltage; the slope factor is the reciprocal of the fitted slope.
solution: The logit linearizes the Boltzmann curve: $\ln(p/(1-p)) = V/k - V_{1/2}/k$. The fitted line has slope $1/k$ and intercept $-V_{1/2}/k$, so $V_{1/2} = -\text{intercept}/\text{slope}$. A probability of exactly 0 or 1 has no finite logit, which is why the prompt requires values strictly between them.
