@article
Passive membranes only respond in proportion to their input. Yet a squid axon, given a brief jolt of depolarization, answers with a stereotyped 100 mV spike that does not shrink as it travels. Hodgkin and Huxley (1952) explained this with a model that treats conductances as voltage dependent, built from measurements with the **voltage clamp**, in which the experimenter holds the voltage at a chosen level and records the current needed to hold it.

### The model

The membrane is a capacitor in parallel with three conductances:

$$C\,\frac{dV}{dt} = I_\text{ext} - g_\text{Na}\, m^3 h\,(V - E_\text{Na}) - g_\text{K}\, n^4 (V - E_\text{K}) - g_\text{L}\,(V - E_\text{L}) .$$

Here $\bar g$ denotes the maximal conductance. We use the standard squid-axon parameters in the common modern convention (the original paper used a different sign and offset for $V$): $C = 1\ \mu\text{F/cm}^2$, $\bar g_\text{Na} = 120$, $\bar g_\text{K} = 36$, $g_\text{L} = 0.3$ mS/cm², $E_\text{Na} = +50$, $E_\text{K} = -77$, $E_\text{L} = -54.387$ mV, resting potential $-65$ mV, temperature $6.3\,^\circ$C. Currents are in µA/cm², so (mS/cm²) × mV = µA/cm².

Each **gating variable** $x \in \{m, h, n\}$ is a probability between 0 and 1 that obeys

$$\frac{dx}{dt} = \alpha_x(V)\,(1 - x) - \beta_x(V)\,x = \frac{x_\infty(V) - x}{\tau_x(V)}, \qquad x_\infty = \frac{\alpha_x}{\alpha_x + \beta_x},\quad \tau_x = \frac{1}{\alpha_x + \beta_x}.$$

The sodium conductance has three activation gates $m$ (fast) and one **inactivation** gate $h$ (slower), so $g_\text{Na} = \bar g_\text{Na} m^3 h$; the potassium conductance has four activation gates $n$, with no inactivation, so $g_\text{K} = \bar g_\text{K} n^4$. The rates (in 1/ms, with $V$ in mV) are

| variable | $\alpha$ | $\beta$ |
|---|---|---|
| $n$ | $0.01(V+55)/(1 - e^{-(V+55)/10})$ | $0.125\,e^{-(V+65)/80}$ |
| $m$ | $0.1(V+40)/(1 - e^{-(V+40)/10})$ | $4\,e^{-(V+65)/18}$ |
| $h$ | $0.07\,e^{-(V+65)/20}$ | $1/(1 + e^{-(V+35)/10})$ |

The two $\alpha$ formulas are $0/0$ at $V = -55$ and $V = -40$ mV; their limits there are $0.1$ and $1.0$ per ms. Rates scale with temperature by $\varphi = Q_{10}^{(T - 6.3)/10}$ with $Q_{10} = 3$.

### Steady states and time constants

| $V$ (mV) | $n_\infty$ | $\tau_n$ (ms) | $m_\infty$ | $\tau_m$ (ms) | $h_\infty$ | $\tau_h$ (ms) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| −65 | 0.318 | 5.46 | 0.053 | 0.237 | 0.596 | 8.52 |
| −40 | 0.679 | 3.52 | 0.501 | 0.501 | 0.050 | 2.52 |
| 0 | 0.909 | 1.65 | 0.974 | 0.239 | 0.003 | 1.03 |

Depolarization raises $m_\infty$ and $n_\infty$ (activation) and lowers $h_\infty$ (inactivation). At rest, the three currents are $-1.22$ (Na), $+4.40$ (K) and $-3.18$ (leak) µA/cm², which sum to zero: the model is at equilibrium at $-65$ mV, with most of the conductance in K+ and the leak.

### The spike from the conductances

After a depolarizing step, $m$ (time constant a fraction of a millisecond) rises first, opening Na+ channels; inward Na+ current depolarizes further, a positive feedback that produces the upstroke. Then $h$ falls (inactivation) and $n$ rises (delayed activation of K+), so the Na+ conductance falls and the K+ conductance grows, ending the spike and driving the voltage below rest. In a voltage clamp at 0 mV, $g_\text{K}$ rises with a sigmoidal delay because it depends on $n^4$, while $g_\text{Na}$ rises and then falls: $m^3$ opens it, $h$ closes it. Modern recordings show dozens of channel types; HH is a minimal model that explains the spike of one axon, and the same formalism with other channels explains many neuron types.

import math


def rates(V):
    """(alpha_n, beta_n, alpha_m, beta_m, alpha_h, beta_h) in 1/ms; V in mV."""

@example
title: The singular point of the sodium activation rate
problem: Compute $m_\infty$ and $\tau_m$ at $V = -40$ mV.
step: The formula for $\alpha_m$ is $0/0$ here: $0.1 (V+40)/(1 - e^{-(V+40)/10})$ with $V + 40 = 0$. Its limit as $x = V + 40 \to 0$ is $0.1 \times 10 = 1.0$ per ms, because $1 - e^{-x/10} \approx x/10$.
step: Compute $\beta_m = 4\,e^{-(V+65)/18} = 4\,e^{-25/18} = 4 \times 0.2494 = 0.9976$ per ms.
step: Then $m_\infty = 1.0/(1.0 + 0.9976) = 0.5006$ and $\tau_m = 1/(1.0 + 0.9976) = 0.5006$ ms.
answer: $m_\infty \approx 0.501$ and $\tau_m \approx 0.501$ ms; the two coincide here because $\alpha_m + \beta_m \approx 2$.

@example
title: Potassium conductance during a clamp step
problem: The voltage is held at $-65$ mV and stepped to $0$ mV. With $n_\infty(-65) = 0.3177$, $n_\infty(0) = 0.9087$ and $\tau_n(0) = 1.645$ ms, what is $g_\text{K}$ after 2 ms?
step: In a clamp the voltage is constant, so $n$ relaxes exponentially from its holding value to the new steady state: $n(t) = n_\infty + (n_0 - n_\infty)e^{-t/\tau_n}$.
step: At $t = 2$ ms, $e^{-2/1.645} = e^{-1.216} = 0.2964$, so $n = 0.9087 + (0.3177 - 0.9087)(0.2964) = 0.7335$.
step: The conductance is $\bar g_\text{K}\,n^4 = 36 \times 0.7335^4 = 36 \times 0.2894 = 10.42$ mS/cm².
answer: About $10.4$ mS/cm², compared with $0.37$ mS/cm² at rest; the fourth power of $n$ makes the onset sigmoidal.

@mistakes
- Dropping the powers: $g_\text{Na}$ is $\bar g_\text{Na} m^3 h$ and $g_\text{K}$ is $\bar g_\text{K} n^4$, not $\bar g\,m$ or $\bar g\,n$; this is what gives the delayed, sigmoidal onset.
- Letting the program crash at $V = -55$ or $-40$ mV: the rate formulas are $0/0$ there and need their limits.
- Mixing conventions: rate functions given for a resting potential of $-65$ mV cannot be combined with ones that measure $V$ as a displacement from rest.
- Saying inactivation is the same as closing by repolarization: the $h$ gate closes during a sustained depolarization, so Na+ channels stay shut until the membrane is repolarized for a while.

```python
import math


def _n_gate(V):
    x = V + 55.0
    a = 0.1 if abs(x) < 1e-7 else 0.01 * x / (1.0 - math.exp(-x / 10.0))
    b = 0.125 * math.exp(-(V + 65.0) / 80.0)
    return a / (a + b), 1.0 / (a + b)


def k_conductance(V_step, times, V_hold=-65.0):
    """K+ conductance (mS/cm^2) after a clamp step, with exact gating relaxation."""
    n0, _ = _n_gate(V_hold)
    n_inf, tau = _n_gate(V_step)
    return [36.0 * (n_inf + (n0 - n_inf) * math.exp(-t / tau)) ** 4 for t in times]
```
hint: First find $n_0$ from the holding voltage and $n_\infty$, $\tau_n$ from the step voltage, then apply the exponential for each time; the conductance is $36\,n^4$.
solution: In a clamp the voltage is constant after the step, so the gating equation is linear and has the exact exponential solution; no time stepping is needed. The fourth test steps to the singular point $V = -55$ mV, where the code must use the limit; there $n_\infty$ is about 0.476 and the long-time conductance is about 1.84 mS/cm². The sigmoidal onset comes from raising $n$ to the fourth power.

model:
```python
import math


def _gates(V):
    y = V + 40.0
    a_m = 1.0 if abs(y) < 1e-7 else 0.1 * y / (1.0 - math.exp(-y / 10.0))
    b_m = 4.0 * math.exp(-(V + 65.0) / 18.0)
    a_h = 0.07 * math.exp(-(V + 65.0) / 20.0)
    b_h = 1.0 / (1.0 + math.exp(-(V + 35.0) / 10.0))
    return (a_m / (a_m + b_m), 1.0 / (a_m + b_m),
            a_h / (a_h + b_h), 1.0 / (a_h + b_h))


def na_conductance(V_step, times, V_hold=-65.0):
    """Na+ conductance (mS/cm^2) after a clamp step: m rises, then h closes it."""
    m0, _, h0, _ = _gates(V_hold)
    m_inf, tau_m, h_inf, tau_h = _gates(V_step)
    out = []
    for t in times:
        m = m_inf + (m0 - m_inf) * math.exp(-t / tau_m)
        h = h_inf + (h0 - h_inf) * math.exp(-t / tau_h)
        out.append(120.0 * m ** 3 * h)
    return out
```
hint: Relax $m$ and $h$ separately with their own $\tau$, then multiply $m^3 h$ by 120; the rise-and-fall shape comes from the fast $m$ and slower $h$.
solution: Because $\tau_m \ll \tau_h$ at depolarized voltages, $m^3$ rises quickly while $h$ is still high, so the conductance peaks near 1 ms and then declines as $h$ falls. The last test checks that the maximum is neither the first nor the last sample.
