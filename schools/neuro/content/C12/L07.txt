@article
A frog sciatic nerve fiber and a squid giant axon do very different things to be fast. The squid grows an axon half a millimeter thick; vertebrates wrap thin axons in insulation. A 10 µm myelinated fiber conducts at about 60 m/s, faster than the 500 µm squid axon, at a fraction of the volume. Why does insulating an axon speed it up, and what happens when the insulation is lost?

### Unmyelinated axons

In an unmyelinated axon, every patch of membrane must charge from rest to threshold, and the charging current comes by axial flow from the active region. Cable theory (week 4) gives the scale: the length constant grows as $\sqrt{d}$ and the time constant does not depend on diameter, so the velocity grows as $\sqrt{d}$:

$$v \approx k\sqrt{d}.$$

Using $k = 0.95$ m/s per $\sqrt{\mu\text{m}}$, an illustrative constant chosen so that a squid giant axon of $d = 500\ \mu\text{m}$ conducts at about $21$ m/s (the order of magnitude measured in squid at room temperature), the model gives $v = 0.95$ m/s for $d = 1\ \mu\text{m}$ and $3.0$ m/s for $d = 10\ \mu\text{m}$. Thin unmyelinated fibers are slow, and doubling the speed needs four times the cross-sectional area.

### Myelin

Oligodendrocytes in the central nervous system and Schwann cells in peripheral nerves wrap an axon in many layers of membrane, forming **myelin**, with gaps called **nodes of Ranvier** about 1 µm long where Na+ channels are concentrated. $n$ membrane layers in series behave like one membrane with

$$R_\text{eff} = n R_m, \qquad C_\text{eff} = \frac{C_m}{n}, \qquad \tau = R_\text{eff} C_\text{eff} = R_m C_m, \qquad \lambda_\text{myelin} = \sqrt{\frac{n R_m\, d}{4 R_i}} = \sqrt{n}\,\lambda_\text{bare}.$$

The time constant is unchanged, but there is far less capacitance to charge (so less current is spent on charging the membrane under the myelin) and far less leak (so more current flows along the axon). The spike jumps from node to node, **saltatory conduction** (from the Latin for leaping), and each node regenerates the spike with its Na+ channels. For mammalian myelinated fibers of roughly 2 to 20 µm the empirical rule of thumb is $v \approx 6\ \text{m/s per}\ \mu\text{m}$ of fiber diameter, so velocity grows in proportion to $d$, not $\sqrt{d}$:

| fiber diameter $d$ (µm) | 1 | 2 | 5 | 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| unmyelinated, $0.95\sqrt{d}$ (m/s) | 0.95 | 1.34 | 2.12 | 3.00 |
| myelinated, $6d$ (m/s) | 6 | 12 | 30 | 60 |

These are model values for orientation; the rule for myelinated fibers is not meant for sub-micrometer axons, and real values depend on species and temperature. Internodes are typically about 100 times the fiber diameter, so a 10 µm fiber has nodes about 1 mm apart.

### The safety factor and demyelination

The spike at one node depolarizes the next through the internode, a passive cable of length $L$ and length constant $\lambda_\text{int}$ (steady-state approximation of week 4). If the spike amplitude above rest is $A$, the depolarization arriving at the next node is $A\,e^{-L/\lambda_\text{int}}$, and the next node fires if it reaches the threshold depolarization $\theta$. The **safety factor** is

$$\text{SF} = \frac{A\,e^{-L/\lambda_\text{int}}}{\theta},$$

normally well above 1, so conduction survives small changes. It fails when SF falls below 1, giving **conduction block**. The longest internode that still conducts is $L_\text{max} = \lambda_\text{int}\ln(A/\theta)$. In demyelinating diseases (such as multiple sclerosis, discussed in week 12) myelin is lost, $\lambda_\text{int}$ shrinks and charge leaks away, so conduction slows or fails.

### In code

Delay is length divided by velocity; with length in mm and velocity in m/s the delay comes out in ms:

```python
import math


def fiber_velocity(d_um, myelinated):
    """Conduction velocity (m/s): 6*d if myelinated, else 0.95*sqrt(d); d in um."""
    return 6.0 * d_um if myelinated else 0.95 * math.sqrt(d_um)

for d, myel in [(10.0, True), (10.0, False), (500.0, False)]:
    print(round(800.0 / fiber_velocity(d, myel), 1))
```

This prints `13.3`, `266.3` and `37.7` (ms): the time for a spike to travel 800 mm along a 10 µm myelinated fiber, a 10 µm unmyelinated fiber and a squid-like 500 µm axon.

@example
title: Safety factor of a node
problem: A spike has amplitude $A = 100$ mV above rest, the threshold depolarization is $\theta = 15$ mV, and an internode of $L = 1.2$ mm has $\lambda_\text{int} = 1.5$ mm. Find the safety factor and the longest internode that still conducts. What happens if demyelination reduces $\lambda_\text{int}$ to 0.5 mm?
step: The arriving depolarization is $100\,e^{-1.2/1.5} = 100\,e^{-0.8} = 44.9$ mV.
step: The safety factor is $44.9/15 = 3.0$.
step: The longest internode is $L_\text{max} = 1.5 \ln(100/15) = 1.5 \times 1.897 = 2.85$ mm.
step: With $\lambda_\text{int} = 0.5$ mm the arriving depolarization is $100\,e^{-2.4} = 9.1$ mV, which is below 15 mV: SF $= 0.6$ and conduction blocks.
answer: SF $\approx 3.0$ and $L_\text{max} \approx 2.85$ mm; after demyelination SF $\approx 0.6$, so the spike fails at that internode.

@mistakes
- Saying myelin raises the time constant: with $n$ layers, $R$ rises by $n$ and $C$ falls by $n$, so $\tau = R_m C_m$ is unchanged; the benefit is less charge to move and less leak.
- Thinking the spike is generated all along a myelinated axon: voltage-gated Na+ channels are clustered at nodes, and the internode conducts passively.
- Mixing units of delay: a distance in millimeters divided by a velocity in m/s gives milliseconds.
- Treating the rules $v \propto \sqrt{d}$ and $v \propto d$ as exact laws: they are approximate scaling rules over limited ranges.

```python
import math


def total_delay(segments):
    """Total delay (ms) along consecutive axon segments (length mm, d um, myelinated)."""
    total = 0.0
    for length_mm, d_um, myelinated in segments:
        v = 6.0 * d_um if myelinated else 0.95 * math.sqrt(d_um)
        total += length_mm / v
    return total
```
hint: Compute each segment's velocity from its own diameter and myelination, then add the delays (not the velocities).
solution: Delays add along a path, so the slowest segment dominates: in the third test, 40 mm of thin unmyelinated fiber contributes 42 ms while 40 mm of myelinated fiber contributes only 1.3 ms. Adding velocities, or averaging them, would be wrong.

model:
```python
import math


def first_block(amp, thr, internodes):
    """Index of the first internode where conduction fails, or None."""
    for i, (length_mm, lam_mm) in enumerate(internodes):
        if amp * math.exp(-length_mm / lam_mm) < thr:
            return i
    return None
```
hint: Check the internodes in order; the amplitude restarts at `amp` after every node, so each internode is independent.
solution: Each node regenerates the spike, so an internode succeeds or fails depending only on its own $L/\lambda$. Arriving exactly at threshold counts as success (the fifth test), which a strict `>` comparison gets wrong; the sixth test is just above it.

model:
```python
import math


def max_internode(amp, thr, lam):
    """Longest internode with safety factor >= 1: lam * ln(amp / thr)."""
    if thr <= 0:
        raise ValueError("threshold must be positive")
    if thr >= amp:
        return 0.0
    return lam * math.log(amp / thr)
```
hint: Set $A e^{-L/\lambda} = \theta$ and solve for $L$; then deal with the edge cases.
solution: Solving $A e^{-L/\lambda} = \theta$ gives $L = \lambda \ln(A/\theta)$. If the threshold is not below the amplitude the logarithm is zero or negative, so no internode length works. The last test, with $A/\theta = 2$, gives $\lambda \ln 2 = 0.693\lambda$.
