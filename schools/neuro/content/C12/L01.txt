@article
A neuron sits in salt water, and inside it is also salt water. Yet the inside holds about 140 mM potassium and the outside only about 5 mM, while sodium is the other way round. If you open only potassium channels in such a membrane, a measurable voltage appears with no battery attached. Where does it come from, and how large is it?

### The ingredients

Cell membranes are lipid bilayers that ions cannot cross on their own; they pass through **ion channels** (and are moved by pumps and transporters, covered in week 11). Two forces act on a permeant ion with charge $z$ (in units of the elementary charge): **diffusion** pushes it down its concentration gradient, and the **electric field** across the membrane pushes it along the voltage gradient. We write $V_m = V_\text{in} - V_\text{out}$, the **membrane potential**, in millivolts (mV).

Typical mammalian concentrations (mM) used throughout this course:

| ion | outside | inside |
|---|---|---|
| K+ | 5 | 140 |
| Na+ | 145 | 12 |
| Cl- | 110 | 10 |
| Ca2+ | 2 | 0.0001 |

These are round textbook values; they vary between cell types and species.

### The Nernst equation

At equilibrium the net flux of the ion through open channels is zero, so the electrochemical potential must be equal on both sides. For an ideal dilute solution that potential is $RT \ln c + zFV$ per mole, which gives the **Nernst equation**

$$E_\text{ion} = \frac{RT}{zF}\,\ln\frac{[\text{ion}]_\text{out}}{[\text{ion}]_\text{in}} .$$

Here $R = 8.314$ J/(mol K) is the gas constant, $F = 96485$ C/mol is the Faraday constant and $T$ is the absolute temperature. At $37\,^\circ$C ($310.15$ K), $RT/F = 26.73$ mV. In base-10 logarithms the prefactor is $61.5$ mV, a figure often quoted for a monovalent ion at body temperature. Equilibrium potentials depend on temperature, so always state it.

Applying it at $37\,^\circ$C:

| ion | $z$ | $E_\text{ion}$ (mV) |
|---|---|---|
| K+ | +1 | −89.06 |
| Na+ | +1 | +66.60 |
| Cl- | −1 | −64.09 |
| Ca2+ | +2 | +132.34 |

Note the sign of $z$: the same ratio gives opposite potentials for a cation and an anion. The **Nernst equation applies to a membrane permeable to one ion**; when several ions leak, the voltage is a compromise (next week). It also is not the same as the **resting potential**: a typical neuron rests near −65 mV, between $E_\text{K}$ and $E_\text{Na}$.

### Driving force and current direction

The quantity $V_m - E_\text{ion}$ is the **driving force**. By convention the current through the channels of one ion is $I = g\,(V_m - E_\text{ion})$, positive for outward current (positive charge leaving the cell). At $V_m = -65$ mV, K+ has driving force $-65 - (-89) = +24$ mV, so opening K+ channels gives outward current; Na+ has $-65 - 66.6 = -131.6$ mV, so opening Na+ channels gives a large inward current. When $V_m = E_\text{ion}$ there is no net flux.

### How little the concentrations change

Does the ion flow that sets up these voltages drain the gradients? Charge the membrane like a capacitor, $Q = C_m A V$. For a spherical cell of radius 10 µm with $C_m = 1$ µF/cm² and $V = 70$ mV, the charge corresponds to about $2.2 \times 10^{-3}$ mM of a monovalent ion, compared with 140 mM inside: roughly one part in $10^{5}$. Gradients are maintained by pumps, but a single electrical event barely changes them, which is why Nernst potentials can be treated as constants in the models of later weeks.

### In code

```python
import math

R, F = 8.314462618, 96485.33212  # J/(mol K), C/mol


def nernst(z, c_out, c_in, temp_c=37.0):
    """Equilibrium potential (mV) of an ion of charge z (concentrations in mM)."""
    rt_over_f = R * (temp_c + 273.15) / F  # volts
    return 1000.0 * rt_over_f / z * math.log(c_out / c_in)

print(round(nernst(1, 5, 140), 2))
print(round(nernst(-1, 110, 10), 2))
```

This prints `-89.06` and `-64.09`. Researchers write the same few lines in NumPy to compute many ions or temperatures at once, but nothing more is needed here.

@example
title: Raised extracellular potassium
problem: At $37\,^\circ$C, a neuron has $[\text{K}^+]_\text{in} = 140$ mM. How does $E_\text{K}$ change when $[\text{K}^+]_\text{out}$ rises from 5 mM to 10 mM, as in some pathological conditions?
step: Use $RT/F = 26.73$ mV at $37\,^\circ$C and $z = +1$, so $E_\text{K} = 26.73 \ln([\text{K}]_\text{out}/140)$.
step: For 10 mM the ratio is $10/140 = 0.0714$ and $\ln 0.0714 = -2.639$.
step: Multiply: $E_\text{K} = 26.73 \times (-2.639) = -70.5$ mV.
step: Compare with the normal $-89.1$ mV: doubling the outside concentration adds $26.73 \ln 2 = 18.5$ mV, so $E_\text{K}$ depolarizes by about 18.5 mV.
answer: $E_\text{K} \approx -70.5$ mV, about 18.5 mV less negative than at 5 mM.

@example
title: Chloride with low internal concentration
problem: A mature neuron keeps $[\text{Cl}^-]_\text{in} = 5$ mM while $[\text{Cl}^-]_\text{out} = 110$ mM. Find $E_\text{Cl}$ at $37\,^\circ$C and the direction of current when chloride channels open at $V_m = -65$ mV.
step: The charge is $z = -1$, so $E_\text{Cl} = -26.73 \ln(110/5)$.
step: $\ln 22 = 3.091$, so $E_\text{Cl} = -26.73 \times 3.091 = -82.6$ mV.
step: The driving force is $V_m - E_\text{Cl} = -65 - (-82.6) = +17.6$ mV, positive, so the current is outward.
step: Outward current means the chloride flux is inward: Cl- enters, carrying negative charge, so the cell hyperpolarizes toward $-82.6$ mV.
answer: $E_\text{Cl} \approx -82.6$ mV; the current is outward (Cl- flows in), which hyperpolarizes the cell.

@mistakes
- Forgetting the sign of $z$: for Cl- use $z = -1$, which flips the sign of $E_\text{Cl}$ relative to a cation with the same concentration ratio.
- Putting the concentrations the wrong way round: the ratio is outside over inside, and with the opposite ratio the sign of every potential flips.
- Treating $E_\text{ion}$ as the resting potential: the Nernst potential is the voltage at which one ion is at equilibrium, not what the membrane sits at when several ions leak.
- Using Celsius in $RT/F$: the temperature must be in kelvin (add 273.15).

def driving_directions(V, E_by_ion):
    """Direction of current (outward positive) through each ion's open channels."""
    out = {}
    for ion, E in E_by_ion.items():
        force = V - E
        if force > 1e-6:

            out[ion] = "inward"

            out[ion] = "zero"

model:
```python
import math

F = 96485.33212


def concentration_change(radius_um, c_spec, V_mV):
    """Concentration change (mM) needed to charge a spherical membrane."""
    r_cm = radius_um * 1e-4
    area = 4.0 * math.pi * r_cm ** 2                  # cm^2
    charge = c_spec * 1e-6 * area * V_mV * 1e-3       # coulombs
    moles = charge / F
    volume_l = 4.0 / 3.0 * math.pi * r_cm ** 3 * 1e-3  # liters
    return moles / volume_l * 1000.0
```
hint: Convert every quantity to base units first: cm for the radius, farads per cm² for the capacitance, volts for the potential.
solution: Charge equals capacitance times voltage; dividing by $F$ gives moles, and dividing by volume gives concentration. Because area scales as $r^2$ and volume as $r^3$, the concentration change scales as $1/r$, which the last test checks: a smaller cell needs a larger relative change, yet still a tiny one.

model:
```python
import math


def closest_ion(V, ions, temp_c=37.0):
    """Name of the ion whose Nernst potential is nearest to V (mV)."""
    rt_f = 8.314462618 * (temp_c + 273.15) / 96485.33212 * 1000.0
    best, best_gap = None, None
    for name, (z, c_out, c_in) in ions.items():
        E = rt_f / z * math.log(c_out / c_in)
        gap = abs(V - E)
        if best_gap is None or gap < best_gap:
            best, best_gap = name, gap
    return best
```
hint: Compute each Nernst potential with its own $z$ (the sign matters for Cl-), then keep the ion with the smallest $|V - E|$.
solution: Loop over the ions, compute $E = (RT/zF)\ln(c_\text{out}/c_\text{in})$ and track the smallest absolute difference. The last test is a near tie: at $V = 0$ mV and 20 °C, $E_\text{Na}$ is +62.9 mV and $E_\text{Cl}$ is −60.6 mV, so Cl- is closer by about 2 mV; getting the sign of $z$ wrong for Cl- would pick Na+ or Ca2+ instead.
