@article
Inject a small, steady current into a neuron and the voltage does not jump: it creeps toward a new level over tens of milliseconds, and when the current stops it creeps back. That slowness is the first computational property of a neuron, because it decides how long an input is remembered. It comes from two ordinary components, a resistor and a capacitor.

### Two parallel components

The lipid bilayer is an insulator between two conducting solutions, so it acts as a **capacitor**: charge $Q = C V$ builds up on its two faces. A typical specific capacitance is $C_m = 1\ \mu\text{F/cm}^2$. The open ion channels let current through, so they act as a **resistor**; the leak conductance has a specific resistance $R_m$, with typical values of 10 to 50 kΩ·cm². For a patch with area $A$, the whole cell has capacitance $C = C_m A$ and **input resistance** $R = R_m/A$.

Currents entering the cell must go somewhere: through the capacitor, which changes the voltage, or through the resistor. With a leak reversal potential $E_L$ (the potential at which the leak current vanishes) and an injected current $I$, conservation of current gives

$$C\,\frac{dV}{dt} = -\frac{V - E_L}{R} + I(t) .$$

Multiplying by $R$ gives $\tau\,dV/dt = -(V - E_L) + R I$ with the **membrane time constant** $\tau = RC$. Units: with $R$ in MΩ and $I$ in pA, $RI$ is in µV, so $RI$ in mV is $R I/1000$. With $C$ in pF, $\tau$ in ms is $R\,C/1000$.

### A current step

Take a cell of area $10{,}000\ \mu\text{m}^2 = 10^{-4}\ \text{cm}^2$ with $R_m = 15\ \text{k}\Omega\,\text{cm}^2$ and $C_m = 1\ \mu\text{F/cm}^2$. Then $C = 100$ pF, $R = 150$ MΩ and $\tau = 15$ ms. Starting at $E_L = -70$ mV, a step of $I = 40$ pA drives the voltage toward $V_\infty = E_L + RI = -70 + 6 = -64$ mV:

$$V(t) = V_\infty + (V_0 - V_\infty)\,e^{-t/\tau} .$$

| time (ms) | 0 | 15 | 30 | 45 | 60 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $V$ (mV) | −70.00 | −66.21 | −64.81 | −64.30 | −64.11 |

At $t = 0$ the capacitor is uncharged, so all of the current charges it; at the end the capacitor current is zero and all of the current crosses the resistor. When the step ends, the voltage relaxes back to $E_L$ with the same $\tau$. After $\tau$ the voltage has covered 63% of the gap, after $2\tau$ 86%, after $3\tau$ 95%.

### Size and conductance

Bigger cells have more membrane: $C$ grows with $A$ while $R$ shrinks, so the product $\tau = R_m C_m$ does not depend on cell size, but the voltage response $RI$ to a given current does. Opening channels, for example synaptic or potassium channels, adds conductance in parallel: $1/R$ grows, so both the steady response and $\tau$ shrink. This is the basis of **shunting**, which week 9 develops.

### Filtering

A sinusoidal current of frequency $f$ produces a voltage oscillation with amplitude $|Z| I$, where the impedance magnitude is

$$|Z(f)| = \frac{R}{\sqrt{1 + (2\pi f\tau)^2}} ,$$

and the voltage lags the current by $\arctan(2\pi f\tau)$. The membrane is a **low-pass filter** with cutoff $f_c = 1/(2\pi\tau)$, where the amplitude has fallen to $1/\sqrt{2}$ of its low-frequency value. For $\tau = 15$ ms, $f_c = 10.6$ Hz. At 20 Hz the gain is $0.469R = 70.3$ MΩ and the lag is $62^\circ$: fast fluctuations are smoothed away.

### Voltage clamp

The experimenter can also hold the voltage and measure current. A step of $\Delta V$ through a series (access) resistance $R_s$ makes the capacitor charge with current $I(t) = (\Delta V/R_s)\,e^{-t/(R_s C)}$. The area under this transient is $Q = C\,\Delta V$, so integrating it estimates $C$: with $I$ in pA, $t$ in ms and $\Delta V$ in mV, $\int I\,dt$ is in fC and $C = Q/\Delta V$ comes out in pF.

### In code

If $I$ is constant during a step of length $\Delta t$, the equation can be advanced exactly:

```python
import math


def rc_trace(currents, dt, R, tau, E_L):
    """Voltage (mV) at the end of each step; currents in pA, R in MOhm, times in ms."""
    decay = math.exp(-dt / tau)
    V, trace = E_L, []
    for I in currents:
        V_inf = E_L + R * I / 1000.0
        V = V_inf + (V - V_inf) * decay
        trace.append(V)
    return trace

print([round(v, 3) for v in rc_trace([40, 40, 40, 0, 0], 5.0, 150.0, 15.0, -70.0)])
```

This prints `[-68.299, -67.081, -66.207, -67.282, -68.053]`: three 5 ms steps of charging, then discharging. Because each step is exact for constant current, this update is stable for any $\Delta t$, unlike the Euler method $V \leftarrow V + (\Delta t/\tau)(-(V - E_L) + RI/1000)$, whose error grows with the step.

@example
title: Time to reach a voltage
problem: The cell in the article ($R = 150$ MΩ, $\tau = 15$ ms, $E_L = -70$ mV) receives a 40 pA step. How long does it take for the voltage to reach $-66$ mV?
step: The steady state is $V_\infty = -70 + 150 \times 40/1000 = -64$ mV, and the voltage starts $6$ mV below it.
step: The target $-66$ mV is $2$ mV below $V_\infty$, so we need $V_\infty - V(t) = 6\,e^{-t/\tau} = 2$.
step: Solve: $e^{-t/\tau} = 1/3$, so $t = \tau \ln 3 = 15 \times 1.0986 = 16.5$ ms.
step: Check: $V(16.5) = -64 - 6\,e^{-1.1} = -64 - 6 \times 0.333 = -66.0$ mV.
answer: About $16.5$ ms.

@example
title: Two cells of different size
problem: Two spherical neurons have $R_m = 15\ \text{k}\Omega\,\text{cm}^2$ and $C_m = 1\ \mu\text{F/cm}^2$. Cell A has area $10{,}000\ \mu\text{m}^2$ and cell B has area $40{,}000\ \mu\text{m}^2$. Compare their input resistances, capacitances, time constants and responses to a 40 pA step.
step: Convert areas: $10^{-4}$ and $4 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{cm}^2$. Capacitances $C = C_m A$ are 100 pF and 400 pF.
step: Input resistances $R = R_m/A$: $15000/10^{-4} = 150$ MΩ for A, and $15000/(4\times10^{-4}) = 37.5$ MΩ for B.
step: Time constants $RC$: A has $150 \times 100/1000 = 15$ ms; B has $37.5 \times 400/1000 = 15$ ms. They are equal.
step: Steady responses $R I$: A gives $6$ mV, B gives $1.5$ mV.
answer: Same $\tau = 15$ ms; the larger cell has four times the capacitance, one quarter of the input resistance and one quarter of the voltage response.

@mistakes
- Mixing units: MΩ times pA gives µV, not mV; divide by 1000 (or use nA) to get millivolts.
- Thinking a bigger cell charges more slowly: $\tau = R_m C_m$ is independent of area; what changes is $R$ and $C$ separately.
- Using Euler steps with $\Delta t$ comparable to $\tau$: the error is proportional to $\Delta t$, and for $\Delta t > 2\tau$ the update oscillates and diverges.
- Confusing the cutoff frequency with $1/\tau$: $f_c = 1/(2\pi\tau)$, because $f$ is in cycles per second and the angular frequency is $2\pi f$.

model:
```python
import math


def euler_max_error(I, R, tau, E_L, t_end, dt):
    """Largest |Euler - exact| (mV) for a current step into a passive membrane."""
    V_inf = E_L + R * I / 1000.0
    V = E_L
    worst = 0.0
    for k in range(1, int(round(t_end / dt)) + 1):
        V += dt / tau * (-(V - E_L) + R * I / 1000.0)
        exact = V_inf + (E_L - V_inf) * math.exp(-k * dt / tau)
        worst = max(worst, abs(V - exact))
    return worst
```
hint: Keep the Euler voltage in one variable and compute the exact value at the same time $k\,dt$ in every step; track the maximum of the absolute difference.
solution: Euler's error is proportional to the step: halving $dt$ roughly halves the largest error, which the last test checks. With no current the voltage never leaves $E_L$ and the error is zero.

```python
import math


def estimate_tau(times, volts, V_inf):
    """Time constant (ms) from a log-linear least-squares fit of the relaxation."""
    ys = [math.log(V_inf - v) for v in volts]
    n = len(times)
    mean_t = sum(times) / n
    mean_y = sum(ys) / n
    sxy = sum((t - mean_t) * (y - mean_y) for t, y in zip(times, ys))
    sxx = sum((t - mean_t) ** 2 for t in times)
    slope = sxy / sxx
    return -1.0 / slope
```
hint: Take the logarithm of the remaining distance $V_\infty - V$; for an exponential it is a straight line in $t$ with slope $-1/\tau$.
solution: Since $V_\infty - V = (V_\infty - V_0)e^{-t/\tau}$, the log of the remaining distance is linear in time with slope $-1/\tau$. Ordinary least squares gives the slope, and $\tau$ is minus its reciprocal. With two points the fit passes through both, as the last test checks.

model:
```python
def capacitance_from_transient(times, currents, I_ss, dV):
    """Capacitance (pF) from the area of a capacitive transient (pA, ms, mV)."""
    charge = 0.0
    for k in range(len(times) - 1):
        dt = times[k + 1] - times[k]
        a = currents[k] - I_ss
        b = currents[k + 1] - I_ss
        charge += 0.5 * (a + b) * dt
    return charge / dV
```
hint: The charge is the area between the current trace and the steady level; with samples at unequal times, add one trapezoid per interval.
solution: Charge in femtocoulombs is the integral of $(I - I_\text{ss})$ in pA·ms, and $C = Q/\Delta V$ comes out in pF. The steady current flowing through the leak must be subtracted, because it does not charge the capacitor; the fourth test uses unequal sample spacing, which the trapezoid rule handles interval by interval.
