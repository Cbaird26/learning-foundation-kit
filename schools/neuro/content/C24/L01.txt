@article
An extracellular electrode near a neuron records a voltage trace with sharp, brief deflections: action potentials. After spike detection and spike sorting (the step that assigns each deflection to one neuron), what is left of the neuron's activity is a list of times. The shape of each spike is thrown away, because every action potential of a neuron is nearly the same size and carries no extra message; all the information is in *when* spikes happen. This lesson builds the vocabulary and the first computations for those lists.

### Spike trains and trials

A **spike train** is the sorted list of spike times of one neuron. In this course, unless a problem says otherwise, times are in **seconds** (s), measured from the start of a trial or from a stimulus onset at $t = 0$ (so a spike before the stimulus has a negative time). An experiment repeats the same condition many times; each repetition is a **trial**, and the data are a list of spike trains, one per trial:

```python
trials = [
    [0.012, 0.085, 0.140, 0.310, 0.455, 0.720],
    [0.050, 0.095, 0.300, 0.610],
    [0.020, 0.230, 0.235, 0.480, 0.490, 0.910, 0.990],
]
```

A **raster** plots one row per trial and one tick per spike at its time. Since the site shows no images, read a raster as a table: row 1 has ticks at 12, 85, 140, 310, 455 and 720 ms, and so on. A vertical stripe of ticks at the same time in many rows means the neuron fires at a consistent time on every trial; a smear with no stripes means the timing varies from trial to trial.

### Counts and rates

The **spike count** $n$ in a window is the number of spikes with $t_0 \le t < t_1$. This window is **half-open**: it includes $t_0$ and excludes $t_1$, so adjacent windows $[0, 0.5)$ and $[0.5, 1)$ never count a spike twice and never lose one. A spike exactly at $0.5$ s belongs to the second window.

The **firing rate** over the window is the count divided by its duration,

$$r = \frac{n}{t_1 - t_0},$$

in hertz (spikes per second) when times are in seconds. For $N$ trials, the **mean rate** pools the counts: $\bar r = \frac{1}{N (t_1 - t_0)} \sum_{j=1}^{N} n_j$. In the data above, the counts in $[0, 1)$ are $6, 4, 7$, so $\bar r = 17/3 \approx 5.67$ Hz. In $[0.2, 0.5)$ the counts are $2, 1, 4$ and $\bar r = 7 / (3 \times 0.3) \approx 7.78$ Hz.

```python
def count_in_window(spikes, t0, t1):
    """Spikes with t0 <= t < t1."""
    return sum(1 for t in spikes if t0 <= t < t1)


def mean_rate(trials, t0, t1):
    """Mean firing rate (Hz) over trials; times in seconds."""
    total = sum(count_in_window(tr, t0, t1) for tr in trials)
    return total / (len(trials) * (t1 - t0))
```

A rate computed from a handful of spikes is uncertain: a count of 2 could easily have been 1 or 4 on another trial. Reporting a rate without the window length and the number of trials is not a complete result.

### Interspike intervals

The **interspike intervals** (ISIs) are the gaps between consecutive spikes, $\Delta_i = t_{i+1} - t_i$. A train of $n$ spikes has $n - 1$ ISIs; one spike or none gives an empty list. ISIs are often reported in milliseconds, so multiply seconds by 1000. A neuron cannot fire twice within its absolute refractory period (about 1 to 2 ms), so very short ISIs in sorted data are a warning that two neurons were merged. If the ISIs were all equal to $\Delta$, the rate would be $1/\Delta$; with irregular ISIs the mean rate over a long window is the count over the duration, and $1/\overline{\Delta}$ is only roughly the same.

### What a rate hides

A rate over a long window averages away timing. A neuron that fires six spikes in a 50 ms burst and is silent otherwise has the same 6 Hz mean rate over a second as one that fires every 167 ms. The next lessons keep time resolution by dividing the window into bins.

@example
title: Rates in two adjacent windows
problem: One trial (times in seconds) has spikes at $0.04, 0.09, 0.31, 0.50, 0.52, 0.75, 0.97$. Compute the firing rate in $[0, 0.5)$ and in $[0.5, 1.0)$, and the ISIs of the spikes in the second window in milliseconds.
step: Count the first window: $0.04, 0.09, 0.31$ lie in $[0, 0.5)$. The spike at exactly $0.50$ is excluded because the window is half-open. So $n = 3$ and $r = 3/0.5 = 6$ Hz.
step: Count the second window: $0.50$ is included, and so are $0.52, 0.75, 0.97$. So $n = 4$ and $r = 4/0.5 = 8$ Hz. The seven spikes are all accounted for, $3 + 4 = 7$.
step: ISIs in the second window: $0.52 - 0.50 = 0.02$ s, $0.75 - 0.52 = 0.23$ s, $0.97 - 0.75 = 0.22$ s.
step: Convert to milliseconds by multiplying by 1000: $20, 230, 220$ ms. Four spikes give three ISIs.
answer: $6$ Hz and $8$ Hz; ISIs of $20, 230, 220$ ms.

@mistakes
- Counting a spike on a window boundary in both windows (or in neither): decide the convention once, half-open $[t_0, t_1)$, and use it everywhere.
- Mixing units: a count over a window in milliseconds divided by its length in milliseconds gives spikes per millisecond, not hertz; multiply by 1000.
- Dividing a pooled count by the duration but forgetting the number of trials, which reports a total rather than a mean rate.
- Computing ISIs with the first spike of a trial and the last spike of the previous trial: ISIs live inside one trial.

```python
def rate_change(trials, base, resp):
    """Mean rate in the response window minus mean rate in the baseline."""
    def mean_rate(window):
        t0, t1 = window
        total = sum(1 for tr in trials for t in tr if t0 <= t < t1)
        return total / (len(trials) * (t1 - t0))

    return mean_rate(resp) - mean_rate(base)
```

model:
```python
def binarize(spikes, t_max, bin_ms):
    """0/1 spike indicator per bin; spikes in ms, half-open bins."""
    n_bins = int(t_max // bin_ms)
    out = [0] * n_bins
    for t in spikes:
        if 0 <= t < n_bins * bin_ms:
            out[int(t // bin_ms)] = 1
    return out
```
hint: The bin of a spike at time $t$ is the integer part of $t$ divided by the bin width. Set that entry to 1 rather than adding 1, and skip spikes outside the bins.
solution: Floor division assigns a spike exactly on an edge to the bin that starts there, matching the half-open convention. Assigning 1 instead of incrementing makes two spikes in one bin count once, and the range test drops spikes at or after the last edge.
