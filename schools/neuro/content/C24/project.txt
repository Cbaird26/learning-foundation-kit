material: **Conventions.** Spike times are in seconds; convert to milliseconds for the PSTH. Bins are half-open: a spike exactly on an edge belongs to the bin that starts there, and a spike at exactly 1 s (none here) would be dropped. The Gaussian kernel for smoothing has weights proportional to exp(-j^2 / 2) for j = -2..2, divided by their sum, and values outside the PSTH count as 0. The cosine fit uses c1 = (2/N) sum r cos(theta), c2 = (2/N) sum r sin(theta), b = mean rate, a = sqrt(c1^2 + c2^2), theta0 = atan2(c2, c1) in degrees in [0, 360). The Fano factor uses the sample variance (divisor n - 1). Use natural logarithms. In the decoder, ties go to the smallest direction.

### Week by week

- **Weeks 1-2: spikes and PSTHs.** A spike train is a sorted list of times; a window is half-open, $[t_0, t_1)$. Rate $= n/(t_1 - t_0)$. A PSTH divides counts by $N w/1000$ (ms bins). Smoothing with a normalized kernel keeps the area; wider kernels lower and widen peaks. Report uncertainty across trials.
- **Weeks 3-4: tuning and Poisson.** Tuning curve = mean rate per stimulus. Cosine fit by the closed form for equally spaced angles; Gaussian center by grid search of the sum of squared errors. The Fano factor $s^2/\bar n$ and the ISI CV equal 1 for a Poisson process; the maximum-likelihood rate is total count over total time.
- **Weeks 5-6: correlations and the Fourier transform.** Noise correlation is a Pearson correlation of counts at a fixed stimulus. Cross-correlogram lags $d = t_B - t_A$ in half-open bins. The DFT has no $1/N$; $f_k = k/(N\Delta t)$, $\Delta f = 1/(N\Delta t)$; amplitude is $2|X_k|/N$; aliasing folds frequencies above the Nyquist frequency.
- **Weeks 7-8: spectra and calcium.** One-sided periodogram $2\Delta t|X_k|^2/N$ obeys Parseval; detrend and window to reduce leakage; band power is $\sum S_k\Delta f$. Calcium: $\Delta F/F_0$, the AR(1) model $c_t = \gamma c_{t-1} + a s_t$, deconvolution $c_t - \gamma c_{t-1}$ clipped at zero, noise amplified by $\sqrt{1 + \gamma^2}$.
- **Weeks 9-10: encoding and decoding.** STA, the LNP model $\lambda = \exp(b + ws)$, the Poisson log-likelihood and its gradient $\sum(y - \mu)x$. Population vector, maximum-likelihood decoding, Fisher information $T\sum f'^2/f$ and the Cramér-Rao bound.
- **Weeks 11-12: populations and pipelines.** Center, covariance with $n - 1$, power iteration, deflation, explained variance, participation ratio. Pipelines: load, validate, analyze, report; parameters in one place, supplied or seeded randomness, bootstrap over trials.

### Common errors

- Counting edge spikes twice, or mixing seconds and milliseconds.
- Dividing by the wrong number of trials, bins or $N$ versus $N - 1$.
- Forgetting the $\Delta$ in the Poisson mean $\lambda\Delta$, or the factor 2 in the one-sided spectrum.
- Pooling stimuli before computing noise correlations or Fano factors.
- Decoding on the data used to fit the tuning.
- Over-reading a result from few trials.

model:
```python
import math


def decode_single(train, test_spikes, t0, t1):
    """Maximum-likelihood stimulus from one neuron's spike count."""
    T = t1 - t0
    n = sum(1 for t in test_spikes if t0 <= t < t1)
    best, best_ll = None, None
    for s in sorted(train):
        trials = train[s]
        total = sum(1 for tr in trials for t in tr if t0 <= t < t1)
        rate = (total + 0.5) / (len(trials) * T)
        ll = n * math.log(rate * T) - rate * T
        if best_ll is None or ll > best_ll:
            best, best_ll = s, ll
    return best
```
hint: Estimate one rate per stimulus from its pooled counts, then compute the log-likelihood of the test count under each and keep the first maximum.
solution: The pseudo-count 0.5 keeps the estimated rate positive, so the logarithm is defined even for a stimulus with no training spikes. Trying stimuli in increasing order and replacing the best only on a strictly larger value sends ties to the smaller stimulus (the fourth test). The last test uses a window of 0.5 s, so the test spike at exactly 0.5 s is excluded and the count is 1.

@exam self
prompt: A colleague records two neurons and finds a narrow peak in their cross-correlogram at lags $+2$ to $+4$ ms (lag $= t_B - t_A$), well above the count expected by chance. She concludes that neuron A excites neuron B. List the analyses and controls you would run before accepting this, and say what additional evidence would be needed to support a causal claim.
hint: Think about stimulus-locked firing rates, the chance level, repeated trials and perturbation.
solution: First compute the chance level, $n_A n_B w/T$, and check that the peak is far above it, with a count large enough that Poisson scatter cannot explain it. Second, test whether common stimulus drive could produce the peak: recompute the correlogram with a shift predictor (shifting trials or using spikes from different trials of the same stimulus) and subtract it; a peak that survives is not due to shared stimulus-locked rate changes. Third, check the timing: a monosynaptic excitatory effect is expected within a few milliseconds and should be asymmetric (a peak at positive lag, none at negative), whereas a common input usually gives a symmetric peak around zero. Fourth, check the data quality: spike sorting errors and a single cell split into two units can produce a peak near zero. Fifth, repeat on separate halves of the data to see that the peak is stable. Even then, a correlogram peak is correlational: a causal claim needs a perturbation, for example stimulating A (optogenetically or electrically) and showing that B's spiking changes with the same short delay, or anatomical evidence of a direct connection.

rubric: Says that correlation is not causation and proposes a perturbation or anatomical evidence for a causal claim.
