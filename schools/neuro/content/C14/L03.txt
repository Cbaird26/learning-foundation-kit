@article
Write a character whose middle shape is halfway between H and A, and put it into the words THE and CAT. Most readers see the same shape as an H in the first word and an A in the second, and never notice. Recognition is not a readout of the stimulus: the same input is interpreted differently depending on its context. Cognitive psychology asks how recognition combines **bottom-up** processing (driven by the stimulus) with **top-down** processing (driven by knowledge and expectation).

### Three accounts of pattern recognition

- **Template matching**: the stimulus is compared with stored templates, one per pattern. It is simple, but it fails for variation in size, orientation, font and handwriting, and implies an unmanageable number of templates.
- **Feature detection**: the stimulus is analyzed into features (lines, curves, angles) that are combined to identify the pattern. Selfridge's Pandemonium model (1959) imagined feature "demons" shouting for letter "demons"; the idea fits the finding of orientation-tuned cells in visual cortex (Hubel and Wiesel, 1959 and 1962).
- **Structural description**: objects are described by parts and the relations among them. In Biederman's (1987) **recognition-by-components** theory, objects are represented as arrangements of a small set of simple volumes called geons (a few dozen), which can be identified from many viewpoints. Evidence includes the difficulty of recognizing objects when the points where parts meet are deleted. Whether human recognition relies on viewpoint-independent parts or on stored views is debated.

### Context and the word superiority effect

In the task of Reicher (1969; also Wheeler, 1970) a letter string is flashed briefly, masked, and you choose between two letters for one position. A letter is identified more accurately when it is in a real word than in a scrambled string, and (surprisingly) more accurately than when it appears alone. The control is the key: both choice letters complete a real word (for example, D or K for WORD versus WORK), so word-based guessing cannot explain the advantage. The **interactive activation model** (McClelland and Rumelhart, 1981) explains it with feature, letter and word units connected by excitatory links in both directions: active word units feed activation back to their letters.

### Perception as inference

An ambiguous input can be handled by Bayes' rule: the posterior probability of an interpretation $h$ given sensory evidence $e$ is

$$P(h \mid e) = \frac{P(e \mid h)\,P(h)}{\sum_{h'} P(e \mid h')\,P(h')} .$$

Here $P(e\mid h)$, the likelihood, is the bottom-up part and $P(h)$, the prior, is the top-down part. If the middle shape fits A slightly better ($P(e\mid A)=0.6$, $P(e\mid H)=0.4$) but the context T_E makes H far more probable ($P(H)=0.9$), the posterior for H is $0.4 \times 0.9 / (0.4 \times 0.9 + 0.6 \times 0.1) = 0.857$. This framing is influential, though it is a description at the computational level; it does not tell us how the brain computes. **Gestalt principles** (Wertheimer, 1923) describe how inputs are organized into wholes: proximity, similarity, closure, good continuation and common fate. Faces illustrate holistic processing: Yin (1969) showed that turning a face upside down impairs recognition much more than turning other familiar objects upside down.

### Measuring perception: signal detection theory

A detection task has two kinds of trial, signal and noise. The observer says "yes" or "no," giving hits $H$ (yes on signal trials) and false alarms $F$ (yes on noise trials). Signal detection theory (Green and Swets, 1966) assumes the internal evidence on noise trials and signal trials follows two overlapping Gaussian distributions with equal spread. Two quantities separate how well signal is discriminated from how willing the observer is to say yes:

$$d' = z(H) - z(F), \qquad c = -\tfrac{1}{2}\,[\,z(H) + z(F)\,],$$

where $z$ is the inverse of the standard normal cumulative distribution. Sensitivity $d'$ is the distance between the distributions in standard-deviation units; $c = 0$ is an unbiased criterion, $c > 0$ is conservative (fewer "yes"), and $c < 0$ is liberal. Payoffs and base rates move $c$ and leave $d'$ about the same; hit rate alone is not a measure of sensitivity.

### How far does expectation reach?

Whether expectations change what we see, or only how we judge, remember or attend to what we see, is the **cognitive penetrability** debate. Early "New Look" claims that, for example, needs and values change perceived size were challenged because the effects could arise from response bias; attention and memory effects are well documented, but demonstrations that beliefs alter perception itself remain contested.
@example
title: Sensitivity and bias from two rates
problem: An observer detects a faint tone with hit rate $H = 0.90$ and false-alarm rate $F = 0.30$. The z-scores are $z(0.90) = 1.282$ and $z(0.30) = -0.524$. Compute $d'$ and $c$ and describe the criterion.
step: $d' = z(H) - z(F) = 1.282 - (-0.524) = 1.806$.
step: $c = -\tfrac{1}{2}(1.282 + (-0.524)) = -\tfrac{1}{2}(0.758) = -0.379$.
step: $c$ is negative, so the observer's criterion is liberal: they say "yes" readily, which buys a high hit rate at the price of many false alarms.
answer: $d' \approx 1.81$, $c \approx -0.38$ (liberal).

@example
title: Combining shape and context
problem: A character's shape fits letter H with likelihood $0.55$ and letter A with likelihood $0.45$. The surrounding context gives prior probabilities $P(H) = 0.9$ and $P(A) = 0.1$. What is the posterior probability of H?
step: Multiply each likelihood by its prior: H gives $0.55 \times 0.9 = 0.495$; A gives $0.45 \times 0.1 = 0.045$.
step: Normalize by the sum, $0.495 + 0.045 = 0.540$.
step: The posterior for H is $0.495/0.540 \approx 0.917$; the shape alone slightly favored H and the context made it strongly so.
answer: About $0.917$.

@mistakes
- Treating a high hit rate as high sensitivity: an observer who says "yes" to nearly everything gets a hit rate near 1 but a false-alarm rate near 1 too, so $d'$ is near 0.
- Calling a negative $c$ "conservative": negative values mean a liberal criterion, positive values a conservative one.
- Concluding from the word superiority effect that words are recognized before letters: the effect shows that word-level knowledge influences letter identification, which interactive models explain with feedback.
- Treating Bayes' rule as a claim about neurons: it is a computational-level description of how evidence and prior knowledge could combine.

@quiz self
prompt: A researcher claims that observers who are told a hill is going to be climbed with a heavy backpack see it as steeper. Explain why this result does not by itself show that expectation changes perception, name two alternative explanations, and describe an experiment or control that could separate perception from judgment.
hint: Ask what else besides visual experience could change the response the observer gives.
solution: The measured response is a verbal or manual estimate, and an estimate can shift because of response bias without any change in what is seen; for example, participants may infer what the researcher wants (demand characteristics), or the instruction may change which cues they attend to or how they scale their reports. To separate perception from judgment, one could use a measure less open to demand, such as a perceptual discrimination task analyzed with signal detection theory so that a change in $d'$ or in the point of subjective equality can be distinguished from a change in criterion, use a task with no obvious link to the manipulation, assess whether participants can guess the hypothesis, and replicate with a blind experimenter. Defenders of cognitive penetrability would respond that such controls can themselves be insensitive, and that some effects survive them; the debate concerns whether the early visual stage is influenced by beliefs or only later stages.
