@article
Is it more likely that a word begins with the letter K or has K as its third letter? Most people say the first position, because words that start with K are easier to bring to mind. In fact K is more common in third position. Tversky and Kahneman (1973) used such examples to show that judgments of frequency rest on **availability**, the ease of recall, which often tracks frequency but not always. This started the **heuristics and biases** program (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974): people use rules of thumb that are useful but produce systematic errors.

### Heuristics and their classic evidence

- **Representativeness**: judging probability by resemblance to a stereotype, which neglects base rates. In the "Linda" problem (Tversky and Kahneman, 1983), a woman is described as outspoken and concerned with social justice, and most participants rate "bank teller and active in the feminist movement" as more probable than "bank teller." That is the **conjunction fallacy**, since $P(A \wedge B) \le P(A)$ always.
- **Availability**: described above.
- **Anchoring and adjustment**: in a classic demonstration a wheel of fortune, visibly random, landed on 10 or 65, and participants then estimated the percentage of African countries in the United Nations. The median estimates were about 25 and about 45. Anchoring effects are among the most robust in the literature, replicated across many labs (for example, in the large multi-site Many Labs project of Klein and colleagues, 2014).

### Bayes' rule and base rates

When a test result is positive, the probability of the condition depends on its base rate $P(D)$, the sensitivity $P(+\mid D)$ and the false-positive rate $P(+\mid \neg D)$:

$$P(D \mid +) = \frac{P(+\mid D)\,P(D)}{P(+\mid D)\,P(D) + P(+\mid \neg D)\,P(\neg D)} .$$

People often give an answer close to the sensitivity, neglecting the base rate. Gigerenzer and Hoffrage (1995) showed that the same problem posed in **natural frequencies** ("of 1000 people, 20 have the condition...") elicits more correct answers than the probability version, which is one of the strongest pieces of evidence for the ecological-rationality critique (Gigerenzer and Todd, 1999) that heuristics are adapted to environments and that errors depend on the format of information. How far this undermines the "biases" interpretation is debated.

### Expected utility and its violations

The normative model is **expected value** (or, with a utility function, **expected utility**, formalized by von Neumann and Morgenstern, 1944). The **Allais paradox** (Allais, 1953) shows a systematic violation. Choice 1: A, \$1 million for certain, versus B, an 89% chance of \$1 million, a 10% chance of \$5 million and a 1% chance of nothing. Choice 2: C, an 11% chance of \$1 million versus D, a 10% chance of \$5 million. Many people choose A and D. But for any utility function, $EU(A) - EU(B) = 0.11\,u(1) - 0.10\,u(5) - 0.01\,u(0)$ and $EU(C) - EU(D) = 0.11\,u(1) - 0.10\,u(5) - 0.01\,u(0)$, which are identical, so preferring A to B implies preferring C to D. The common 89% consequence should not matter, but it does.

### Prospect theory

Kahneman and Tversky (1979) proposed that people evaluate outcomes as gains and losses relative to a **reference point**, with three features: **diminishing sensitivity** (the value function is concave for gains and convex for losses), **loss aversion** (losses loom larger than equal gains), and **probability weighting** (small probabilities are overweighted and moderate to large ones underweighted). In cumulative prospect theory (Tversky and Kahneman, 1992), median parameter estimates were:

$$v(x) = x^{\alpha}\ (x \ge 0), \quad v(x) = -\lambda\,(-x)^{\beta}\ (x < 0), \qquad \alpha = \beta = 0.88,\ \lambda = 2.25,$$

$$w(p) = \frac{p^{\gamma}}{\big(p^{\gamma} + (1-p)^{\gamma}\big)^{1/\gamma}}, \qquad \gamma = 0.61 \text{ for gains.}$$

With these values $v(100) = 57.5$, $v(-100) = -129.5$, and $w(0.01) = 0.055$ but $w(0.5) = 0.42$. The framing of the **Asian disease problem** (Tversky and Kahneman, 1981) shows the reference point at work. Describing a program as saving 200 of 600 people for sure, versus a one-third chance of saving all and a two-thirds chance of saving none, leads about 72% to choose the sure option. Describing the same outcomes as 400 of 600 dying for sure, versus a one-third chance that nobody dies and a two-thirds chance that all die, leads about 78% to choose the gamble. Gain and loss framing effects have been replicated in large projects, with effect sizes that depend on wording and sample. Loss aversion is a core idea but its size and generality are debated: some researchers argue that it is weaker or absent for small stakes or that attention to losses explains part of it.
@example
title: A positive test from natural frequencies
problem: A condition affects 2% of a population. A test is positive for 90% of people who have it and for 7% of people who do not. What is the probability of having the condition given a positive test?
step: Take 1000 people: 20 have the condition and 980 do not.
step: Of the 20 who have it, $0.90 \times 20 = 18$ test positive; of the 980 who do not, $0.07 \times 980 = 68.6$ test positive.
step: All positives: $18 + 68.6 = 86.6$; the probability is $18/86.6 \approx 0.208$.
step: The answer is far below the 90% sensitivity because the condition is rare: most positives come from the large group without it.
answer: About $0.21$.

@example
title: Risk attitudes from the value function
problem: Use $v(x) = x^{0.88}$ for gains and $v(x) = -2.25(-x)^{0.88}$ for losses, with probabilities used as given ($w(p) = p$) to isolate the value function. Compare a sure gain of 50 with a 50% chance of 100 (otherwise 0), and a sure loss of 50 with a 50% chance of losing 100.
step: $v(50) = 31.27$, $v(100) = 57.54$, so the gamble's value is $0.5 \times 57.54 = 28.77$, lower than 31.27: the sure gain is preferred (risk aversion for gains).
step: $v(-50) = -70.35$ and $v(-100) = -129.47$, so the gamble's value is $0.5 \times (-129.47) = -64.74$, higher than $-70.35$: the gamble is preferred (risk seeking for losses).
step: Concave value for gains and convex for losses produce this reflection, a pattern also shown in the framing example. With the weighting function the values change (since $w(0.5) = 0.42$), so this is an illustration of the shape, not a prediction.
answer: The sure gain is preferred, and the risky loss is preferred.

@mistakes
- Answering a base-rate problem with the test's sensitivity: the posterior also depends on the base rate and the false-positive rate.
- Assuming that every judgment bias means irrationality: some errors shrink when the same problem is posed in natural frequencies, and a heuristic can be accurate in the environment it evolved for.
- Reading the value function as measuring utility of total wealth: it is defined over changes relative to a reference point.
- Treating loss aversion as a single settled number: estimates vary with stakes, domain and method.

choice: Expected utility is only defined for outcomes in dollars, and these are in millions.

solution: With utilities $u(0)$, $u(1)$ and $u(5)$, both differences equal $0.11\,u(1) - 0.10\,u(5) - 0.01\,u(0)$. A preference for A over B therefore means a preference for C over D, and the common choice of A with D violates expected utility.

@practice ms
prompt: Which statements about the conjunction rule and the Linda problem are correct? Select all that apply.
choice*: The probability of "A and B" can never exceed the probability of "A."
choice*: Ranking "bank teller and feminist" as more probable than "bank teller" violates the conjunction rule.
choice: The conjunction rule applies only when both events are unlikely.
choice: The Linda problem shows that people cannot reason about probability in any format.
hint: The rule follows from set inclusion: everyone in the group "A and B" is in the group "A."
solution: Since every case of $A \wedge B$ is a case of $A$, $P(A \wedge B) \le P(A)$ for all events. The Linda ranking violates it. The rule holds whatever the probabilities are, and violations are reduced in some frequency formats, so the finding does not show a general inability to reason about probability.

@quiz self
prompt: Evaluate the claim that the heuristics-and-biases findings show that human judgment is irrational. Present what the findings show, what the natural-frequency and ecological-rationality critique replies, and what evidence would help to decide between the readings.
hint: Consider both the robust phenomena (anchoring, base-rate neglect in probability format) and the effects of changing the format or the environment.
solution: The robust findings (anchoring, conjunction errors, framing, neglect of base rates in probability formats) show that judgments depart systematically from the normative standards of probability and expected utility, so the descriptive claim of systematic error is well supported. The critique replies that a departure from a formal standard is not proof of irrationality: heuristics may be accurate in natural environments and cheap to use (fast and frugal heuristics), and many errors shrink when the same problem is posed in natural frequencies, suggesting that the mind handles frequencies better than single-event probabilities. Defenders of the biases program note that some errors persist in frequency formats and that the format effect shows what helps people, not that they are rational in general. Evidence that could help to decide: whether performance on matched problems in several formats and real environments predicts real-life outcomes; whether the heuristics' accuracy in the environments where people use them is high; and whether individual differences in analytic reasoning predict resistance to biases. The disagreement concerns the interpretation of normative standards as much as the facts.
rubric: States which phenomena are robust and what they show about departures from normative standards.
rubric: Explains the natural-frequency and ecological-rationality reply accurately, including its limits.
rubric: Proposes evidence that could decide between readings and notes that the disagreement is partly about the standard.
