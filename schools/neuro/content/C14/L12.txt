@article
A patient can repeat only two digits yet learns new facts and events normally; another cannot form new memories yet repeats seven digits perfectly. If short-term memory and long-term memory were the same system, how could damage separate them? This is the logic of cognitive neuroscience: use the brain to test claims about the mind, and use the claims of cognitive psychology to interpret the brain.

### Levels

Marr (1982) distinguished three **levels of analysis**: the computational level (what problem is solved and why), the algorithmic level (which representations and procedures solve it) and the implementational level (how it is physically realized). Most cognitive psychology works at the first two levels, and neuroscience at the third. The levels constrain each other without being deducible from each other, and how much brain data can decide between cognitive theories is a matter of ongoing argument: some researchers argue that imaging has rarely decided between cognitive theories (Coltheart, 2006), others that converging evidence from behavior and brain does so regularly.

### Dissociations

A **single dissociation** is a patient who is impaired on task A but normal on task B. It is weak evidence for separable systems, because A might simply be harder or more sensitive to damage. A **double dissociation** (two patients or groups with opposite patterns) is stronger evidence, since no single difficulty ordering can produce both. The patient K.F. (Shallice and Warrington, 1970) had a very small digit span with intact long-term learning, and H.M. (Scoville and Milner, 1957) had intact span with profound loss of new long-term learning. Together they support at least partly separate systems for short-term and long-term storage, a conclusion that is also qualified: the two systems interact, and later accounts of working memory (week 4) treat it as the active part of long-term memory in some versions. Limits of lesion studies: lesions are rarely selective, patients differ, and the brain reorganizes after damage.

### Imaging and its inferences

fMRI measures the **BOLD signal**, an indirect, slow hemodynamic correlate of neural activity (the neuroscience methods courses cover it in detail). Kanwisher, McDermott and Chun (1997) found a region in the fusiform gyrus responding more to faces than to other objects, the fusiform face area. The tempting inference runs backward: a region was active, so the process it is "for" was engaged. This **reverse inference** (Poldrack, 2006) is valid only to the degree that the region is selective. By Bayes' rule, the odds that a process is engaged given activation equal the prior odds times the ratio of activation rates:

$$\frac{P(X \mid \text{act})}{P(\neg X \mid \text{act})} = \frac{P(\text{act} \mid X)}{P(\text{act} \mid \neg X)} \cdot \frac{P(X)}{P(\neg X)} .$$

If a region activates in 80% of tasks that engage process $X$ and in 40% of tasks that do not, the ratio is 2; with half of tasks engaging $X$, the posterior probability is $2/3$. A region active across many kinds of tasks is a poor indicator of any one of them.

### EEG and event-related potentials

Scalp EEG has millisecond resolution but poor spatial resolution. An **event-related potential** (ERP) is the average EEG time-locked to an event. The signal in a single trial is small compared with the background activity, so averaging over $N$ trials reduces random noise in proportion to $1/\sqrt{N}$ (if the noise is independent across trials): with a single-trial noise standard deviation of 20 µV, 100 trials give $20/\sqrt{100} = 2$ µV. Well-known components include the N400, larger for words that do not fit a sentence's meaning (Kutas and Hillyard, 1980), enhanced early visual components for attended locations, and the **contralateral delay activity**, a sustained difference over the hemisphere opposite to remembered items that grows as the number of remembered items increases and levels off near an individual's capacity (Vogel and Machizawa, 2004).

### Neurons and models

In monkeys, neurons in prefrontal cortex stay active during the delay of a spatial memory task (Fuster and Alexander, 1971; Funahashi, Bruce and Goldman-Rakic, 1989), a candidate neural basis of working memory, though some researchers argue that information can also be held in activity-silent changes of synaptic state. A connectionist model of the Stroop task (Cohen, Dunbar and McClelland, 1990) explains the asymmetry that words interfere with color naming more than colors interfere with word reading: the word-reading pathway is stronger because of more practice, and attention to the task biases the weaker pathway.

### Cautions

- **Small samples**: the sampling distribution of a correlation is wide at small $n$ (see the example below); low power in neuroscience was documented by Button and colleagues (2013).
- **Circular analysis**: selecting voxels with the data that are then used to estimate the effect inflates it (Kriegeskorte and colleagues, 2009).
- **Statistical inference**: some cluster-based fMRI methods had inflated false-positive rates (Eklund, Nichols and Knutsson, 2016).
- **Reliability**: the test-retest reliability of many task-fMRI measures is modest, which limits studies of individual differences (see week 11).
@example
title: Reverse inference with a posterior probability
problem: A region is active in 90% of tasks that engage process $X$ and in 50% of tasks that do not. Suppose 30% of tasks engage $X$. What is the probability that $X$ is engaged given that the region is active?
step: Likelihood ratio: $0.90/0.50 = 1.8$.
step: Prior odds: $0.30/0.70 = 0.4286$; posterior odds $= 1.8 \times 0.4286 = 0.7714$.
step: Convert to a probability: $0.7714/(1 + 0.7714) \approx 0.4355$; directly, $0.27/(0.27 + 0.35) = 0.4355$.
step: Even strong activation raises the probability only to about 44%, because many tasks that do not engage $X$ also activate the region.
answer: About $0.44$.

@example
title: How wide is a correlation at n = 20?
problem: A study of 20 people finds a brain-behavior correlation of $r = 0.50$. Compute a 95% confidence interval with the Fisher transformation.
step: $z = \tanh^{-1}(0.50) = 0.5493$, with standard error $1/\sqrt{n - 3} = 1/\sqrt{17} = 0.2425$.
step: Limits in $z$: $0.5493 \pm 1.96 \times 0.2425$, which is $0.0740$ to $1.0246$.
step: Transform back with $\tanh$: the limits are $0.074$ and $0.772$.
step: The data are compatible with a correlation near zero and with one near $0.8$: a sample this small cannot say how strong the relation is.
answer: 95% CI about $0.07$ to $0.77$.

@mistakes
- Treating activation of a region as proof that a specific process occurred: reverse inference depends on how selective the region is.
- Concluding from a single dissociation that two functions are served by separate systems: a harder task can be affected more by any damage.
- Reading fMRI as a direct measure of spiking: BOLD is a slow, indirect correlate of neural activity.
- Averaging ERP trials with large artifacts (blinks) included: averaging reduces independent noise, not systematic contamination.

@quiz self
prompt: A famous study reports that a region of the fusiform gyrus responds more to faces than to other objects and concludes that this region is a module for face recognition. Critique this conclusion, name the kinds of evidence that would strengthen or weaken it, and propose a better test.
hint: Consider reverse inference, the difference between correlation and necessity, and what other categories or expertise might do to the same region.
solution: Greater activation to faces shows a correlation between a region and a stimulus class. It does not show that the region is necessary for face recognition (a lesion or stimulation study is needed), that it is selective for faces (other categories, or objects of expertise, may also drive it, and activation to non-face stimuli would weaken the claim), or that the response reflects face-specific computation and not, for example, attention or low-level image properties that differ between categories. The conclusion that the region is a module also depends on the resolution of the method, since neighboring populations of neurons may be mixed within a voxel. Evidence that would strengthen it: a region that responds to faces across stimuli, tasks and viewpoints and not to carefully matched control objects; patients with damage to the region who have selectively impaired face recognition, with intact object recognition (and the reverse in other patients, a double dissociation); stimulation of the region that disrupts face perception; and replication with an independent localizer, avoiding circular analysis. Evidence that would weaken it: equal responses to objects of expertise or to non-face stimuli with matched low-level properties, or intact face recognition after damage. A better test combines a functional localizer in each participant, a selectivity analysis on independent data, and a lesion or stimulation experiment.
rubric: Separates correlation from necessity and notes the reverse-inference problem.
rubric: Names evidence that would strengthen or weaken the claim, including a dissociation or stimulation.
rubric: Proposes a test with an independent localizer or independent data and a causal manipulation.
