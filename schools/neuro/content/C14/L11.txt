@article
You are asked the capital of Australia and feel certain; asked the name of an actor, you are sure you know it and cannot produce it, and then it comes to you an hour later. **Metacognition**, cognition about one's own cognition (Flavell, 1979), asks how good these feelings are. People in a **tip-of-the-tongue** state can often report the first letter and the number of syllables of the word they cannot retrieve (Brown and McNeill, 1966), and a **feeling of knowing** predicts later recognition of items that cannot be recalled (Hart, 1965). So the feelings carry real information, but not perfect information.

### Monitoring and control

Nelson and Narens (1990) separated an object level (the cognition itself) from a meta level that **monitors** it (information flows up: judgments of learning, feelings of knowing, confidence) and **controls** it (decisions flow down: how long to study an item, whether to keep searching memory, whether to answer). A **judgment of learning** (JOL) is a prediction of later recall. JOLs made immediately after studying are poor predictors; JOLs made after a delay, when the learner must try to retrieve the item, predict much better (Nelson and Dunlosky, 1991). Monitoring is partly based on cues such as how fluent processing feels, and these can mislead: Rhodes and Castel (2008) found that words in a large font received higher JOLs than words in a small font although recall did not differ. Learners also prefer strategies that feel effective. In Kornell and Bjork (2008), people studied paintings by several artists, either blocked by artist or interleaved, then classified new paintings by the same artists. Interleaving gave better classification, but most participants judged blocking to be as good or better.

### Calibration and resolution

Suppose a person gives a confidence $p_i$ (between 0 and 1) for answer $i$, and the outcome is $o_i = 1$ if the answer is correct and 0 otherwise. **Calibration** asks whether confidence matches accuracy: someone who says "80% sure" on many items should be right about 80% of the time. The **Brier score** (Brier, 1950, for weather forecasts) combines calibration and discrimination in one number, lower being better:

$$\text{Brier} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (p_i - o_i)^2 .$$

Example with five answers, confidences 0.9, 0.6, 0.8, 0.7 and 0.5 and outcomes 1, 0, 1, 1 and 0: the Brier score is $(0.01 + 0.36 + 0.04 + 0.09 + 0.25)/5 = 0.15$. The mean confidence is 0.70 while the proportion correct is 0.60, so this person is overconfident by 0.10. Overconfidence is well documented for hard general-knowledge questions; it often shrinks or reverses for easy ones (the **hard-easy effect**; Lichtenstein and Fischhoff, 1977).

**Resolution** (metacognitive sensitivity) asks whether confidence is higher for correct than for incorrect answers, regardless of its overall level. A traditional measure is the Goodman-Kruskal **gamma** (Nelson, 1984): among all pairs of items that differ in correctness and in confidence, the proportion in which the more confident item is the correct one (concordant) minus the proportion in which it is the incorrect one (discordant),

$$\gamma = \frac{C - D}{C + D}.$$

A signal-detection approach, **meta-d'** (Maniscalco and Lau, 2012), asks how well confidence ratings discriminate correct from incorrect responses, in the units of the task's own $d'$; its ratio to $d'$ indicates metacognitive efficiency.

```python
def brier_score(conf, outcomes):
    """Mean squared gap between confidence (0-1) and outcome (0 or 1)."""
    return sum((p - o) ** 2 for p, o in zip(conf, outcomes)) / len(conf)
```

### The Dunning-Kruger pattern

Kruger and Dunning (1999) reported that people scoring in the bottom quartile on tests of logic, grammar and humor greatly overestimated their percentile rank, whereas top scorers slightly underestimated theirs. They explained it as a double burden: the skills needed to perform are also those needed to judge performance. The pattern in the data is replicable, but its interpretation is debated: because any imperfect estimate regresses toward the mean, and because most people think they are better than average, the same pattern can arise from statistical regression and measurement error without a special metacognitive deficit (Krueger and Mueller, 2002). Both contributions may be real.

### Individual differences and reliability

Many cognitive tasks were built to produce robust differences between conditions, not to rank people. **Reliability** is the share of variance in observed scores due to stable differences between people; it limits correlations with anything else. A split-half correlation $r_{\text{half}}$ understates the reliability of the full test, and the **Spearman-Brown** formula corrects it: $r_{\text{full}} = 2\,r_{\text{half}}/(1 + r_{\text{half}})$. The reliability of a **difference score** $X - Y$ with equal variances is

$$r_{\text{diff}} = \frac{\tfrac{1}{2}(r_{xx} + r_{yy}) - r_{xy}}{1 - r_{xy}},$$

which is low when the two measures are highly correlated: this is the case for Stroop interference or the RT cost in a flanker task, because both conditions share most of their variance. Hedge, Powell and Sumner (2018) called this the **reliability paradox**: some of the most replicable experimental effects have only modest test-retest reliability, which makes them poor tools for individual differences even if they are excellent for group comparisons. A correlation between measures with reliabilities $r_{xx}$ and $r_{yy}$ can be corrected for attenuation, $r_{\text{true}} = r_{\text{obs}}/\sqrt{r_{xx}\,r_{yy}}$, but that correction relies on good reliability estimates and gives unstable results when they are low. Working memory capacity from complex span correlates moderately with fluid reasoning and reading comprehension (week 4); the causal interpretation is debated.
@example
title: Correcting a split-half reliability
problem: The correlation between odd and even trials of a task is 0.62. Estimate the reliability of the full test and say what it implies for a correlation with another measure.
step: Apply the Spearman-Brown formula: $r_{\text{full}} = 2(0.62)/(1 + 0.62) = 1.24/1.62 \approx 0.765$.
step: Interpretation: a test twice as long is more reliable than either half, so the half-test correlation of 0.62 understates the reliability of the whole test.
step: Because $r_{xx} = 0.765$, the observed correlation between this measure and any other measure with reliability $r_{yy}$ cannot exceed $\sqrt{0.765\,r_{yy}}$; even with a perfectly reliable partner, it is capped near $\sqrt{0.765} \approx 0.87$.
answer: $r_{\text{full}} \approx 0.77$.

@example
title: Brier score and overconfidence
problem: A participant gives confidences 0.8, 0.9 and 0.6 for three answers, which turn out to be correct, incorrect and correct. Compute the Brier score, the mean confidence and the accuracy.
step: Squared gaps: $(0.8 - 1)^2 = 0.04$, $(0.9 - 0)^2 = 0.81$ and $(0.6 - 1)^2 = 0.16$.
step: Brier score $= (0.04 + 0.81 + 0.16)/3 \approx 0.337$.
step: Mean confidence $= (0.8 + 0.9 + 0.6)/3 \approx 0.767$ and accuracy $= 2/3 \approx 0.667$, so the participant is overconfident by about 0.10.
answer: Brier $\approx 0.337$; confidence 0.767 versus accuracy 0.667.

@mistakes
- Treating overall overconfidence as poor resolution: a person can be overconfident and still be more confident for correct than for incorrect answers.
- Treating a robust group effect as a good individual-differences measure: Stroop-style difference scores can have low reliability.
- Interpreting the Dunning-Kruger pattern as proof of a unique deficit in the unskilled: regression to the mean and better-than-average beliefs produce a similar pattern.
- Applying the correction for attenuation with a guessed reliability: the corrected correlation can exceed 1 or be wildly off.

model:
```python
def gamma_correlation(conf, correct):
    """Goodman-Kruskal gamma between confidence and correctness."""
    conc = disc = 0
    for i in range(len(conf)):
        for j in range(i + 1, len(conf)):
            if correct[i] == correct[j] or conf[i] == conf[j]:
                continue
            if (conf[i] > conf[j]) == (correct[i] > correct[j]):
                conc += 1
            else:
                disc += 1
    if conc + disc == 0:
        return None
    return (conc - disc) / (conc + disc)
```
hint: Loop over all pairs (i, j) with i < j, skip pairs tied on either variable, and count whether the more confident item is the more correct one.
solution: Pairs tied in correctness (both right or both wrong) or in confidence carry no information about resolution, so they are skipped. For the second test there are four concordant and five discordant pairs, so gamma is $-1/9$, and the tied-confidence and all-correct tests have no usable pair, which a division without a guard would turn into an error.

@quiz self
prompt: Kruger and Dunning found that low scorers overestimated their rank. Critique the interpretation that low skill causes poor self-assessment, give the alternative explanation, and propose an analysis or experiment that could separate the two.
hint: Ask what a noisy self-estimate of performance would look like when the data are sorted into quartiles by actual score.
solution: The claim is that the same lack of skill that produces poor performance prevents people from judging it. The alternative is statistical: self-estimates are noisy and correlated only imperfectly with real performance, so when people are grouped by actual score, the lowest quartile must on average estimate higher than its true rank and the top quartile lower (regression toward the mean), and a general better-than-average belief adds to it. A simulation could show the pattern: generate true ability, add independent noise to the self-estimate, and sort by ability. Tests that separate the accounts include comparing the pattern with simulated data from the regression-only model with measured reliabilities, using an independent measure of the self-assessment (to avoid the same error in the sorting variable and the score), and an intervention study in which low scorers are trained and then asked to reassess their earlier performance: a skill-based account predicts improved accuracy of their judgment after training. Both processes may contribute, so the aim is to estimate their sizes.
