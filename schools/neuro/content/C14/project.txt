Compute each person's mean (or median) RT or accuracy per condition, then the paired differences. step: Compare with the original: is your effect in the same direction, how large is it relative to the published effect and your interval, and would the original size lie inside your interval? Consider differences in sample, task version, number of trials, device and timing precision (a browser records RT with some measurement error), and statistical power with your small sample.

material: A starting point for the analysis code (you must write and test your own version before you compare it with a library result):

```python
import math
import statistics


def paired_summary(cond_a, cond_b):
    """Mean difference, SD of differences, t and d_z for paired scores."""
    diffs = [b - a for a, b in zip(cond_a, cond_b)]
    n = len(diffs)
    mean = sum(diffs) / n
    sd = statistics.stdev(diffs)
    return {"n": n, "mean_diff": mean, "sd_diff": sd,
            "t": mean / (sd / math.sqrt(n)), "dz": mean / sd}
```

### Week by week

1. **Methods.** RT and accuracy are the data; Donders's subtraction and additive factors infer stages; Sternberg: $\text{RT} = a + b\,s$; speed-accuracy trade-off; summarize with medians, trimming and windows set in advance.
2. **Attention.** Cocktail party, filter, attenuation and late selection; search slopes (feature near 0, conjunction about 25 ms per item, absent about twice present); cueing benefit and cost; Stroop and flanker conflict.
3. **Perception.** Templates, features, structural descriptions; word superiority; perception as Bayesian inference; signal detection: $d' = z(H) - z(F)$, $c = -\tfrac12[z(H) + z(F)]$.
4. **Working memory.** Multicomponent model; chunking; span by interpolation; capacity $K = N(H - F)$ and $K = N(H - F)/(1 - F)$; slots versus resources.
5. **Long-term memory.** Episodic, semantic, nondeclarative; levels of processing and transfer-appropriate processing; generation, spacing, testing effects; encoding specificity; Cohen's $d$.
6. **Forgetting and false memory.** Exponential versus power forgetting (fit in log space); interference; DRM; misinformation; eyewitness procedures.
7. **Concepts.** Classical, prototype, exemplar, theory-based; $s = e^{-c\,d}$ and the choice rule.
8. **Imagery.** $\text{RT} = a + b\,\theta$, rate $= 1000/b$ degrees per second; effective angle $\min(\theta, 360 - \theta)$; depictive versus propositional.
9. **Problem solving and reasoning.** Problem space; Tower of Hanoi ($3^n$ states, $2^n - 1$ moves); fixation, set, insight; Wason tasks.
10. **Judgment and decision.** Heuristics; Bayes' rule with natural frequencies; Allais; prospect theory ($\alpha = 0.88$, $\lambda = 2.25$, $\gamma = 0.61$); framing.
11. **Metacognition and differences.** Brier score, gamma, Spearman-Brown, difference-score reliability, correction for attenuation.
12. **Brain.** Marr's levels; double dissociation; reverse inference; ERP averaging ($\sigma/\sqrt{N}$); cautions about small samples and circular analysis.

### Common errors

- Reading shorter RTs as faster processing without checking accuracy.
- Reading a linear RT function, a prototype result or an activation as proof of one mechanism.
- Confusing sensitivity with bias, correlation with causation, reverse with forward inference.
- Using the wrong formula for the task (single-probe versus whole-display $K$) or the wrong sign convention for $c$.

prompt: In a Stroop replication, 16 volunteers each have a paired difference (incongruent minus congruent mean RT). The mean difference is 48 ms and the standard deviation of the differences is 20 ms. What is the paired $t$ statistic (with 15 degrees of freedom)?
answer: 9.6
tol: 0.05
check: 48 / (20 / sqrt(16))
hint: $t = \bar{d}/(s_d/\sqrt{n})$.
solution: The standard error is $20/\sqrt{16} = 5$ ms, so $t = 48/5 = 9.6$. The standardized effect $d_z = 48/20 = 2.4$ is large, because every person shows the effect with little spread.

prompt: An original study reported a Stroop effect of 80 ms (significant). A careful replication with 40 participants finds 45 ms with a 95% interval from 30 to 60 ms. Which description is most defensible?
choice*: The effect replicated in direction and significance, with a smaller estimated size, and the difference from the original should be explained by sample, task and measurement differences, not described as a failure.
choice: The replication failed because the effect is smaller than the original.
choice: The replication confirms the original size, because both are significant.
choice: The result cannot be interpreted because the sizes differ.
hint: Compare the direction, the interval and the original size rather than only whether both are significant.
solution: The replication found a reliably positive effect, but its interval excludes the original 80 ms, so the original estimate was probably too large or the methods differed. A significant result in both studies does not mean they found the same size, and a smaller effect is not by itself a failure to replicate.

prompt: Design a study to compare retrieval practice with restudy for learning a list of 24 words, with a delayed test and a forgetting-curve analysis. State the design (conditions, delays, counterbalancing), the measures, how you would fit and compare an exponential and a power function to the group recall data, and two cautions in interpreting the fit and the testing-effect result.
hint: Think about matched items, at least four delays, and fitting each function in log space and comparing errors in recall units, and about averaging artifacts and effect size.
solution: Use a within-person design in which each participant studies two matched lists of 12 words, one with restudy and one with retrieval practice, with counterbalanced assignment of lists to conditions and order. Test free recall at several delays between participants or sessions (for example 10 minutes, 1 day, 2 days and 1 week), so the forgetting curve has at least four time points per condition, with each delay tested on different participants if repeated testing would itself act as retrieval practice. Measure the proportion recalled per person and condition, and compute the difference and an effect size (Cohen's $d_z$ for paired scores) at each delay with an interval. For the curve, fit $R = a e^{-kt}$ by regressing $\log R$ on $t$ and $R = a t^{-b}$ by regressing $\log R$ on $\log t$ (with positive delays and nonzero recall), convert predictions back to recall, and compare sums of squared errors in recall units. Cautions: a power function fitting group averages does not prove that individuals follow a power law, since averaging exponentials with different rates mimics it, and the comparison depends on the delays; and the testing effect may be absent or reversed at short delays and depends on feedback and on matching practice to the final test, so one study should not be generalized. Explicit consent and anonymous data would apply to the volunteers.
