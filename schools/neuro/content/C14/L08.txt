@article
Which way does a tilted letter R turn if you spin it upright? Which is farther, the corner of a room from the door or from the window? Most people answer by forming a picture in the mind's eye and mentally rotating or scanning it. Do such pictures exist, and do they work like pictures? Reaction times provide the main evidence.

### Mental rotation

In the experiment of Shepard and Metzler (1971), observers saw two drawings of three-dimensional block figures and decided whether they showed the same object, possibly rotated, or mirror-image objects. For "same" pairs, RT increased linearly with the angular difference between the two figures. The slope corresponded to a rotation rate of roughly 60° per second, and rotations in the picture plane and in depth gave similar rates. The natural interpretation is that people mentally rotate one figure through intermediate orientations, at a cost per degree:

$$\text{RT} = a + b\,\theta, \qquad \text{rate} = \frac{1000}{b}\ \text{deg/s} \quad (b \text{ in ms per degree}) .$$

| angle $\theta$ (degrees) | 0 | 40 | 80 | 120 | 160 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| mean RT for "same" pairs (ms) | 1000 | 1700 | 2380 | 3100 | 3780 |

These illustrative values give $a = 1000$ ms and $b = 17.4$ ms per degree, a rate of 57.5° per second. For letters and digits shown at angles away from upright, Cooper and Shepard (1973) found that RT depends on the shortest rotation to the upright, so a 240° tilt takes as long as a 120° tilt: the effective angle is $\min(\theta, 360° - \theta)$. Practice reduces the rotation cost for particular stimuli, and strategies vary: some people compare parts of a figure and not whole figures, so a linear function is a common pattern and not a law.

### Image scanning

Kosslyn, Ball and Reiser (1978) had people memorize a map of an island with seven landmarks, then imagine a dot moving from one landmark to another and press a key on arrival. RT increased linearly with the distance between the landmarks. Size matters too: details of small images are verified more slowly than details of large images. These results led Kosslyn to propose that images are **depictive**, like pictures in a spatial medium, so that distances in the image correspond to distances in the world.

### Pictures or descriptions?

Pylyshyn (1973 and later) argued that mental representations are **propositional** (symbolic descriptions) and that the results are explained by **tacit knowledge**: people know how long a real scan or a real rotation takes, and they produce RTs that simulate what they expect. A relevant test: in a study by Intons-Peterson (1983), participants' expectations about the outcome of the experiment influenced the size of scanning effects, which showed that demand characteristics can contribute. Defenders of depictive imagery point to many experiments that rule out this explanation and to neural evidence. Imagery tasks activate visual cortex, including early visual areas, in many imaging and stimulation studies, but the result depends on the task and the person, and studies of patients with brain damage show both patterns in which perception and imagery fail together and patterns in which they separate. The debate about the format of imagery has not been settled; most researchers accept that imagery is a real cognitive function and argue about how it is implemented.

### Vividness and individual differences

People differ greatly in how vivid they report their imagery, assessed by questionnaires such as the Vividness of Visual Imagery Questionnaire (Marks, 1973). **Aphantasia** (a term proposed by Zeman and colleagues in 2015) describes people who report little or no voluntary visual imagery. Many such people perform ordinary spatial and memory tasks competently, which suggests that they may use other strategies and raises questions about what questionnaires measure and how imagery relates to performance.

### Cognitive maps

Tolman (1948) argued from rat behavior that animals learn spatial layouts (cognitive maps) and not just sequences of turns. Human spatial knowledge is systematically distorted. Stevens and Coupe (1978) found that many people judged San Diego to be west of Reno, though Reno lies farther west; the distortion is consistent with storing locations in a hierarchy (California is west of Nevada) and not as coordinates. Maps in the head combine landmark, route and survey knowledge, and the brain basis includes place and grid cells in the hippocampal formation (see the neuroscience courses).
@example
title: Estimating a rotation rate
problem: Mean RTs for "same" pairs are 1300 ms at 20°, 2700 ms at 100° and 4100 ms at 180°, and they lie on a straight line. Find the slope, the intercept and the implied rotation rate.
step: Slope $b = (2700 - 1300)/(100 - 20) = 1400/80 = 17.5$ ms per degree; check with the last point: $(4100 - 2700)/80 = 17.5$.
step: Intercept $a = 1300 - 17.5 \times 20 = 950$ ms, the time for processes that do not depend on angle (encoding the figures, deciding, responding).
step: Rate $= 1000/17.5 \approx 57.1$ degrees per second.
step: The intercept is a time at 0° that is not the time to rotate, so only the slope speaks to rotation.
answer: $b = 17.5$ ms/deg, $a = 950$ ms, about 57 degrees per second.

@example
title: The shortest route to upright
problem: For letters, RT is $500 + 2.5\,\theta_{\text{eff}}$ ms where $\theta_{\text{eff}} = \min(\theta, 360 - \theta)$. Predict the RT for a letter tilted 240°.
step: Compute the shortest rotation: $\min(240, 360 - 240) = \min(240, 120) = 120$ degrees.
step: Substitute: $\text{RT} = 500 + 2.5 \times 120 = 800$ ms.
step: Using 240 directly would give 1100 ms. The data on letters follow the shortest route, so 800 ms is the prediction.
answer: 800 ms.

@mistakes
- Reading the intercept of a rotation function as the "time to rotate": it contains angle-independent stages, and only the slope reflects rotation.
- Ignoring that rotation beyond 180° is done by the shorter route in the opposite direction, so RT does not keep rising to 360°.
- Treating the scanning or rotation data as proof of pictures in the head: the propositional view explains them with tacit knowledge and expectations, and the debate is open.
- Assuming that people who report no imagery cannot do spatial tasks: many perform normally, perhaps with other strategies.

@quiz self
prompt: A researcher claims that a linear relation between RT and angle in a rotation task shows that people have picture-like images that they rotate. Evaluate this claim: say what a defender of the propositional view would reply and propose one experiment that could help to decide.
hint: Ask what else could produce an RT that grows with angle, and what manipulation could change one explanation but not the other.
solution: A linear RT-angle function is what a continuous analogue rotation would produce, but a propositional account can also produce it: participants may compare structural descriptions, with more transformation steps needed for larger angular differences, or may produce RTs that simulate what they expect rotation to take. A proponent of the propositional view would add that the format of the representation is not identified by a pattern of RTs alone, because an analogue process and a symbolic process with graded steps can look alike. One experiment: vary participants' expectations about rotation (for example, tell some that large rotations are as fast as small ones, after giving everyone practice), and see whether the slope tracks the expectation; also test whether the slope depends on stimulus complexity (a feature that differs between a whole-object rotation and a part-comparison strategy), measure eye movements, and compare with brain activity in regions engaged by visual-spatial tasks. A result in which slopes shifted with instructions would show that expectations contribute; a slope that survived such manipulation would be hard for a pure tacit-knowledge account, though it would not by itself prove pictorial representation.
rubric: Explains that a linear function does not by itself fix the representational format.

rubric: Proposes a manipulation (expectation, complexity or other) with a prediction for each view.
