@article
You cannot watch a thought, but you can time one. Press a key as soon as a light comes on, and it takes you about a quarter of a second. Press the left key for a red light and the right key for a green one, and it takes noticeably longer. The stimulus is the same kind of event in both tasks, so the extra time must reflect something the mind did in the second task and not the first. This is the core move of **cognitive psychology**: treat the mind as a system that transforms inputs into outputs, and infer the transformations from how behavior changes when the task changes. This is the **information-processing view**: perception, memory, decision and response are stages that take time and can be studied by their effects on speed and accuracy.

### Donders's subtraction

Donders (1868) proposed subtracting reaction times (RTs) across tasks that differ by one added mental stage. In **simple RT** there is one stimulus and one response. In **go/no-go** there are two stimuli, and you respond to only one; this adds stimulus discrimination. In **choice RT** there are two stimuli and two responses; this adds response selection. With illustrative values of 230, 290 and 360 ms for the three tasks, discrimination takes about 290 − 230 = 60 ms and selection about 360 − 290 = 70 ms.

The method assumes **pure insertion**: adding a stage changes nothing else. Sternberg (1969) argued this cannot be taken for granted, and proposed the **additive-factor method**: if two experimental factors affect different stages, their effects on mean RT add; if the effects interact, the factors probably influence a common stage. This logic is still used, but it relies on assumptions about strictly sequential stages that later work (cascade and parallel models) questioned.

### Sternberg's memory scanning

In Sternberg's (1966) task you hold a list of 1 to 6 digits in mind, then a probe digit appears and you say whether it was in the list. Mean RT rose linearly with list size $s$:

$$\text{RT} = a + b\,s .$$

The intercept $a$ (several hundred ms) bundles stages that do not depend on list size (encoding the probe, responding). The slope $b$, about 38 ms per item in the original report, was read as the time to compare the probe with one memory item. The slope was similar for "yes" and "no" probes, which suggested the scan is **exhaustive**: it continues through the whole list even after a match. This interpretation is debated; some parallel-processing models predict the same linear function, so a linear RT function does not by itself prove serial comparison.

### Accuracy and the speed-accuracy trade-off

RT is only half the data. People can respond faster by being sloppier, so a condition with shorter RTs and more errors may differ in **response criterion** and not in processing speed. This is a **speed-accuracy trade-off**. Remedies: report both measures, instruct and train participants to a common accuracy, measure RT at several deadlines, or use combined scores such as the inverse efficiency score (mean correct RT divided by proportion correct), whose assumptions are themselves debated.

### Summarizing RTs

RT distributions are right-skewed: a few very slow trials (a lapse of attention) pull the mean up, and anticipations (very fast, before the stimulus is processed) are not real responses. Common practice analyzes correct trials only, drops trials outside a window fixed before looking at the data, and reports medians or trimmed means. Analysis decisions made after seeing the results inflate false positives.

| trial | RT (ms) | correct |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 512 | 1 |
| 2 | 478 | 1 |
| 3 | 455 | 1 |
| 4 | 1340 | 1 |
| 5 | 139 | 0 |
| 6 | 498 | 0 |
| 7 | 467 | 1 |
| 8 | 481 | 1 |

The mean of all eight RTs is 546.25 ms. Over correct trials the mean is 622.2 ms (inflated by the 1340 ms lapse), while the median is 479.5 ms. Keeping only trials with RTs from 200 to 1000 ms leaves six trials, five of them correct, with a mean correct RT of 478.6 ms and accuracy 83.3%.

```python
def mean_correct_rt(trials):
    """Mean RT (ms) over correct trials; None if there are none."""
    good = [t["rt"] for t in trials if t["correct"] == 1]
    return sum(good) / len(good) if good else None
```

title: Donders's stage times
problem: A participant has mean RTs of 236 ms in simple RT, 301 ms in go/no-go and 372 ms in choice RT. Estimate the durations of stimulus discrimination and response selection under pure insertion, and say what the choice-minus-simple difference represents.
step: Discrimination is the extra stage in go/no-go: $301 - 236 = 65$ ms.
step: Response selection is the extra stage in choice RT beyond go/no-go: $372 - 301 = 71$ ms.
step: Choice minus simple is $372 - 236 = 136$ ms, which is the sum $65 + 71$: both added stages together.
step: These are estimates only if pure insertion holds; if adding a stage also changes the others, the subtraction mixes effects.
answer: Discrimination about 65 ms, selection about 71 ms, together 136 ms.

@example
title: Mean versus median with a lapse
problem: Seven RTs (ms) from one condition are 388, 402, 395, 1020, 410, 391 and 399. Compute the mean and the median and say which better describes the typical trial.
step: Sum: $388 + 402 + 395 + 1020 + 410 + 391 + 399 = 3405$, so the mean is $3405/7 \approx 486.4$ ms.
step: Sort: 388, 391, 395, 399, 402, 410, 1020. The middle (fourth) value is 399 ms.
step: Six of seven RTs lie between 388 and 410 ms, so 399 ms describes them; the single 1020 ms lapse pulls the mean about 87 ms above that.
answer: Mean about 486.4 ms, median 399 ms; the median is the better summary of a typical trial.

@mistakes
- Treating a shorter mean RT as faster processing without checking accuracy: the condition may simply have been answered more carelessly.
- Reading a linear RT function as proof of serial processing: some parallel models predict the same line.
- Choosing exclusion windows or summary statistics after seeing which one produces a significant result.
- Reading the intercept of an RT function as "the time of one stage": it bundles every stage that does not depend on the manipulated variable.

model:
```python
import statistics


def window_summary(trials, lo, hi):
    """Count, median correct RT and accuracy of trials inside [lo, hi]."""
    kept = [t for t in trials if lo <= t["rt"] <= hi]
    if not kept:
        return (0, None, None)
    good = [t["rt"] for t in kept if t["correct"] == 1]
    median = statistics.median(good) if good else None
    return (len(kept), median, len(good) / len(kept))
```
hint: Filter first, then compute the median from the correct kept trials only and the accuracy from all kept trials.
solution: Filtering with inclusive bounds gives the kept set. The median uses only correct kept trials, but accuracy divides by every kept trial. The tests check the inclusive edges (350 and 420 are kept) and the empty and all-error cases, where a typical solution crashes taking a median of nothing.

@quiz self
prompt: A researcher wants to know how long response selection takes. Describe how Donders's subtraction would estimate it, state the pure-insertion assumption, and describe how the additive-factor method could test whether two factors (stimulus clarity and number of response alternatives) affect separate stages.
hint: Think about what each task adds, and about what an interaction between two factors would mean.
solution: Compare go/no-go (two stimuli, one response) with choice RT (two stimuli, two responses): the difference in mean RT estimates response selection if adding the second response inserts that stage and changes nothing else (pure insertion). That assumption can fail, for instance if participants change strategy when two responses are possible. In the additive-factor method one crosses stimulus clarity (clear versus degraded) with the number of response alternatives (two versus four) and tests whether the two effects on mean RT add. Additivity suggests the factors affect different stages (encoding and response selection); an interaction suggests they influence at least one shared stage. Both methods assume sequential, discrete stages, which parallel and cascade models question.
