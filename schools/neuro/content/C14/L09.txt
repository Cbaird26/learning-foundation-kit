@article
Give someone a candle, a box of thumbtacks and a book of matches, and ask them to fix the candle to a wall so that it burns without dripping on the table. Many people try to tack the candle to the wall or melt wax to stick it on, and fail. The solution is to tack the box itself to the wall and stand the candle in it. In Duncker's (1945) version, people were less likely to solve it when the tacks were presented inside the box than when the box was presented empty. The box in use as a container is hard to see as a platform: **functional fixedness**. Problem solving is partly a matter of finding the right representation.

### Problems as search

Newell and Simon (1972) described a problem as a **problem space**: an initial state, a goal state, **operators** that move from one state to another, and constraints. Solving is search through this space. Three strategies:

- **Brute force**: examine states systematically; sure but often too slow.
- **Hill climbing**: move to whichever neighbor looks closer to the goal; fast, but it can stall at a local peak, for example when a move that seems to go away from the goal is needed.
- **Means-ends analysis**: compare the current state with the goal, pick the largest difference and set a subgoal of reducing it.

The **Tower of Hanoi** shows all three. With $n$ disks on three pegs, every disk can be on any of three pegs (the order on a peg is forced), so there are $3^n$ states. Means-ends analysis solves $n$ disks by moving the top $n-1$ disks to the spare peg, moving the largest disk, and moving the $n-1$ disks back on top. The minimum number of moves $T(n)$ satisfies $T(n) = 2\,T(n-1) + 1$ with $T(1) = 1$, whose solution is $T(n) = 2^n - 1$.

### Mental set and insight

Luchins (1942) gave water-jar problems: measure a target amount using three jars of given capacities. After several problems all solved by filling the middle jar and then removing the first jar once and the third jar twice (for jars 21, 127 and 3 and a target of 100, $127 - 21 - 2 \times 3 = 100$), many participants applied this method even to problems with a simpler solution (for jars 23, 49 and 3 and a target of 20, $23 - 3 = 20$), and some failed problems that the method could not solve. This is **mental set** (Einstellung): a method that has worked is used even when it is not the best.

**Insight** is the sudden restructuring of a problem. Metcalfe and Wiebe (1987) had people rate how "warm" they felt during problems. For algebra problems warmth rose steadily as people approached the answer; for insight problems it stayed low and jumped just before the solution. The special-process view holds that insight involves a distinct restructuring and the opposing "business as usual" view holds that insight problems are solved by ordinary processes of search, memory and attention, with only the experience being abrupt. Both views are active. **Analogy** helps when it is noticed: Gick and Holyoak (1980) showed that few people spontaneously used a story about a divided army to solve the tumor-radiation problem, but many more did when told that the story was relevant, so the difficulty lies in noticing structural similarity under different surface features.

### Reasoning with conditionals

A conditional "if $p$, then $q$" supports two valid inferences: modus ponens ($p$, so $q$) and modus tollens (not $q$, so not $p$); affirming the consequent ($q$, so $p$) and denying the antecedent (not $p$, so not $q$) are invalid. In the **Wason selection task** (Wason, 1966) four cards show E, K, 4 and 7, each with a letter on one side and a number on the other. Which cards must be turned to test "if a card has a vowel on one side, it has an even number on the other"? The correct answer is E (modus ponens: an odd number behind it would break the rule) and 7 (modus tollens: a vowel behind it would break the rule). Typically only a small minority (about one in ten) choose these cards; most choose E and 4, looking for confirmation, though 4 cannot falsify the rule. When the content is a social rule ("if a person is drinking beer, then the person must be over the legal drinking age," with cards for a beer drinker, a soda drinker, an older person and a younger person), most people are correct (Griggs and Cox, 1982). Explanations differ: Cosmides (1989) argued for a specialized mechanism for detecting cheaters on social contracts; others propose pragmatic reasoning schemas, relevance or probabilistic information gain. Which is right is debated.

Another well-known pattern is **belief bias** (Evans, Barston and Pollard, 1983): people accept believable conclusions of invalid syllogisms more often than unbelievable ones and reject unbelievable valid ones more often. **Mental models** theory (Johnson-Laird, 1983) says that people reason by building and inspecting models of the possibilities and that errors arise when too few are built; dual-process accounts distinguish fast intuitive and slower analytic reasoning, a division some researchers question.

### Testing hypotheses

In Wason's (1960) 2-4-6 task, participants try to discover a rule that the triple 2, 4, 6 satisfies by proposing triples and being told whether each fits. The true rule is "any increasing sequence." Many people propose triples such as 8, 10, 12 that fit their guess ("add two") and hear "yes," never testing triples that would break it. A **positive test strategy** is not always an error (it is informative when the hypothesis is broader than or overlaps the true rule, because a positive test can then hear "no"), but it fails when the true rule is broader than the hypothesis, as here, because every positive test then hears "yes."
@example
title: Counting states and moves in the Tower of Hanoi
problem: For a Tower of Hanoi with 4 disks, count the states and find the minimum number of moves using the recurrence $T(n) = 2T(n-1) + 1$.
step: States: each of the 4 disks is on one of 3 pegs, giving $3^4 = 81$ states.
step: Recurrence: $T(1) = 1$, $T(2) = 2 \times 1 + 1 = 3$, $T(3) = 2 \times 3 + 1 = 7$, $T(4) = 2 \times 7 + 1 = 15$.
step: This matches $2^4 - 1 = 15$. The minimum path visits only 16 of the 81 states, so a solver who understands the recursion avoids most of the space.
answer: 81 states and 15 moves.

@example
title: Mental set with water jars
problem: A set of problems was solved by $B - A - 2C$. For jars $A = 23$, $B = 49$ and $C = 3$ and a target of 20, give the learned method's result and a simpler method.
step: Learned method: $49 - 23 - 2 \times 3 = 20$.
step: Simpler method: $23 - 3 = 20$, one fill and one pour.
step: Both reach 20, but participants who had practiced the long method often kept using it, and on a problem where only the simple method works, they fail. The pattern shows that the previously successful procedure is used even when a shorter one exists.
answer: Both give 20; the direct method $A - C$ is simpler, and people with a mental set often miss it.

@mistakes
- Treating functional fixedness or mental set as stupidity: they are by-products of reusing representations and procedures that usually work.
- Reading the Wason result as "people are irrational": performance is much better with social content, and accounts of why differ.
- Choosing the card that shows the consequent (the even number 4) in the selection task: no result on its other side could falsify the rule.
- Treating insight as proven to be a special mechanism: the evidence on warmth ratings is consistent with that view, and ordinary-process accounts also explain much of it.

@quiz self
prompt: A student in the 2-4-6 task proposes 8-10-12, 20-22-24 and 100-102-104 and is told yes each time, and concludes that the rule is "add two." Explain what went wrong, what tests would have shown it, and when a positive test strategy is nevertheless sensible.
hint: Ask whether any of the tests could have produced a "no" if the true rule were broader.
solution: All three proposals fit the guessed rule, and any triple that fits "add two" also fits the true rule "any increasing sequence," so every answer was bound to be yes: the tests could not discriminate. Tests that would have helped try to break the guess, such as 1-2-3, 5-20-100 or 10-11-13, which a person who believes "add two" predicts will be rejected; hearing yes for 1-2-3 would refute the hypothesis. A positive test strategy is sensible when the hypothesis is broader than or overlaps the true rule (so a positive test can hear no for a wrong guess) and when the true rule is rare, as in many real situations; it is uninformative when the real rule is broader than the hypothesis, because every positive test then hears yes. The lesson is that informative tests are those whose outcomes differ depending on whether the hypothesis is true.
rubric: Explains that the proposed triples were all consistent with both rules, so they could not discriminate.
rubric: Gives at least one test that violates the guessed rule and says what outcome would refute it.
rubric: States when a positive test strategy is reasonable and when it fails.
