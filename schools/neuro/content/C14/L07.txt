@article
Try to define "game": board games, solitaire, football, a toddler's tossing of a ball. No single feature is shared by all of them, and yet you know what is and is not a game. Concepts are the mental representations of categories, and they raise a puzzle: if not a definition, what do we use to decide that something is a bird or a game, and why are some members better examples than others?

### Four accounts

- **Classical view**: a concept is a list of necessary and sufficient features. It predicts sharp boundaries and equal membership. It struggles with the "game" example (Wittgenstein's family resemblances) and with the finding below that members differ in goodness.
- **Prototype view** (Rosch, 1975): a concept is a summary, the central tendency of its members. Category membership is similarity to the prototype.
- **Exemplar view** (Medin and Schaffer, 1978): a concept is the set of stored instances; a new item is categorized by its similarity to all of them.
- **Theory-based view** (Murphy and Medin, 1985): categories are held together by explanations and background knowledge, not only by similarity. A set such as "things to carry out of a burning house" is coherent because of a goal.

### Evidence

**Typicality.** People rate a robin a better bird than a penguin, and confirm "a robin is a bird" faster than "a penguin is a bird." Rosch and Mervis (1975) showed that typical members share many features with other members (a **family resemblance** structure) and few with other categories. **Basic level.** Categories at a middle level of abstraction (chair, dog) are named first and used most (Rosch and colleagues, 1976).

**Dot patterns.** Posner and Keele (1968) generated categories by distorting a prototype dot pattern. After training on distortions only, people classified the never-seen prototype about as well as the training patterns, and after a delay the prototype held up better than the patterns seen. This was taken as evidence for abstraction. Amnesic patients can learn such categories despite poor memory for the training items (Knowlton and Squire, 1993), which suggests that category learning can use a system separate from episodic memory.

**Exemplars.** The prototype result does not decide the question: the prototype is similar to many stored items, so an exemplar model predicts good performance on it too. Evidence for exemplars comes from sensitivity to specific old items, to the variability of the category, and to correlations among features that a mean cannot represent. Today many researchers favor models that combine rules, prototypes and exemplars; which one dominates depends on the structure and the amount of learning.

### A computational comparison

Let each item be a point in a space of dimensions, such as size and brightness. For dimensions that can be attended to separately, the **city-block distance** between items $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ is $d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) = \sum_i |x_i - y_i|$. Similarity falls exponentially with distance (Shepard, 1987): $s = e^{-c\,d}$, where $c$ is a **sensitivity** parameter. In the generalized context model (Nosofsky, 1986), the probability of choosing category A for item $\mathbf{x}$ is the summed similarity to A's exemplars relative to the summed similarity to both categories:

$$P(A \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{\sum_{\mathbf{a} \in A} s(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a})}{\sum_{\mathbf{a} \in A} s(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a}) + \sum_{\mathbf{b} \in B} s(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{b})} .$$

A prototype model uses the same rule with one term per category, the similarity to the category mean $\bar{\mathbf{a}}$ and $\bar{\mathbf{b}}$.

```python
import math


def exemplar_prob(x, cat_a, cat_b, c):
    """P(A | x) in an exemplar model with city-block distance."""
    def total(cat):
        return sum(math.exp(-c * sum(abs(p - q) for p, q in zip(x, item)))
                   for item in cat)
    return total(cat_a) / (total(cat_a) + total(cat_b))
```

With A = (1,1), (2,1), (1,2), B = (5,5), (6,5), (5,6), item $\mathbf{x}$ = (2,2) and $c = 0.5$, the exemplar model gives $P(A) = 0.935$ and the prototype model gives 0.935 as well: for compact, well-separated categories the two models agree. They disagree when a category is spread out so that its mean lies in an unrepresentative place, as in the first worked example. This is why experiments designed to separate the models use categories with specific structure and then compare predictions with data using a fit measure such as the sum of squared errors, often after fitting $c$.
@example
title: A stimulus on which the models disagree
problem: Category A has exemplars (0, 0) and (8, 0); category B has exemplars (4, 3) and (4, 5). Take $c = 1$ and test item $\mathbf{x}$ = (4, 0). Compute $P(A)$ under the exemplar model and the prototype model.
step: Exemplar model, category A: distances are $4$ and $4$, so $\sum s = 2e^{-4} = 0.0366$.
step: Exemplar model, category B: distances are $3$ and $5$, so $\sum s = e^{-3} + e^{-5} = 0.0498 + 0.0067 = 0.0565$; $P(A) = 0.0366/(0.0366 + 0.0565) = 0.393$.
step: Prototype model: the mean of A is (4, 0), at distance 0 from $\mathbf{x}$, so $s_A = 1$; the mean of B is (4, 4), at distance 4, so $s_B = e^{-4} = 0.0183$; $P(A) = 1/(1 + 0.0183) = 0.982$.
step: The prototype model is nearly certain that the item belongs to A, while the exemplar model leans toward B because the item sits between A's two far-apart exemplars and close to B's. Data on such items can separate the accounts.
answer: Exemplar $P(A) \approx 0.39$; prototype $P(A) \approx 0.98$.

@mistakes
- Treating the prototype result of Posner and Keele as proof of prototypes: exemplar models also predict good performance on a never-seen central item.
- Averaging distances instead of similarities in an exemplar model: similarity falls off exponentially, so close exemplars dominate the sum.
- Assuming that the prototype is an actual member or a "best example": it is a summary and may correspond to no real item.
- Treating the theory-based view as a rival computation: it points to the role of knowledge, and models of categories increasingly combine similarity with knowledge.

model:
```python
import math


def prototype_prob(x, cat_a, cat_b, c):
    """P(A | x) when each category is represented by its mean item."""
    def mean(cat):
        return [sum(dim) / len(cat) for dim in zip(*cat)]

    def sim(proto):
        return math.exp(-c * sum(abs(p - q) for p, q in zip(x, proto)))

    s_a, s_b = sim(mean(cat_a)), sim(mean(cat_b))
    return s_a / (s_a + s_b)
```
hint: Use `zip(*cat)` to group the values of each dimension, average each group, and then compute one distance per category.
solution: The only change from the exemplar model is that each category contributes one similarity, to its mean, in place of a sum over all members. The last test is the one where the models part: the mean of A2 is exactly at the test item, so the prototype model is nearly certain of A, whereas the exemplar model (which would give about 0.47) is not.

@quiz self
prompt: Design an experiment that can separate a prototype model from an exemplar model of category learning. Describe the category structure and test items, the prediction of each model, and how you would compare the models with the data.
hint: Look for test items on which the two models make opposite predictions, as when a category's mean lies close to the other category's members.
solution: Build two-dimensional categories in which category A has two widely separated clusters of exemplars (for example at (0, 0) and (8, 0)) and category B has exemplars in between but off the line (at (4, 3) and (4, 5)). Train participants with feedback on the exemplars of both categories, then test, without feedback, items on the line between A's clusters (such as (4, 0)) along with trained items and other transfer items. The prototype model, whose A mean lies at (4, 0), predicts a strong preference for A on that item; the exemplar model, whose summed similarity favors the closer B members at moderate sensitivity, predicts a weaker preference or a preference for B. Fit each model to individual or group proportions of A responses, with $c$ fit by least squares or maximum likelihood, and compare sums of squared errors with a penalty for parameters (the models have the same number of free parameters here). Counterbalance which clusters are A, use several such items, and check that participants learned the training items to criterion. Even then, models can be mimicked by others (for example rule-plus-exception models), so converging evidence is needed.
