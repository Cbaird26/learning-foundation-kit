@article
Read a seven-digit phone number once, and you can dial it. Repeat it silently and you can hold it for a minute; be interrupted by a question and it is gone. **Working memory** is the system that holds a small amount of information in an active state and lets you manipulate it, for reasoning, comprehension and planning. Its limits are among the most replicated facts in cognitive psychology, but their cause is still debated.

### From short-term store to working memory

Early **modal model** accounts (Atkinson and Shiffrin, 1968) treated short-term memory as a store between sensory input and long-term memory. In the Brown-Peterson task (Brown, 1958; Peterson and Peterson, 1959) people saw a three-letter trigram, counted backward by threes for a delay, and recalled it. Recall fell steeply with delay, to about 10% after 18 s. This seemed to show rapid decay. But Keppel and Underwood (1962) found little forgetting on the first trials of the session; forgetting grows over trials as earlier trigrams interfere (**proactive interference**). So the result does not by itself separate decay from interference.

### The capacity limit and chunks

Miller (1956) summarized immediate memory span as about seven items, plus or minus two, and stressed that what counts is the number of **chunks**, meaningful units, not the number of items. Chase and Simon (1973) showed chunking in chess: masters recalled positions from real games much better than novices, but both groups did poorly on random arrangements of the same pieces. When rehearsal and chunking are controlled, capacity is lower, about four chunks (Cowan, 2001), a figure that is itself a matter of debate.

### The multicomponent model

Baddeley and Hitch (1974) proposed that working memory has parts, and Baddeley (2000) added an episodic buffer:

- **Phonological loop**: a phonological store that fades in about two seconds unless refreshed by subvocal rehearsal.
- **Visuospatial sketchpad**: holds visual and spatial information.
- **Central executive**: attention control, coordinating the other parts.
- **Episodic buffer**: binds information from the parts and from long-term memory into episodes.

Evidence for the loop: the **phonological similarity effect**, in which similar-sounding letters (B, C, D, G, T) are recalled worse than dissimilar ones (Conrad, 1964); the **word-length effect**, in which span is smaller for long than for short words (Baddeley, Thomson and Buchanan, 1975), approximately as many words as can be spoken in about two seconds; and **articulatory suppression** (repeating an irrelevant syllable), which disrupts rehearsal and removes these effects for visually presented lists. Evidence for separate systems: verbal and spatial secondary tasks interfere with matching primary tasks more than with the other kind. In neuropsychology, a patient with severely reduced digit span but normal long-term learning (K.F., Shallice and Warrington, 1970) is the mirror image of H.M. (week 12). Alternatives hold that working memory is the active part of long-term memory plus attention (Cowan's embedded-processes view; the executive-attention view), and the word-length effect has been challenged when stimuli are controlled for other properties.

### Measuring capacity

**Span.** For each list length, record the proportion of lists recalled correctly. The span can be defined as the length at which this proportion crosses 0.5, found by linear interpolation. With lengths 4 to 8 and proportions 1.0, 0.9, 0.7, 0.4 and 0.1, the curve crosses 0.5 between lengths 6 (0.7) and 7 (0.4):

$$\text{span} = 6 + \frac{0.7 - 0.5}{0.7 - 0.4} = 6.67 .$$

Simple span tasks mostly tap storage. **Complex span** tasks (reading span, Daneman and Carpenter, 1980; operation span, Turner and Engle, 1989) interleave processing with storage, and predict reading comprehension and fluid reasoning.

**Visual capacity.** In change detection (Luck and Vogel, 1997) $N$ colored squares appear, vanish for about a second, and reappear with one possibly changed; observers say "changed" or "same." With $H$ the hit rate and $F$ the false-alarm rate, Cowan's estimate for single-probe displays is $K = N(H - F)$, and Pashler's for whole-display change is

$$K = N\,\frac{H - F}{1 - F} .$$

Luck and Vogel found that $K$ was about three to four objects and stayed the same whether objects had one feature or several, which they took as evidence for object-based slots. The slot view is contested: **resource models** (Bays and Husain, 2008) argue that memory is a continuous resource shared among items, so more items means less precision and no hard limit, whereas Zhang and Luck (2008) found evidence for a limited number of items stored with fixed precision. Both views can fit much of the data.

```python
def capacity_k(n_items, hit_rate, false_alarm_rate, whole_display=False):
    """Estimated number of items held in a change-detection task."""
    diff = hit_rate - false_alarm_rate
    if whole_display:
        diff = diff / (1 - false_alarm_rate)
    return n_items * diff
```
@example
title: Two estimates of K
problem: In a change-detection task with $N = 8$ squares, an observer has $H = 0.65$ and $F = 0.15$. Compute $K$ with the single-probe formula and the whole-display formula, and say why the two differ.
step: Single-probe: $K = N(H - F) = 8 \times 0.50 = 4.0$ items.
step: Whole-display: $K = N(H - F)/(1 - F) = 8 \times 0.50 / 0.85 \approx 4.71$ items.
step: They differ because they assume different guessing models. In a single-probe task the observer guesses only when the probed item is not in memory. In a whole-display task the observer detects any change whose item is stored and can guess "changed" on every trial, so $F$ estimates the guessing rate directly and dividing by $1 - F$ rescales $H - F$ to the trials on which guessing could not produce a hit.
answer: $K = 4.0$ (single-probe) and $K \approx 4.71$ (whole-display).

@mistakes
- Treating "seven plus or minus two" as items: it is the number of chunks, and it overstates capacity when chunking and rehearsal are uncontrolled.
- Reading the Brown-Peterson curve as proof of decay: counting backward adds interfering material and proactive interference builds over trials.
- Using the single-probe formula for whole-display change data (or the reverse): the two formulas assume different tasks.
- Saying capacity is "four items" as settled fact: slot and resource accounts both fit much of the data and the number depends on the stimuli and the task.

model:
```python
def interpolated_span(lengths, props):
    """Length at which the recall curve crosses 0.5, by interpolation."""
    for i, p in enumerate(props):
        if p < 0.5:
            if i == 0:
                return None
            frac = (props[i - 1] - 0.5) / (props[i - 1] - p)
            return lengths[i - 1] + frac * (lengths[i] - lengths[i - 1])
    return float(lengths[-1])
```
hint: Scan for the first proportion below 0.5. The crossing lies between that point and the one before it; the fraction of the way is (previous - 0.5) divided by (previous - current).
solution: The fraction of the gap covered is $(p_{k} - 0.5)/(p_{k} - p_{k+1})$, multiplied by the length gap so that unevenly spaced lengths work. The tests include a gap of 2 between lengths, the first point already below 0.5, no crossing at all, and a proportion of exactly 0.5, which counts as not yet below.

@quiz self
prompt: Luck and Vogel found that observers' estimated capacity in change detection was about the same for objects defined by one feature and for objects defined by several features. Explain what a slot model and a resource model each predict for the effect of the number of items and the number of features, and say what kind of data (other than hit and false-alarm rates) could help to tell the models apart.
hint: Think about whether the models predict a hard ceiling on the number of items and what they predict for the precision of each stored item.
solution: A slot model says that working memory holds a small number of objects (each a bound bundle of features), so capacity in items is about constant whatever the number of features per object, and performance for sets under capacity should be near perfect. A resource model says that a continuous pool is divided among all items, so as items increase each is stored with lower precision and errors increase gradually with no hard ceiling; the number of features per object could also cost resource. Hit and false-alarm rates in same-different tasks are a coarse measure because they mix capacity and precision. Continuous-report tasks, where observers reproduce a remembered color or orientation, give the whole distribution of errors: a resource model predicts the error spread widening smoothly with set size, whereas a slot model predicts a mixture of accurate responses (with fixed precision) and random guesses for items beyond capacity. Researchers have used such error distributions, with both accounts able to fit much of the data, so the question remains debated.
rubric: States the slot prediction (a hard limit of objects, constant over features per object).
rubric: States the resource prediction (graded decline of precision with load, no hard ceiling).
rubric: Proposes continuous-report error distributions or another measure of precision and says what each model predicts.
