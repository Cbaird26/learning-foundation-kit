@article
Ebbinghaus (1885) learned lists of nonsense syllables until he could recite them perfectly, then measured how much effort was needed to relearn them after a delay. The measure, **savings**, is the fraction of original effort saved:

$$\text{savings} = \frac{T_{\text{original}} - T_{\text{relearn}}}{T_{\text{original}}} .$$

Savings fell quickly at first and then slowly. This **forgetting curve** has been found with many materials: most forgetting happens early, and the rate of loss slows.

### What shape is the curve?

Two functions are common: exponential, $R = a\,e^{-kt}$, in which a constant fraction is lost per unit time, and power, $R = a\,t^{-b}$, in which the rate of loss slows over time. Taking logarithms makes both linear, so each can be fit by least squares: $\log R = \log a - k\,t$ (exponential; plot log retention against $t$) and $\log R = \log a - b \log t$ (power; plot against $\log t$). Here is illustrative recall data and the two fits (retention is the proportion recalled):

| delay $t$ (hours) | 1 | 2 | 4 | 8 | 24 | 48 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| proportion recalled | 0.62 | 0.54 | 0.46 | 0.40 | 0.30 | 0.25 |

Fitting in log space gives the exponential $R = 0.525\,e^{-0.0174\,t}$, with a sum of squared errors (SSE) of 0.0186 in retention units, and the power function $R = 0.633\,t^{-0.235}$, with SSE of 0.00035. The power function fits far better here, as in Wixted and Ebbesen's (1991) analysis of many data sets. A caution is that averaging many individual exponential curves with different rates produces an average that looks like a power function (Anderson and Tweney, 1997), so a good power fit to group data does not prove that an individual's forgetting follows a power law. Fits also depend on the range of delays and the measure (recall, recognition, savings).

```python
import math


def fit_exponential(ts, rs):
    """Fit R = a * exp(-k t) by least squares on log R; return (a, k)."""
    n = len(ts)
    ys = [math.log(r) for r in rs]
    mt, my = sum(ts) / n, sum(ys) / n
    slope = (sum((t - mt) * (y - my) for t, y in zip(ts, ys))
             / sum((t - mt) ** 2 for t in ts))
    return math.exp(my - slope * mt), -slope
```

### Why do we forget?

- **Decay**: traces fade with time. Hard to test, because time is always filled with other events.
- **Interference**: other memories compete. Learning new material impairs recall of old (**retroactive interference**, studied by McGeoch and others) and old material impairs learning of new (**proactive**). Jenkins and Dallenbach (1924) found that people forgot less over hours spent asleep than over the same hours spent awake, a result usually interpreted as less interference during sleep, though sleep may also help consolidation.
- **Cue-dependent forgetting**: the memory is available but the cues for it are not (Tulving; see week 5).
- **Retrieval-induced forgetting** (Anderson, Bjork and Bjork, 1994): after practicing retrieval of some items from a category, people recall unpracticed items from the same category worse than items from categories that were not practiced at all. The effect has been replicated in many studies and is usually explained by inhibition, though competition accounts exist.

### Memory as reconstruction

Memory does not replay a recording. Bartlett (1932) had people reproduce a folk story across repeated retellings and reported distortions toward their own expectations; his method was informal, but the notion of reconstruction guided later work. In the **DRM paradigm** (Deese, 1959; Roediger and McDermott, 1995), people study words that are all associates of a word that is not presented (bed, rest, awake, tired, dream... for the lure sleep). Many people later recall or recognize the lure, often at rates approaching those of words they really studied and often with a subjective feeling of remembering. The source monitoring framework (Johnson and colleagues, 1993) explains such errors as mistakes in attributing a mental experience to its source.

### The misinformation effect

Loftus and Palmer (1974) showed film clips of traffic accidents and asked how fast the cars were going when they "smashed into" each other, or "collided," "bumped," "hit" or "contacted." Mean speed estimates ranged from about 41 mph for smashed to about 32 mph for contacted. In a second experiment, a week later, 32% of participants who had heard "smashed" said that they had seen broken glass (none was shown), versus 14% for "hit" and 12% for a control group asked no speed question (50 per group). The misinformation effect is among the best replicated findings in memory research. What it means is debated: that the original trace is overwritten or altered (Loftus's view), or that both versions coexist and the later information is simply what people report when the original is weak or unavailable (McCloskey and Zaragoza, 1985).

### Eyewitness identification

Wells (1978) separated **estimator variables**, factors in the event that researchers can only estimate their effect on (lighting, distance, stress), from **system variables**, the procedures the justice system controls. Common recommendations include double-blind lineup administration (the officer does not know which person is the suspect), telling the witness that the culprit may not be in the lineup, recording the witness's confidence immediately, and avoiding feedback, because confidence rises after confirming feedback. Reviews of the lab and field data conclude that confidence at the first test under pristine conditions (a fair lineup, no suggestion, no feedback) is informative about accuracy, whereas later confidence, such as that stated in court, is much less so (Wixted and Wells, 2017). Guidelines and procedures differ across jurisdictions and change over time.
@example
title: Fitting an exponential in log space
problem: Retention is 0.80 at $t=1$ hour and 0.50 at $t=4$ hours. Assume $R = a\,e^{-kt}$ and find $k$, $a$ and the predicted retention at $t=10$.
step: Take logs: $\log 0.80 = -0.2231$ and $\log 0.50 = -0.6931$.
step: The slope is $-k = (-0.6931 + 0.2231)/(4 - 1) = -0.1567$, so $k \approx 0.157$ per hour.
step: The intercept is $\log a = -0.2231 + 0.1567 \times 1 = -0.0664$, so $a = e^{-0.0664} \approx 0.936$.
step: At $t = 10$: $R = 0.936\,e^{-1.567} \approx 0.936 \times 0.2087 \approx 0.195$.
answer: $k \approx 0.157$ per hour, $a \approx 0.936$, and $R(10) \approx 0.195$.

@example
title: Is the "smashed" effect reliable?
problem: In the second Loftus and Palmer experiment 16 of 50 "smashed" participants and 7 of 50 "hit" participants said they saw broken glass. Compute a two-proportion z statistic.
step: The proportions are $0.32$ and $0.14$; the pooled proportion is $(16 + 7)/100 = 0.23$.
step: The standard error is $\sqrt{0.23 \times 0.77 \times (1/50 + 1/50)} = \sqrt{0.007084} \approx 0.0842$.
step: $z = (0.32 - 0.14)/0.0842 \approx 2.14$, which exceeds 1.96, the two-sided 5% cutoff.
step: A single significant result in one sample is not proof of the interpretation: it shows that the wording changed reports, not whether memory was altered.
answer: $z \approx 2.14$.

@mistakes
- Assuming that a good power-law fit to averaged data means each person's forgetting is a power function: averaging can produce power-like curves.
- Treating the misinformation effect as proof that memories are overwritten: it is well replicated, but whether the original trace is changed or merely inaccessible is debated.
- Equating confidence with accuracy for eyewitnesses without regard to when the confidence was stated: later confidence is influenced by feedback.
- Fitting the power function with $t = 0$: $\log 0$ is undefined, so delays must be positive.

model:
```python
import math


def _line(xs, ys):
    n = len(xs)
    mx, my = sum(xs) / n, sum(ys) / n
    slope = (sum((x - mx) * (y - my) for x, y in zip(xs, ys))
             / sum((x - mx) ** 2 for x in xs))
    return slope, my - slope * mx


def better_fit(ts, rs):
    """Name of the function (exponential or power) with the smaller SSE."""
    logs = [math.log(r) for r in rs]
    s_e, i_e = _line(ts, logs)
    s_p, i_p = _line([math.log(t) for t in ts], logs)
    sse_e = sum((r - math.exp(i_e + s_e * t)) ** 2 for t, r in zip(ts, rs))
    sse_p = sum((r - math.exp(i_p + s_p * math.log(t))) ** 2
                for t, r in zip(ts, rs))
    return "exponential" if sse_e < sse_p else "power"
```
hint: Fit two lines in log space (one against $t$, one against $\log t$), turn each back into predicted retention with `math.exp`, and compare the squared errors.
solution: The log-space fits give each function's parameters, but the comparison must use predicted retention, otherwise errors at small retention values are over-weighted. The tests include exact exponential and exact power data, which have an SSE of nearly zero for the right function, and three noisy cases where the better function depends on how the curve flattens over the range of delays.

@quiz self
prompt: Loftus and Palmer found that the verb in a question changed speed estimates and later reports of broken glass. Explain two different interpretations of the broken-glass result (one that the memory itself was altered, one that it was not), and design a follow-up that could distinguish them.
hint: Think about what a witness would report if both the original and the suggested information remained available.
solution: One interpretation is that the leading wording was integrated into the memory, so the trace was changed and witnesses now remember glass. The other is that the original memory remains and witnesses, uncertain about the scene, use the suggestion as a basis for their report (a response or guessing bias), as McCloskey and Zaragoza argued. A follow-up could use the modified test of McCloskey and Zaragoza: show the event, give misinformation for some details (a hammer) and neutral information for others, and then, instead of offering the original item against the misinformation, offer the original item against a new alternative item that was never mentioned (a screwdriver). If the original traces were destroyed, the misled group should do worse than controls even on this test; if they coexist, the misled group should do as well as controls, because the original is available to choose. Sensible additions include counterbalanced details, a delay manipulation, and source-monitoring questions asking whether the witness saw or was told the detail. Results to date show that misinformation effects are robust, while the evidence about erasure of the original memory remains disputed.
