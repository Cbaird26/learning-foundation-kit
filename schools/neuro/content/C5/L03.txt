@article
**Energy** is a bookkeeping quantity that makes many motion problems far easier than tracking forces moment by moment. The unit is the joule: $1\ \text{J} = 1\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$.

### Work

A constant force $F$ acting through a displacement $d$ does **work**

$$W = Fd\cos\theta,$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between the force and the displacement. Work is a scalar and can be negative (a force opposing the motion, such as friction). A force perpendicular to the motion, such as the normal force on a horizontally sliding block, does zero work. Carrying a bag at constant height does no work *against gravity*, because gravity is perpendicular to the displacement.

### Kinetic energy and the work-energy theorem

The **kinetic energy** of a mass $m$ moving at speed $v$ is $KE = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$. The **work-energy theorem** says the net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy:

$$W_{\text{net}} = \Delta KE = \tfrac{1}{2}mv_f^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}mv_i^2.$$

### Potential energy

Near Earth's surface the gravitational potential energy relative to a chosen reference height is $PE_g = mgh$, with $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$. Only height *differences* matter. A spring stretched or compressed by $x$ from its relaxed length stores $PE_s = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$, where $k$ is the spring constant in N/m.

### Conservation of energy

A force is **conservative** (gravity, ideal springs) if the work it does depends only on the endpoints. With only conservative forces doing work,

$$KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f.$$

If friction or air resistance does work, mechanical energy is converted to thermal energy: $KE_i + PE_i + W_{\text{nc}} = KE_f + PE_f$, with $W_{\text{nc}}$ negative for friction. Total energy, including heat, is always conserved. This is why a fall can be solved without knowing the time: $mgh = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ gives $v = \sqrt{2gh}$, independent of mass.

### Power

**Power** is the rate of doing work or transferring energy, $P = W/t$, in watts ($1\ \text{W} = 1\ \text{J/s}$). When a force moves something at constant speed along its direction, $P = Fv$. A person at rest uses a basal power of roughly $80$ W; a food Calorie (kilocalorie) equals about $4184$ J. Because the body is not perfectly efficient, much of the energy from food becomes heat; this theme returns in the thermodynamics weeks.

@example
title: Speed at the bottom of a fall
problem: A $2.0\ \text{kg}$ object is released from rest $5.0\ \text{m}$ above the ground. Ignoring air resistance and using $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$, find its speed just before impact.
step: Choose the ground as $PE = 0$. Initially $KE_i = 0$ and $PE_i = mgh = (2.0)(9.8)(5.0) = 98\ \text{J}$.
step: Just before impact $PE_f = 0$, so all $98$ J is kinetic: $\tfrac{1}{2}(2.0)v^2 = 98$.
step: Solve: $v^2 = 98$, so $v = 9.9\ \text{m/s}$. Note that the mass cancels: $v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2(9.8)(5.0)}$.
step: Compare with kinematics: $v^2 = 2g\,\Delta y$ gives the same result with less bookkeeping about time.

@mistakes
- Forgetting the $\cos\theta$ factor or using the angle to the surface instead of the angle between force and displacement.
- Using $\tfrac{1}{2}mv$ or $mv^2$ for kinetic energy, or forgetting to square the speed.
- Applying conservation of mechanical energy when friction acts without including the thermal energy it produces.
- Mixing joules and kilocalories, or confusing power (J/s) with energy (J).

@practice num
prompt: A person pulls a sled with a rope that makes a $60^\circ$ angle with the horizontal ground, applying $50\ \text{N}$ of tension over a horizontal displacement of $10\ \text{m}$. How much work does the rope do, in joules?
answer: 250
check: 50*10*math.cos(math.radians(60))
tol: 0.5
unit: J
hint: Only the component of force along the displacement does work.
solution: $W = Fd\cos\theta = (50)(10)\cos 60^\circ = 250\ \text{J}$.

@practice num
prompt: A $70\ \text{kg}$ runner moves at $4.0\ \text{m/s}$. What is the runner's kinetic energy in joules?
answer: 560
check: 0.5*70*4.0**2
unit: J
hint: Square the speed before multiplying.
solution: $KE = \tfrac{1}{2}(70)(4.0)^2 = 560\ \text{J}$.

@practice num
prompt: A $2.0\ \text{kg}$ book is lifted at constant speed $1.5\ \text{m}$ onto a shelf. Using $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$, how much gravitational potential energy does it gain, in joules?
answer: 29.4
check: 2.0*9.8*1.5
tol: 0.05

solution: $\Delta PE = (2.0)(9.8)(1.5) = 29.4\ \text{J}$.

@practice num
prompt: A $60\ \text{kg}$ person climbs a flight of stairs rising $3.0\ \text{m}$ in $5.0\ \text{s}$. Using $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$, what average power is used to raise the person's body, in watts?
answer: 353
check: 60*9.8*3.0/5.0
tol: 1
unit: W
hint: First find the work done against gravity.
hint: Power is work divided by time.
solution: $W = mgh = (60)(9.8)(3.0) = 1764\ \text{J}$; $P = 1764/5.0 = 353\ \text{W}$.

@quiz num
prompt: A $0.50\ \text{kg}$ ball is dropped from rest from a height of $10\ \text{m}$. Ignoring air resistance and using $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$, what is its speed just before it lands, in m/s?
answer: 14.0
check: (2*9.8*10)**0.5
tol: 0.05
unit: m/s
hint: Set the initial potential energy equal to the final kinetic energy.
solution: $mgh = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ gives $v = \sqrt{2(9.8)(10)} = 14.0\ \text{m/s}$.

@quiz num
prompt: A spring of constant $k = 200\ \text{N/m}$ is compressed $0.10\ \text{m}$ from its relaxed length. How much energy is stored in the spring, in joules?
answer: 1.0
check: 0.5*200*0.10**2
tol: 0.01
unit: J
hint: Use the spring energy formula with the compression distance squared.
solution: $PE_s = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(200)(0.10)^2 = 1.0\ \text{J}$.
