@article
A force can push an object along a line, but it can also make it turn. The turning effect is the **torque**.

### Torque

For a force $F$ applied at distance $r$ from a pivot, at angle $\theta$ between the force and the line from the pivot to the point of application,

$$\tau = rF\sin\theta = r_\perp F,$$

where $r_\perp = r\sin\theta$ is the **lever arm**, the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force. The unit is N$\cdot$m (not joules, although the dimensions match). Torque tending to rotate counterclockwise is conventionally positive and clockwise negative; you may choose either as long as you are consistent. A force whose line of action passes through the pivot has zero torque.

### Static equilibrium

An object at rest and staying at rest must satisfy both conditions:

$$\sum \vec F = 0 \quad\text{and}\quad \sum \tau = 0 .$$

The second condition holds about *any* pivot point, so choose the one that eliminates the most unknowns, usually where an unknown force acts. The weight of a uniform object acts at its centre, called the **center of gravity** (for ordinary Earth-bound objects, the same as the center of mass).

Procedure for an equilibrium problem:

1. Draw a free-body diagram with all forces and their points of application.
2. Choose a pivot that removes an unknown force.
3. Write $\sum\tau = 0$ and solve for one unknown.
4. Write $\sum F_x = 0$ and $\sum F_y = 0$ for the rest.

### Rotational kinematics

Angle is measured in radians, with $2\pi$ rad $= 360^\circ$. **Angular velocity** $\omega = \Delta\theta/\Delta t$ (rad/s) and **angular acceleration** $\alpha = \Delta\omega/\Delta t$ (rad/s$^2$) obey the same equations as linear motion, for example $\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t$. A point at radius $r$ has speed $v = r\omega$ and tangential acceleration $a_t = r\alpha$. To convert, $1\ \text{rev/min} = 2\pi/60\ \text{rad/s}$.

### Moment of inertia and Newton's second law for rotation

The rotational analogue of mass is the **moment of inertia** $I = \sum m_i r_i^2$ (kg$\cdot$m$^2$), which depends on how the mass is distributed about the axis: mass far from the axis counts more. Then

$$\sum \tau = I\alpha,$$

and rotational kinetic energy is $\tfrac{1}{2}I\omega^2$. This is why a runner swings bent legs forward: bending the knee reduces $I$ about the hip, so the same muscle torque gives a larger angular acceleration. Use $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ whenever weight appears.

@example
title: Balancing a plank
problem: A uniform $4.0\ \text{m}$ plank of mass $10\ \text{kg}$ is balanced on a pivot at its centre. A $50\ \text{kg}$ child sits $1.0\ \text{m}$ to the left of the pivot. How far to the right of the pivot must a $30\ \text{kg}$ child sit? Use $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.
step: Take the pivot at the centre. The plank's own weight acts at the centre, so it has zero lever arm and contributes no torque.
step: Equilibrium requires equal and opposite torques: $m_1 g r_1 = m_2 g r_2$, and $g$ cancels.
step: $(50)(1.0) = (30)\,r_2$, so $r_2 = 50/30 = 1.67\ \text{m}$.
step: Check that this fits on the plank: it extends $2.0$ m from the centre, so $1.67$ m is possible. Always compare an answer with the geometry.

@mistakes
- Using the full distance $r$ instead of the perpendicular lever arm $r\sin\theta$.
- Writing only $\sum F = 0$ and forgetting the torque condition (a pair of equal and opposite forces at different points still rotates the object).
- Measuring all lever arms from different pivots in one torque equation.
- Treating the weight of an extended object as acting at one end instead of at its centre of gravity.

@practice num
prompt: A $40\ \text{N}$ force is applied perpendicular to a wrench handle at $0.25\ \text{m}$ from the bolt. What torque does it exert, in N$\cdot$m?
answer: 10
check: 0.25*40
unit: N m
hint: When the force is perpendicular to the lever, $\sin\theta = 1$.
solution: $\tau = rF = (0.25)(40) = 10\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$.

@practice num
prompt: A $30\ \text{kg}$ child sits $2.0\ \text{m}$ from the pivot of a light, balanced seesaw. At what distance from the pivot, on the other side, must a $40\ \text{kg}$ child sit to balance it, in metres?
answer: 1.5
check: 30*2.0/40
unit: m
hint: Torques about the pivot must cancel; $g$ cancels from both sides.
solution: $(30)(2.0) = (40)r$, so $r = 1.5\ \text{m}$.

@practice mc
prompt: Two equal and opposite forces act on a rod at two different points, along parallel lines that do not coincide. Which statement is correct?
choice: The rod is in equilibrium because the forces cancel
choice*: The net force is zero but the net torque is not, so the rod rotates
choice: The net torque is zero but the net force is not
choice: The rod accelerates linearly
hint: Check both conditions for equilibrium separately.
solution: The vector sum of the forces is zero, but their lines of action are separated by a lever arm, so they exert a net torque (a couple).

@practice num
prompt: A uniform $6.0\ \text{m}$ plank of mass $20\ \text{kg}$ rests on two supports at its ends. A $60\ \text{kg}$ person stands $2.0\ \text{m}$ from the left end. Using $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$, what upward force does the right support exert, in newtons?
answer: 294
check: (20*9.8*3.0 + 60*9.8*2.0)/6.0
tol: 0.5
unit: N
hint: Take the pivot at the left support so that its unknown force has zero torque.
solution: $F_R(6.0) = (20)(9.8)(3.0) + (60)(9.8)(2.0) = 588 + 1176$, so $F_R = 1764/6.0 = 294\ \text{N}$.

@practice num
prompt: A centrifuge rotor spins at $120\ \text{rev/min}$. What is its angular velocity in rad/s? Give your answer to two decimal places.
answer: 12.57
check: 120*2*math.pi/60
tol: 0.01
unit: rad/s
hint: One revolution is $2\pi$ rad and one minute is 60 s.
solution: $\omega = 120(2\pi)/60 = 4\pi = 12.57\ \text{rad/s}$.

@quiz num
prompt: A dumbbell consists of two $2.0\ \text{kg}$ point masses on a light rod, each $0.50\ \text{m}$ from the axis of rotation through the centre. A net torque of $1.5\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$ acts about this axis. What is the angular acceleration in rad/s$^2$?
answer: 1.5
check: 1.5/(2*2.0*0.50**2)
tol: 0.01
unit: rad/s^2
hint: First compute the moment of inertia as the sum of $mr^2$ over both masses.
solution: $I = 2(2.0)(0.50)^2 = 1.0\ \text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2$. $\alpha = \tau/I = 1.5\ \text{rad/s}^2$.

@quiz num
prompt: A uniform horizontal beam of length $2.0\ \text{m}$ and mass $4.0\ \text{kg}$ is hinged to a wall at one end. A cable attached to the other end makes $30^\circ$ with the horizontal and holds the beam level. A $5.0\ \text{kg}$ sign hangs from the far end of the beam. Using $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$, what is the tension in the cable, in newtons?
answer: 137.2
check: (4.0*9.8*1.0 + 5.0*9.8*2.0)/(2.0*math.sin(math.radians(30)))
tol: 0.3
unit: N
hint: Take the hinge as the pivot. Only the vertical component of the tension has a lever arm.
hint: $T\sin 30^\circ\,(2.0) = m_b g\,(1.0) + m_s g\,(2.0)$.
solution: $T(0.50)(2.0) = (4.0)(9.8)(1.0) + (5.0)(9.8)(2.0) = 39.2 + 98 = 137.2$, so $T = 137.2\ \text{N}$.
