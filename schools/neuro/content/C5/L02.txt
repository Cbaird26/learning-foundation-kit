@article
A **force** is a push or pull, measured in newtons (N); $1\ \text{N} = 1\ \text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2$. Forces are vectors. Newton's three laws tell you how they change motion.

### The three laws

1. **Inertia.** An object keeps its velocity (including rest) unless a net external force acts on it.
2. **$\sum \vec F = m\vec a$.** The acceleration is proportional to the net force and inversely proportional to the mass, and points along the net force.
3. **Action-reaction.** If object A pushes on object B with force $\vec F$, then B pushes on A with $-\vec F$. The two forces act on *different* bodies, so they never cancel in a free-body diagram.

### Common forces

- **Weight** $W = mg$, downward, where $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$. Mass (kg) is the amount of matter; weight (N) is a force.
- **Normal force** $N$, perpendicular to a surface, whatever value keeps the object from passing through it. It is not always equal to $mg$.
- **Tension** $T$ in a rope or tendon pulls along the rope; an ideal light rope has the same tension throughout.
- **Kinetic friction** $f_k = \mu_k N$ opposes sliding. **Static friction** satisfies $f_s \le \mu_s N$ and adjusts to prevent sliding up to that limit.

### Free-body diagrams

A free-body diagram (FBD) shows one body as a dot with only the forces acting *on it* drawn as arrows. The procedure:

1. Isolate one body.
2. Draw every force on it (weight, normal, tension, friction, applied).
3. Choose axes, ideally with one along the acceleration.
4. Write $\sum F_x = ma_x$ and $\sum F_y = ma_y$.

If there is no acceleration along an axis, the forces along it balance.

### Two connected bodies

Draw a separate FBD for each body and write Newton's second law for each. Connected bodies that stay taut share the same acceleration magnitude, and a light rope over a frictionless pulley gives the same tension on both sides. Then you have as many equations as unknowns (typically $a$ and $T$). Adding the equations eliminates $T$.

### Apparent weight

In an elevator accelerating upward with acceleration $a$, a person on a scale has $N - mg = ma$, so the scale reads $N = m(g + a)$, larger than $mg$. Accelerating downward, $N = m(g - a)$, smaller. In free fall $N = 0$: weightlessness means no normal force, not no gravity.

Use $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ throughout.

@example
title: A block on a table pulled by a hanging mass
problem: A $5.0\ \text{kg}$ block rests on a frictionless table and is tied by a light rope over a frictionless pulley to a $3.0\ \text{kg}$ block hanging off the edge. Find the acceleration and the rope tension. Use $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.
step: FBD of the table block: horizontal tension $T$ is the only unbalanced force, so $T = m_1 a$ with $m_1 = 5.0$ kg. Weight and normal force cancel vertically.
step: FBD of the hanging block (down positive): $m_2 g - T = m_2 a$ with $m_2 = 3.0$ kg.
step: Add the two equations: $m_2 g = (m_1 + m_2)a$, so $a = \dfrac{(3.0)(9.8)}{8.0} = 3.68\ \text{m/s}^2$.
step: Then $T = m_1 a = (5.0)(3.675) = 18.4\ \text{N}$, which is less than the hanging weight $29.4$ N, as it must be because that block accelerates downward.

@mistakes
- Drawing the reaction force on the same free-body diagram as its action force; they act on different objects.
- Assuming $N = mg$ regardless of ramps, extra pushes or elevator acceleration.
- Confusing mass (kg) with weight (N), or forgetting that $g$ must be used to convert.
- Using the total mass of connected bodies in an equation about the tension without isolating one body.

@practice num
prompt: A $4.0\ \text{kg}$ block on a frictionless horizontal table is tied by a light rope over a frictionless pulley to a $2.0\ \text{kg}$ block hanging over the edge. Using $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$, find the acceleration of the blocks in m/s$^2$ to two decimal places.
answer: 3.27
check: 2.0*9.8/(4.0 + 2.0)
tol: 0.01
unit: m/s^2
hint: Write one equation for each block and add them so the tension cancels.
hint: The only external force driving the system along the rope is the hanging block's weight.
solution: $m_2 g = (m_1 + m_2)a$, so $a = (2.0)(9.8)/6.0 = 3.27\ \text{m/s}^2$.

@practice num
prompt: A $10\ \text{kg}$ crate on a horizontal floor is pulled by a horizontal $50\ \text{N}$ force. The coefficient of kinetic friction is $\mu_k = 0.30$. Using $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$, find the acceleration in m/s$^2$ to two decimal places.
answer: 2.06
check: (50 - 0.30*10*9.8)/10
tol: 0.01
unit: m/s^2
hint: On a level floor the normal force equals the weight when there is no vertical acceleration.
hint: Net force is $50 - \mu_k N$.
solution: $N = mg = 98$ N, so $f_k = (0.30)(98) = 29.4$ N. Then $a = (50 - 29.4)/10 = 2.06\ \text{m/s}^2$.

@quiz num
prompt: Two blocks of mass $3.0\ \text{kg}$ and $5.0\ \text{kg}$ hang from the two ends of a light rope that passes over a frictionless pulley. Using $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$, what is the tension in the rope in newtons, to the nearest tenth?
answer: 36.8
check: 2*3.0*5.0*9.8/(3.0 + 5.0)
tol: 0.1
unit: N
hint: Write $m_2 g - T = m_2 a$ and $T - m_1 g = m_1 a$.
hint: Add them to find $a$, then substitute into either equation.
solution: $a = (5.0 - 3.0)(9.8)/8.0 = 2.45\ \text{m/s}^2$. $T = m_1(g + a) = 3.0(12.25) = 36.75 \approx 36.8\ \text{N}$.

@quiz num
prompt: An $80\ \text{kg}$ person stands on a scale in an elevator that accelerates downward at $1.5\ \text{m/s}^2$. Using $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$, what force does the scale read, in newtons?
answer: 664
check: 80*(9.8 - 1.5)
unit: N
tol: 0.5
hint: Take down as positive and write $mg - N = ma$.
solution: $N = m(g - a) = 80(9.8 - 1.5) = 664\ \text{N}$.
