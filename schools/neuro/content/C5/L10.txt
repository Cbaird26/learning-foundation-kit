@article
Thermodynamics governs how the body turns food into movement and heat.

### The first law

The **first law of thermodynamics** is conservation of energy for a system:

$$\Delta U = Q - W,$$

where $\Delta U$ is the change in internal energy, $Q$ is heat added *to* the system and $W$ is work done *by* the system. Heat absorbed raises $\Delta U$; work done by the system lowers it. A system in a steady state has $\Delta U = 0$, so heat in equals work out.

### The second law and efficiency

The **second law** says heat flows spontaneously from hot to cold, and no cyclic process can convert heat completely into work; some energy is always rejected to a cooler reservoir. The **efficiency** is

$$e = \frac{W_{\text{out}}}{E_{\text{in}}}.$$

The human body is a poor engine: muscles are about $20$ to $25\%$ efficient. If the metabolic power is $P_{\text{met}}$, the mechanical power is $eP_{\text{met}}$ and the remaining $(1 - e)P_{\text{met}}$ appears as heat. Entropy, which measures the number of ways energy can be spread out, increases in any spontaneous process in an isolated system; living organisms maintain order locally by increasing entropy in their surroundings.

### Metabolic rate

The **basal metabolic rate (BMR)** is the energy use at rest, about $80$ W for an adult, i.e. roughly $1700$ kcal/day, because $1\ \text{kcal} = 4184\ \text{J}$. Light activity might use $120$ to $200$ W; hard exercise can reach $1000$ W or more. Nearly all of this energy eventually leaves as heat, so the body must lose it or its temperature would rise.

### Heat loss pathways

At rest in a cool room the body loses heat mostly by radiation and convection. Radiation obeys the Stefan-Boltzmann law:

$$P_{\text{net}} = e_{\text{rad}}\,\sigma A\,(T_{\text{skin}}^4 - T_{\text{env}}^4),$$

with $\sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{W/(m}^2\cdot\text{K}^4)$, skin emissivity $e_{\text{rad}} \approx 0.97$, area $A$ in m$^2$, and temperatures in kelvin. During hard exercise, evaporation of sweat dominates. Each kilogram of sweat evaporated removes about $2.42 \times 10^6$ J, so for a heat power $P$ lost entirely by evaporation, the sweat evaporated in time $t$ is

$$m = \frac{Pt}{L_v}, \qquad L_v = 2.42 \times 10^6\ \text{J/kg}.$$

Sweat that drips off without evaporating removes no heat, which is why the useful rate is lower than the measured sweat loss. Without heat loss, the temperature of a body of mass $M$ with specific heat $c = 3500\ \text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$ rises as $\Delta T = Q/(Mc)$.

### Clinical connection

Hyperthermia and heat stroke occur when heat production exceeds heat loss, especially in humid air that blocks evaporation. Fever is a regulated rise in the hypothalamic set point, and shivering is muscle activity that increases heat production.

@example
title: Heat produced in exercise
problem: A cyclist produces $120\ \text{W}$ of mechanical power with an efficiency of $25\%$. How much power does the cyclist's body generate metabolically, and how much appears as heat?
step: Efficiency is $e = P_{\text{mech}}/P_{\text{met}}$, so $P_{\text{met}} = P_{\text{mech}}/e = 120/0.25 = 480\ \text{W}$.
step: The rest is heat: $P_{\text{heat}} = 480 - 120 = 360\ \text{W}$, three times the mechanical output.
step: In an hour this is $360 \times 3600 = 1.3 \times 10^6\ \text{J}$ of heat that must be lost, for example by evaporating $1.3 \times 10^6/2.42 \times 10^6 = 0.54\ \text{kg}$ of sweat.

@mistakes
- Using the wrong sign convention for $W$: in $\Delta U = Q - W$, work done *by* the system is positive.
- Calling the work output the metabolic power, and so missing the heat that accounts for the other $75\%$.
- Using Celsius temperatures in the radiation law.
- Assuming that all sweat produced removes heat; only the evaporated sweat does.

@quiz num
prompt: An athlete of mass $70\ \text{kg}$ (specific heat $3500\ \text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$) has $600\ \text{W}$ of excess heat that is not lost, for $10$ minutes. By how many kelvin would the body temperature rise? Give two decimal places.
answer: 1.47
check: 600*600/(70*3500)
tol: 0.02
unit: K
hint: Find the total heat in joules, then use $Q = mc\Delta T$.
