@article
A **wave** transfers energy without transferring matter. In a **transverse** wave the medium moves perpendicular to the direction of travel (a wave on a string); in a **longitudinal** wave it moves parallel (sound). Sound is a longitudinal pressure wave with alternating compressions and rarefactions.

### Wave speed

Every periodic wave obeys

$$v = f\lambda,$$

where $f$ is frequency (Hz) and $\lambda$ is wavelength (m). The speed is set by the medium, not the source. In air at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$ sound travels at $343\ \text{m/s}$; in soft tissue at about $1540\ \text{m/s}$; faster in bone. When a wave moves into a new medium, $f$ stays the same and $\lambda$ changes with $v$. Audible sound is about $20$ Hz to $20$ kHz; **ultrasound** is above $20$ kHz, and diagnostic imaging uses roughly $1$ to $15$ MHz.

### Intensity and decibels

**Intensity** is power per area, $I = P/A$, in W/m$^2$. For a point source radiating equally in all directions, $I = P/(4\pi r^2)$: doubling the distance cuts the intensity to one quarter. The ear responds over a vast range, so we use the logarithmic **sound level**

$$\beta = 10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{I}{I_0}\right)\ \text{dB}, \qquad I_0 = 1.0 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{W/m}^2,$$

where $I_0$ is near the threshold of hearing. Each factor of $10$ in intensity adds $10$ dB, and doubling the intensity adds about $3$ dB. Conversation is near $60$ dB, and prolonged exposure to levels above about $85$ dB can damage hearing. Because the scale is logarithmic, you cannot add decibels of two sources directly; add their intensities instead.

### Standing waves and resonance

A tube closed at one end (the ear canal is a rough model) has its lowest resonance at $f_1 = v/(4L)$, with odd harmonics only. A tube open at both ends has $f_n = nv/(2L)$. These resonances boost sensitivity to some frequencies.

### Doppler effect

If a source and observer approach each other, the observed frequency rises; if they separate, it falls. For sound reflected from blood moving at speed $v_b$ at angle $\theta$ to the beam, using ultrasound frequency $f_0$ and sound speed $c$,

$$\Delta f \approx \frac{2f_0\,v_b\cos\theta}{c}.$$

This is how Doppler ultrasound measures blood velocity and detects a stenosis.

### Ultrasound imaging

A transducer sends a short pulse and listens for echoes from boundaries where tissue properties change. If the echo returns after a time $t$, the reflecting boundary lies at depth

$$d = \frac{vt}{2},$$

because the pulse travels there and back. Higher frequency gives a shorter wavelength and better resolution, but is absorbed more and so penetrates less deeply. Imaging details smaller than about one wavelength is not possible, so a $5$ MHz beam in tissue ($\lambda \approx 0.31$ mm) has finer resolution than a $2$ MHz beam. Gas and bone reflect nearly all of the beam, which is why a gel is used to remove air between probe and skin.

@example
title: Decibels from intensity
problem: A sound has an intensity of $2.0 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{W/m}^2$. What is its sound level? Take $I_0 = 1.0 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{W/m}^2$.
step: Form the ratio: $I/I_0 = 2.0 \times 10^{-5}/10^{-12} = 2.0 \times 10^7$.
step: Take the base-10 logarithm: $\log_{10}(2.0 \times 10^7) = 7.301$.
step: Multiply by $10$: $\beta = 73\ \text{dB}$.
step: Sanity check: $10^{-5}$ W/m$^2$ is exactly $70$ dB, and doubling the intensity adds about $3$ dB.

@mistakes
- Using the natural logarithm, or $20\log$, instead of $10\log_{10}$ for intensity.
- Adding decibel levels of two sources directly instead of adding intensities.
- Forgetting the factor $\tfrac{1}{2}$ in $d = vt/2$ for echoes.
- Assuming the frequency of ultrasound changes entering tissue; the wavelength changes and the frequency does not.
