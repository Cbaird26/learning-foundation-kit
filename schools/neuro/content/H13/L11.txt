@article
**Globalization** is the growing connection of economies, societies and cultures across borders through trade, investment, migration, communication and the spread of ideas, technologies and diseases. It is not new: the Silk Road, the Indian Ocean trade and the Columbian Exchange were earlier waves. The current wave accelerated after 1945.

### Drivers

- **Institutions.** At Bretton Woods (1944), delegates created the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the World Bank. The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT, 1947) lowered tariffs, and the World Trade Organization (WTO) replaced it in 1995. Regional groups such as the European Union (formed in stages from the 1950s) integrated economies.
- **Technology.** Containerized shipping (from the 1950s), jet aircraft, satellites and the internet reduced the cost of moving goods and information.
- **Policy shifts.** China began market-oriented reforms in 1978 and later joined the WTO in 2001. Many countries opened their economies in the 1980s and 1990s, and the Soviet bloc's end brought new markets.
- **Supply chains.** Firms spread production of a single product across several countries.

### Comparative advantage

A country has an **absolute advantage** if it can make a good with fewer resources than another. **Comparative advantage** is about **opportunity cost**: the value of what you give up. Even if one country is better at making everything, both can gain by specializing in the good where the opportunity cost is lower, and trading.

Example: In one day of labor, Country A can make 6 units of cloth or 3 units of wine, and Country B can make 2 units of cloth or 2 units of wine. The opportunity cost of one unit of wine is 2 cloth in A and 1 cloth in B. So B has the comparative advantage in wine, and A in cloth, even though A has an absolute advantage in both goods (it makes more of each per day). Trade at an exchange rate between those opportunity costs benefits both.

### Viewpoints

Supporters argue that trade lowers prices, raises overall output, and helped many millions of people in East Asia leave extreme poverty. Critics point to job losses in some industries and regions, widening inequality within countries, weaker labor and environmental standards, and dependence on distant supply chains. Economists generally agree that trade creates both winners and losers, but disagree about how large the effects are and what policies should respond. Policy questions of this kind involve values as well as facts, and this course does not choose among them.

### Health and globalization

Faster travel carries pathogens quickly. SARS (2003) and COVID-19, first recognized in late 2019 and declared a pandemic by the WHO in March 2020, spread rapidly through air travel. Globalization also supports vaccine development, shared research and international health programs. Medical supply chains and unequal access to medicines have been debated.

### Rule of 70

A quantity growing at $r$ percent per year doubles in roughly $70 / r$ years. This helps compare growth rates in an economy.

@example
title: Doubling time of an economy
problem: An economy grows at 5 percent per year. About how long will it take to double in size?
step: Use the rule of 70: doubling time is about $70 / r$.
step: Substitute $r = 5$: $70 / 5 = 14$ years.
step: Check roughly: $1.05^{14} \approx 1.98$, which is about double.

@mistakes
- Treating globalization as something that began only in the twentieth century.
- Confusing absolute advantage (who makes more) with comparative advantage (who gives up less).
- Saying that economists agree on the net effect of trade for every group; they agree it has winners and losers.
- Giving a personal verdict on a contested policy as though it were a historical fact.
