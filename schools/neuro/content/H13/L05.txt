@article
In the 1400s and 1500s, ships began to connect oceans that had not been regularly linked before. The result was a world in which crops, animals, diseases, people, silver and ideas moved across the Atlantic and Pacific.

### Why the voyages began

Motives were mixed. European states and merchants wanted direct access to Asian spices and silk without paying intermediaries on overland routes; rulers sought revenue and rivalry with other states; Christian missionary aims were common; and new technologies such as improved sailing ships (the caravel), the magnetic compass and better maps made long voyages possible. Portuguese expeditions went down the African coast, and in 1498 a Portuguese fleet reached India by sailing around Africa. In 1492 a Spanish-sponsored expedition crossed the Atlantic and reached islands in the Caribbean. The Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) divided claims between Spain and Portugal. A Spanish expedition completed the first circumnavigation in 1519 to 1522.

Exploration is a European-centered word: the places reached were already home to millions of people with their own societies, such as the Aztec (Mexica) and Inca empires, and many Asian and African states traded on their own terms. Ming China sent large naval expeditions in the early 1400s but then limited them.

### The Columbian Exchange

Historians use **Columbian Exchange** for the transfer of plants, animals, people and diseases between the Eastern and Western Hemispheres after 1492.
- **From the Americas:** maize, potatoes, cassava, tomatoes, chili peppers, cacao, tobacco. Potatoes and maize supported population growth in Europe, Africa and China.
- **To the Americas:** wheat, rice, sugarcane, coffee, horses, cattle, pigs, sheep.
- **Diseases to the Americas:** smallpox, measles, influenza and others.

### Disease and population

Indigenous peoples of the Americas had not been exposed to many Old World infections, so they had little acquired immunity. Smallpox and other diseases spread quickly, often ahead of Europeans themselves. War, forced labor, enslavement and the loss of food supplies compounded the toll. Estimates of the pre-1492 population of the Americas vary widely (from roughly 10 million to over 100 million), and the size of the decline is debated, but historians agree that many regions lost most of their people within a century or two. Old World travelers also carried diseases the other way; the origin of syphilis in Europe remains debated.

### Silver, sugar and slavery

Large silver mines in the Americas, such as Potosí, financed trade with Asia; much silver ended up in China. Sugar plantations in the Caribbean and Brazil relied on enslaved labor. Between the 1500s and 1860s, about 12.5 million Africans were forced onto ships bound for the Americas and about 10.7 million survived the voyage, according to a widely used database of recorded voyages; these are estimates drawn from incomplete records. Enslaved people and their descendants shaped the cultures, foods and medical knowledge of the Americas, and their communities resisted slavery in many ways.

### Perspectives

Spanish accounts at the time described conquest as a religious and political mission, while some contemporaries, such as members of religious orders, criticized the treatment of Indigenous peoples. Indigenous accounts, preserved in codices and oral histories, describe the conquest as a catastrophe and describe the actions of their own leaders and allies; many Indigenous groups allied with Spain against rivals. Modern historians combine these sources.

title: Mortality on the Middle Passage
problem: A database records 12.5 million Africans embarked on slave voyages and 10.7 million disembarked alive. What percentage did not survive the voyage? Round to one decimal place.
step: Find how many did not survive: $12.5 - 10.7 = 1.8$ million.
step: Divide by the number embarked: $1.8 / 12.5 = 0.144$.
step: Convert to a percentage: $14.4\%$.
step: This is an estimate from incomplete records, and it does not include deaths during capture or in the first years of forced labor.

@mistakes
- Saying that Europeans "discovered" the Americas without noting that millions of people already lived there.
- Attributing the population collapse to disease alone; war, enslavement, forced labor and famine contributed.
- Treating the Columbian Exchange as one-way; crops such as potatoes and maize changed Old World diets.
- Quoting a single exact number for the pre-contact population or the slave trade; these are estimates.
