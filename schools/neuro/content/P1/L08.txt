@article
Early visual neurons have center-surround receptive fields, and V1 neurons respond to oriented edges. Barlow (1961) proposed that sensory systems are shaped to **transmit as much information as possible** about the natural world with limited resources: a limited range of firing rates, noise, and metabolic cost. This is the **efficient coding hypothesis**. Making it a theory means choosing what is limited and then solving an optimization problem; the answer depends on that choice.

### One neuron with a bounded response: histogram equalization

A neuron's response $r\in[0, r_{\max}]$ is a monotonic function of a stimulus $s$ with density $p(s)$, and the output is read with fixed resolution (many distinguishable levels, negligible noise). The output entropy is largest when $r$ is uniform on $[0, r_{\max}]$, since the uniform density maximizes entropy on a bounded interval. By the change-of-variables rule, $r = r_{\max}F(s)$, where $F(s) = \int_{-\infty}^sp$ is the cumulative distribution, gives a uniform $r$ (the probability integral transform). Laughlin (1981) found that the contrast–response function of large monopolar cells in the blowfly eye resembles the cumulative distribution of natural contrasts, a classic test of the idea.

**Example.** Let $s$ be exponential with rate $\lambda = 1$ and let the neuron have 8 equally likely output levels (3 bits). The level boundaries are the quantiles $s_k = -\ln(1 - k/8)$ for $k = 1,\dots,7$: $0.134, 0.288, 0.470, 0.693, 0.981, 1.386, 2.079$. A neuron with equally spaced boundaries at $0.5, 1.0,\dots,3.5$ instead has level probabilities $0.393, 0.239, 0.145, 0.088, 0.053, 0.032, 0.020, 0.030$ and entropy $2.38$ bits: wasted capacity because rare stimuli get as many levels as common ones.

### With noise: tuning slope follows the stimulus density

When the response is noisy, discriminability matters, measured by Fisher information $J(s)$. For a neural population with small noise, the information is (Brunel and Nadal, 1998)
$$I \approx H(s) + \tfrac12\int p(s)\log\frac{J(s)}{2\pi e}\,ds .$$
For one neuron with additive Gaussian noise of variance $\sigma^2$ and a monotone response $f(s)$ increasing from 0 to $r_{\max}$, $J = f'(s)^2/\sigma^2$ and $\int f'\,ds = r_{\max}$. Maximize $\int p\log f'\,ds$ subject to that constraint: the Lagrangian condition $p(s)/f'(s) = \mu$ gives $f'(s) = r_{\max}\,p(s)$, which is again $f = r_{\max}F$. In the noisy regime the slope is largest where stimuli are most common, as in histogram equalization. Other noise models and constraints give different optima, so "the" efficient code is relative to the assumptions.

### Many channels: water-filling

Several independent channels $i$ (say spatial-frequency components) have noise variances $N_i$, and we allocate signal variance $P_i \ge 0$ under a budget $\sum_iP_i = P$ (a proxy for metabolic cost). Maximizing $\sum_i\tfrac12\log_2(1 + P_i/N_i)$ with a Lagrange multiplier gives $1/(N_i + P_i) = \mu$ for active channels, so
$$P_i = \max(0, \nu - N_i),\qquad \nu = \frac{P + \sum_{i\in\text{active}}N_i}{n_{\text{active}}} .$$
Noisy channels (large $N_i$) receive nothing; the "water level" $\nu$ is common. With $N = (1, 2, 4, 8)$ and $P = 10$: three channels are active, $\nu = (10 + 7)/3 = 5.667 > 4$ and $< 8$, so $P = (4.667, 3.667, 1.667, 0)$ and the information is $2.254$ bits. For natural images, whose power falls as $1/f^2$ with spatial frequency $f$ (Field, 1987), the noiseless solution equalizes output power across frequencies (**whitening**: gain proportional to $f$), and adding noise makes the filter band-pass, the center-surround shape that Atick and Redlich (1990) derived for retinal ganglion cells.

### Sparse coding

Efficient coding in the sense above removes second-order redundancy (correlations). Olshausen and Field (1996) asked for representations in which only a few units are active for any image: the image $\mathbf x$ is approximated by a linear combination of dictionary elements, $\mathbf x\approx\boldsymbol\Phi\mathbf a$, with the cost
$$E(\mathbf a) = \tfrac12\|\mathbf x - \boldsymbol\Phi\mathbf a\|^2 + \lambda\sum_i|a_i| ,$$
the Laplace (sparse) prior corresponding to the $L_1$ penalty. Learning $\boldsymbol\Phi$ by gradient descent on natural-image patches produced localized, oriented, bandpass filters resembling V1 simple-cell receptive fields; independent component analysis (Bell and Sejnowski, 1997) gave similar filters.

**Exact solution for orthonormal $\boldsymbol\Phi$.** Then $\|\mathbf x - \boldsymbol\Phi\mathbf a\|^2 = \|\mathbf c - \mathbf a\|^2$ with $\mathbf c = \boldsymbol\Phi^\top\mathbf x$, and the problem decouples: minimize $\tfrac12(c - a)^2 + \lambda|a|$. For $a > 0$ the derivative is $a - c + \lambda$, so $a = c - \lambda$ if $c > \lambda$; symmetrically for $a<0$; otherwise $a = 0$:
$$a = \operatorname{sign}(c)\,\max(|c| - \lambda,\ 0)\qquad\text{(soft threshold)}.$$
Small coefficients are set to zero and large ones are shrunk by $\lambda$. For $\mathbf c = (1.6, -0.5, 0.9, -2.2)$ and $\lambda = 0.6$: $\mathbf a = (1.0, 0, 0.3, -1.6)$ and $E = 2.405$. With an overcomplete dictionary the solution requires iteration (for example repeated gradient and soft-threshold steps), and its neural implementation by competition between units is itself a research topic.

### What is open

The theories are flexible, which is both their strength and weakness. Efficient coding explains adaptation to stimulus statistics and center-surround filters, but it predicts different codes under different cost functions (information, energy, task-relevant information), and it says little about which features matter for behavior. Sparse coding reproduces V1-like filters, but equally good fits arise from other objectives, and measuring sparseness in real populations depends on how responses are counted. Whether cortex is "efficient" in a quantitative sense, rather than merely compatible, remains debated.

@example
title: Equalizing a response function
problem: Stimulus intensity $s\ge0$ is exponentially distributed with rate $\lambda = 0.5$ (mean 2). A neuron with maximum rate $r_{\max} = 50$ Hz has the information-maximizing response $r = r_{\max}F(s)$. What are its responses at $s = 1$ and $s = 4$, and at which intensity is the response half of $r_{\max}$?
step: The cumulative distribution is $F(s) = 1 - e^{-\lambda s} = 1 - e^{-0.5s}$.
step: At $s = 1$: $r = 50(1 - e^{-0.5}) = 50\times0.3935 = 19.67$ Hz. At $s = 4$: $r = 50(1 - e^{-2}) = 50\times0.8647 = 43.23$ Hz.
step: Half of $r_{\max}$ means $F(s) = 0.5$, so $e^{-0.5s} = 0.5$ and $s = 2\ln 2 = 1.386$, the median of the stimulus distribution.
answer: $19.67$ Hz at $s=1$, $43.23$ Hz at $s=4$; half-maximal response at the median, $s = 1.386$.

@mistakes
- Equating efficient coding with maximizing firing rate: the goal is information per unit cost, and the optimal code usually has most cells responding weakly most of the time.
- Applying histogram equalization when the noise is large and unequal across the stimulus range: the optimum then depends on the noise model, as the Fisher-information derivation shows.
- Thinking sparse coding is the same as dimensionality reduction: the sparse code has more (or equally many) units than input dimensions, and few are active at once.
- Treating a good fit of learned filters to V1 as proof that V1 is a sparse coder: other objectives give similar filters.

@practice self
prompt: Derive the optimal tuning curve $f(s)$ of a single neuron with additive Gaussian response noise of constant variance, a monotone response from 0 to $r_{\max}$, and the information approximation $I \approx H(s) + \tfrac12\int p\log J\,ds$ with $J = f'^2/\sigma^2$. State the assumptions, compare with histogram equalization of a noiseless neuron, and give one situation in which the result would change.
hint: Write the constraint as $\int f'\,ds = r_{\max}$ and use a Lagrange multiplier on $\int p\log f'\,ds$.
solution: Maximize $\int p(s)\log f'(s)\,ds - \mu\int f'(s)\,ds$ over $f' \ge 0$. The pointwise condition is $p/f' - \mu = 0$, so $f' = p/\mu$, and the constraint $\int f' = r_{\max}$ gives $\mu = 1/r_{\max}$, so $f' = r_{\max}p(s)$ and $f(s) = r_{\max}F(s)$: the same function as histogram equalization of a noiseless neuron, although the reason differs (equalizing the output distribution versus maximizing the average log discriminability). The assumptions are a low-noise (high Fisher information) regime where the Gaussian approximation of the information holds, noise independent of the stimulus and of the response level, and a hard bound $r_{\max}$ as the only cost. If the noise variance grows with the rate, as for Poisson spiking ($\sigma^2\propto f$), then $J = f'^2/f$ and the optimal slope changes: with $g = \sqrt f$, $J = 4g'^2$ and the same argument gives a square-root-transformed optimum $\sqrt{f}\propto F(s)$, so $f\propto F(s)^2$. A metabolic cost on spikes would also change it.
rubric: Sets up the Lagrangian correctly and obtains $f' = r_{\max}p(s)$.
rubric: Identifies the result with $r_{\max}F(s)$ and states the assumptions behind the Fisher-information approximation.
rubric: Gives a valid situation (rate-dependent noise or a metabolic cost) in which the optimum changes.
