@article
Two neurons receive the same slowly increasing current. One begins to fire at 2 Hz and speeds up smoothly; the other is silent until the current reaches a critical value and then fires abruptly at 40 Hz. Hodgkin (1948) classified such behavior in axons, and the dynamical-systems view explains the difference by the **bifurcation** through which the resting state is lost. Last week's analysis (fixed points, Jacobian, trace and determinant) is all the machinery needed.

### Two ways to start firing

A neuron has **type I** excitability if its firing rate rises continuously from zero as the current increases through threshold, and **type II** if the rate jumps from zero to a finite value. In the language of Week 1:

- **Type II**: a Hopf bifurcation of the resting state. At onset the oscillation has the frequency $\omega/2\pi$ of the ringing of the stable spiral just below, which is finite. The FitzHugh–Nagumo model of Week 1 is of this kind (period about 22.8 at onset in its time units).
- **Type I**: a saddle-node on an invariant circle (SNIC). A stable node and a saddle lie on a closed loop in phase space; they merge and vanish, and what remains is a loop along which the state moves slowly through the "ghost" of the vanished fixed points. The period diverges as the bifurcation is approached.

### The quadratic normal form

Near a SNIC, every model reduces (after a change of variables) to the **quadratic integrate-and-fire** (QIF) normal form

$$\tau\,\dot v = v^2 + I, \qquad v \to +\infty \text{ is a spike, after which } v \text{ is reset to } -\infty.$$

Here $v$ is dimensionless and $\tau$ is a time constant. For $I < 0$ there are fixed points $v^* = \pm\sqrt{-I}$ (stable for the minus sign: $f'(v) = 2v/\tau$), which collide at $I = 0$ and vanish. For $I > 0$ the variable passes from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$ in a finite time:

$$T = \tau\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dv}{v^2 + I} = \frac{\tau}{\sqrt I}\Big[\arctan\frac{v}{\sqrt I}\Big]_{-\infty}^{\infty} = \frac{\pi\tau}{\sqrt I}, \qquad f = \frac{1}{T} = \frac{\sqrt I}{\pi\tau}.$$

The rate grows like $\sqrt{I - I_c}$ from zero: the signature of type I. With $\tau = 1$: $I = 0.25, 1, 4, 9$ give $T = 6.283, 3.142, 1.571, 1.047$.

### Phase response curves

Define the **phase** $\phi \in [0, 2\pi)$ of an oscillator so that $\phi = 0$ is the spike and $\dot\phi = \omega = 2\pi/T$ is constant. A small kick of size $\delta$ in the voltage variable at phase $\phi$ shifts the later phase by $\Delta\phi = Z(\phi)\,\delta$, where $Z$ is the **phase response curve** (PRC); positive $Z$ is an advance. For the QIF, time remaining until the spike from $v$ is $T_{\rm rem}(v) = \frac{\tau}{\sqrt I}\big(\frac{\pi}{2} - \arctan\frac{v}{\sqrt I}\big)$, so a kick $\delta$ advances the spike by
$$\Delta t = T_{\rm rem}(v) - T_{\rm rem}(v + \delta) \approx \frac{\tau\,\delta}{v^2 + I}.$$
Writing $v = -\sqrt I\cot(\phi/2)$ (so that $\phi = \pi$ is $v = 0$) gives $v^2 + I = I/\sin^2(\phi/2)$ and
$$\Delta t \approx \frac{\tau\,\delta\,(1 - \cos\phi)}{2I}, \qquad Z(\phi) = \frac{1 - \cos\phi}{\sqrt I}\quad(\text{radians per unit } v),$$
since $\Delta\phi = \omega\,\Delta t$ and $\omega = 2\pi/T = 2\sqrt I/\tau$. The type I PRC is **non-negative**: zero at the spike and largest at mid-cycle. Check at $I = 0.25$, $\tau=1$, $\phi = \pi$ ($v=0$) and $\delta = 0.05$: the formula gives $0.05\cdot 2/0.5 = 0.2$; the exact difference is $0.1993$.

For a **Hopf** oscillation, take the normal form $\dot z = (\mu + i\omega_0)z - |z|^2 z$ with $z = x + iy$. The cycle has radius $r = \sqrt\mu$ and phase $\phi = \arg z$, with $\dot\phi = \omega_0$ at every radius, so the isochrons are radial lines. A kick $\delta$ in $x$ at phase $\phi$ moves the point to $(r\cos\phi + \delta,\ r\sin\phi)$, whose angle is $\phi - \delta\sin\phi/r$ to first order:
$$Z(\phi) = -\frac{\sin\phi}{r}.$$
This PRC has a **negative lobe**: an excitatory kick in the first half of the cycle delays the next spike, one in the second half advances it. (Real type II neurons have PRCs of this general form, usually a skewed version of it.)

### Why it matters

In **weak coupling theory** each oscillator is described only by its phase; the effect of a synaptic input on a neuron's phase is the PRC multiplied by the input, averaged over a cycle to give an interaction function of the phase difference. The shape of $Z$ therefore constrains synchrony: a non-negative PRC with a flat start and a peak late in the cycle behaves differently under excitatory coupling from a PRC with an early negative lobe, and analyses in the 1990s showed that the answer depends on the PRC together with the time course of the synapse. It is not a one-line rule. Two caveats: the first-order PRC is valid only for kicks small enough that $\delta/(v^2+I)$ stays small, and the normal forms say nothing about the spike shape or about adaptation, which can switch a neuron from one class to the other. Measuring PRCs experimentally means delivering small pulses at random phases and regressing the spike-time shifts on phase.

@example
title: Time remaining in the quadratic model
problem: A QIF neuron $\tau\dot v = v^2 + I$ with $\tau = 1$ and $I = 4$ is at $v_0 = 2$. How long until it spikes, and what fraction of the full period is that?
step: The period is $T = \pi/\sqrt I = \pi/2 \approx 1.5708$.
step: The time remaining from $v$ is $T_{\rm rem} = \frac{1}{\sqrt I}\big(\frac{\pi}{2} - \arctan\frac{v}{\sqrt I}\big)$. With $v/\sqrt I = 2/2 = 1$ and $\arctan 1 = \pi/4$, this is $\frac12\big(\frac{\pi}{2} - \frac{\pi}{4}\big) = \frac{\pi}{8} \approx 0.3927$.
step: The fraction of the period is $0.3927/1.5708 = 1/4$: the neuron is three quarters of the way around its cycle.
answer: About $0.393$ time units, one quarter of the period.

@example
title: A kick to a Hopf oscillator
problem: A Hopf normal-form oscillator has $\mu = 0.09$ and $\omega_0 = 1$. A depolarizing kick of $\delta = 0.02$ (in $x$) arrives at phase $\phi = 3\pi/2$. By how much does the phase change, and in which direction?
step: The cycle radius is $r = \sqrt{0.09} = 0.3$.
step: Apply $\Delta\phi = -\delta\sin\phi/r$. At $\phi = 3\pi/2$, $\sin\phi = -1$, so $\Delta\phi = 0.02/0.3 \approx +0.0667$ rad.
step: The sign is positive, so the oscillator is advanced. In time, $\Delta t = \Delta\phi/\omega_0 \approx 0.0667$ time units.
answer: An advance of $0.0667$ rad, about $0.0667$ time units.

@mistakes
- Saying type I neurons "fire at low rates" in general: the statement is about the continuity of the f–I curve at onset. A type I model can fire fast at high current; the point is that arbitrarily low rates are reachable.
- Using the first-order PRC for a large kick: $\delta/(v^2 + I)$ must be small. In the QIF the formula is off (too high or too low, depending on the phase) when the kick moves $v$ over a range where $v^2 + I$ changes appreciably.
- Mixing phase and time: a phase shift in radians is $\omega\,\Delta t$, with $\omega = 2\pi/T$, not $\Delta t$ itself.
- Assuming the class is a fixed property of the neuron: adding slow adaptation or a different holding potential can change the bifurcation, so the class is a property of the model at given parameters.

answer: 0.0938

solution: $1 - \cos\phi = 1.5$, so $\Delta t = 0.08 \times 1.5/1.28 = 0.09375 \approx 0.0938$. (The exact shift is $0.0978$; the first-order value is accurate to about 4%.)

@practice num
prompt: A Hopf normal-form oscillator has $\mu = 0.16$ and PRC $Z(\phi) = -\sin\phi/r$ for kicks in $x$. A kick $\delta = 0.02$ arrives at $\phi = 7\pi/6$. What is the phase shift $\Delta\phi$ in radians, to three decimal places? Positive means advance.
answer: 0.025

@practice self
prompt: Derive the period of the quadratic integrate-and-fire neuron $\tau\,\dot v = v^2 + I$ (for $I > 0$, with firing at $v = +\infty$ and reset to $-\infty$), then derive its first-order phase response curve to a voltage kick. Show that it is non-negative, and explain in two sentences why the Hopf normal form's PRC differs qualitatively.
hint: Integrate $dt = \tau\,dv/(v^2 + I)$ over the real line; for the PRC differentiate the time remaining with respect to $v$.
solution: Separating variables, $T = \tau\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dv/(v^2+I) = \pi\tau/\sqrt I$. The time remaining from $v$ is $T_{\rm rem}(v) = \tfrac{\tau}{\sqrt I}\big(\tfrac{\pi}{2} - \arctan(v/\sqrt I)\big)$, so a kick $\delta$ advances the spike by $T_{\rm rem}(v) - T_{\rm rem}(v+\delta) \approx -T_{\rm rem}'(v)\,\delta = \tau\delta/(v^2 + I)$. This is positive for all $v$, so a depolarizing kick can only advance the spike. With $v = -\sqrt I\cot(\phi/2)$, $v^2 + I = I/\sin^2(\phi/2)$, giving $\Delta t = \tau\delta(1 - \cos\phi)/(2I)$ and, with $\Delta\phi = 2\pi\Delta t/T$, a PRC proportional to $1 - \cos\phi$, zero at the spike and maximal at mid-cycle. The Hopf PRC is $-\sin\phi/r$ because the isochrons of a Hopf oscillation are radial lines, so a kick along $x$ changes the phase by the tangential component of the displacement, which changes sign over the cycle; the SNIC oscillation instead spends its time near the ghost of the vanished fixed points, where the same kick always pushes the state toward the exit and never backward.

@quiz mc
prompt: A Hopf oscillator has PRC $Z(\phi) = -\sin\phi/r$. A brief excitatory (positive $x$) kick arrives at phase $\phi = \pi/3$, in the first half of the cycle after the spike. What is the effect?
choice*: The next spike is delayed, because $\sin(\pi/3) > 0$ makes $Z$ negative.
choice: The next spike is advanced, because excitatory input always advances a neuron.
choice: There is no effect, because Hopf isochrons are radial and the kick is radial.
choice: The cycle's period is permanently changed, because the kick alters the radius of the limit cycle.
hint: Evaluate the sign of $-\sin\phi$ at $\pi/3$.
solution: $\sin(\pi/3) > 0$ gives $Z < 0$, a delay. Excitation advances only where $Z > 0$. The kick's radial component decays back to the cycle (which is attracting) and does not change the long-run period; only the phase shift persists.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which observations are consistent with a neuron near a saddle-node on an invariant circle (type I)?
choice*: Arbitrarily low firing rates can be obtained by tuning the current close to threshold.
choice*: The interspike interval grows without bound as the current approaches threshold from above.
choice*: A brief depolarizing pulse delivered at any phase never delays the next spike (to first order).
