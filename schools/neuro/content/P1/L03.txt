@article
A cortical column contains tens of thousands of spiking neurons, yet its average activity often looks like the output of a small differential equation. Why can we replace $10^4$ spiking cells by two numbers, $r_E(t)$ and $r_I(t)$? **Mean-field theory** answers: when many weakly correlated neurons receive statistically similar input, population averages obey closed equations. This week states the resulting **rate models**, with their assumptions, and analyzes them with last week's tools.

### From neurons to a rate equation

Let $r_i(t)$ be the firing rate (Hz) of population $i$, $\mathbf W$ the matrix of effective couplings (dimensionless, in units of rate input per unit rate output), and $\mathbf h$ the external drive (in Hz-equivalent units). The standard rate model is

$$\tau_i\,\dot r_i = -r_i + F\Big(\sum_j W_{ij} r_j + h_i\Big),$$

with a nonnegative, increasing **gain function** $F$. Wilson and Cowan (1972) derived equations of this form for excitatory and inhibitory populations, with $F$ sigmoidal because of a spread of thresholds in the population. For a derivation from spiking neurons one needs: (i) large populations with asynchronous firing, so that input fluctuations average out; (ii) a time-scale separation, since the rate equation treats the population as relaxing to $F(\cdot)$ with time constant $\tau_i$ (the membrane or synaptic time constant, depending on which dominates), which fails for inputs that change faster than that; and (iii) a gain function $F$ obtained from the single-neuron f–I relation under noise. Rate models are therefore *effective*; they can miss synchrony, fast transients and correlations. We use the **threshold-linear** gain $F(x) = [x]_+ = \max(x, 0)$, which allows exact solutions.

### One population: a pitchfork

For one population with $F = \tanh$, $\tau\dot r = -r + \tanh(w r + h)$. With $h = 0$, $r = 0$ is a fixed point with slope $-1 + w$ (in units of $1/\tau$): stable for $w < 1$. At $w = 1$ a **pitchfork** creates two further fixed points $\pm r^*$ with $r^* = \tanh(w r^*)$, which are stable and give two memory states. Strong recurrent excitation creates **bistability**, the basis of Week 5.

### Two populations: fixed point and stability

For $\mathbf r = (r_E, r_I)^\top$, $\mathbf W = \begin{pmatrix} w_{EE} & -w_{EI} \\ w_{IE} & -w_{II}\end{pmatrix}$ with all $w \ge 0$. When both rates are positive, $F$ is the identity and the fixed point solves $(\mathbf 1 - \mathbf W)\mathbf r^* = \mathbf h$:

$$\mathbf r^* = \frac{1}{D}\begin{pmatrix} 1 + w_{II} & -w_{EI} \\ w_{IE} & 1 - w_{EE}\end{pmatrix}\mathbf h, \qquad D = (1 - w_{EE})(1 + w_{II}) + w_{EI}w_{IE}.$$

The Jacobian is $\mathbf M = \operatorname{diag}(1/\tau_E, 1/\tau_I)(\mathbf W - \mathbf 1)$, so
$$\operatorname{tr}\mathbf M = \frac{w_{EE} - 1}{\tau_E} - \frac{1 + w_{II}}{\tau_I}, \qquad \det\mathbf M = \frac{D}{\tau_E\tau_I}.$$
Stability needs $\operatorname{tr} < 0$ and $D > 0$.

Take $w_{EE} = 2$, $w_{EI} = 2.5$, $w_{IE} = 2$, $w_{II} = 1$, $\tau_E = 20$ ms and $\tau_I = 10$ ms. Then $D = (-1)(2) + 5 = 3$, $\operatorname{tr} = 1/20 - 2/10 = -0.15$ ms$^{-1}$ and $\det = 0.015$: eigenvalues $-0.075 \pm 0.0968\,i$ ms$^{-1}$, a stable spiral. For $\mathbf h = (10, 5)$ the fixed point is $\mathbf r^* = (2.5, 5.0)$ Hz.

**Inhibition stabilization.** Here $w_{EE} = 2 > 1$: the excitatory population alone would be unstable (its self-feedback exceeds 1), yet the full network is stable because feedback inhibition is fast and strong. Such a network is an **inhibition-stabilized network** (ISN). Its signature is the **paradoxical effect** (Tsodyks, Skaggs, Sejnowski and McNaughton, 1997): from the inverse above, $\partial r_I^*/\partial h_I = (1 - w_{EE})/D$, which is negative exactly when $w_{EE} > 1$, given $D > 0$. Driving the inhibitory cells harder lowers their steady-state rate. At $h_I = 6$ the rates become $(1.667, 4.667)$. Inhibitory cells fall because excitation falls more, removing their own drive. Experiments in visual cortex (for example Sanzeni and colleagues, 2020, in mouse visual cortex) have been interpreted as showing this operating regime, though inferring it from data depends on the gain function and on which cells are perturbed, and is debated.

**Oscillation.** The trace changes sign when $(w_{EE}-1)/\tau_E = (1 + w_{II})/\tau_I$. Keeping the weights above, $\tau_I = 2\tau_E = 40$ ms gives $\operatorname{tr} = 0$, $\det = 3/800$, and a Hopf bifurcation at $\omega = \sqrt{3/800} = 0.0612$ rad/ms, that is $f = 9.75$ Hz. Slower inhibition destabilizes the ISN into an oscillation in the E–I loop, a standard model for rhythms.

### In code

```python
def rate_run(W, tau, h, T, dt, r0):
    """Euler integration of tau_i dr_i/dt = -r_i + [sum_j W_ij r_j + h_i]_+."""
    n = len(h)
    r = list(r0)
    for _ in range(int(round(T / dt))):
        x = [sum(W[i][j] * r[j] for j in range(n)) + h[i] for i in range(n)]
        r = [r[i] + dt / tau[i] * (-r[i] + max(x[i], 0.0)) for i in range(n)]
    return r
```

All entries of `x` are computed from the old `r` before any rate is updated (simultaneous update). Research code writes `r += dt/tau * (-r + np.maximum(W @ r + h, 0))` with NumPy arrays.

@example
title: Fixed point and stability of a two-population network
problem: A threshold-linear network has $w_{EE} = 1.2$, $w_{EI} = 1.5$, $w_{IE} = 1.0$, $w_{II} = 0.5$, $\tau_E = 20$ ms, $\tau_I = 5$ ms, drive $\mathbf h = (3, 1)$. Find $\mathbf r^*$ and decide stability.
step: Compute $D = (1 - 1.2)(1 + 0.5) + 1.5 \times 1.0 = -0.3 + 1.5 = 1.2 > 0$.
step: Fixed point: $r_E^* = (1.5 \times 3 - 1.5 \times 1)/1.2 = 3/1.2 = 2.5$ and $r_I^* = (1.0 \times 3 + (1 - 1.2)\times 1)/1.2 = 2.8/1.2 = 2.333$. Both are positive, so the linear formulas apply.
step: Trace: $\operatorname{tr} = 0.2/20 - 1.5/5 = 0.01 - 0.3 = -0.29 < 0$. Determinant: $1.2/100 = 0.012 > 0$. So the fixed point is stable.
step: Since $w_{EE} = 1.2 > 1$ the network is an ISN, and $\partial r_I^*/\partial h_I = (1 - 1.2)/1.2 = -1/6 < 0$: the paradoxical response.
answer: $\mathbf r^* = (2.5, 2.333)$ Hz, stable (an ISN with a paradoxical inhibitory response).

@mistakes
- Using the linear fixed-point formula when a population is silent: if the formula gives a negative rate, the rectification is active and the true fixed point has that population at zero (recompute with the reduced network).
- Calling any $w_{EE} > 1$ network unstable: the excitatory subnetwork alone is unstable, but with fast enough inhibition the full network may be stable. Stability needs $\operatorname{tr} < 0$ and $D > 0$.
- Concluding that a paradoxical response means inhibition is weak or ineffective: it signals the opposite, that inhibition is needed to stabilize recurrent excitation.
- Treating the rate equation as exact for spiking networks: it assumes asynchronous activity and slowly varying input.

model:
```python
def step_response(W, tau, h1, h2, T, dt):
    """Final rates after drive h1, then after drive h2, from rest."""
    n = len(tau)

    def run(h, r):
        for _ in range(int(round(T / dt))):
            x = [sum(W[i][j] * r[j] for j in range(n)) + h[i]
                 for i in range(n)]
            r = [r[i] + dt / tau[i] * (-r[i] + max(x[i], 0.0))
                 for i in range(n)]
        return r

    first = run(h1, [0.0] * n)
    return first, run(h2, first)
```

In the test with `h2 = [6.0, 12.0]` the larger inhibitory drive silences the excitatory population, so the rectification is essential: without it the excitatory rate would go negative (the linear fixed point is $-2.4$ Hz).

@practice self
prompt: For the two-population linear-regime network $\mathbf r^* = (\mathbf 1 - \mathbf W)^{-1}\mathbf h$ with $\mathbf W = \begin{pmatrix} w_{EE} & -w_{EI}\\ w_{IE} & -w_{II}\end{pmatrix}$ (all $w \ge 0$), (i) show that stability requires $D = \det(\mathbf 1 - \mathbf W) > 0$ and $\operatorname{tr}\mathbf M < 0$, (ii) derive $\partial r_I^*/\partial h_I$, and (iii) explain why a network with $w_{EE} > 1$ can be stable only because of inhibition and what an experiment would need to do to test for this regime.
hint: The Jacobian is $\operatorname{diag}(1/\tau)(\mathbf W - \mathbf 1)$; for a $2\times 2$ matrix $\det(\mathbf W - \mathbf 1) = \det(\mathbf 1 - \mathbf W)$.
solution: (i) Linearizing $\tau_i\dot r_i = -r_i + \sum_j W_{ij}r_j + h_i$ gives $\dot{\delta\mathbf r} = \mathbf M\,\delta\mathbf r$ with $\mathbf M = \operatorname{diag}(1/\tau)(\mathbf W - \mathbf 1)$. For a real $2\times 2$ matrix both eigenvalues have negative real part exactly when $\operatorname{tr}\mathbf M < 0$ and $\det\mathbf M > 0$, and $\det\mathbf M = \det(\mathbf W - \mathbf 1)/(\tau_E\tau_I) = D/(\tau_E\tau_I)$ with $D = (1 - w_{EE})(1 + w_{II}) + w_{EI}w_{IE}$. (ii) By Cramer's rule or the explicit inverse, $r_I^* = (w_{IE}h_E + (1 - w_{EE})h_I)/D$, so $\partial r_I^*/\partial h_I = (1 - w_{EE})/D$. (iii) With $D > 0$ required for stability, this derivative is negative iff $w_{EE} > 1$. If $w_{EE} > 1$, the excitatory population with inhibition frozen would have eigenvalue $(w_{EE} - 1)/\tau_E > 0$ and run away; stability then requires the trace condition, which says inhibition must respond faster (relative to $\tau_E$) and strongly. To test the regime one must increase the drive to inhibitory cells and measure that their own rate falls while excitatory rate falls too; the inference holds only if the cells perturbed and recorded are the relevant population and the network is near the linear regime, and a negative result may reflect a nonlinear gain function.
