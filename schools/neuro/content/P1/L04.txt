@article
A cortical neuron receives input from thousands of cells, yet its spikes are close to Poisson: intervals vary with a coefficient of variation near 1. Softky and Koch (1993) pointed out the puzzle: a neuron summing thousands of independent excitatory inputs should average them out and fire regularly. How can a large, strongly driven network fire irregularly? Van Vreeswijk and Sompolinsky (1996) showed that strong, fast inhibition cancelling excitation resolves it, and that the resulting **balanced state** has a simple closed-form solution.

### The scaling that creates balance

Each neuron receives $K \gg 1$ synapses from each of two populations $E$ and $I$ (rates $m_E$, $m_I$), plus external input from $K$ synapses at rate $m_0$. Take synaptic strengths of order $1/\sqrt K$: $J_{ab}/\sqrt K$ from population $b$ to population $a$ (with $J_{aE} > 0$ and $J_{aI} < 0$), and external strength $E_a/\sqrt K$. Rates are in a convenient unit (fraction of maximal rate, as in binary-neuron models). The mean input to population $a$ is

$$u_a = \sqrt K\Big(\sum_b J_{ab}\,m_b + E_a m_0\Big) - \theta,$$

with threshold $\theta = O(1)$. The sum of $K$ independent inputs has variance
$$\sigma_a^2 = \sum_b J_{ab}^2\,m_b + E_a^2 m_0 = O(1),$$
since $K\,(J/\sqrt K)^2 = J^2$. If the bracket were not close to zero, $u_a$ would be of order $\sqrt K \to \infty$: all neurons would be silent or saturated, and no stable state with finite rates could exist. A stable state with $m_a = O(1)$ therefore requires the bracket to vanish to leading order. This is the **balance condition**:

$$\mathbf J\,\mathbf m + \mathbf E\,m_0 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathbf m = -\mathbf J^{-1}\mathbf E\,m_0 .$$

The mean excitatory and inhibitory inputs, each of order $\sqrt K$, cancel down to order 1. Notice what the solution does not contain: the neuron's gain function and the threshold. The rates are **linear in the external rate** $m_0$ and set only by the connectivity.

### Solving the two-population case

Write $\mathbf J = \begin{pmatrix} J_{EE} & -|J_{EI}| \\ J_{IE} & -|J_{II}|\end{pmatrix}$ and $\Delta = \det\mathbf J = |J_{EI}|J_{IE} - J_{EE}|J_{II}|$. Then

$$m_E = \frac{|J_{II}|E_E - |J_{EI}|E_I}{\Delta}\,m_0, \qquad m_I = \frac{J_{IE}E_E - J_{EE}E_I}{\Delta}\,m_0 .$$

For $\Delta > 0$ both rates are positive when
$$\frac{E_E}{E_I} > \frac{|J_{EI}|}{|J_{II}|} > \frac{J_{EE}}{J_{IE}} .$$
(The second inequality is $\Delta > 0$ and is needed for the balanced fixed point to be stable.) Take $J_{EE} = 1$, $J_{EI} = -2$, $J_{IE} = 1$, $J_{II} = -1.8$, $E_E = 1$, $E_I = 0.6$. Then $\Delta = 2 - 1.8 = 0.2$, the inequalities read $1.667 > 1.111 > 1$, and $\mathbf m = (3, 2)\,m_0$: with $m_0 = 0.1$ the balanced rates are $m_E = 0.3$, $m_I = 0.2$.

### Corrections and a check

At finite $K$ the cancellation is imperfect and the true rates deviate by $O(1/\sqrt K)$. For binary neurons with Gaussian input a neuron is active with probability $m_a = \Phi(u_a/\sigma_a)$ ($\Phi$ the standard normal distribution function). Solving these two self-consistency equations numerically with $\theta = 1$, $m_0 = 0.1$ and the parameters above (damped iteration) gives

| $K$ | $m_E$ | $m_I$ |
|---|---|---|
| 100 | 0.191 | 0.133 |
| 1,000 | 0.247 | 0.168 |
| 10,000 | 0.280 | 0.188 |
| 100,000 | 0.293 | 0.196 |
| balance ($K\to\infty$) | 0.300 | 0.200 |

The gap closes roughly as $1/\sqrt K$, as the theory says.

### Why firing is irregular

Net mean input is $O(1)$, comparable to its standard deviation $\sigma = O(1)$ and to the distance to threshold. Hence spikes are driven by *fluctuations* of the input, which are large because the large excitatory and inhibitory currents do not fluctuate in step. A neuron near threshold driven by large fluctuations fires irregularly, with $\mathrm{CV} \approx 1$. Van Vreeswijk and Sompolinsky found that the deterministic network is then chaotic, so the irregularity is generated internally without any noise source. Brunel (2000) mapped the phases of randomly connected spiking E–I networks, including the asynchronous irregular state, with the synchronous and oscillatory states that appear when the balance of delays and strengths changes. The balanced state is also **asynchronous**: pairwise correlations are small, $O(1/\sqrt K)$ or smaller, because shared input is cancelled along with the mean.

### Limits

The theory assumes sparse random connectivity, $\sqrt K$ scaling (real synapses may scale differently), fast inhibition and no strong structure; with clustering or low-rank structure the picture changes. Whether the real cortex sits in the strongly balanced regime, or in a weakly balanced one where rates respond nonlinearly (see last week's inhibition-stabilized networks), is a matter of continuing measurement and debate.

@example
title: Balanced rates for a given connectivity
problem: A two-population network has $J_{EE} = 2$, $J_{EI} = -3$, $J_{IE} = 3$, $J_{II} = -2.5$, external strengths $E_E = 1.2$, $E_I = 0.8$ and external rate $m_0 = 5$ Hz. Find the balanced rates.
step: Compute $\Delta = |J_{EI}|J_{IE} - J_{EE}|J_{II}| = 3 \times 3 - 2 \times 2.5 = 9 - 5 = 4 > 0$.
step: Excitatory rate: $m_E = (|J_{II}|E_E - |J_{EI}|E_I)\,m_0/\Delta = (2.5 \times 1.2 - 3 \times 0.8) \times 5/4 = (3 - 2.4) \times 1.25 = 0.75$ Hz.
step: Inhibitory rate: $m_I = (J_{IE}E_E - J_{EE}E_I)\,m_0/\Delta = (3 \times 1.2 - 2 \times 0.8) \times 1.25 = (3.6 - 1.6) \times 1.25 = 2.5$ Hz.
step: Check the inequalities: $E_E/E_I = 1.5 > 3/2.5 = 1.2 > 2/3 = 0.667$. Both hold, so the rates are positive and consistent. Check balance: $J_{EE}m_E + J_{EI}m_I + E_E m_0 = 1.5 - 7.5 + 6 = 0$.
answer: $m_E = 0.75$ Hz and $m_I = 2.5$ Hz.

@example
title: Mean-to-fluctuation ratio of one input class
problem: A neuron receives $K = 900$ excitatory inputs, each with strength $J/\sqrt K$ ($J = 1$) from neurons firing as independent Poisson processes at rate $m = 0.04$ (in the unit where the window is 1). What are the mean and standard deviation of the excitatory input alone?
step: The mean is $K\,(J/\sqrt K)\,m = \sqrt K\,J\,m = 30 \times 0.04 = 1.2$.
step: The variance of a sum of independent Poisson counts is $K\,(J/\sqrt K)^2\,m = J^2 m = 0.04$, so the standard deviation is $0.2$.
step: The mean is $6\times$ its standard deviation (ratio $\sqrt{Km} = 6$). Unopposed, the neuron would be driven far above threshold; with inhibition cancelling the mean down to $O(1)$ the remaining mean is comparable to the fluctuations.
answer: Mean $1.2$, standard deviation $0.2$; the ratio is $\sqrt{Km} = 6$.

@mistakes
- Thinking balance requires the excitatory and inhibitory *rates* to be equal: it is the weighted *currents* $J_{aE}m_E$ and $J_{aI}m_I$ plus the external input that cancel, and the rates $m_E$, $m_I$ can differ.
- Expecting the rates to depend on the neuron's gain function or threshold: at leading order they do not, and they are linear in $m_0$; those details enter only at order $1/\sqrt K$.
- Forgetting the positivity conditions: the formula can return a negative rate, which means no balanced state exists for that parameter set.
- Equating irregular firing with noise sources: in the balanced state the irregularity is generated by the recurrent network itself, with deterministic neurons.

@practice self
prompt: Derive the balanced-state rates for a two-population network with $\sqrt K$ scaling, starting from the mean and variance of the synaptic input. State the conditions for positive rates, explain why the rates do not depend on the neurons' gain function, and explain why the state fires irregularly.
hint: Show that the mean input is of order $\sqrt K$ unless the bracket vanishes, while the variance is $O(1)$.
solution: Each neuron receives $K$ inputs from each population with strengths $J_{ab}/\sqrt K$. The mean input is $\sqrt K(\sum_b J_{ab}m_b + E_a m_0) - \theta$ and the variance, from $K$ independent inputs, is $K (J^2/K) m = O(1)$. If the bracket does not vanish, the mean is of order $\sqrt K$, so neurons would be pinned silent or saturated and no state with rates of order 1 would be self-consistent; hence $\mathbf J\mathbf m + \mathbf E m_0 = 0$ to leading order, giving $m_E = (|J_{II}|E_E - |J_{EI}|E_I)m_0/\Delta$ and $m_I = (J_{IE}E_E - J_{EE}E_I)m_0/\Delta$ with $\Delta = |J_{EI}|J_{IE} - J_{EE}|J_{II}|$. Positivity with $\Delta > 0$ requires $E_E/E_I > |J_{EI}|/|J_{II}| > J_{EE}/J_{IE}$. The gain function and threshold appear only in the $O(1)$ remainder of the mean input, which becomes an $O(1/\sqrt K)$ correction to the rates, so they drop out at leading order. The firing is irregular because the net mean is $O(1)$, comparable to the standard deviation: spikes are triggered by fluctuations of two large, imperfectly correlated currents, a process closer to a random threshold crossing than to regular integration.
