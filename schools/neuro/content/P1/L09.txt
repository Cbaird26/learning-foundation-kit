@article
The same retinal image fits many scenes, and a noisy spike count fits many stimuli. A system that must act under this ambiguity should represent not a single guess but how sure it is. The **Bayesian brain** hypothesis (reviewed by Knill and Pouget, 2004) says that perception and action approximate Bayesian inference: combine prior knowledge $p(s)$ with a likelihood $p(r\mid s)$ into the **posterior** $p(s\mid r)\propto p(r\mid s)\,p(s)$. As a statement about behavior it is well supported in many tasks; as a statement about neurons it is a family of proposals, which this lesson separates.

### Gaussian inference

If the prior is $s\sim\mathcal N(\mu_p,\sigma_p^2)$ and one measurement is $x = s + \text{noise}$ with variance $\sigma^2$, the posterior is Gaussian. Working with **precisions** $1/\sigma^2$, they add:
$$\frac1{\sigma_{\rm post}^2} = \frac1{\sigma_p^2} + \frac1{\sigma^2},\qquad \mu_{\rm post} = \sigma_{\rm post}^2\Big(\frac{\mu_p}{\sigma_p^2} + \frac{x}{\sigma^2}\Big).$$
The posterior mean is a precision-weighted average, always pulled toward the more reliable source. The same algebra with a flat prior and two independent measurements $x_1, x_2$ (**cue combination**) gives $\mu = (x_1/\sigma_1^2 + x_2/\sigma_2^2)\,\sigma_{12}^2$ with $\sigma_{12}^{-2} = \sigma_1^{-2} + \sigma_2^{-2}$. With $\sigma_1 = 3$, $\sigma_2 = 6$, $x_1 = 2$ and $x_2 = 8$: $\mu = 3.2$ and $\sigma_{12} = 2.683$, smaller than either cue's $\sigma$. Ernst and Banks (2002) showed that human judgments of object size from vision and touch combine in this precision-weighted way, with the weights shifting as visual noise was added. Weiss, Simoncelli and Adelson (2002) explained several motion illusions at low contrast by a prior favoring slow speeds acting on a noisy measurement. These are results about behavior.

### A probabilistic population code

How might neurons carry a likelihood? Ma, Beck, Latham and Pouget (2006) showed that Poisson-like variability gives a simple answer. Let $N$ independent Poisson neurons have counts $r_i$ with means $f_i(s)$ in a fixed window. Then
$$\log p(\mathbf r\mid s) = \sum_ir_i\log f_i(s) - \sum_if_i(s) + \text{const}.$$
Take Gaussian tuning $f_i(s) = r_{\max}\exp(-(s - s_i)^2/2w^2)$ with preferred stimuli $s_i$ spread uniformly, so that $\sum_if_i(s)$ does not depend on $s$ (a good approximation away from the edges). Then $\log f_i = \log r_{\max} - (s - s_i)^2/2w^2$ and, with a flat prior,
$$\log p(s\mid\mathbf r) = -\frac1{2w^2}\sum_ir_i(s - s_i)^2 + \text{const}.$$
Completing the square shows that the posterior is Gaussian with
$$\hat s = \frac{\sum_ir_is_i}{\sum_ir_i},\qquad \sigma_s^2 = \frac{w^2}{R},\quad R = \sum_ir_i .$$
The mean is the center of mass of the activity, and the **total spike count $R$ sets the certainty**: doubling the gain (all rates, say by raising contrast) halves the posterior variance without moving the mean. A numerical check with $w = 10^\circ$, neurons spaced every $2^\circ$ and $r_{\max} = 5$ gave a total $R = 62.7$ spikes for a stimulus at $3^\circ$; the exact posterior computed on a grid has mean $3.0$ and standard deviation $1.263$, equal to $w/\sqrt R$.

**Cue combination by addition.** If two populations with the same tuning encode the same stimulus from different cues, with counts $\mathbf r_1$ and $\mathbf r_2$, the product of the two likelihoods corresponds to the population $\mathbf r_1 + \mathbf r_2$: $\hat s = (R_1\hat s_1 + R_2\hat s_2)/(R_1 + R_2)$ and $\sigma^{-2} = (R_1 + R_2)/w^2 = \sigma_1^{-2} + \sigma_2^{-2}$. Optimal combination is then just summing activity, which a network can do. With $R_1 = 20$, $\hat s_1 = -4$, $R_2 = 60$, $\hat s_2 = 6$ and $w = 10$: $\hat s = 3.5$ and $\sigma = 1.118$ (single-cue values $2.236$ and $1.291$).

### Sampling and other proposals

An alternative holds that neural variability itself represents uncertainty: each moment's activity is a **sample** from the posterior, so a wider posterior produces more variable activity (Hoyer and Hyvärinen, 2003; Fiser, Berkes, Orbán and Lengyel, 2010). Orbán and colleagues (2016) argued that variability in visual cortex, including its dependence on contrast and its relation to spontaneous activity, matches such a code. The two proposals make different predictions: in the population-code account certainty is carried by the gain of the response and noise is a nuisance, while in the sampling account noise is the representation and the code is read over time. Further proposals use the shape of the tuning pattern or deterministic dynamics. Experiments can test specific predictions (for example, how variability and correlations change with stimulus uncertainty), but each prediction depends on additional modeling assumptions, so the issue is open.

### Critiques

Behavior that looks optimal need not require a Bayesian brain: a fitted prior can absorb nearly any bias (Bowers and Davis, 2012, called some uses "just-so stories"), and observers are often suboptimal (reviewed by Rahnev and Denison, 2018). A fair test fixes the generative model independently, measures the prior or likelihood in separate conditions, and compares against alternative decision rules.

@example
title: Prior and measurement
problem: A stimulus has prior $\mathcal N(0, 10^2)$ (units of degrees). A measurement $x = 8$ has noise standard deviation $5$. Find the posterior mean and standard deviation.
step: Precisions: prior $1/100 = 0.01$, measurement $1/25 = 0.04$. Posterior precision $0.05$, so $\sigma_{\rm post}^2 = 20$ and $\sigma_{\rm post} = 4.47$.
step: The posterior mean is $\sigma_{\rm post}^2(0/100 + 8/25) = 20\times0.32 = 6.4$.
step: Equivalent weights: the measurement gets weight $0.04/0.05 = 0.8$, the prior $0.2$, so $0.8\times8 + 0.2\times0 = 6.4$. The estimate is pulled $1.6^\circ$ toward the prior.
answer: Posterior mean $6.4^\circ$, standard deviation $4.47^\circ$.

@mistakes
- Averaging cues with equal weights, or weights proportional to the standard deviations: optimal weights are proportional to the precisions $1/\sigma^2$.
- Treating the posterior as narrower than any cue only in special cases: with independent Gaussian cues the combined variance is always smaller than the smaller single-cue variance.
- Concluding that neurons implement Bayes' rule because behavior is near-optimal: the behavior constrains the computation, not the circuit.
- Confusing the likelihood with the posterior: the population code in the lesson carries the likelihood; the posterior also needs the prior, which can be added if it is represented too.

@practice self
prompt: Derive the posterior over $s$ carried by independent Poisson neurons with tuning curves $f_i(s) = r_{\max}\exp(-(s-s_i)^2/2w^2)$ and uniformly spread preferred stimuli, and show how two such populations driven by two independent cues are combined optimally by adding their spike counts. State the assumptions, and explain how a sampling-based account would differ in what carries uncertainty.
hint: Write $\log p(\mathbf r\mid s)$ using the Poisson probability, drop terms that do not depend on $s$, and complete the square.
solution: For independent Poisson counts $p(\mathbf r\mid s) = \prod_ie^{-f_i}f_i^{r_i}/r_i!$, so $\log p = \sum_ir_i\log f_i(s) - \sum_if_i(s) - \sum\log r_i!$. With uniformly dense $s_i$, $\sum_if_i(s)$ is constant in $s$; $\log f_i = \log r_{\max} - (s-s_i)^2/2w^2$, so with a flat prior $\log p(s\mid\mathbf r) = -\frac1{2w^2}\sum_ir_i(s-s_i)^2 + \text{const}$. Completing the square gives a Gaussian with mean $\hat s = \sum r_is_i/R$ and variance $w^2/R$ with $R = \sum r_i$. For two independent cues the posterior is proportional to the product of the two likelihoods, whose exponents add: $-\frac1{2w^2}\sum_i(r_{1i} + r_{2i})(s-s_i)^2$, the same form with counts $\mathbf r_1 + \mathbf r_2$, so $\hat s = (R_1\hat s_1 + R_2\hat s_2)/(R_1 + R_2)$ and precision $(R_1+R_2)/w^2 = \sigma_1^{-2} + \sigma_2^{-2}$, which is optimal combination. The assumptions are independent Poisson variability, equal-width Gaussian tuning, uniform coverage (constant $\sum f_i$), identical tuning for the two populations, and a flat prior. In a sampling account the activity at each instant is a sample from the posterior, so uncertainty is carried by the spread of the activity over time and across units, not by the total count, and the code is read by averaging over time rather than by a single-trial population readout.
