@article
Inject a steady current into a neuron and one of three things happens: the voltage returns to rest, it fires repetitively, or, for very strong current, it fires a few spikes and then sits at a depolarized value. The membrane has not changed; the **vector field** has. Dynamical-systems theory turns "what does this neuron do for this input?" into a question about fixed points and their stability, and the answer changes only at special parameter values called **bifurcations**. (You know the integrate-and-fire neuron and the Hodgkin–Huxley model from earlier courses; here we use a reduced model that keeps the geometry and nothing else.)

### The FitzHugh–Nagumo model

The FitzHugh–Nagumo (FHN) model has a fast voltage-like variable $v$ and a slow recovery variable $w$ (dimensionless):

$$\dot v = v - \frac{v^3}{3} - w + I, \qquad \dot w = \varepsilon\,(v + a - b\,w).$$

Take $a = 0.7$, $b = 0.8$, $\varepsilon = 0.08$ (a common choice, time in arbitrary units); $I$ stands for injected current. The $v$-nullcline is the cubic $w = v - v^3/3 + I$ and the $w$-nullcline is the line $w = (v + a)/b$. A **fixed point** $(v^*, w^*)$ sits where they cross. Setting $\dot w = 0$ gives $w^* = (v^*+a)/b$; substituting into $\dot v = 0$ gives one cubic in $v^*$:

$$v^* - \frac{v^{*3}}{3} - \frac{v^* + a}{b} + I = 0 .$$

Its derivative with respect to $v^*$ is $1 - v^{*2} - 1/b$, which is negative for every $v^*$ when $b < 1$, so for $b = 0.8$ the cubic is monotonic and there is exactly one fixed point for every $I$. For $I = 0$ it is $v^* = -1.1994$, $w^* = -0.6243$.

### Linearization and the trace–determinant plane

Near a fixed point, $\delta\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{J}\,\delta\mathbf{x}$ with the Jacobian

$$\mathbf{J} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - v^{*2} & -1 \\ \varepsilon & -\varepsilon b \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \operatorname{tr}\mathbf{J} = 1 - v^{*2} - \varepsilon b, \quad \det\mathbf{J} = \varepsilon\,\big(1 - b(1 - v^{*2})\big).$$

The eigenvalues are $\lambda_{\pm} = \tfrac12\big(\operatorname{tr} \pm \sqrt{\operatorname{tr}^2 - 4\det}\big)$. The classification follows from signs alone. If $\det < 0$ the eigenvalues are real with opposite signs: a **saddle**. If $\det > 0$ and $\operatorname{tr} < 0$ the point is stable, a **node** when $\operatorname{tr}^2 \ge 4\det$ and a **spiral** (damped oscillation) otherwise; $\operatorname{tr} > 0$ gives the unstable versions. At $I=0$ the trace is $-0.5026$ and the determinant is $0.1081$, so $\lambda = -0.2513 \pm 0.2119\,i$: a stable spiral. Perturbations ring while they decay, which is the subthreshold resonance of many neurons.

### Where stability is lost

Stability is lost when an eigenvalue's real part crosses zero as $I$ changes. There are two generic ways in a planar system.

1. **Saddle-node**: one real eigenvalue crosses zero, so $\det \mathbf{J} = 0$ with $\operatorname{tr} \ne 0$. Two fixed points collide and annihilate.
2. **Hopf**: a complex pair crosses the imaginary axis at $\pm i\omega$, so $\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{J} = 0$ with $\det \mathbf{J} = \omega^2 > 0$.

For FHN, $\det\mathbf{J} > 0$ whenever $b(1 - v^{*2}) < 1$, which holds for $b = 0.8$ at every fixed point, so no saddle-node occurs; stability is lost by Hopf. Setting $\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{J} = 0$ gives $v^{*2} = 1 - \varepsilon b = 0.936$, so $v^* = \pm 0.96747$. Solving the fixed-point cubic for $I$ at each root:

| $v^*$ | $w^*$ | critical $I$ | $\omega = \sqrt{\det\mathbf{J}}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $-0.96747$ | $-0.33434$ | $0.3313$ | $0.2755$ |
| $+0.96747$ | $+2.08434$ | $1.4187$ | $0.2755$ |

For $0.3313 < I < 1.4187$ the fixed point is unstable (at $I = 0.5$ it has $\lambda = 0.1441 \pm 0.1915\,i$) and the neuron fires repetitively with period near $2\pi/\omega = 22.8$ at onset; beyond $1.4187$ the fixed point is stable again and the cell is silent at a depolarized value (depolarization block).

The linear analysis tells you where stability is lost, not what is born. Whether the oscillation grows from zero amplitude (**supercritical** Hopf) or appears at finite amplitude (**subcritical**) depends on the sign of the first Lyapunov coefficient, a third-order calculation. For small $\varepsilon$ the amplitude grows over a very narrow range of $I$ (a canard-like explosion); in the simulation below, $I = 0.30$ is silent and $I = 0.34$ already gives full-size spikes.

### In code

```python
def fhn_run(I, T, dt, a=0.7, b=0.8, eps=0.08):
    """Euler-integrate FHN from (v, w) = (-1.2, -0.6); return final (v, w)."""
    v, w = -1.2, -0.6
    for _ in range(int(round(T / dt))):
        dv = v - v ** 3 / 3 - w + I
        dw = eps * (v + a - b * w)
        v, w = v + dt * dv, w + dt * dw
    return v, w
```

Both derivatives use the old $(v, w)$; updating `v` first and then using the new `v` in `dw` is a different (and less accurate) scheme. With $I = 0$ and $T = 400$ the state converges to the fixed point; with $I = 0.5$ it never does. Euler is acceptable here because the fastest rate is about 1 per time unit and $\Delta t = 0.05$; research code uses adaptive Runge–Kutta integrators, and bifurcation software (continuation methods) follows fixed points and cycles as $I$ varies, finding bifurcations without simulation.

@example
title: Classifying a linear fixed point
problem: A fixed point has Jacobian $\mathbf{J} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ -3 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$. Classify it and give the oscillation frequency and decay time of small perturbations.
step: Compute the trace and determinant: $\operatorname{tr} = -1 + (-1) = -2$ and $\det = (-1)(-1) - (2)(-3) = 1 + 6 = 7$.
step: The determinant is positive and the trace negative, so the point is stable. The discriminant is $\operatorname{tr}^2 - 4\det = 4 - 28 = -24 < 0$, so the eigenvalues are complex: a stable spiral.
step: The eigenvalues are $\lambda = \tfrac12(-2 \pm i\sqrt{24}) = -1 \pm i\sqrt{6}$.
step: Read off the dynamics: perturbations oscillate at angular frequency $\sqrt{6} \approx 2.449$ and decay as $e^{-t}$, with decay time $1/|\operatorname{Re}\lambda| = 1$.
answer: A stable spiral, $\lambda = -1 \pm i\sqrt{6}$: ringing at angular frequency about $2.45$ with amplitude decaying by $e$ per unit time.

@example
title: A pitchfork in one dimension
problem: For $\dot x = \mu x - x^3$, find the fixed points for $\mu = -1$ and for $\mu = 4$ and decide their stability.
step: Fixed points satisfy $x(\mu - x^2) = 0$, so $x = 0$ always, and $x = \pm\sqrt{\mu}$ exists only for $\mu > 0$.
step: In one dimension the eigenvalue is the slope $f'(x) = \mu - 3x^2$. At $x = 0$ the slope is $\mu$.
step: For $\mu = -1$ only $x = 0$ exists and its slope is $-1 < 0$: stable.
step: For $\mu = 4$ the origin has slope $+4$ (unstable) and $x = \pm 2$ have slope $4 - 12 = -8$ (stable). The bifurcation at $\mu = 0$ exchanged the stable origin for two stable branches.
answer: $\mu = -1$: one stable fixed point at $0$. $\mu = 4$: $0$ unstable, $\pm 2$ stable (eigenvalue $-8$).

@mistakes
- Using the trace alone: $\operatorname{tr} < 0$ does not imply stability if $\det < 0$ (a saddle). Stability of a planar fixed point needs both $\operatorname{tr} < 0$ and $\det > 0$.
- Reading a Hopf point off the nullcline picture without checking $\det > 0$: $\operatorname{tr} = 0$ with $\det < 0$ is a saddle, not an oscillation onset.
- Treating a bifurcation value as proof that a limit cycle appears and is stable: linearization says only that the fixed point changes stability; the cycle's existence and stability need further analysis or simulation.
- Evaluating the Jacobian at the wrong point: it must be computed at the fixed point for the given $I$, and $v^*$ changes with $I$.

model:
```python
def classify(tr, det):
    """Classify a planar fixed point from trace and determinant."""
    if det < 0:
        return 'saddle'
    if abs(tr) < 1e-9:
        return 'center'
    kind = 'stable' if tr < 0 else 'unstable'
    shape = 'node' if tr * tr - 4 * det >= 0 else 'spiral'
    return kind + ' ' + shape
```
hint: Test the determinant first; the discriminant $\operatorname{tr}^2 - 4\det$ separates nodes from spirals only after you know the point is not a saddle.
solution: The order of tests matters: a negative determinant makes a saddle even when the trace is negative, and the case `tr == 0` must be handled before the stable/unstable split. The repeated-eigenvalue case ($\operatorname{tr}^2 = 4\det$) is assigned to nodes by the stated rule.

prompt: For the FitzHugh–Nagumo model $\dot v = v - v^3/3 - w + I$, $\dot w = \varepsilon(v + a - bw)$ with $0 < b < 1$ and $0 < \varepsilon b < 1$, derive (i) the Jacobian at a fixed point, (ii) the value of $v^*$ at which the trace vanishes, (iii) the critical current in terms of $\varepsilon, a, b$, and (iv) the angular frequency of the oscillation at onset. Explain why no saddle-node bifurcation occurs.
hint: The trace is $1 - v^{*2} - \varepsilon b$. The fixed point satisfies $w^* = (v^* + a)/b$.
solution: (i) $\mathbf{J} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - v^{*2} & -1 \\ \varepsilon & -\varepsilon b\end{pmatrix}$. (ii) $\operatorname{tr} = 1 - v^{*2} - \varepsilon b = 0$ gives $v^{*} = \pm\sqrt{1 - \varepsilon b}$, real because $\varepsilon b < 1$. (iii) Substituting $w^* = (v^*+a)/b$ into $\dot v = 0$ gives $I = -v^* + v^{*3}/3 + (v^*+a)/b$, evaluated at $v^* = \pm\sqrt{1-\varepsilon b}$; there are two critical currents, one on each side of the cubic's middle branch. (iv) At $\operatorname{tr} = 0$ the eigenvalues are $\pm i\sqrt{\det}$ with $\det = \varepsilon(1 - b(1 - v^{*2})) = \varepsilon(1 - \varepsilon b^2)$, so $\omega = \sqrt{\varepsilon(1 - \varepsilon b^2)}$; this is positive because $\varepsilon b^2 < b < 1$. No saddle-node: a saddle-node needs $\det = 0$, but $b(1 - v^{*2}) \le b < 1$ makes $\det \ge \varepsilon(1 - b) > 0$ at every fixed point. The linearization fixes where stability is lost and the frequency of the emerging oscillation, but not whether the cycle is stable or born at small amplitude.

model:
```python
def count_spikes(I, T, dt):
    """Count armed upward crossings of v = 1 in an Euler FHN run."""
    a, b, eps = 0.7, 0.8, 0.08
    v, w = -1.2, -0.6
    armed, n = True, 0
    for _ in range(int(round(T / dt))):
        dv = v - v ** 3 / 3 - w + I
        dw = eps * (v + a - b * w)
        v, w = v + dt * dv, w + dt * dw
        if armed and v > 1.0:
            n += 1
            armed = False
        elif v < 0.0:
            armed = True
    return n
```
hint: Keep a boolean `armed`; count and disarm when `v > 1.0` while armed, and re-arm when `v < 0.0`.
solution: Without hysteresis a voltage hovering near 1.0 would be counted repeatedly. The strong-current case gives one spike: the initial transient crosses 1.0 once and the state then settles at a stable depolarized fixed point above the upper Hopf value, a depolarization block.
