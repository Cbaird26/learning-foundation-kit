overview: Choose one theoretical result from the course (the balanced state, a ring attractor, or an efficient-coding prediction), derive it carefully, check it against a simulation you write yourself, extend it in one direction, and write a workshop-style paper of 4 to 6 pages. The paper must state its assumptions and scaling limits, and must end with how an experiment could test or falsify the result.
goal: Derive a result from stated assumptions with every step justified, and state exactly what it predicts.
goal: Verify the derivation numerically with code you wrote without libraries, and explain any discrepancy.
goal: Extend the result in one well-defined direction and report what changes.
goal: Write the paper so a referee could check each claim, including how an experiment would test it.
step: Pick one option from the menu in the first material block (balanced state, ring attractor, or efficient code) and state the claim you will derive in one sentence, with its assumptions listed.

Carry out the derivation or simulation for the extension and compare with the base case.
step: Write the paper with the sections in the third material block. Include a table comparing derived and simulated values, a statement of limits (what you did not test), and a prediction section that names a measurement, the quantity that would change, and a result that would count against your result.

material: **Simulation template (Euler integration of a rate network; adapt it to your option).**
```python
def simulate(rhs, x0, T, dt):
    """Euler integration of dx/dt = rhs(x); returns the list of states."""
    x = list(x0)
    traj = [list(x)]
    for _ in range(int(round(T / dt))):
        dx = rhs(x)
        x = [xi + dt * di for xi, di in zip(x, dx)]
        traj.append(list(x))
    return traj
```

## Week-by-week key ideas

1. **Dynamical systems.** Fixed points, the Jacobian, trace and determinant; saddle-node ($\det = 0$) and Hopf ($\operatorname{tr} = 0$, $\det > 0$) bifurcations; FitzHugh–Nagumo as the worked model.
2. **Excitability.** Type I (SNIC, quadratic form, $f = \sqrt I/\pi\tau$, non-negative PRC $\propto 1 - \cos\phi$) versus type II (Hopf, finite onset frequency, PRC $-\sin\phi/r$).
3. **Rate models.** $\tau\dot{\mathbf r} = -\mathbf r + F(\mathbf W\mathbf r + \mathbf h)$; two-population fixed point, trace and determinant; inhibition stabilization and the paradoxical effect ($w_{EE} > 1$).
4. **Balanced networks.** $\sqrt K$ scaling, $\mathbf m = -\mathbf J^{-1}\mathbf E m_0$, positivity inequalities, $1/\sqrt K$ corrections, irregular firing.
5. **Attractors.** Hopfield energy, asynchronous updates, crosstalk $\Phi(-1/\sqrt\alpha)$, capacity $0.138$; continuous attractors need a zero eigenvalue.
6. **Ring attractors.** Cosine-mode eigenvalue $J_1/2$, threshold $J_1 = 2$, bump width $\theta_c - \sin\theta_c\cos\theta_c = 2\pi/J_1$, zero mode, drift $J_s/(J_1\tau)$ and $\varepsilon/(\tau B)$.
7. **Information.** $H$, $I(S;R)$, Gaussian channel $\tfrac12\log_2(1 + \mathrm{SNR})$, Fisher information and Cramér–Rao, plug-in bias.
8. **Efficient and sparse coding.** $r = r_{\max}F(s)$, slope proportional to $p(s)$, water-filling, soft threshold.
9. **Bayesian brain.** Precision-weighted averaging; PPC posterior with mean $\sum r_is_i/R$ and variance $w^2/R$; sampling versus population codes.
10. **Learning rules.** Hebbian growth along the top eigenvector; Oja's fixed points ($\|\mathbf w\|^2 = 1/\alpha$, eigenvalues $-2\lambda_k$ and $\lambda_j - \lambda_k$); BCM and sliding thresholds; subtractive normalization.
11. **Population dynamics.** Variance explained, participation ratio, low rank and rank-plus-input dimension, skew-symmetric rotations; controls for task complexity.
12. **Open problems.** Coupling strength, fine-tuned attractors, uncertainty codes, credit assignment, degeneracy.

## Common errors

- Using the trace without the determinant; forgetting that the Jacobian is evaluated at the fixed point for the given input.
- Wrong normalization of a convolution kernel (the $1/2\pi$ in the ring model changes the threshold).
- Calling the natural logarithm "bits".
- Treating a good fit as proof of mechanism, or low PCA dimension as low circuit dimension.

@exam num
prompt: A two-population threshold-linear rate network has $w_{EE} = 2.4$, $w_{EI} = 2.0$, $w_{IE} = 3.0$, $w_{II} = 0.8$ and $\tau_E = 14$ ms. The inhibitory time constant is $\tau_I = 18$ ms, at which the trace of the Jacobian $\operatorname{tr}\mathbf M = (w_{EE}-1)/\tau_E - (1 + w_{II})/\tau_I$ vanishes. With both populations active, find the oscillation frequency at this Hopf point, $f = \sqrt{\det\mathbf M}/2\pi$ with $\det\mathbf M = D/(\tau_E\tau_I)$ and $D = (1 - w_{EE})(1 + w_{II}) + w_{EI}w_{IE}$, in hertz to one decimal place.
answer: 18.7
tol: 0.1
unit: Hz
check: sqrt(((1 - 2.4) * (1 + 0.8) + 2.0 * 3.0) / (14 * 18)) / (2 * pi) * 1000
hint: Compute $D$, then $\omega$ in rad/ms, then $f = 1000\omega/2\pi$.
solution: $D = (-1.4)(1.8) + 6 = 3.48$; the trace is $1.4/14 - 1.8/18 = 0.1 - 0.1 = 0$. Then $\omega = \sqrt{3.48/252} = 0.1175$ rad/ms and $f = 18.7$ Hz.

@exam num
prompt: A population of independent Poisson neurons with Gaussian tuning and uniformly spread preferred stimuli carries a Gaussian posterior (flat prior) with variance $w^2/R$, where $R$ is the total spike count. By how many bits does the differential entropy of the posterior, $\tfrac12\log_2(2\pi e\sigma^2)$, fall when the total count rises from $R = 25$ to $R = 100$ (for example, from a higher-contrast stimulus)?
answer: 1
unit: bits
check: 0.5 * log2(2 * pi * e * (1 / 25)) - 0.5 * log2(2 * pi * e * (1 / 100))
hint: The posterior variance is $w^2/R$, and $w$ cancels in the difference.
solution: The entropy difference is $\tfrac12\log_2(\sigma_{25}^2/\sigma_{100}^2) = \tfrac12\log_2(100/25) = \tfrac12\log_2 4 = 1$ bit. Each fourfold increase in total spikes buys one bit of certainty in this code.

@exam self
prompt: In a timed answer (about 30 minutes), analyze a threshold-linear ring attractor with kernel $W(\theta) = J_0 + J_1\cos\theta$ acting through $\frac1{2\pi}\int d\theta'$: (i) derive the condition on $J_1$ for the uniform state to lose stability and name the bifurcation type of the resulting pattern; (ii) show why the bump can be placed anywhere and what this implies for the Jacobian's spectrum; (iii) derive how a weak input $\varepsilon\cos(\theta - \psi)$ moves the bump; (iv) say what happens to the zero eigenvalue when the kernel has a small heterogeneity, and name two experimental predictions that distinguish an exact ring attractor from an approximate one.
hint: Use the Fourier-mode eigenvalue $J_1/2$, the derivative of the bump with respect to its center, and projection onto it.
solution: (i) Perturbations of the uniform state $r = h/(1 - J_0)$ decompose into Fourier modes of the rotation-invariant kernel; the mean mode has eigenvalue $J_0 - 1$ and the $\cos\theta,\sin\theta$ modes have $J_1/2 - 1$ (in units of $1/\tau$), since $\frac1{2\pi}\int\cos(\theta-\theta')\cos\theta'd\theta' = \frac12\cos\theta$. The uniform state loses stability when $J_1 > 2$; the unstable mode is a doubly degenerate pair (cosine and sine), a symmetry-breaking pitchfork-type instability of the rotation group that produces a bump whose position is set by the initial perturbation. (ii) The kernel depends only on $\theta - \theta'$, so if $r_0(\theta)$ is a steady state then so is $r_0(\theta - \phi)$ for every $\phi$; differentiating with respect to $\phi$ shows $u = r_0'$ satisfies the linearized equation with eigenvalue zero: a neutral (Goldstone) mode, with all other eigenvalues negative for a stable bump. (iii) Write $r = r_0(\theta - \phi(t)) + \delta$; projecting the linearized dynamics on $u$ (valid because the operator on the active set is self-adjoint and $Ku = 0$) gives $\tau\dot\phi = -\langle u,\delta I\rangle/\langle u,u\rangle$. For $\delta I = \varepsilon\cos(\theta-\psi)$ and $u = -B\sin(\theta - \phi)$ this yields $\dot\phi = \varepsilon\sin(\psi - \phi)/(\tau B)$: the bump is attracted to $\psi$. (iv) A heterogeneity $\delta h(\theta)$ adds a $\phi$-dependent term to the same projection, so the neutral direction acquires a potential: the zero eigenvalue becomes slightly nonzero near stable points and the bump drifts to a few preferred positions, with drift rate proportional to the heterogeneity. Two predictions: in the dark, the decoded heading should diffuse and drift at a rate that grows with imposed heterogeneity (an exact attractor shows only diffusion from noise), and a brief perturbation that displaces the bump should leave it at the new position (neutral) in an exact attractor but see it relax to preferred positions in an approximate one.
rubric: Derives $J_1 > 2$ from the Fourier eigenvalue with the correct normalization and identifies the symmetry-breaking instability.
rubric: Explains the zero mode from rotation invariance and projects onto it to obtain the attraction to the input.
rubric: Describes the effect of heterogeneity on the zero eigenvalue and gives two distinct, testable predictions.
