@article
Recordings now capture hundreds or thousands of neurons, and a striking finding is that their joint activity during a task is often described by a handful of variables. In motor cortex during reaching, for example, population activity traced smooth trajectories whose main feature was a rotation (Churchland and colleagues, 2012; what the rotation implies is debated, as discussed below). What is the claim exactly, how is it measured, and what does it imply about the circuit?

### Measuring dimensionality

Let $\mathbf X$ be the $N\times T$ matrix of activity (neurons by time points or by trial-averaged conditions), each row with its mean subtracted. The covariance $\mathbf C = \mathbf X\mathbf X^\top/T$ has eigenvalues $\lambda_1\ge\dots\ge\lambda_N\ge0$, the variances along the principal components (PCs). Two summaries are common.

1. **Variance explained**: the fraction $\sum_{k\le m}\lambda_k/\sum_k\lambda_k$ captured by the first $m$ PCs, and the number of PCs needed to reach a criterion such as 90%.
2. **Participation ratio**
$$\mathrm{PR} = \frac{(\sum_k\lambda_k)^2}{\sum_k\lambda_k^2},$$
a threshold-free estimate of the effective number of dimensions. It equals $k$ if $k$ eigenvalues are equal and the rest are zero, and $1$ if one dominates.

For eigenvalues $(5, 3, 1, 0.5, 0.5)$: the first two PCs explain $8/10 = 80\%$, three explain 90%, and $\mathrm{PR} = 100/35.5 = 2.82$. For the covariance $\begin{pmatrix}4&2\\2&3\end{pmatrix}$ the eigenvalues are $5.562$ and $1.438$ and $\mathrm{PR} = 49/33 = 1.485$.

### Caution 1: dimension depends on the task and the sampling

Low dimensionality may reflect the experiment rather than the circuit. A task with few conditions and smooth temporal structure forces trial-averaged activity into a few dimensions, whatever the network. Gao and Ganguli (2015) showed that the dimensionality that can be recovered from a recording is bounded by the complexity of the task and by the smoothness of the neural responses, and that sampling more neurons does not reveal more dimensions unless the task is richer; Elsayed and Cunningham (2017) proposed surrogate data that preserve the simple statistics (single-neuron smoothness, correlations across conditions) to check whether "structure" survives. A defensible claim reports dimensionality for a stated task, number of conditions and neurons, and compares with such surrogates.

### Caution 2: PCA finds linear subspaces, not manifolds

A ring attractor (Week 6) has a one-dimensional manifold but its activity bump moves around a circle, so its PCA has two large eigenvalues (the $\cos$ and $\sin$ components, equal by symmetry; the non-sinusoidal bump shape adds smaller ones) while the intrinsic dimension is 1. PCA dimension overestimates intrinsic dimension for curved manifolds; nonlinear methods estimate it directly but need more data and assumptions.

### Low-rank connectivity and low-dimensional dynamics

A cause of low-dimensional activity is low-rank connectivity. Take $\tau\dot{\mathbf x} = -\mathbf x + \mathbf W\phi(\mathbf x) + \mathbf I(t)$ with $\mathbf W = \frac1N\sum_{r=1}^R\mathbf m_r\mathbf n_r^\top$. The recurrent input $\mathbf W\phi(\mathbf x)$ always lies in $\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf m_r\}$. Solving for the part of $\mathbf x$ not generated by input, $\mathbf x(t) = e^{-t/\tau}\mathbf x(0) + \sum_r\kappa_r(t)\mathbf m_r$ + input-driven terms: after transients, activity lives in a space of dimension at most $R$ plus the number of independent inputs. For $R = 1$ and a linear gain the single variable $\kappa = \mathbf n^\top\mathbf x/N$ obeys $\tau\dot\kappa = (-1 + \mathbf n^\top\mathbf m/N)\kappa$: the only nonzero eigenvalue of $\mathbf W$ is $\mathbf n^\top\mathbf m/N$ (a rank-one matrix has all others zero), so the dynamics decay with time constant $\tau/(1 - \mathbf n^\top\mathbf m/N)$ and become unstable if the overlap exceeds 1. Nonlinear gain makes $\kappa$ obey a nonlinear equation with possible fixed points and so a low-dimensional computation (Mastrogiuseppe and Ostojic, 2018, analyzed this class of networks). Low rank gives a mechanistic reading of low dimensionality; it is not the only one.

### Rotations and linear dynamics

If activity in a plane follows $\dot{\mathbf z} = \mathbf M\mathbf z$ with $\mathbf M$ skew-symmetric, $\mathbf M = \begin{pmatrix}0&-\omega\\\omega&0\end{pmatrix}$, the eigenvalues are $\pm i\omega$ and the trajectory is a circle with period $2\pi/\omega$. Churchland and colleagues fit a skew-symmetric $\mathbf M$ to population activity (jPCA, found by least squares among skew-symmetric matrices) and found that motor cortex trajectories rotate with a consistent phase. The fit identifies a pattern; it does not by itself say whether the rotation arises from the cortical circuit, from input, or from the way the muscles are controlled, and shuffled controls are needed to show that the rotations exceed what the smoothness of the signals implies.

### Where the field stands

Low-dimensional structure in neural population activity is a robust descriptive finding in many tasks. What it implies is contested: whether it reflects circuit constraints (a manifold that the network cannot leave), task simplicity, or analysis choices. The most convincing studies perturb the activity (for example with brain–computer interfaces) to test whether the population can leave the observed subspace.

@example
title: Variance explained and participation ratio
problem: A population has covariance eigenvalues $(6, 3, 2, 1)$. Find the fraction of variance in the first two PCs and the participation ratio.
step: The total variance is $6 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 12$; the first two PCs hold $9/12 = 75\%$.
step: The sum of squared eigenvalues is $36 + 9 + 4 + 1 = 50$.
step: $\mathrm{PR} = 12^2/50 = 144/50 = 2.88$.
step: Interpretation: the activity has an effective dimension of about 2.9, between the two PCs that hold three quarters of the variance and the four that carry all of it.
answer: 75% of the variance in the first two PCs; $\mathrm{PR} = 2.88$.

@example
title: Period of a rotation
problem: A skew-symmetric fit in a jPCA-style plane gives $\dot{\mathbf z} = \mathbf M\mathbf z$ with $M_{12} = -0.02$ and $M_{21} = 0.02$ per millisecond. What is the period of rotation?
step: For $\mathbf M = \begin{pmatrix}0&-\omega\\\omega&0\end{pmatrix}$, $\omega = 0.02$ rad/ms.
step: The eigenvalues are $\pm i\omega$, so the period is $2\pi/\omega = 6.2832/0.02 = 314.2$ ms.
answer: About $314$ ms per rotation (counterclockwise, since $M_{21} > 0$).

@mistakes
- Equating "10 PCs explain 90% of the variance" with "the circuit is 10-dimensional": the number depends on the task, the number of conditions, the smoothness of the signals and the threshold chosen.
- Using PCA dimension as the intrinsic dimension of a curved manifold: a ring needs two linear dimensions for a one-dimensional manifold.
- Believing that more neurons always reveal more dimensions: without a richer task the extra neurons mostly add noise or repeat the same few dimensions.
- Ignoring that a rotation in a PCA plane can arise from smooth, oscillating inputs or from analysis choices, not only from recurrent dynamics.

@practice self
prompt: A paper records 200 neurons in one cortical area during a task with two conditions and reports that "activity is low-dimensional: 10 PCs explain 90% of the variance, implying a low-dimensional circuit". Write a referee paragraph that evaluates this inference: identify what the data could and could not show, and name the controls or additional analyses you would request.
hint: Ask what the task, the number of conditions, the smoothness of the responses and the choice of 90% do to the number of PCs.
solution: The measurement (10 PCs for 90% of the variance, which could be quantified further with the participation ratio) is not itself in doubt, but the inference to a low-dimensional circuit is not supported. With only two conditions and smooth, trial-averaged responses, the number of dimensions that activity can occupy is limited by the task, not by the circuit: a richer population could look the same, and a higher-dimensional circuit would still produce low-dimensional averages (Gao and Ganguli, 2015). I would ask the authors to (i) test with many more conditions or a task that varies several variables independently, (ii) compare with surrogate data that preserve single-neuron smoothness and correlations across conditions but randomize the population structure (Elsayed and Cunningham, 2017), (iii) report dimensionality as a function of the number of neurons and trials with cross-validation, since noise and the arbitrary 90% criterion affect the count, (iv) report a threshold-free measure such as the participation ratio, (v) check whether the activity could be explained by task variables directly, and (vi) ideally perturb activity to ask whether the population can leave the subspace. The conclusion should be restricted to "activity during this task occupies about ten dimensions" unless these controls show the structure exceeds what task and smoothness imply.

rubric: Requests surrogate-data or shuffle controls and a richer task or more conditions.
rubric: Notes noise, cross-validation and the arbitrariness of the 90% criterion, or asks for the participation ratio.
