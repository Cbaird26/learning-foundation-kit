@article
Eleven weeks have given you exact results: bifurcations, rate models, the balanced state, ring attractors, efficient and Bayesian codes, learning rules. The frontier is where those results meet data and disagree about which model describes the brain. This lesson sets out five open problems. For each, learn the competing proposals, what each predicts, and what would decide among them. Where a claim is mathematically derived (for example, Oja's rule converges to the principal component) it is not open; what is open is whether the brain realizes it.

### 1. How strongly coupled is cortex?

*Balanced-network view.* Recurrent excitation and inhibition are large and cancel; with $\sqrt K$ scaling, rates respond linearly to input at leading order and firing is irregular (Week 4). *Weak-coupling or nonlinear view.* Individual connections are small compared with the total drive, and responses depend on the neuron's nonlinearity; strong nonlinear amplification can stabilize with fewer connections than the balanced limit requires. *Inhibition-stabilized networks* (Week 3) lie between: strong excitatory feedback with inhibition stabilizing it, for which paradoxical responses to inhibitory-cell stimulation are the signature. Optogenetic experiments in mouse visual cortex have been interpreted as showing inhibition stabilization (Sanzeni and colleagues, 2020), and such conclusions are conditional on the assumed model and on which cells were perturbed. What would decide it: perturbations of identified cell classes at several input strengths, with a model fitted to all of them, not to one.

### 2. Do brains use fine-tuned attractors?

A continuous attractor (Week 6) holds a variable exactly but needs symmetric, tuned connectivity; heterogeneity causes drift. Proposals: (a) the brain *does* tune: homeostatic or learning rules maintain the required symmetry; (b) *approximate* attractors suffice: slow drift over seconds is acceptable and is corrected by sensory landmarks; (c) memory is held by *transient or sequential dynamics* with no exact attractor. The fly heading system shows attractor-like dynamics with a bump on a ring of neurons (Kim and colleagues, 2017), which supports (a) or (b) there; for mammalian head-direction and working-memory circuits the evidence is less direct. A useful number: with heterogeneity $\varepsilon$ the initial drift rate is $\varepsilon/(\tau B)$ (Week 6), so for the Week 6 parameters a two percent heterogeneity moves the bump by a degree in a few tens of milliseconds unless it is corrected.

### 3. How is uncertainty represented?

Population codes carry the likelihood in the gain and shape of activity (Week 9); sampling codes use variability as the representation; others hold only a point estimate plus a separate confidence signal. Behavior shows that humans use uncertainty in many tasks; neural data show contrast-dependent variability that is compatible with more than one account. What would decide it: a task where the proposals make different predictions about trial-to-trial covariance, with the generative model specified independently.

### 4. How does the brain assign credit?

Gradient descent on a deep network trains it by backpropagation, which needs the same weights forward and backward (**weight transport**) and separate forward and backward phases, features that are hard to reconcile with known synapses. Proposals: feedback pathways with random fixed weights that the forward weights learn to align with (feedback alignment, Lillicrap and colleagues, 2016); predictive-coding schemes with local error units that approximate gradients under conditions (reviewed by Whittington and Bogacz, 2019); dendritic compartments carrying errors; neuromodulated three-factor rules driven by reward; and the position that the brain does not follow gradients at all but relies on evolved structure and sparse feedback. No proposal has been shown to be used by cortex.

### 5. What counts as an explanation?

Different circuit parameters can produce the same activity: model **degeneracy** (Prinz, Bucher and Marder, 2004, found similar rhythms from widely different conductances in a small circuit). If many parameter sets fit the data, fitting data does not identify the model. Low-dimensional activity (Week 11) can reflect circuit constraints, task simplicity or analysis. Marr's levels (computation, algorithm, implementation) say what a theory is about, but they do not say how to choose among theories at the same level. Constraints that help: independent measurements of parameters, connectome constraints, perturbations, and out-of-sample predictions.

### How to referee a theory paper

Ask: (1) Are the equations, parameters and assumptions stated, and are scaling limits ($N$, $K\to\infty$, time-scale separation) explicit? (2) Are fixed points and stability checked analytically and numerically? (3) Does the numerical check test the claim or only illustrate it? (4) What are the alternative models, and does any prediction separate them? (5) Is the central claim falsifiable by an experiment someone could run? (6) Are claims stated at the strength the evidence supports?

@example
title: Parameter degeneracy
problem: A model has 7 parameters, each with 10 plausible values. A scan finds that 0.2% of all combinations reproduce the recorded firing pattern within tolerance. How many distinct parameter sets fit, and what follows for inferring parameters from the fit?
step: The number of combinations is $10^7 = 10{,}000{,}000$.
step: A fraction $0.002$ fit: $0.002\times10^7 = 20{,}000$ parameter sets.
step: Conclusion: with 20,000 parameter sets equally consistent with the data, the fit alone does not identify the parameters; further constraints (independent measurements, perturbations, new conditions) are needed to narrow them, and a parameter's fitted value should be reported with the range of values that fit.
answer: $20{,}000$ sets fit; the data do not identify the parameters without further constraints.

@mistakes
- Treating an open problem as settled by one study: each problem here has data compatible with several proposals, and most decisive tests need predictions that differ between them.
- Confusing a mathematical result with a biological claim: a derivation shows what follows from the assumptions; whether cortex satisfies them is empirical.
- Concluding from a model's fit that its mechanism operates in the brain: other models, or other parameters of the same model, may fit as well.
- Dismissing an idea because it has not been demonstrated: absence of evidence for a mechanism is not evidence of absence, especially where the needed experiment is hard.

The authors conclude that cortex represents uncertainty by sampling. Which is the strongest objection that does not depend on which representation is actually used?
choice*: Reproducing these statistics does not show the model is unique: a different model (for example a gain-coded population code) might reproduce them equally well, so the authors need a prediction that separates the accounts.
choice: Sampling codes cannot reproduce Fano factors, so the model must be wrong.

prompt: In a ring attractor a heterogeneity of amplitude $\varepsilon = 0.02$ in the input, with $\tau = 20$ ms and bump amplitude parameter $B = 1.5$, acts at the position where the heterogeneity pushes most strongly. The drift rate is $\dot\phi = \varepsilon/(\tau B)$. How many milliseconds does it take to move the bump by $1^\circ$ at this initial rate? Give the answer to the nearest millisecond.
answer: 26
tol: 1
unit: ms
check: (pi / 180) / (0.02 / (20 * 1.5))
hint: Convert $1^\circ$ to radians, then divide by the drift rate in rad/ms.
solution: $\dot\phi = 0.02/30 = 6.67\times10^{-4}$ rad/ms and $1^\circ = 0.01745$ rad, so the time is $0.01745/6.67\times10^{-4} = 26.2$ ms. Without correction a $2\%$ heterogeneity moves the bump by a degree in about a fortieth of a second, so a working system needs error correction.

prompt: Which feature of standard backpropagation is most often cited as hard to reconcile with known cortical circuitry?
choice*: It needs the backward pathway to use the same weights as the forward pathway (weight transport).

@practice self
prompt: A manuscript claims that "a ring attractor network explains head-direction coding in mammals". It presents a threshold-linear ring model with a cosine kernel, shows by simulation that a bump persists for 60 s in the dark, shows that tuning curves resemble recorded cells, and concludes that mammals use exactly this wiring. Write the main paragraph of a referee report (about 200 words) that states what the paper establishes, what it does not, and which analyses or experiments you request.
hint: Use the referee checklist: assumptions, stability and fine tuning, alternatives, predictions, claim strength.
solution: The manuscript shows that a symmetric cosine-kernel ring network can sustain a bump and produce tuning-like responses; this is a useful existence proof, but the central claim, that mammals use this wiring, goes beyond it. First, the model has an exact continuous attractor, which requires tuned symmetric connectivity; the authors should show robustness by adding realistic heterogeneity in weights and inputs (the drift rate scales with the heterogeneity, so a single 60 s simulation without it says little about whether the mechanism survives biological noise), and by testing the role of the landmark correction they assume. Second, similar tuning curves and persistence are expected from several other models (for example sequences or weakly structured recurrent networks with slow dynamics), so the authors must compare against at least one alternative and identify a prediction that differs: for example, the geometry of population activity on a ring across conditions and sleep, or the response to a perturbation that displaces the bump. Third, parameters and scaling should be stated, and the stability analysis (zero mode and the other eigenvalues) should be verified numerically. I recommend that the conclusion be restated as "a ring attractor is sufficient to explain these data" with the alternatives and the needed discriminating experiments discussed.
rubric: States clearly what is established (sufficiency, existence proof) and what is not (that mammals use this wiring).
rubric: Raises fine tuning and requests tests with heterogeneity or noise.
rubric: Names an alternative model and a discriminating prediction or experiment.
rubric: Asks for stated parameters, stability checks and a restated conclusion.

@quiz self
prompt: Design an experiment that could distinguish a probabilistic population code from a sampling code for uncertainty in a sensory cortical area. State the task, the manipulation of uncertainty, the neural measurement, the prediction of each account, and a result that would be ambiguous.
hint: The two accounts put uncertainty in different places: the gain and shape of the response, or the variability of activity.
solution: Use a visual orientation estimation task in which uncertainty is manipulated independently of mean response, for example by changing stimulus contrast or the width of a noisy orientation distribution, and record a large population on many trials per condition (with the same stimulus repeated, so trial-to-trial variability can be measured). In a gain-coded population code the posterior width is read from the total activity and its shape (Week 9: variance $w^2/R$), so higher uncertainty should show lower response gain, with variability a nuisance whose Fano factor stays roughly constant across uncertainty. In a sampling code, higher uncertainty should show wider trial-to-trial spread of the population state along the dimensions of the posterior, with noise correlations aligned to those dimensions, while the mean gain need not change. The analysis would compare, within a condition, the covariance of the population response with the decoded posterior width, and compare it across conditions, with the generative model fixed by an independent psychophysical measurement of each subject's uncertainty. An ambiguous result is one in which both gain and covariance change together with contrast, which both accounts can accommodate with extra assumptions, or where the changes are small compared with sources of variability unrelated to the stimulus, such as arousal or eye movements, which would have to be measured and regressed out.
rubric: Specifies a task and a manipulation of uncertainty that is dissociated from mean response.
rubric: States different predictions for the two accounts (gain versus variability and correlations).
rubric: Includes a control (independent estimate of the generative model or of non-stimulus variability) and an ambiguous outcome.
