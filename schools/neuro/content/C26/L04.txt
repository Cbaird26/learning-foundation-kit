@article
A radiologist and a trainee look at the same scans. The radiologist says "abnormal" on 80% of the abnormal scans and on 25% of the normal ones. Is she better at detecting abnormality than a trainee who says "abnormal" on 90% of the abnormal and 55% of the normal? Hit rates alone cannot answer: the trainee may simply be more willing to say yes. **Signal detection theory** (Green and Swets, 1966) separates sensitivity from willingness to respond.

### The equal-variance model

On each trial the observer gets a noisy internal evidence value $x$. On noise-only trials $x \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$; on signal trials $x \sim \mathcal{N}(d', 1)$. The observer says "yes" when $x$ exceeds a **criterion** $k$. Then

$$H = P(\text{yes}\mid\text{signal}) = \Phi(d' - k), \qquad F = P(\text{yes}\mid\text{noise}) = \Phi(-k),$$

where $\Phi$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function and $z = \Phi^{-1}$. The **sensitivity** $d' = z(H) - z(F)$ is the separation of the two distributions in standard deviations. It is customary to measure the criterion from the midpoint, $c = k - d'/2$, so that

$$c = -\tfrac12\big[z(H) + z(F)\big], \qquad H = \Phi(d'/2 - c), \quad F = \Phi(-d'/2 - c).$$

A positive $c$ is a conservative observer (fewer yes responses), a negative $c$ a liberal one. The likelihood ratio at the criterion is $\ln\beta = d'c$. In the example, $z(0.8) = 0.8416$ and $z(0.25) = -0.6745$, so the radiologist has $d' = 1.516$ and $c = -0.084$. The trainee has $z(0.9) = 1.2816$ and $z(0.55) = 0.1257$, so $d' = 1.156$ and $c = -0.704$: lower sensitivity and a much more liberal criterion.

### From counts to estimates, and a correction

With $n_s$ signal and $n_n$ noise trials and observed hits $h$ and false alarms $f$, a rate of exactly 0 or 1 gives $z = \mp\infty$. A common remedy, the **log-linear correction**, uses $H = (h + 0.5)/(n_s + 1)$ and $F = (f + 0.5)/(n_n + 1)$. It keeps the estimates finite but biases them slightly toward zero, and it is a convention, not a law.

### SDT as a likelihood model

SDT is a choice model: for a given $(d', c)$ it assigns probability $H$ to "yes" on each signal trial and $F$ on each noise trial. Treating trials as independent, the log-likelihood of the counts (dropping the binomial coefficients, which do not depend on the parameters) is

$$\log L = h\log H + (n_s - h)\log(1-H) + f\log F + (n_n - f)\log(1-F).$$

Its maximum is at the closed-form $d'$ and $c$ computed from the raw rates, because $(H, F)$ and $(d', c)$ are one-to-one. A grid search finds the same answer to within the grid spacing.

```python
from statistics import NormalDist

N = NormalDist()

def dprime_c(H, F):
    zh, zf = N.inv_cdf(H), N.inv_cdf(F)
    return zh - zf, -(zh + zf) / 2

def predicted_hf(d, c):
    return N.cdf(d / 2 - c), N.cdf(-d / 2 - c)

d, c = dprime_c(0.8, 0.25)
print(round(d, 3), round(c, 3))
print([round(x, 2) for x in predicted_hf(d, c)])
```

This prints `1.516 -0.084` and `[0.8, 0.25]`, confirming the round trip.

### Probit and logistic

The "yes" probability for an evidence difference follows a normal CDF (a **probit** link), whereas the softmax uses the logistic function. The two are close: $\Phi(x) \approx 1/(1 + e^{-1.702x})$ to within $0.01$ for every $x$. A softmax $\beta$ on a value difference therefore corresponds to a probit with noise standard deviation $s \approx 1.702/\beta$, and the choice between them is rarely decided by the data.

### What the model assumes

Equal variance is an assumption. Many recognition-memory experiments find ROC curves whose $z$-transformed slope differs from 1, which suggests that signal evidence is more variable than noise evidence; models with an unequal-variance signal distribution and dual-process models are rival accounts of such data, and the debate is not settled. When variances differ, $d'$ is no longer a single number independent of the criterion.

@example
title: Sensitivity and criterion from counts
problem: An observer says "yes" on 33 of 40 signal trials and on 12 of 40 noise trials. Use the raw rates to find $d'$ and $c$.
step: $H = 33/40 = 0.825$ and $F = 12/40 = 0.30$.
step: $z(0.825) = 0.9346$ and $z(0.30) = -0.5244$.
step: $d' = 0.9346 - (-0.5244) = 1.4590$.
step: $c = -\tfrac12(0.9346 - 0.5244) = -0.2051$, a slightly liberal observer.
answer: $d' \approx 1.459$ and $c \approx -0.205$.

@mistakes
- Using the hit rate as a measure of sensitivity: a liberal criterion raises $H$ without any increase in $d'$.
- Feeding $H = 1$ or $F = 0$ into the inverse CDF: it is infinite, so apply a stated correction first.
- Getting the sign of $c$ wrong: with $c = -\tfrac12(z(H) + z(F))$, positive means conservative.
- Reading $d'$ as a property of the stimulus alone: it depends on the observer's internal noise as well, and on the equal-variance assumption.

model:
```python
from statistics import NormalDist


def sdt_from_counts(hits, n_signal, fas, n_noise):
    """d' and c from counts with the log-linear correction."""
    z = NormalDist().inv_cdf
    zh = z((hits + 0.5) / (n_signal + 1))
    zf = z((fas + 0.5) / (n_noise + 1))
    return zh - zf, -(zh + zf) / 2
```
hint: Correct each rate first, then take $z$ of each; the correction is what keeps perfect scores finite.
solution: With 40 hits in 40 trials the corrected hit rate is $40.5/41 = 0.9878$, so $z$ is finite. Equal hits and false alarms give $d' = 0$, and a person who never says yes (0 and 0) gets a large positive $c$, a very conservative criterion.

model:
```python
from statistics import NormalDist
import math


def fit_sdt_grid(hits, n_signal, fas, n_noise, d_grid, c_grid):
    """Grid-search maximum likelihood for equal-variance SDT counts."""
    cdf = NormalDist().cdf
    best = None
    for d in d_grid:
        for c in c_grid:
            H = cdf(d / 2 - c)
            F = cdf(-d / 2 - c)
            ll = (hits * math.log(H) + (n_signal - hits) * math.log(1 - H)
                  + fas * math.log(F) + (n_noise - fas) * math.log(1 - F))
            if best is None or ll > best[2]:
                best = (d, c, ll)
    return best
```
hint: For each pair compute $H$ and $F$, then the four-term log-likelihood, and keep the largest.
solution: The two closed-form values here are $d' = 1.516$, $c = 0.084$ for the first data set and $d' = 1.516$, $c = -0.084$ for the second, and the grid answers $1.5$ and $\pm 0.1$ are the nearest grid points, so the grid search and the closed form agree to within the grid spacing.

model:
```python
from statistics import NormalDist
import math


def ll_probit(choices, deltas, s):
    """Log-likelihood of binary choices with p(1) = Phi(delta / s)."""
    cdf = NormalDist().cdf
    total = 0.0
    for c, d in zip(choices, deltas):
        p1 = cdf(d / s)
        total += math.log(p1 if c == 1 else 1.0 - p1)
    return total
```
hint: The only change from the softmax likelihood is the function that turns $\Delta_t/s$ into a probability.
solution: The probit and logistic links give similar likelihoods when $s \approx 1.702/\beta$, so a data set rarely distinguishes them; the choice between them is mostly a convention. A noise standard deviation $s$ plays the role of $1/\beta$.

@quiz self
prompt: An experimenter compares two groups with $d'$ and finds equal values, but the recognition ROC for one group has a $z$-transformed slope of 0.7 instead of 1. Explain what the slope implies for the equal-variance model and for the meaning of $d'$, and give two rival accounts of such a slope.
hint: The $z$-ROC slope equals the ratio of noise to signal standard deviations in the Gaussian model.
solution: In the Gaussian model the slope of the $z$-ROC is $\sigma_{\text{noise}}/\sigma_{\text{signal}}$, so a slope of 0.7 means the signal distribution is about $1/0.7 \approx 1.4$ times as variable as the noise distribution. Then a single $d'$ with both standard deviations set to 1 does not describe the data: the separation measured in standard deviations depends on which distribution is the reference, and $d'$ changes with the criterion used to compute it, so equal $d'$ values may hide a real difference. One account keeps the single-process Gaussian model with unequal variances, possibly because stimuli differ in strength. A rival account is a dual-process model in which a threshold recollection process adds to a familiarity signal. Researchers disagree about which account is right, and the data from a single ROC rarely settle it, so additional manipulations or measures are needed.
