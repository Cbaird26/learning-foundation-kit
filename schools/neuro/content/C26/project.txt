## Week-by-week key ideas

1. **Why model.** A model maps parameters to predictions; fit it, then test it on new conditions. Marr's levels: computational, algorithmic, implementational. Model mimicry means a good fit does not identify the mechanism.
2. **Choice and likelihood.** Softmax: $p(1) = 1/(1 + e^{-\beta\Delta})$. Log-likelihood $\sum_t \log p_t(c_t)$, never positive; chance is $\log 0.5$ per binary choice.
3. **Maximum likelihood.** $\hat\theta = \arg\max \log L$; a grid search finds it to within the grid; a flat curve means a poorly determined parameter (values within 1.92 of the maximum form an approximate 95% interval); watch the edge of the grid.
4. **Signal detection.** $d' = z(H) - z(F)$, $c = -\tfrac12[z(H) + z(F)]$, $H = \Phi(d'/2 - c)$, $F = \Phi(-d'/2 - c)$; log-linear correction for extreme rates; the probit and logistic links are close.
5. **Drift-diffusion.** Euler step $x \leftarrow x + v\,dt + s\sqrt{dt}\,\xi$; for $x_0 = 0$ and constant parameters, $P(\text{upper}) = 1/(1 + e^{-2va/s^2})$ and $\mathbb{E}[\text{DT}] = (a/v)\tanh(av/s^2)$.
6. **Speed and accuracy.** $\mathbb{E}[\text{DT}] = (a/v)(2P - 1)$; boundary changes move along the trade-off; drift changes shift it; RT includes $t_0$.
7. **Reinforcement learning.** $\delta = r - Q$, $Q \leftarrow Q + \alpha\delta$; softmax on $Q$; fit $\alpha$ and $\beta$ by grid search; $\alpha$ and $\beta$ can trade off.
8. **Comparison.** $\text{AIC} = 2k - 2\ell$, $\text{BIC} = k\ln n - 2\ell$, Akaike weights, likelihood-ratio test for nested models ($D = 2\Delta\ell$).
9. **Bayesian models.** Posterior $\propto$ likelihood $\times$ prior; size principle $(1/|h|)^n$; precision-weighted Gaussian combination.
10. **Categorization.** Similarity $e^{-c\,d}$; GCM sums similarity over exemplars; the prototype model uses means; recognition as summed similarity.
11. **Connectionism.** Delta rule $w \leftarrow w + \eta(t - y)x$; XOR needs a hidden layer; Rescorla–Wagner uses the summed prediction error and gives blocking.
12. **Recovery and practice.** Bias, RMSE, correlation of recovered parameters; model-recovery confusion matrix; check absolute fit and report the design.

## Common errors

- Computing the choice probability after the update instead of before it.
- Reporting the best grid value at the edge of the grid.
- Applying closed-form DDM results to a biased start or to variable parameters.
- Using hit rate as sensitivity; extreme rates in the inverse CDF.
- Comparing AIC on different data; treating "best of the models tried" as "a good model."
- Judging parameter recovery by correlation only.

model:
```python
import math


def bayes_learner_ll(choices, rewards, beta):
    """Log-likelihood of a softmax chooser using Beta(1, 1) posterior means."""
    wins = [0, 0]
    losses = [0, 0]
    total = 0.0
    for c, r in zip(choices, rewards):
        m = [(1 + wins[i]) / (2 + wins[i] + losses[i]) for i in (0, 1)]
        p1 = 1.0 / (1.0 + math.exp(-beta * (m[1] - m[0])))
        total += math.log(p1 if c == 1 else 1.0 - p1)
        if r == 1:
            wins[c] += 1
        else:
            losses[c] += 1
    return total
```
hint: Keep two small lists of counts, compute both posterior means before each trial, and update only the chosen arm's counts afterward.
solution: The posterior mean under a uniform prior is a delta rule with a learning rate of $1/(n + 3)$ after $n$ earlier trials with that arm, so this learner weights all past outcomes equally and adapts slowly after a reversal. With `beta = 0` the choice probabilities are all 0.5 whatever the counts, which the second test checks.

@exam self
prompt: Suppose that in a 40-trial bandit study like this course's project, the delta-rule learner has a lower AIC than the Bayesian learner but a higher BIC (that is, the criteria disagree), and parameter recovery shows that the learning rate and inverse temperature of the delta-rule model are strongly correlated across simulated data sets. A collaborator concludes that "people learn by a delta rule, with a learning rate of 0.2." Write a critical assessment of 150 to 250 words: what the evidence supports, what it does not, and two further analyses or experiments you would run.
hint: Use what you know about AIC and BIC, parameter trade-offs, model mimicry and Marr's levels.
solution: The evidence supports that, on these 40 trials, the delta-rule model with two parameters captures the choices better than a one-parameter Bayesian learner under AIC, but not under BIC, so the support is weak and depends on how heavily complexity is penalized: AIC aims at prediction, BIC at identifying the better model among those tried, and a disagreement means the data do not decide. The learning rate of 0.2 is not well determined: when the learning rate and the inverse temperature trade off, a slower learner who is decisive and a faster learner who is noisier fit almost equally well, so a point estimate overstates what is known and an interval should be reported. The conclusion about "how people learn" goes beyond a comparison of two models: other models (asymmetric learning rates, perseveration, a Bayesian learner that assumes change) may fit as well, so mimicry cannot be excluded, and the fit is a claim at the algorithmic level that does not say anything about neural implementation. Two analyses would help: a model-recovery study with this design to see how often each model wins when it is true, and a new experiment with a reward schedule that makes the models' predictions differ more, such as frequent reversals, tested with held-out trials and more participants.
