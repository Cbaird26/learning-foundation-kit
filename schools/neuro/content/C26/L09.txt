@article
A child hears the word "dax" for three animals, all dalmatians. Does "dax" mean dalmatians, dogs, or animals? All three are consistent with the examples. Yet people strongly favor "dalmatian," and the more dalmatians they see, the more sure they become, even though no example ever contradicts "dog." A purely logical learner would never prefer the narrow meaning. A **Bayesian** model explains the preference, and it also explains how the mind combines a noisy measurement with expectations.

### Bayes' rule

For hypotheses $h$ and data $d$,

$$P(h \mid d) = \frac{P(d \mid h)\,P(h)}{\sum_{h'} P(d \mid h')\,P(h')}.$$

The **prior** $P(h)$ is belief before the data, the **likelihood** $P(d \mid h)$ is how probable the data are if $h$ is true, and the **posterior** is the updated belief. The model's claims are in the likelihood and the prior; Bayes' rule only says how to combine them.

### Concept learning and the size principle

A small hypothesis space over the numbers 1 to 20 has four hypotheses with equal priors of $0.25$: "powers of 2" $\{1,2,4,8,16\}$ (size 5), "even numbers" (size 10), "multiples of 5" $\{5,10,15,20\}$ (size 4) and "all numbers" (size 20). Suppose examples are drawn at random from the true concept (**strong sampling**). Then each example consistent with $h$ has probability $1/|h|$, and $n$ independent consistent examples have likelihood $(1/|h|)^n$; if any example lies outside $h$, the likelihood is $0$. Smaller hypotheses therefore win the more consistent data arrive: the **size principle**. Under **weak sampling**, where examples are chosen independently of the concept and only labeled as members, the likelihood is just 1 for any consistent hypothesis and the size principle disappears.

```python
def posterior(hyps, prior, data):
    scores = {}
    for name, members in hyps.items():
        if all(x in members for x in data):
            scores[name] = prior[name] * (1 / len(members)) ** len(data)
        else:
            scores[name] = 0.0
    total = sum(scores.values())
    return {name: s / total for name, s in scores.items()}

hyps = {"powers of 2": {1, 2, 4, 8, 16}, "evens": set(range(2, 21, 2)),
        "multiples of 5": {5, 10, 15, 20}, "all numbers": set(range(1, 21))}
prior = {name: 0.25 for name in hyps}
for data in ([16], [16, 8], [16, 8, 2]):
    p = posterior(hyps, prior, data)
    print(data, round(p["powers of 2"], 3))
```

This prints $0.571$, $0.762$ and $0.877$: after the single example 16 the weights are proportional to $0.2, 0.1, 0.05$ for powers of 2, evens and all numbers, and each further consistent example multiplies the weight of each hypothesis by $1/|h|$, favoring the smaller. After $[16, 8, 2]$ the posterior is $(0.877, 0.110, 0.000, 0.014)$. The probability that a new number $y$ belongs to the concept is the **generalization probability** $\sum_h P(h \mid d)\,\mathbf{1}[y \in h]$. For $y = 10$ it is $0.110 + 0.014 = 0.123$.

### Perception: Gaussian prior and measurement

For a continuous quantity, take a prior $\mathcal{N}(\mu_p, \sigma_p^2)$ and a noisy measurement $x \sim \mathcal{N}(s, \sigma_x^2)$ of the true value $s$. The posterior is Gaussian with

$$\mu_{\text{post}} = \frac{\mu_p/\sigma_p^2 + x/\sigma_x^2}{1/\sigma_p^2 + 1/\sigma_x^2}, \qquad \sigma_{\text{post}}^2 = \frac{1}{1/\sigma_p^2 + 1/\sigma_x^2}.$$

The posterior mean is a weighted average in which each source is weighted by its **precision** $1/\sigma^2$. With $\mu_p = 0$, $\sigma_p = 2$, $x = 6$ and $\sigma_x = 3$, the precisions are $0.25$ and $0.111$, so $\mu_{\text{post}} = 1.846$ and $\sigma_{\text{post}} = 1.664$: the estimate is pulled strongly toward the prior because the measurement is noisier. Combining two **cues** is the same calculation with a flat prior; reliability-weighted combination of visual and haptic information has been reported in classic experiments (for example Ernst and Banks, 2002), though not all studies find optimal weighting.

### What do Bayesian models explain?

Bayesian models have explained many patterns (shrinkage toward expectations, the size principle, cue weighting). Critics point out that, with free choices of prior and likelihood, a Bayesian model can be fitted to almost any result and may merely redescribe behavior; they ask for priors measured independently of the behavior being explained, comparison with non-Bayesian alternatives, and algorithmic accounts of how the brain could approximate the computation. Defenders reply that the models make quantitative predictions with few parameters, that priors can be constrained by environmental statistics, and that stating the computational problem (Marr's top level) is valuable even when the algorithm is left open. Both sides agree that the approach says what an ideal learner would conclude, not necessarily how people reach their conclusions.

@example
title: A posterior from two hypotheses
problem: Two hypotheses have equal priors: a small concept of size 10 and a large concept of size 40, and both contain every example. Three examples are observed. Find the posterior probability of the small concept under strong sampling.
step: Likelihoods: small $(1/10)^3 = 0.001$, large $(1/40)^3 = 0.000015625$.
step: With equal priors the posterior is proportional to the likelihood: $0.001 / (0.001 + 0.000015625)$.
step: Equivalent form: $1/(1 + (10/40)^3) = 1/(1 + 0.015625) = 0.9846$.
step: With a single example the same calculation gives $1/(1 + 10/40) = 0.8$, so more consistent data increase the preference.
answer: $0.985$ (to three decimals).

@mistakes
- Forgetting the likelihood is zero for any hypothesis that contradicts an example: such hypotheses must drop out.
- Using the size principle when examples were not sampled from the concept: under weak sampling consistent hypotheses stay in proportion to their priors.
- Averaging a measurement with a prior using the standard deviations as weights: the weights are the precisions $1/\sigma^2$.
- Forgetting to renormalize: the posterior must sum to 1 over the hypotheses.

```python
def posterior(hyps, prior, data, sampling="strong"):
    """Posterior over hypotheses with strong or weak sampling likelihoods."""
    scores = {}
    for name, members in hyps.items():
        if all(x in members for x in data):
            if sampling == "strong":
                like = (1 / len(members)) ** len(data)
            else:
                like = 1.0
            scores[name] = prior[name] * like
        else:
            scores[name] = 0.0
    total = 0.0
    for s in scores.values():
        total += s
    if total == 0.0:
        return {name: 0.0 for name in hyps}
    return {name: s / total for name, s in scores.items()}
```
hint: Compute an unnormalized score for each hypothesis, check for a zero total, and then divide by the total.
solution: With weak sampling, the two consistent hypotheses keep their prior ratio of $0.5 : 0.3$, giving $0.625$ and $0.375$, whereas strong sampling moves the weight to the smaller set because each example is more probable under it. The zero-total check handles examples that no hypothesis contains.

```python
def generalize(hyps, prior, data, y):
    """Probability that y is in the concept, averaging over hypotheses."""
    weights = {}
    for name, members in hyps.items():
        if all(x in members for x in data):
            weights[name] = prior[name] * (1 / len(members)) ** len(data)
        else:
            weights[name] = 0.0
    total = 0.0
    for w in weights.values():
        total += w
    if total == 0.0:
        return 0.0
    inside = 0.0
    for name, members in hyps.items():
        if y in members:
            inside += weights[name]
    return inside / total
```
hint: Compute the weight of every hypothesis, then divide the total weight of the hypotheses that contain `y` by the overall total.
solution: The generalization probability averages the hypotheses' predictions with posterior weights. After examples 1 and 3 the narrow set $\{1,2,3,4\}$ holds most of the weight, so 4 is judged very likely (0.81) while 5, which lies only in the other two hypotheses, is judged much less likely (0.26). A number in every hypothesis gets probability 1.

model:
```python
import math


def gaussian_posterior(mu_p, sd_p, x, sd_x):
    """Posterior mean and sd for a Gaussian prior and one Gaussian measurement."""
    prec_p = 1.0 / sd_p ** 2
    prec_x = 1.0 / sd_x ** 2
    mean = (mu_p * prec_p + x * prec_x) / (prec_p + prec_x)
    return mean, math.sqrt(1.0 / (prec_p + prec_x))
```
hint: Convert the two standard deviations to precisions, average the means weighted by precision, and invert the summed precision for the variance.
solution: The posterior precision is the sum of the prior and measurement precisions, so the posterior is always narrower than either source. When the measurement is very precise, the posterior collapses onto it, as the third test shows, and when both are equally reliable the mean is halfway between them.

@quiz self
prompt: A researcher shows that a Bayesian model with a Gaussian prior fits how people judge speed, and concludes that the brain "computes posteriors." Give the strongest version of the argument in favor of this claim, the strongest objection, and one analysis or experiment that would help decide how much the fit shows.
hint: Think about which parts of the model are fitted and which are measured, and about Marr's levels.
solution: In favor: the model has few parameters, it predicts that the estimate shifts toward the prior more as the stimulus becomes less reliable, such as when contrast is lowered, and the fitted prior resembles the distribution of speeds in natural scenes. These are quantitative, risky predictions that a non-probabilistic account would need to reproduce separately. Objection: the prior and the noise level are free parameters, so many models that shrink noisy estimates toward a central value would fit equally well; the fit shows that behavior is as if a prior were used, a claim at the computational level, not that neurons represent and combine probability distributions. An analysis that would help is to compare the Bayesian model with alternatives that shrink estimates without being Bayesian, using a criterion that penalizes parameters, and to test whether a prior measured independently from natural statistics predicts the behavior without refitting; neural evidence for representations of uncertainty would address the algorithmic and implementational claims.
