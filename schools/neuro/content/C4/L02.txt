@article
**Kinetics** asks how fast a reaction goes. Thermodynamics tells you whether a reaction can happen; kinetics tells you whether it happens on the timescale of a heartbeat, a day or a century. Drug clearance, enzyme action and radioactive decay all follow the same kinetic rules.

### Rate and the rate law

The **rate** of a reaction $A \to \text{products}$ is the change in concentration per unit time, $-\Delta[A]/\Delta t$. The **rate law** relates rate to concentrations:

$$\text{rate} = k[A]^m[B]^n.$$

The exponents $m$ and $n$ are the **reaction orders** and must be found by experiment, not read from the balanced equation. The **rate constant** $k$ depends on temperature (and catalyst) but not on concentration. Its units depend on the overall order: $\text{M}^{1-\text{order}}\,\text{s}^{-1}$.

### Method of initial rates

Run the reaction several times, changing one concentration at a time, and measure the initial rate. If doubling $[A]$ doubles the rate, the order in $A$ is 1. If doubling $[A]$ quadruples the rate, the order is 2. If the rate does not change, the order is 0. Formally, order $=\log(\text{rate ratio})/\log(\text{concentration ratio})$. Once the orders are known, substitute any one experiment to solve for $k$.

### Integrated rate laws

Integrating the rate law gives concentration as a function of time:

| Order | Integrated law | Linear plot | Half-life |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | $[A] = [A]_0 - kt$ | $[A]$ vs $t$ | $[A]_0/(2k)$ |
| 1 | $\ln[A] = \ln[A]_0 - kt$ | $\ln[A]$ vs $t$ | $\ln 2/k$ |
| 2 | $1/[A] = 1/[A]_0 + kt$ | $1/[A]$ vs $t$ | $1/(k[A]_0)$ |

The slope of the linear plot gives $k$ (with sign: $-k$ for orders 0 and 1, $+k$ for order 2). The plot that comes out straight tells you the order.

### Half-life

The **half-life** $t_{1/2}$ is the time for a concentration to fall to half its value. For a first-order process it is a constant, $0.693/k$, independent of how much is present. That is why drugs eliminated by first-order kinetics show a fixed half-life, and why after $n$ half-lives the fraction remaining is $(1/2)^n$. For second-order reactions the half-life lengthens as the reactant is consumed; for zero-order reactions it shortens.

### Zero-order in medicine

Some processes saturate. Ethanol elimination by alcohol dehydrogenase proceeds at a roughly constant amount per hour at ordinary concentrations, which is zero-order behavior: the enzyme is saturated, so the rate does not depend on $[\text{ethanol}]$.

### Finding a rate law and half-life from data

A typical task: given initial rates for several mixtures, (1) compare experiments that differ in one concentration to find each order, (2) compute $k$ with units, and (3) use the integrated law for the identified order to find $t_{1/2}$ or the concentration after a given time. Always carry units; they check your order.

@example
title: Rate law from initial rates
problem: For $A + B \to \text{products}$, initial rates were: (1) $[A] = 0.10$, $[B] = 0.10$, rate $= 2.0\times10^{-3}$ M/s; (2) $[A] = 0.20$, $[B] = 0.10$, rate $= 4.0\times10^{-3}$ M/s; (3) $[A] = 0.10$, $[B] = 0.20$, rate $= 8.0\times10^{-3}$ M/s. Find the rate law and $k$.
step: Experiments 1 and 2 change only $[A]$, which doubles. The rate doubles, so the order in $A$ is 1.
step: Experiments 1 and 3 change only $[B]$, which doubles. The rate quadruples, so the order in $B$ is 2.
step: The rate law is $\text{rate} = k[A][B]^2$.
step: Use experiment 1: $k = \dfrac{2.0\times10^{-3}}{(0.10)(0.10)^2} = 2.0\ \text{M}^{-2}\text{s}^{-1}$.

@example
title: Half-life and time for a first-order decay
problem: A first-order reaction has $k = 3.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{s}^{-1}$. Find its half-life and the time for $75\%$ of the reactant to react.
step: Half-life: $t_{1/2} = \ln 2/k = 0.693/(3.0\times10^{-3}) = 231\ \text{s}$.
step: If $75\%$ has reacted, $25\%$ remains, which is $(1/2)^2$: two half-lives.
step: $t = 2 \times 231 = 462\ \text{s}$. The same result comes from $t = \ln(4)/k$.

@mistakes
- Reading the exponents in the rate law from the balanced equation's coefficients.
- Comparing two experiments in which more than one concentration changes.
- Using the first-order half-life formula for a second-order reaction, or the reverse.
- Dropping or mixing up the units of $k$, which hides an incorrect order.

@practice num
prompt: A reaction obeys $\text{rate} = k[A]^2$ with $k = 0.50\ \text{M}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$. What is the rate when $[A] = 0.20\ \text{M}$?
answer: 0.020
check: 0.50 * 0.20**2
tol: 1%
unit: M/s
hint: Substitute $k$ and $[A]$ directly into the rate law.
solution: $\text{rate} = (0.50)(0.20)^2 = 0.50 \times 0.040 = 0.020\ \text{M/s}$.

@practice num
prompt: When $[A]$ is doubled from $0.10\ \text{M}$ to $0.20\ \text{M}$ with everything else constant, the initial rate rises from $2.0\times10^{-3}$ to $8.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{M/s}$. What is the reaction order in $A$?
answer: 2
check: round(log(8.0e-3 / 2.0e-3) / log(0.20 / 0.10))
hint: Compare the rate ratio with the concentration ratio.
hint: The rate ratio is $4$ and the concentration ratio is $2$. What power of $2$ gives $4$?
solution: Rate ratio $= 4$, concentration ratio $= 2$, and $2^2 = 4$, so the order is $2$.

@practice num
prompt: A first-order reaction has $k = 0.0462\ \text{min}^{-1}$. Find its half-life in minutes.
answer: 15.0
check: log(2) / 0.0462
tol: 1%
unit: min
hint: For first order, $t_{1/2}$ depends only on $k$.
solution: $t_{1/2} = \ln 2/k = 0.693/0.0462 = 15.0\ \text{min}$.

@practice num
prompt: A second-order reaction has $k = 0.20\ \text{M}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$ and $[A]_0 = 0.50\ \text{M}$. Find $[A]$ after $10\ \text{s}$.
answer: 0.25
check: 1 / (1 / 0.50 + 0.20 * 10)
tol: 1%
unit: M
hint: Use $1/[A] = 1/[A]_0 + kt$.
hint: $1/[A]_0 = 2.0\ \text{M}^{-1}$ and $kt = 2.0\ \text{M}^{-1}$.
solution: $1/[A] = 2.0 + 2.0 = 4.0\ \text{M}^{-1}$, so $[A] = 0.25\ \text{M}$.

@practice mc
prompt: Which plot gives a straight line for a first-order reaction?
choice: $[A]$ versus time
choice*: $\ln[A]$ versus time
choice: $1/[A]$ versus time
choice: Rate versus time
hint: Integrating $-d[A]/dt = k[A]$ gives a logarithmic relation.
solution: The first-order integrated law is $\ln[A] = \ln[A]_0 - kt$, so $\ln[A]$ against time is linear with slope $-k$. Linear $[A]$ indicates zero order and linear $1/[A]$ indicates second order.

@quiz num
prompt: A first-order reaction's concentration falls from $0.800\ \text{M}$ to $0.100\ \text{M}$ in $60.0\ \text{s}$. Find the rate constant in s$^{-1}$.
answer: 0.03466
check: log(0.800 / 0.100) / 60.0
tol: 1%
unit: s^-1
hint: Use $\ln([A]_0/[A]) = kt$.
hint: $0.800/0.100 = 8$, and $\ln 8 = 2.079$.
solution: $k = \ln(8)/60.0 = 2.079/60.0 = 0.0347\ \text{s}^{-1}$. (Three half-lives passed, so $t_{1/2} = 20.0\ \text{s}$ and $k = 0.693/20.0$ agrees.)

@quiz num
prompt: A second-order reaction has $k = 0.025\ \text{M}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$ and an initial concentration of $0.40\ \text{M}$. What is its first half-life in seconds?
answer: 100
check: 1 / (0.025 * 0.40)
tol: 1%
unit: s
hint: The second-order half-life depends on the starting concentration.
solution: $t_{1/2} = 1/(k[A]_0) = 1/(0.025 \times 0.40) = 1/0.010 = 100\ \text{s}$.
