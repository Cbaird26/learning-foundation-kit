@article
A **Brønsted-Lowry acid** donates a proton ($\text{H}^+$); a **base** accepts one. When an acid loses a proton, what remains is its **conjugate base**; when a base gains one, it becomes its **conjugate acid**. In $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{CH}_3\text{COO}^- + \text{H}_3\text{O}^+$, acetic acid and acetate are one conjugate pair, and water and hydronium are the other.

### Water, pH and pOH

Water autoionizes: $2\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_3\text{O}^+ + \text{OH}^-$, with

$$K_w = [\text{H}^+][\text{OH}^-] = 1.0\times10^{-14} \quad (25\ \text{°C}).$$

Define $\text{pH} = -\log_{10}[\text{H}^+]$ and $\text{pOH} = -\log_{10}[\text{OH}^-]$, so $\text{pH} + \text{pOH} = 14.00$ at $25\ \text{°C}$. Each pH unit is a tenfold change in $[\text{H}^+]$. Arterial blood has pH $7.35$–$7.45$ (about $[\text{H}^+] = 40\ \text{nmol/L}$); gastric fluid is about pH $1.5$–$3.5$.

### Strong acids and bases

**Strong** acids (HCl, HBr, HI, HNO$_3$, H$_2$SO$_4$ for the first proton, HClO$_4$) ionize completely, so $[\text{H}^+]$ equals the acid concentration. **Strong** bases (the Group 1 hydroxides, and Ca(OH)$_2$, Sr(OH)$_2$, Ba(OH)$_2$) dissociate completely; count the hydroxide ions per formula unit, so $0.010\ \text{M}$ Ba(OH)$_2$ gives $[\text{OH}^-] = 0.020\ \text{M}$. For very dilute solutions near $10^{-7}\ \text{M}$, water's own ions matter.

### Weak acids and bases

A **weak** acid ionizes only partially:

$$K_a = \frac{[\text{H}^+][\text{A}^-]}{[\text{HA}]}, \qquad \text{p}K_a = -\log K_a.$$

A smaller $K_a$ (larger p$K_a$) means a weaker acid. For a weak base $B + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{BH}^+ + \text{OH}^-$ the constant is $K_b$. For any conjugate pair,

$$K_a \times K_b = K_w, \qquad \text{p}K_a + \text{p}K_b = 14.$$

So the conjugate base of a weak acid is a weak base, and the conjugate base of a strong acid (like $\text{Cl}^-$) has no basic strength in water.

### Solving a weak-acid problem

For initial concentration $C$, an ICE table gives $K_a = x^2/(C - x)$ where $x = [\text{H}^+]$. If $x \ll C$ (check that it is under $5\%$ of $C$), then

$$x \approx \sqrt{K_aC}.$$

The **percent ionization** is $(x/C)\times 100\%$. It increases as the solution is diluted. The same method with $K_b$ gives $[\text{OH}^-]$ for a weak base; then find pOH and pH.

### Polyprotic acids and salts

A **polyprotic** acid loses protons stepwise with $K_{a1} \gg K_{a2}$; for the pH, the first ionization usually dominates. Salts of weak acids or bases change pH: sodium acetate solution is basic (acetate is a weak base), ammonium chloride is acidic (ammonium is a weak acid), and NaCl is neutral.

@example
title: pH of a weak acid
problem: Find the pH of $0.10\ \text{M}$ acetic acid ($K_a = 1.8\times10^{-5}$).
step: Write $K_a = x^2/(0.10 - x)$ and assume $x \ll 0.10$, so $x^2 = (1.8\times10^{-5})(0.10) = 1.8\times10^{-6}$.
step: $x = \sqrt{1.8\times10^{-6}} = 1.34\times10^{-3}\ \text{M} = [\text{H}^+]$.
step: Check the assumption: $1.34\times10^{-3}/0.10 = 1.3\%$, which is under $5\%$.
step: $\text{pH} = -\log(1.34\times10^{-3}) = 2.87$.

@example
title: pH of a weak base
problem: Find the pH of $0.020\ \text{M}$ ammonia ($K_b = 1.8\times10^{-5}$).
step: $K_b = x^2/(0.020 - x)$ with $x = [\text{OH}^-]$. Neglect $x$: $x^2 = (1.8\times10^{-5})(0.020) = 3.6\times10^{-7}$.
step: $x = 6.0\times10^{-4}\ \text{M}$, which is $3\%$ of $0.020$, so the approximation holds.
step: $\text{pOH} = -\log(6.0\times10^{-4}) = 3.22$.
step: $\text{pH} = 14.00 - 3.22 = 10.78$.

@mistakes
- Treating a weak acid as fully ionized, so that $[\text{H}^+]$ is set equal to the acid concentration.
- Forgetting to double $[\text{OH}^-]$ for Ba(OH)$_2$ or Ca(OH)$_2$.
- Reporting pOH when the question asks for pH of a base.
- Skipping the $5\%$ check before using the square-root shortcut.

@practice num
prompt: Hydrofluoric acid has $K_a = 6.8\times10^{-4}$. Find $K_b$ of the fluoride ion at $25\ \text{°C}$.
answer: 1.47e-11
check: 1.0e-14 / 6.8e-4
tol: 1%
hint: For a conjugate pair, $K_aK_b = K_w$.
solution: $K_b = K_w/K_a = (1.0\times10^{-14})/(6.8\times10^{-4}) = 1.47\times10^{-11}$.

@practice num
prompt: Formic acid has $K_a = 1.8\times10^{-4}$. What is the percent ionization in a $0.20\ \text{M}$ solution? (Use the approximation $x = \sqrt{K_aC}$.)

check: math.sqrt(1.8e-4 * 0.20) / 0.20 * 100
tol: 0.1
unit: %
hint: Find $x = [\text{H}^+]$ first, then divide by the initial concentration.
solution: $x = \sqrt{(1.8\times10^{-4})(0.20)} = 6.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{M}$. Percent ionization $= 6.0\times10^{-3}/0.20 \times 100 = 3.0\%$.

@quiz mc
prompt: Equal volumes of $0.10\ \text{M}$ HCl and $0.10\ \text{M}$ acetic acid are compared. Which statement is correct?
choice*: The HCl solution has the lower pH, but both require the same amount of NaOH for complete neutralization.
choice: Acetic acid needs less NaOH because it is a weak acid.
choice: The two solutions have the same pH because their concentrations are equal.
choice: HCl needs more NaOH because it is a stronger acid.
hint: pH reflects $[\text{H}^+]$ already ionized; neutralization depends on total acid available.
solution: HCl is fully ionized, so its $[\text{H}^+]$ is higher and its pH lower. Weak acid molecules replenish $\text{H}^+$ as it is consumed, so both solutions contain $0.10\ \text{mol/L}$ of titratable acid and need equal NaOH.
