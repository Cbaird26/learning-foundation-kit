@article
A **buffer** resists changes in pH when small amounts of strong acid or base are added. It contains a weak acid (HA) and its conjugate base ($\text{A}^-$) in comparable amounts. Added $\text{H}^+$ is consumed by $\text{A}^-$ ($\text{A}^- + \text{H}^+ \to \text{HA}$); added $\text{OH}^-$ is consumed by HA ($\text{HA} + \text{OH}^- \to \text{A}^- + \text{H}_2\text{O}$). Either way the ratio $[\text{A}^-]/[\text{HA}]$ shifts only a little.

### The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation

Take the logarithm of $K_a = [\text{H}^+][\text{A}^-]/[\text{HA}]$ and rearrange:

$$\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a + \log\frac{[\text{A}^-]}{[\text{HA}]}.$$

Because the ratio matters, you may use moles instead of concentrations when both species are in the same volume. Key consequences:

- When $[\text{A}^-] = [\text{HA}]$, $\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a$.
- Each factor of $10$ in the ratio moves the pH by one unit.
- A buffer works best within $\pm 1$ pH unit of its p$K_a$ (ratios from $1{:}10$ to $10{:}1$).
- **Buffer capacity** (the amount of acid or base it can absorb) grows with the total concentrations of HA and $\text{A}^-$.

### Adding strong acid or base: stoichiometry first

Treat addition as a stoichiometric reaction, then apply Henderson-Hasselbalch. Adding $n$ mol of strong acid *decreases* $\text{A}^-$ by $n$ and *increases* HA by $n$. Adding strong base does the reverse. Only after this step do you compute the new ratio.

### The bicarbonate buffer of blood

The main extracellular buffer is

$$\text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_2\text{CO}_3 \rightleftharpoons \text{H}^+ + \text{HCO}_3^-.$$

Because dissolved $\text{CO}_2$ is in equilibrium with $\text{H}_2\text{CO}_3$, clinicians write the equation in terms of $P_{\text{CO}_2}$ in mmHg, with p$K_a = 6.1$ and a solubility factor $0.03\ \text{mmol/(L·mmHg)}$:

$$\text{pH} = 6.1 + \log\frac{[\text{HCO}_3^-]}{0.03\times P_{\text{CO}_2}}.$$

For normal values ($[\text{HCO}_3^-] = 24\ \text{mEq/L}$, $P_{\text{CO}_2} = 40\ \text{mmHg}$), the denominator is $1.2\ \text{mmol/L}$ and the ratio is $20{:}1$, giving pH $7.40$. This ratio is far from $1{:}1$, which by the $\pm 1$ rule looks like a poor buffer, but it is an *open* system: the lungs remove $\text{CO}_2$ (changing the denominator within minutes) and the kidneys regenerate bicarbonate (changing the numerator over days). The pH changes when the ratio changes: a fall in $[\text{HCO}_3^-]$ (metabolic acidosis) or a rise in $P_{\text{CO}_2}$ (respiratory acidosis) lowers it.

### Choosing a buffer

Choose a weak acid whose p$K_a$ is close to the target pH: acetic acid/acetate (p$K_a$ $4.76$) near pH 5, $\text{H}_2\text{PO}_4^-/\text{HPO}_4^{2-}$ (p$K_a$ $7.21$) near physiological pH, and $\text{NH}_4^+/\text{NH}_3$ (p$K_a$ $9.25$) near pH 9.

@example
title: pH of normal blood
problem: Find the pH of plasma with $[\text{HCO}_3^-] = 24\ \text{mEq/L}$ and $P_{\text{CO}_2} = 40\ \text{mmHg}$.
step: Compute dissolved $\text{CO}_2$: $0.03 \times 40 = 1.2\ \text{mmol/L}$.
step: The ratio is $24/1.2 = 20$.
step: $\text{pH} = 6.1 + \log(20) = 6.1 + 1.301 = 7.40$.

@example
title: Buffer after adding strong acid
problem: A buffer of $0.50\ \text{L}$ contains $0.20\ \text{M}$ acetic acid and $0.20\ \text{M}$ sodium acetate (p$K_a = 4.74$). Find the pH after adding $0.020\ \text{mol}$ of HCl (assume no volume change).
step: Initial moles: $0.20 \times 0.50 = 0.10\ \text{mol}$ of each of HA and $\text{A}^-$.
step: The added $\text{H}^+$ converts $\text{A}^-$ to HA: $\text{A}^- = 0.10 - 0.020 = 0.080\ \text{mol}$ and $\text{HA} = 0.10 + 0.020 = 0.120\ \text{mol}$.
step: $\text{pH} = 4.74 + \log(0.080/0.120) = 4.74 - 0.176 = 4.56$.
step: The pH fell by only $0.18$ units, whereas the same HCl added to unbuffered water would have given a far larger drop.

@mistakes
- Putting the ratio upside down: it is base over acid in the numerator.
- Adding strong acid to the base concentration instead of subtracting it (and vice versa).
- Forgetting the factor $0.03$ when using $P_{\text{CO}_2}$.
- Choosing a buffer whose p$K_a$ is more than about one unit from the target pH.

@practice num
prompt: A buffer has $[\text{A}^-]/[\text{HA}] = 10$ and the acid has p$K_a = 4.76$. What is the pH?
answer: 5.76
check: 4.76 + log10(10)
tol: 0.01
hint: Use $\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a + \log([\text{A}^-]/[\text{HA}])$.
solution: $\log(10) = 1$, so $\text{pH} = 4.76 + 1 = 5.76$.

@practice num
prompt: A phosphate buffer ($\text{H}_2\text{PO}_4^-/\text{HPO}_4^{2-}$, p$K_a = 7.21$) is to have pH $7.40$. What must $[\text{HPO}_4^{2-}]/[\text{H}_2\text{PO}_4^-]$ be?
answer: 1.55
check: 10 ** (7.40 - 7.21)
tol: 1%
hint: Rearrange to $\text{ratio} = 10^{\text{pH} - \text{p}K_a}$.
solution: $\text{ratio} = 10^{7.40 - 7.21} = 10^{0.19} = 1.55$.

@practice num
prompt: A patient's plasma has $[\text{HCO}_3^-] = 12\ \text{mEq/L}$ and $P_{\text{CO}_2} = 40\ \text{mmHg}$. Using p$K_a = 6.1$ and solubility $0.03\ \text{mmol/(L·mmHg)}$, find the pH.
answer: 7.10
check: 6.1 + log10(12 / (0.03 * 40))
tol: 0.01
hint: First compute dissolved $\text{CO}_2 = 0.03 \times P_{\text{CO}_2}$.
solution: Dissolved $\text{CO}_2 = 1.2\ \text{mmol/L}$, the ratio is $12/1.2 = 10$, and $\text{pH} = 6.1 + 1 = 7.10$.

@practice num
prompt: Plasma has $[\text{HCO}_3^-] = 24\ \text{mEq/L}$ and $P_{\text{CO}_2} = 60\ \text{mmHg}$. Using p$K_a = 6.1$ and solubility $0.03\ \text{mmol/(L·mmHg)}$, find the pH.
answer: 7.225
check: 6.1 + log10(24 / (0.03 * 60))
tol: 0.01
hint: Dissolved $\text{CO}_2$ is now $0.03 \times 60 = 1.8\ \text{mmol/L}$.
solution: The ratio is $24/1.8 = 13.3$, $\log(13.3) = 1.125$, so $\text{pH} = 7.23$. The rise in $P_{\text{CO}_2}$ lowers the ratio and the pH.

@quiz num
prompt: A buffer contains $0.30\ \text{M}$ $\text{NH}_3$ and $0.20\ \text{M}$ $\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$. The p$K_a$ of $\text{NH}_4^+$ is $9.25$. Find the pH.
answer: 9.426

solution: $\text{pH} = 9.25 + \log(0.30/0.20) = 9.25 + 0.176 = 9.43$.

@quiz num
prompt: A patient has arterial pH $7.52$ and $[\text{HCO}_3^-] = 24\ \text{mEq/L}$. Using p$K_a = 6.1$ and solubility $0.03\ \text{mmol/(L·mmHg)}$, find $P_{\text{CO}_2}$ in mmHg.
answer: 30.4
check: 24 / (0.03 * 10 ** (7.52 - 6.1))

solution: $0.03P_{\text{CO}_2} = 24/26.3 = 0.912$, so $P_{\text{CO}_2} = 30.4\ \text{mmHg}$, a low value consistent with respiratory alkalosis (hyperventilation).
