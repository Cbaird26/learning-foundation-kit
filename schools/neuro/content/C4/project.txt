You will work from the supplied data below, show every calculation, and write a short interpretation for each case.
goal: Compute blood pH from $[\text{HCO}_3^-]$ and $P_{\text{CO}_2}$ for several clinical situations.
goal: Compare the response of an open buffer system (lungs hold $P_{\text{CO}_2}$ constant) with a closed one (no gas exchange) to the same added acid.
goal: Classify each case as metabolic or respiratory acidosis or alkalosis and explain the compensation.
goal: Connect the model to equilibrium (Le Chatelier), acid-base and kinetic ideas from the course.
step: Write the model. State $\text{pH} = 6.1 + \log\big([\text{HCO}_3^-]/(0.03 \times P_{\text{CO}_2})\big)$ with $[\text{HCO}_3^-]$ in mmol/L and $P_{\text{CO}_2}$ in mmHg. Write the coupled equilibria $\text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_2\text{CO}_3 \rightleftharpoons \text{H}^+ + \text{HCO}_3^-$ and explain where the $0.03$ and the $6.1$ come from.
step: Compute pH for the five invented cases below. Normal reference ranges: pH $7.35$ to $7.45$, $[\text{HCO}_3^-]$ $22$ to $26$ mEq/L, $P_{\text{CO}_2}$ $35$ to $45$ mmHg.
Case A, healthy adult: $[\text{HCO}_3^-] = 24$, $P_{\text{CO}_2} = 40$.
Case B, uncontrolled diabetes with deep rapid breathing: $[\text{HCO}_3^-] = 10$, $P_{\text{CO}_2} = 25$.
Case C, opioid overdose with slow shallow breathing: $[\text{HCO}_3^-] = 26$, $P_{\text{CO}_2} = 70$.
Case D, prolonged vomiting: $[\text{HCO}_3^-] = 36$, $P_{\text{CO}_2} = 48$.
Case E, panic-related hyperventilation: $[\text{HCO}_3^-] = 22$, $P_{\text{CO}_2} = 28$.

step: Add acid to the buffer. Start from Case A and add $5\ \text{mmol}$ of strong acid per liter. First, treat the system as open: each added $\text{H}^+$ converts one $\text{HCO}_3^-$ to $\text{CO}_2$, which is exhaled so $P_{\text{CO}_2}$ stays at $40$. Then treat it as closed: the $\text{CO}_2$ stays dissolved and adds to the denominator. Compute both pH values and compare.

### Week by week

1. **Colligative properties.** Properties depend on particle number: $\Delta T = iKm$, $P = X_{\text{solvent}}P^\circ$, $\Pi = iMRT$. Molality uses kilograms of solvent; water moves toward higher solute concentration.
2. **Kinetics.** Orders come from experiment (initial rates). Integrated laws and linear plots: $\ln[A]$ (first), $1/[A]$ (second). First-order $t_{1/2} = \ln 2/k$ is constant.
3. **Mechanisms and catalysis.** The slow step sets the rate law; intermediates must not appear in it. Arrhenius: $\ln(k_2/k_1) = (E_a/R)(1/T_1 - 1/T_2)$. Catalysts lower $E_a$ and leave $K$ unchanged. Michaelis-Menten: $v = V_{\max}[S]/(K_m + [S])$.
4. **Equilibrium.** $K$ from products over reactants, $K_p = K_c(RT)^{\Delta n}$, compare $Q$ with $K$, apply Le Chatelier; only temperature changes $K$.
5. **Acids and bases.** $K_w$, pH, pOH, $K_aK_b = K_w$; weak acid $x \approx \sqrt{K_aC}$ with the $5\%$ check.
6. **Buffers.** $\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a + \log([\text{A}^-]/[\text{HA}])$; stoichiometry first for added acid or base; bicarbonate uses $0.03P_{\text{CO}_2}$ and p$K_a$ $6.1$.
7. **Titrations.** Work in moles; pH at half-equivalence equals p$K_a$; weak acid with strong base has an equivalence pH above $7$.
8. **Solubility.** $K_{sp}$, molar solubility ($s^2$ or $4s^3$), common ion, $Q$ versus $K_{sp}$, acid dissolves salts of basic anions.
9. **Entropy and free energy.** $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$, $\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K$, $\Delta G = \Delta G^\circ + RT\ln Q$.
10. **Electrochemistry.** $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}}$, $\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ$, Nernst equation.
11. **Nuclear chemistry.** Balance $A$ and $Z$; $N = N_0(1/2)^{t/t_{1/2}}$; $E = \Delta m c^2$.
12. **Coordination chemistry.** Oxidation state, d count, high versus low spin; heme $\text{Fe}^{2+}$, CO binding, methemoglobin, chelation.

### Most common errors

Using volume or solution mass for molality, reading orders from coefficients, leaving an intermediate in a rate law, inverting the buffer ratio, using concentrations before mixing, mixing kJ with J, using Celsius in $RT$, and forgetting $n$ in the electrochemical equations.

prompt: A patient with diabetic ketoacidosis has $[\text{HCO}_3^-] = 15\ \text{mEq/L}$ and, after respiratory compensation, $P_{\text{CO}_2} = 28\ \text{mmHg}$. Using p$K_a = 6.1$ and solubility $0.03\ \text{mmol/(L·mmHg)}$, find the arterial pH.
answer: 7.352
check: 6.1 + log10(15 / (0.03 * 28))
tol: 0.01
hint: Dissolved $\text{CO}_2$ is $0.03 \times 28 = 0.84\ \text{mmol/L}$.
hint: The base-to-acid ratio is $15/0.84 = 17.9$.
solution: $\text{pH} = 6.1 + \log(15/0.84) = 6.1 + \log(17.86) = 6.1 + 1.252 = 7.35$. Without compensation ($P_{\text{CO}_2} = 40$) the ratio would be $12.5$ and the pH $7.20$, so hyperventilation brings the pH almost back to the normal range.

prompt: $40.0\ \text{mL}$ of $0.150\ \text{M}$ acetic acid ($K_a = 1.8\times10^{-5}$) is titrated with $0.100\ \text{M}$ NaOH. Find the pH at the equivalence point.
answer: 8.761
check: 14 + log10(math.sqrt(1.0e-14 / 1.8e-5 * (0.150 * 0.040) / (0.040 + 0.150 * 0.040 / 0.100)))
tol: 0.02
hint: First find the volume of NaOH needed and the total volume at equivalence.
hint: At equivalence all the acid is acetate: $[\text{A}^-] = 6.00\times10^{-3}\ \text{mol}/0.100\ \text{L} = 0.0600\ \text{M}$. Use $K_b = K_w/K_a$.
solution: Moles of acid $= 0.150 \times 0.0400 = 6.00\times10^{-3}$, so $60.0\ \text{mL}$ of base is needed and the total volume is $100.0\ \text{mL}$. $[\text{A}^-] = 0.0600\ \text{M}$, $K_b = 5.56\times10^{-10}$, $[\text{OH}^-] = \sqrt{(5.56\times10^{-10})(0.0600)} = 5.77\times10^{-6}\ \text{M}$, $\text{pOH} = 5.24$, and $\text{pH} = 8.76$.

@exam self
prompt: A previously healthy man has severe diarrhea for two days and loses large amounts of bicarbonate-rich fluid. His arterial blood shows $[\text{HCO}_3^-] = 14\ \text{mEq/L}$ and a high respiratory rate. Using the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation and Le Chatelier's principle, explain the effect on pH and why hyperventilation is the expected compensation. Then state why the compensation is only partial, and name what the kidneys would do over the following days.
hint: Write the ratio $[\text{HCO}_3^-]/(0.03P_{\text{CO}_2})$ and ask which part changes first and which part the lungs can change.
solution: Loss of bicarbonate lowers the numerator of the ratio $[\text{HCO}_3^-]/(0.03P_{\text{CO}_2})$, and with $P_{\text{CO}_2}$ unchanged, the log term falls, so pH falls (metabolic acidosis; at $P_{\text{CO}_2} = 40$ the pH would be $6.1 + \log(14/1.2) = 7.17$). The acidemia stimulates chemoreceptors and raises ventilation. Exhaling more $\text{CO}_2$ removes a reactant from $\text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{H}^+ + \text{HCO}_3^-$ (via carbonic acid), shifting the system toward $\text{CO}_2$ and consuming $\text{H}^+$. Lowering $P_{\text{CO}_2}$ shrinks the denominator and moves the ratio back toward $20{:}1$. Compensation is partial because the respiratory drive that lowers $P_{\text{CO}_2}$ is limited by the stimulus and by the work of breathing, and the ratio is restored toward but not to normal. Over days the kidneys excrete more acid as ammonium and regenerate bicarbonate, correcting the primary deficit.
rubric: Explains that lower $[\text{HCO}_3^-]$ lowers the base-to-acid ratio and the pH.
rubric: Uses Le Chatelier's principle: removing $\text{CO}_2$ shifts the equilibrium and consumes $\text{H}^+$.
rubric: Says that hyperventilation lowers $P_{\text{CO}_2}$ and returns the ratio toward $20{:}1$, but only partially.
rubric: States that the kidneys excrete acid and regenerate bicarbonate over days.
