@article
After deep brain stimulation, a patient with Parkinson's disease can move again and says that the stimulator gave back the person he used to be. Another patient with the same treatment says, "I do not feel like myself." Both statements are reports of experience, and both matter. How should we think about treatments that change brain function and sometimes mood, motivation or personality?

### A history to learn from

In the 1930s and 1940s, prefrontal **leucotomy** (lobotomy), which cuts connections in the frontal lobes, was introduced by Moniz, who received the 1949 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, and spread widely in several countries, in some cases in a simplified form done outside operating rooms. Many patients were operated on without adequate consent, evidence or follow-up, some were left with serious loss of initiative and judgment, and the procedure declined from the 1950s as drugs and criticism spread. Lessons commonly drawn: demand evidence from controlled studies, protect people who cannot easily refuse, require independent review, and be honest about uncertainty. Modern psychiatric neurosurgery uses small, targeted procedures (such as a cingulotomy or capsulotomy for some people with severe, treatment-resistant obsessive-compulsive disorder or depression) in specialized centers under protocols, with review boards.

### Deep brain stimulation

**Deep brain stimulation** (DBS) is a treatment in which electrodes are implanted in a targeted brain region and connected to a pulse generator, usually placed under the skin of the chest, which delivers electrical stimulation. It was developed for movement disorders, and has become a standard treatment for some people with Parkinson's disease, essential tremor and dystonia whose symptoms are not controlled by drugs. It has been studied for obsessive-compulsive disorder, depression and other conditions; Mayberg and colleagues (2005) reported improvement in a small series of people with treatment-resistant depression with stimulation of the subcallosal cingulate region. Controlled trials for depression have had mixed results, and for psychiatric uses the status of regulatory approval varies, so check the current evidence and rules. Unlike a lesion, stimulation can be adjusted or switched off, though implanted hardware and surgical risks remain. Its mechanism is not fully understood. Decisions about stimulation belong to specialist teams; nothing here is guidance for treatment.

### Ethical issues in psychiatric DBS

**Consent.** People with severe depression or obsessive-compulsive disorder may be desperate, and illness may affect decision-making; capacity must be assessed for this decision, and the therapeutic misconception is a concern. **Evidence.** Sham-controlled trials of surgery raise questions about risk to those in the sham group, while uncontrolled trials cannot separate effects of treatment from expectation. **Obligations after the trial.** Participants who benefit need continued care, battery replacement and a plan if funding ends. **Effects on mood and behavior.** Reports exist of stimulation producing elation or impulsivity, in at least one reported case of Parkinson's disease with conflicting preferences when the stimulation was on and off, and the question arises who decides when the person's judgment is itself changed.

### Identity and authenticity

Some patients report that their sense of self changed after stimulation: in different directions, with some feeling alienated and others feeling restored. Philosophers disagree about what this shows.

**Stimulation threatens identity.** On a view stressing **psychological continuity** (as in Parfit's work on personal identity), a person remains the same through continuous connections of memory, character and intention. Changes in personality that the person did not choose could break these, and if a device alters the motives that guide actions, the actions may not be fully the person's own, raising questions of autonomy and responsibility.

**Stimulation does not threaten identity.** On a **narrative** view, identity is an ongoing story that a person can interpret and revise, and a change is a threat only if the person cannot integrate it. Depression, Parkinson's disease and ordinary life changes (drugs, therapy, aging) also alter personality, and many patients see treatment as restoring a self that illness had hidden. On this view the central test is whether the person endorses the change, not that a device caused it. Baylis and others have argued, with empirical support from patient accounts, that fears of identity loss from DBS have been overstated, while others reply that rare but serious cases still need to be taken seriously and studied over long periods.

Both sides agree that follow-up should include the patient's own account and the views of those close to the patient.

### Numbers in trials

The **Yale–Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale** (Y-BOCS) scores severity from 0 to 40, and a common criterion for response is a reduction of at least 35% from baseline. If a trial compares the share of responders in two arms, the **number needed to treat** is the reciprocal of the absolute difference in response rates.

@example
title: Response on a symptom scale
problem: A participant's Y-BOCS score falls from 30 at baseline to 18 after treatment. What is the percent reduction, and does it meet a 35% response criterion?
step: Compute the change: $30 - 18 = 12$ points.
step: Compute the percent reduction: $12/30 = 0.40$, or 40%.
step: Compare with the criterion: 40% is at least 35%, so the participant is a responder.
step: Interpret: a response is a statistical convention, not proof of a good life; many studies also measure function and quality of life.
answer: A 40% reduction, which meets the response criterion.

@mistakes
- Treating DBS as a lesion. Stimulation can be adjusted or turned off, though surgery, hardware and long-term follow-up remain.
- Treating reports of identity change as proof that stimulation always alters the self, or as proof that fears are baseless. Patient reports differ, and both views have serious defenders.
- Treating response on a symptom scale as the whole benefit. Function, quality of life, side effects and the person's own view also matter.

prompt: A supplied case: a patient, aged 34, was injured in a car accident two years ago. For two years the patient has had sleep–wake cycles and open eyes but has shown no consistent response to commands at the bedside. A research team performs an fMRI imagery test, and the patient shows activity in the expected regions when asked to imagine playing tennis, but not on a second testing session a week later. The family is divided: the patient's partner says the patient once said they would never want to live "like this", and the patient's parent asks that all treatment continue. Analyze this case in about 300 words: what the results do and do not show, the strongest argument for continuing treatment and for considering withdrawal, whose views matter and how to decide, and what additional assessments you would seek.
hint: Consider covert awareness, test limits, the patient's previous wishes, the surrogate's role and repeated structured assessment.
solution: What the results show: one positive imagery test suggests that the patient may be able to understand and follow instructions, so the patient may not be unaware; failure on the second session does not show unawareness, because fluctuation, fatigue or test limits can produce a negative result. They do not show the patient's quality of life or prognosis. Additional assessments: repeated standardized behavioral assessments by trained examiners (such as a coma recovery scale), further imaging or EEG tests, and review of medications and treatable conditions. Strongest argument for continuing: the diagnosis is uncertain, covert awareness is possible, some patients recover, and an irreversible decision should not be made on uncertain evidence. Strongest argument for considering withdrawal: the patient's own values matter and were reported, long periods of unconsciousness or severe disability may be what the patient rejected, and treatment can be a burden without benefit. Whose views matter: the patient's previously stated wishes come first, from reliable evidence about what they said and in what context (the partner's report should be weighed against the parent's, with other witnesses and written records); the legal surrogate decides according to law and the patient's wishes or best interests, and the clinical team informs and supports. Disagreements are best addressed by an ethics consultation and time, not haste. A good answer states its weighing and the strongest objection to it.

@quiz self
prompt: A patient with a movement disorder says after deep brain stimulation, "I feel like a different person." A neurologist says that this is just a side effect to adjust. A philosopher says that it may be a change of identity. Write about 200 words explaining the strongest version of the view that stimulation can threaten personal identity, the strongest version of the view that it does not, and what evidence about the patient would help decide how to respond.
hint: Use psychological continuity and narrative identity, and think about what the patient and those who know them say.
solution: The strongest version of the threat view is that identity depends on continuity of character, memory and motives, and stimulation can alter motives and mood without the person having chosen it, so actions may be less fully their own and the person may feel alienated from their life. The strongest version of the no-threat view is that identity is a narrative that the person can revise, that illness itself changes the self, and that the central question is whether the person can integrate and endorse the change; many patients feel restored, and a feeling of difference need not mean a different person. Evidence that would help: the patient's own account over time, including whether they endorse the change on reflection; accounts from family and friends who knew the patient before; assessment of mood, impulse control and insight; whether adjusting the treatment changes the experience; and whether the patient feels able to make decisions about the treatment. This suggests treating the report as a clinical and ethical signal to explore, not to dismiss or to accept at once.

rubric: Identifies kinds of evidence (patient's endorsement, observers' accounts, assessments, effect of adjustment) that bear on the case.
rubric: Does not treat either view as the only reasonable one.
