@article
Who decides what a brain-reading headset may record, or whether an implant may be marketed, or what a researcher may do with a volunteer's scan? The answer is not one law but many layers, and the layers move at different speeds. This week we map them and compare ways to govern a technology that changes faster than most law.

### Layers of governance

**Constitutional law** sets basic rights; **statutes** and **regulations** create binding rules and agencies; **soft law**, such as recommendations and guidelines from international bodies, is not legally binding but shapes laws and professional conduct; **professional oversight** includes review boards and committees; and **technical standards** and company practices fill gaps. Binding and non-binding instruments interact: a recommendation may be copied into a statute years later.

### Research ethics instruments

The **Declaration of Helsinki** of the World Medical Association was last revised on 19 October 2024 in Helsinki, the first revision since 2013; it now speaks of research participants rather than subjects and stresses community engagement and vulnerability. In the United States, research with people is governed by the **Common Rule** (45 CFR 46; the revised rule's general compliance date was 21 January 2019) and reviewed by institutional review boards; research with animals is reviewed by institutional animal care and use committees under the Animal Welfare Act and Public Health Service policy; the European Union regulates animal research under Directive 2010/63/EU. The 3Rs come from Russell and Burch (1959). In clinical practice, the DSM-5-TR was published in 2022 and the ICD-11 came into effect on 1 January 2022; classifications influence diagnosis, insurance and law, and they are revised, so check the current versions.

### Instruments on neural data and neurotechnology

- **OECD (11 December 2019):** the Council adopted the Recommendation on Responsible Innovation in Neurotechnology, which the OECD calls the first international standard in this field. It has nine principles and is not legally binding. Among its themes are responsible innovation, safety assessment, inclusivity and safeguarding personal brain data.
- **Chile (October 2021):** a constitutional amendment (Law 21.383) protects brain activity and the information drawn from it. In August 2023 Chile's Supreme Court ordered a company to delete a former senator's brain data recorded by a consumer headset.
- **United States:** there is no federal neural data law. By April 2026 four states had enacted protections treating neural data as sensitive in their privacy laws: Colorado (2024), California (2024; in force 1 January 2025), Montana (2025; in force 1 October 2025) and Connecticut (2025; in force 1 July 2026). The MIND Act, introduced in the Senate in September 2025, would direct the Federal Trade Commission to study neural data. More state bills are pending, so check the current list.
- **UNESCO (12 November 2025):** member states adopted at the General Conference the Recommendation on the Ethics of Neurotechnology, the first global standard on the topic. It is not legally binding. It treats neural data as sensitive and calls for protecting mental privacy and freedom of thought, with special care for children and for non-medical uses.

Other existing law applies indirectly. Regulators of medical devices generally require evidence of safety and effectiveness before implanted or treatment devices are marketed for medical claims, whereas consumer "wellness" products often face lighter rules, which is a gap. In the European Union, the General Data Protection Regulation (applying since 2018) gives special protection to certain categories of data such as health data and biometric data used to identify people; it does not mention neural data by name, and whether neural data count depends on the context and the use. In the United States, the federal health privacy law generally covers health care providers, insurers and their business associates, and many consumer device companies fall outside it.

### Three approaches

**New rights and laws specifically for neural data.** Defenders argue that existing categories were not designed for data from the brain, that clear and specific rules give certainty to people and firms, that early action avoids harms that are hard to undo, and that constitutional or statutory neurorights have symbolic and practical force. Critics reply that definitions of neural data are hard to draw and may become outdated, that special rules may cover trivial signals while missing other sensitive data, and that they may impose costs on beneficial medical and research uses.

**Adapting existing law.** Defenders argue that privacy, consumer protection, medical device, anti-discrimination and human rights law already apply and can be interpreted and enforced for neural data, avoiding gaps between overlapping rules. Critics reply that existing law leaves consumer devices and inferences outside its scope, and that enforcement is slow.

**Flexible soft law and self-regulation.** Defenders argue that technology moves faster than legislatures, that principles can be updated, that international standards help where laws differ and that overregulation harms innovation. Critics reply that non-binding standards are not enforceable and industry may not follow them.

A related puzzle is the **Collingridge dilemma** (Collingridge, 1980): when a technology is new its effects are not yet known, and when its effects are known it is entrenched and hard to change. A precautionary approach emphasizes acting early under uncertainty, while a pro-innovation approach emphasizes the cost of delay. Each has strong reasons, and the answer depends on how severe, reversible and likely the harms are.

Remember to check the current text of every instrument: laws, guidelines and classifications change.

@mistakes
- Treating non-binding recommendations as laws. The OECD and UNESCO recommendations are not legally binding, though they influence laws and practice.
- Saying "the law" without a place and a date. Neural data law differs between countries and states and changes quickly.
- Assuming that no law applies because neural data are not named. Privacy, consumer protection, medical device and other law may apply, with gaps.
