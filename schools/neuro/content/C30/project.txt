@project
title: Neuroethics brief on one neurotechnology
overview: Write a 3,000-word analysis of one neurotechnology: a consumer EEG headset, a brain–computer interface or deep brain stimulation for depression. Your brief explains the science, weighs benefits and risks, presents the main ethical positions in their strongest form, states the current law as of the date you write, and ends with a recommendation and its strongest objection.
goal: Explain accurately what the technology does and what the evidence shows, with the limits of that evidence.
goal: Present at least three ethical positions in their strongest versions, without making any one of them the answer by default.
goal: State the current law and standards with their dates and legal force, and tell the reader to check the current text.
goal: Make a recommendation, defend it with reasons and answer its strongest objection.
step: Choose one of the three technologies and write one paragraph that defines it generically, describing the type of device and the problem it addresses, and does not name any company or product.
step: Collect at least six sources (papers, review articles, official texts such as the UNESCO Recommendation, the OECD Recommendation, a state law or a regulator's guidance, and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy). Note for each what it claims, how strong its evidence is and the date you read it.
step: Write the science section (about 600 words): how the device works, the best evidence on benefit and harm with sample sizes and effect sizes where known, and what remains uncertain. Compute at least one number yourself, such as a positive predictive value, a number needed to treat or an information transfer rate, from the supplied material or from a published value, and show the calculation.
step: Write the benefits and risks section (about 500 words) using the four principles (autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence, justice), covering consent, vulnerable groups, long-term obligations, neural data and fairness of access.
step: Write the positions section (about 800 words): present three views in their strongest form with the evidence behind each (for example for headsets: permissive and consumer-choice, protective neurorights, and existing-law-is-enough; for a BCI or DBS: restoration and autonomy, risk and identity, and justice and access), and the strongest reply each can make to the others.
step: Write the law and governance section (about 500 words) using the dated facts in the supplied table and any further law you can verify from an official text. Say which instruments are binding, which are not, and what remains unregulated. Describe laws you cannot verify only in general terms.
step: Write the recommendation section (about 400 words): state one recommendation (for example a policy, a trial design or a design requirement), the reasons, and the strongest objection to it with your reply. Revise the whole brief for accuracy, remove any claim you cannot support, and check word count and citations.

@review
**Week 1: what neuroethics is.** Two branches (ethics of neuroscience; neuroscience of ethics); consequentialism, deontology, virtue ethics; Belmont principles (respect for persons, beneficence, justice); the four principles (autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence, justice); the is–ought gap; positive predictive value depends on base rate.

**Week 2: people in research.** Elements of consent (disclosure, understanding, voluntariness, capacity, authorization); four abilities in capacity (understand, appreciate, reason, express a choice); the therapeutic misconception; IRBs and the Common Rule; Declaration of Helsinki (revised 19 October 2024); incidental findings, expected number $np$ and chance of at least one $1-(1-p)^n$.

**Week 3: animals.** The 3Rs; IACUCs, Animal Welfare Act and Public Health Service policy; Directive 2010/63/EU; interests, rights and benefit views; validity of models; group size $n \approx 2((1.96+0.84)/d)^2$.

**Week 4: BCIs and implants.** Components and recording methods; landmark research systems with their papers; the Wolpaw formula; risks over the life of a device; obligations after a trial; agency and extended mind.

**Week 5: stimulation and enhancement.** TMS and tDCS in general terms; small and inconsistent evidence in healthy people; treatment versus enhancement; autonomy, fairness, coercion and authenticity; probability of superiority $\tfrac12(1+\operatorname{erf}(d/2))$.

**Week 6: mental privacy.** Decoding studies and their limits; neural data; proposed neurorights; the dated measures (UNESCO 12 November 2025; OECD 11 December 2019; Chile 2021 and 2023; US states and the MIND Act); binomial chance probabilities.

**Week 7: the courtroom.** Frye and Daubert; Roper and Graham; group-to-individual inference; reverse inference; base rates; classification accuracy $\Phi(d/2)$.

**Week 8: free will.** Libet (1983), Soon (2008), Schurger (2012); libertarian, hard incompatibilist and compatibilist views; responsibility and law; $z$ test for accuracy.

**Week 9: death and consciousness.** Death by neurologic criteria; UWS, MCS, locked-in syndrome; covert awareness; views on the definition of death; Glasgow Coma Scale.

**Week 10: psychiatric neurosurgery and DBS.** Leucotomy history; DBS in general terms; consent and trials; identity (continuity versus narrative); response and number needed to treat.

**Week 11: governance.** Layers of law; binding and non-binding instruments; three approaches; the Collingridge dilemma.

**Week 12: communication.** Neuromyths and the evidence; seductive allure; exaggeration; multiple comparisons.

**Key formulas.** PPV from sensitivity, specificity and prevalence; $1/\text{ARR}$ for number needed to treat; Wolpaw bits; $1-(1-p)^n$; $\Phi(d/2)$; $m\alpha$ and $\alpha/m$.

**Self-check.** Can you give each side of every debate so that its defenders would agree? Can you state each legal fact with its date and say whether it is binding? Can you compute a PPV by hand?

**Common errors.** Treating a contested view as settled; treating a recommendation as binding law; stating law without a date; forgetting the base rate; treating group differences as individual predictions.

@exam num
prompt: A hypothetical brain-based screening test for a condition has sensitivity 90% and specificity 95%. In the population tested, 2% have the condition. A person tests positive. What is the probability that the person has the condition? Give the answer as a percent to one decimal place.
answer: 26.9
tol: 0.1
unit: %
check: 100 * 0.9 * 0.02 / (0.9 * 0.02 + 0.05 * 0.98)
hint: In 1,000 people, count true positives and false positives, then divide the true positives by all positives.
solution: Of 1,000 people, 20 have the condition and 18 of them test positive. Of the 980 without it, 49 test positive. So $18/(18+49) = 0.269$, or 26.9%. A test that sounds accurate gives a positive result that is wrong most of the time when the condition is uncommon.

@exam self
prompt: Write an essay of about 500 words on this question: should an employer be permitted to ask employees to wear a brain-activity monitor that estimates attention? Present the strongest case for permitting it and the strongest case against, using at least four ideas from the course (for example consent and coercion, the limits of decoding, base rates, mental privacy and neural data law, and enhancement). State the current law with dates and say it must be checked. End with your own judgment and the strongest objection to it.
hint: Plan a paragraph for each side, one for the evidence, one for law, and one for your judgment and its objection.
solution: A strong essay begins by stating what is being asked and what the device can infer: consumer EEG supports limited inferences about states such as attention, and its estimates are noisy, so their use in decisions about workers raises error and fairness problems, and base rates matter if the monitor flags rare conditions. The case for permitting it: workers may consent, monitoring of work is common, the device may help workers manage fatigue and safety in some jobs (such as driving), and a ban is paternalistic if data are protected and use is voluntary. The case against: consent is compromised when an employer asks, because refusal may carry costs; neural data are sensitive, hard to anonymize and may reveal health or mood; false readings could be used unfairly; and mental privacy and freedom of thought are at stake. Law: UNESCO's recommendation (12 November 2025) and the OECD's (11 December 2019) are not legally binding but call for care with neural data, especially in non-medical uses; Chile amended its constitution in 2021 to protect brain activity and information drawn from it; in the United States there is no federal neural data law, and by April 2026 four states (Colorado, California, Montana, Connecticut) had enacted protections, so the answer depends on place, and the current text must be checked. A reasoned judgment might permit voluntary use with data under the worker's control and a ban on employment decisions based on the data, with the strongest objection that voluntariness is hard to guarantee in the workplace; the reply is that independent oversight and penalties for pressure reduce but do not remove the problem. Credit comes from the quality of the reasoning, not from the side chosen.

rubric: Uses at least four course ideas accurately (for example coercion, limits of decoding, base rates, neural data law).
rubric: States the dated legal facts correctly and says that they must be checked.
rubric: Reaches a judgment and states and answers the strongest objection to it.
