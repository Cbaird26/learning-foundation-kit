@article
A young adult has a severe brain injury and lies in an intensive care unit with a ventilator. The eyes are closed and there is no response to voice or pain. Is the patient alive? Is the patient aware? Could the patient recover? Each question has a different answer for different conditions, and the differences matter for families, clinicians and the law.

### Death by neurologic criteria

**Death by neurologic criteria**, often called **brain death**, is the irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem. In the United States, the Uniform Determination of Death Act (1981) provides that an individual is dead who has sustained either irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory functions or irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem. A committee at Harvard Medical School proposed criteria for irreversible coma in 1968. Today, death determined by neurologic criteria is accepted in most countries, with procedures set by national guidelines and law; in the United States a 2023 consensus guideline from several medical societies covers adults and children. The determination is made by qualified clinicians after excluding reversible conditions (such as drugs or severe cold) and after clinical tests for absent brain and brainstem function, with ancillary tests in some cases. Rules differ across places and change, so check current guidelines and laws. A few jurisdictions require some accommodation of families' religious objections.

### Disorders of consciousness

A **coma** is a state of unarousable unresponsiveness: the eyes stay closed. In **unresponsive wakefulness syndrome** (UWS, formerly the vegetative state; Jennett and Plum coined "persistent vegetative state" in 1972), the patient opens the eyes and has sleep–wake cycles but shows only reflexive behavior and no evidence of awareness. In the **minimally conscious state** (MCS, defined by Giacino and colleagues in 2002) there is inconsistent but reproducible behavioral evidence of awareness, such as following a command or purposeful movement. **Locked-in syndrome** is different: the person is conscious but has almost complete paralysis, often with eye movements preserved. The **Glasgow Coma Scale** scores eye opening (1 to 4), verbal response (1 to 5) and motor response (1 to 6), giving a total from 3 to 15.

### Covert awareness

Behavior is the usual basis for diagnosis, but a person may be aware without being able to show it. Owen and colleagues (2006) asked a patient who met the criteria for the vegetative state to imagine playing tennis or walking through the rooms of a house; fMRI showed activity in regions that were also active in healthy volunteers doing the same imagery. Monti and colleagues (2010) tested 54 patients with similar tasks and found that 5 could willfully modulate their brain activity on command. These results show that some patients who appear unresponsive can understand and follow instructions. They also raise the issue of **misdiagnosis**: studies using standardized behavioral assessments found that diagnosis at the bedside, without repeated structured examination, missed signs of awareness in a substantial number of patients. Limits: the tests need preserved hearing, language and attention, a negative result does not show unconsciousness, and the methods are not yet standard everywhere. As of 2026 there is no consensus on how consciousness arises or on what test would settle it.

### The definition of death

**The whole-brain view** holds that death is the loss of the organism's integrated functioning, which the irreversible loss of all brain function marks. **The higher-brain view** holds that what matters about a person is the capacity for consciousness, so permanent loss of that capacity is death of the person, which would include patients in permanent unconsciousness who still breathe. Critics say it makes death depend on a philosophical view of personhood that many do not share. **Critics of brain death** (among them Shewmon, who described patients who maintained some bodily functions for long periods) argue that the whole-brain criterion is not met by some patients who are declared dead, since some integrating functions, such as hormonal regulation, can persist, and that the real justification is to allow organ donation. Defenders reply that the loss of the brain's integrative role is what matters, that the described functions are limited and do not amount to the integration of an organism, and that determination requires careful testing. Many policy makers keep the present criteria because they are widely accepted and clear, while debating the philosophy.

### Decisions about care

Questions of withdrawing life-sustaining treatment for patients in UWS or MCS involve surrogate decisions based on the patient's known wishes or best interests. In the United States, *Cruzan v. Director, Missouri Department of Health* (1990) was a Supreme Court decision that assumed a competent person has a constitutionally protected liberty interest in refusing unwanted treatment and held that a state may require clear and convincing evidence of an incompetent patient's wishes. Arguments for **continuing** treatment stress uncertainty of prognosis and diagnosis, the possibility of covert awareness and the value of every life. Arguments for **withdrawal** stress the patient's own values, the burdens of prolonged treatment and that survival without awareness may not benefit the person. Clinical decisions are for clinicians, patients and families together.

@example
title: Cases missed by a test
problem: In a hypothetical group of 200 patients who are behaviorally unresponsive, 12% have covert awareness. A brain-imaging test detects covert awareness in 60% of those who have it. How many patients with covert awareness does the test miss?
step: Number with covert awareness: $200 \times 0.12 = 24$.
step: Number detected: $24 \times 0.60 = 14.4$.
step: Number missed: $24 - 14.4 = 9.6$, or $24 \times 0.40$.
step: Interpret: a negative result cannot show the absence of awareness, because roughly 4 in 10 who have it are missed, so a negative test must be interpreted with caution.
answer: About 9.6 patients are missed.

@mistakes
- Treating brain death as the same as coma or the vegetative state. In death by neurologic criteria all brain function, including the brainstem, has irreversibly ceased; patients in coma or UWS have some brain function and are not declared dead.
- Treating a negative imaging or EEG result as proof of unconsciousness. The tests miss some people with awareness.
- Treating the philosophical views of death as settled. Whole-brain, higher-brain and critical views each have serious defenders, while legal criteria are set by law.

@practice self
prompt: Compare the whole-brain view and the higher-brain view of the definition of death. Present the strongest case for each, give the strongest objection to each, and say what each view implies for a patient in permanent unconsciousness who breathes without a ventilator. State, without choosing a side, which facts or values the dispute turns on.
hint: Consider what each view treats as the essential function and what each does to organ donation, families and the law.
solution: The whole-brain view says that death is the irreversible loss of the organism's integrated functioning, marked by loss of all functions of the entire brain. Its strongest case is that it is clear, widely accepted in law and medicine, and tracks a biological change that most people see as the end of a life. Its strongest objection is that some patients meeting the criteria retain limited integrative functions, so the criteria may not capture the theory behind them. The higher-brain view says that what matters about a person is the capacity for consciousness, so its permanent loss is death of the person. Its strongest case is that it fits the intuition that a person who can never again be conscious has ended, whatever the body does. Its strongest objection is that it declares breathing patients dead against the views of many families and the public, and it depends on a view of personhood that is disputed. For a patient in permanent unconsciousness who breathes unaided, the whole-brain view says the patient is alive, so decisions about care are decisions about a living patient, while the higher-brain view would say the person has died. The dispute turns on whether death is an event for the organism or for the person, on how much integration is enough, and on how far law should follow shared convictions. Credit comes from the quality of the reasoning, not from the side chosen.
