@article
Press a button whenever you feel like it. You feel that the decision is yours. Now suppose that a brain signal begins to rise before you are aware of deciding. Does that show the feeling of choosing is an illusion? This puzzle sits at the meeting point of neuroscience, philosophy and law.

### Libet's experiment

Libet and colleagues (1983) asked participants to flex a wrist at a time of their own choosing while watching a spot move around a clock face (one revolution took about 2.56 seconds). Participants reported where the spot was when they first felt the urge to move (called W). Electrodes recorded the **readiness potential** (RP), a slow negative shift in the EEG over the motor areas that precedes voluntary movement and had been described earlier. For unplanned movements, the RP began on average about 550 ms before the movement; W was reported about 200 ms before it. So the RP began about 350 ms before the reported awareness of the urge. Libet suggested that the brain "initiates" voluntary acts unconsciously, and that consciousness might still veto an act in the short time left.

### Criticisms and later work

The result has been questioned on several grounds. Reports of the time of the urge, made by watching a clock, are open to error and bias (the time the urge is judged to occur can be affected by later events). The RP is found by averaging the EEG backward from the moment of movement over many trials, which leaves out trials in which an RP-like drift did not lead to movement. Schurger, Sitt and Dehaene (2012) proposed that the RP may reflect a **stochastic accumulator**: random fluctuations in neural activity drift upward until they cross a threshold and trigger movement, so that averaged backward from the crossing, the signal appears to ramp up, without a decision taking place earlier. On this account the RP is not a sign of an unconscious decision.

Soon and colleagues (2008) used fMRI while participants chose to press a left or right button. Patterns of activity in frontopolar and parietal cortex carried information about the upcoming choice several seconds before the participants reported awareness of deciding, but predicted the choice with an accuracy of only about 60%, where 50% is chance. Even if one accepts the measurements, the results show that brain activity before a choice is statistically related to it, not that the choice was fixed.

### Philosophical positions

Whether we have free will depends on what it is, and informed philosophers disagree.

**Libertarianism** holds that free will requires that our choices are not determined by prior causes, so that we could have done otherwise in the very same circumstances, and that we have it. Defenders appeal to the experience of agency and to accounts in which agents are the source of their actions, and argue that without it, responsibility is lost. Critics ask how indeterminism, which only adds chance, could increase control.

**Hard incompatibilism** holds that free will in the sense required for deserving blame is incompatible with both determinism and indeterminism, and we lack it. Defenders argue that we are not the ultimate source of our character and circumstances. Critics reply that the demand for ultimate sourcehood is too strong.

**Compatibilism** holds that free will and responsibility are compatible with determinism because freedom consists in acting from one's own reasons and values, with a capacity to respond to reasons. Frankfurt (1969) argued that responsibility does not require the ability to do otherwise, and Strawson (1962) argued that our practice of holding people responsible rests on reactive attitudes such as resentment and gratitude that are not undermined by determinism. Critics argue that this changes the subject from the kind of freedom that matters.

### Responsibility and the law

Greene and Cohen (2004) argued that neuroscience will increase the pressure on retributive intuitions by showing mechanisms behind choice, favoring a forward-looking approach to punishment such as deterrence and protection. Morse and others reply that law requires rationality and control, not metaphysical freedom, and that explaining a cause of behavior does not excuse it: all behavior has causes, so causation alone excuses no one. Experiments on the effects of belief in free will on behavior have given mixed results on replication, so claims about what happens if people lose that belief should be held cautiously.

### Statistics of prediction

If a decoder predicts a binary choice on $n$ independent trials with observed accuracy $\hat p$, the test statistic against chance ($p = 0.5$) is $z = (\hat p - 0.5)/\sqrt{0.25/n}$. A large $z$ means accuracy above chance, but accuracy slightly above 50% is far from determining the choice.

@example
title: Is 60% accuracy above chance?
problem: A decoder predicts a binary choice with accuracy $\hat p = 0.60$ on $n = 100$ independent trials. Compute the $z$ statistic against chance and say what the result does and does not show.
step: The standard error under chance is $\sqrt{0.25/100} = 0.05$.
step: Then $z = (0.60 - 0.50)/0.05 = 2.0$.
step: A $z$ of 2.0 corresponds to a two-sided p-value of about 0.046, so accuracy is above chance at the 0.05 level.
step: But the decoder is wrong on 40% of trials, so the brain activity is only weakly predictive; the result does not show that choices are determined, and that conclusion would also need a philosophical argument about what freedom requires.
answer: $z = 2.0$: accuracy is above chance, but modest.

@mistakes
- Treating the readiness potential as a proven unconscious decision. Its interpretation is disputed, and an accumulator account explains it without one.
- Treating a prediction that is only somewhat better than chance as proof that choices are determined.
- Assuming that "free will does not exist" is the scientific consensus or that the opposite is. Both free will and its absence are positions held for philosophical reasons, and the evidence leaves the question open.

The strongest argument for a reduction is that responsibility depends on the capacity to control behavior, that documented impairment lowers culpability for the same reason that age and incapacity do, and that sentencing should reflect culpability. The strongest argument against is that many people with similar damage do not offend, that damage may also raise the risk of future violence and so support longer incapacitation, that his behavior may show planning and understanding, and that reduced control is a matter of degree for which scans are poor evidence. A compatibilist would ask whether the damage reduced the capacity to respond to reasons enough to lower responsibility, treating this as an empirical question about capacities, while a hard incompatibilist, who denies desert in general, might say that all offenders are in a similar position and shift the question to protection, deterrence and treatment, in which the risk the damage poses matters. A good analysis states the weighing and its strongest objection.
