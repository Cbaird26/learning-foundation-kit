@article
Students sit an exam; some have slept well, some have had coffee, a few have taken a prescription drug that was not prescribed to them, and a few have used a device that passes a weak current through the scalp. Which of these is acceptable? Why do we treat some ways of improving performance as normal and others as cheating or worse? This week we separate what stimulation and drugs can be shown to do from the harder question of what we may permit.

### What stimulation does

**Transcranial magnetic stimulation** (TMS) uses a brief magnetic pulse from a coil outside the head to induce a small electrical current in the cortex beneath it, which can excite neurons or, with some patterns, reduce their excitability for a time. It is used in research (a temporary, reversible "virtual lesion" to test whether a region is needed for a task, a causal method) and, delivered by clinicians, as a treatment for some people with depression who have not responded to medication. **Transcranial direct current stimulation** (tDCS) passes a weak current between electrodes on the scalp and is thought to shift the excitability of neurons beneath them. It is cheap and easy to build, so do-it-yourself use exists, which raises safety questions because the intensity, duration, electrode placement and the person's condition matter. As a matter of safety, this course does not give settings or instructions; use of stimulation is a matter for researchers under ethical review and for clinicians. Reviews of studies in healthy adults have generally found small and inconsistent effects of tDCS on cognitive tasks, with concerns about small samples and variable methods. The same cautions apply to other cognitive enhancers: the effects of stimulant drugs on healthy people are modest and vary across tasks and individuals. As of 2026, then, claims of large enhancement should be treated with skepticism.

### Treatment and enhancement

**Treatment** aims to restore normal function in a person with a disease or disability; **enhancement** aims to improve function beyond what is typical. The distinction guides much policy, since medicine's mission is usually described as treating illness. But the line is hard to draw: a vaccine protects healthy people, education improves normal capacities, and normal variation in memory or attention is continuous with what we call disorder. A definition of "normal" can build in social judgment, and what counts as a disorder has changed over time. Critics of the distinction say that the question is really about risks, benefits and fairness in each case; defenders say that it still marks a morally relevant difference between healing and improvement.

### The debate

**For permitting enhancement.** Defenders argue from **autonomy**: adults should be free to alter their own minds if they accept the risks, as they do with caffeine, education and training. They argue from **continuity**: we already improve cognition by many means, and there is no principled line between acceptable and unacceptable ones except safety and fairness. They note possible benefits to individuals and society (Harris argues that we have reason to welcome safe enhancement), and that a ban would be hard to enforce.

**For restriction or caution.** Critics raise four concerns. **Safety and evidence**: long-term effects are unknown, and benefits are small relative to risks for healthy people. **Fairness**: if enhancements are costly, they may increase advantages for the rich; and if the benefit is **positional** (a good that matters only relative to others, such as rank in a competition), then when everyone improves no one gains in rank while all bear costs. **Coercion**: if some use enhancers, others may feel pressure to follow, so choice is less free than it seems, especially in schools and workplaces. **Authenticity and human nature**: Sandel argues that a focus on mastery erodes an appreciation of life as a gift and undermines humility and solidarity, and Fukuyama argues that human nature underpins equal rights. Replies: these concerns also apply to education and wealth, which we accept, and an appeal to nature or giftedness needs an account of why it limits what people choose.

The debate does not turn on facts alone, but facts matter: the size of effects, the risks, and who would use enhancers.

### Effects in numbers

A standardized effect size $d$ is the difference in group means divided by the common standard deviation. If two normal distributions with equal spread differ by $d$, the probability that a randomly chosen member of the enhanced group scores higher than a randomly chosen member of the other group (the **probability of superiority**) is $\Phi(d/\sqrt 2) = \tfrac12\left(1 + \operatorname{erf}(d/2)\right)$. For $d = 0.2$ this is $\tfrac12(1 + \operatorname{erf}(0.1)) \approx 0.556$: even a real average effect leaves the two distributions heavily overlapping.

@example
title: What a small average effect means for one person
problem: A hypothetical enhancer raises scores on a task by $d = 0.3$ standard deviations on average, with equal spread in the two groups. What is the probability that a randomly chosen person who used it scores higher than a randomly chosen person who did not? And what percentile would a person who would otherwise score at the 50th percentile reach?
step: Probability of superiority: $\tfrac12(1 + \operatorname{erf}(d/2)) = \tfrac12(1 + \operatorname{erf}(0.15))$.
step: With $\operatorname{erf}(0.15) \approx 0.168$, this is $\tfrac12 (1.168) \approx 0.584$.
step: Percentile: a person at the mean shifts by $0.3$ standard deviations, so their new percentile is $\Phi(0.3)$.
step: $\Phi(0.3) \approx 0.618$, so the person moves from the 50th to about the 62nd percentile.
answer: About 0.584 for the probability of superiority, and about the 62nd percentile.

@mistakes
- Treating a statistically significant effect as a large one. A small standardized effect can be significant in a large sample and still leave most individuals unchanged.
- Treating the treatment–enhancement line as clear and decisive, or as meaningless. It is contested, and both sides have serious arguments.
- Treating positional benefits as if they were absolute. When all students improve equally, class rank does not change.

@practice self
prompt: A university is considering a policy on students' use of non-prescribed stimulant drugs to improve exam performance. Write a balanced analysis in about 250 words: the strongest argument for allowing such use, the strongest argument for prohibiting it, the empirical facts on which the question depends, and a reply each side could give to the other.
hint: Think about autonomy, fairness, coercion, safety and the size of the effect.
solution: The strongest argument for allowing it is autonomy and continuity: adults may use caffeine and tutoring to perform better, and a ban cannot be enforced evenly and drives use underground, which is less safe. The strongest argument for prohibiting it combines safety, fairness and coercion: the drugs have risks and may be misused, access is uneven, grades are largely positional, and if some students use them, others may feel pressure to do the same. The empirical facts that matter are the size and consistency of the effect in healthy students (reported to be modest and variable), the risks for people without a medical need, and how many students use them. The pro side can reply that the same fairness worry applies to private tutoring, which is not banned, and that safety is a reason to regulate, not prohibit. The restriction side can reply that the pressure in a competitive grade system is a real harm even if the drug is modest, and that the difference from caffeine is the risk and the need for medical oversight. A good analysis may end by saying which facts would change its conclusion. Credit comes from the quality of reasoning, not from the side chosen.
