@article
Imagine that a research team builds a brain-scan score that predicts, years ahead, which healthy adults will later develop dementia. Suppose that among people who will develop it the score is high 90% of the time (its **sensitivity**), and among people who will not it is low 80% of the time (its **specificity**). Suppose also that 10% of the people scanned will develop the condition (the **base rate**, or prevalence). Out of 1,000 people, 100 will develop it and the score flags 90 of them. Of the 900 who will not, the score wrongly flags $0.20 \times 900 = 180$. A flagged person is therefore right to expect the condition with probability $90/(90+180) = 1/3$. This is the score's **positive predictive value** (PPV). Should the team tell people their scores? Should an employer or an insurer be allowed to ask? Neuroscience supplies the numbers in this puzzle, but it does not by itself answer the questions. That gap is where neuroethics lives.

### Two branches

**Neuroethics** is the study of the ethical, legal and social questions raised by brain science and its technologies. It is usually divided into two branches. The **ethics of neuroscience** asks what we may and may not do in studying and altering brains: how to treat research participants and animals, what to do with a scan that reveals a tumor, whether to enhance healthy brains, who owns neural data. The **neuroscience of ethics** asks what brain research can tell us about moral thinking itself: which processes are engaged when people judge, choose and feel responsible, and whether such findings should change how we think about morality and the law. The two interact. A finding about how people attribute responsibility (the second branch) matters for how courts should treat brain evidence (the first).

### Tools for reasoning

Three families of moral theory recur. **Consequentialism** judges actions by their outcomes (a study is justified if its expected benefits exceed its harms). **Deontology** holds that some duties and rights constrain what may be done whatever the outcome (a person may not be used merely as a means). **Virtue ethics** asks what a person of good character would do. Each captures something the others struggle with, and informed philosophers defend all three.

Bioethics also uses principles that are more portable than any theory. The **Belmont Report** (1979), a United States statement on research with people, names three: **respect for persons** (autonomy, and protection for those with diminished autonomy), **beneficence** (maximize possible benefits and minimize possible harms) and **justice** (a fair distribution of the burdens and benefits of research). The influential framework of Beauchamp and Childress lists four: respect for **autonomy**, **beneficence**, **nonmaleficence** (do not harm) and **justice**. Principles often conflict. Returning a risk score may respect autonomy and still cause anxiety, and withholding it may protect against harm while being paternalistic.

### What makes brain science special

Three features recur in later weeks. The brain is closely tied to what people regard as themselves, so interventions raise questions of identity and authenticity. Brain data may reveal information that a person has not chosen to share. And brain technologies can restore function or can enhance it, so the line between treatment and enhancement becomes a question.

### From is to ought

Facts about the brain do not by themselves entail moral conclusions: that a region is active during a moral judgment does not show the judgment is right or wrong. This standard caution is called the **is–ought gap**. Neuroscience can still matter to ethics in at least two ways: it can correct empirical assumptions on which a moral argument relies (for example, about what a brain scan can show), and it can change the facts that principles are applied to (for example, a new intervention that was not possible before). Both uses require the careful reading of evidence taught in earlier courses, including the difference between forward and reverse inference, correlation and causation, and group averages and individual cases.

@example
title: How many people must be treated for one extra complication?
problem: A procedure under study raises the risk of a serious complication from 12% in a comparison group to 20% in the treated group. How many people must undergo the procedure for one additional complication to be expected? Treat the risks as fixed probabilities.
step: Compute the absolute increase in risk: $0.20 - 0.12 = 0.08$, or 8 percentage points.
step: The number of people per extra event is the reciprocal of the absolute difference: $1/0.08 = 12.5$.
step: Interpret: among 12.5 people treated we expect one more complication than among 12.5 people not treated. In practice we say about 13 people, and compare this number with the number needed to benefit.
answer: 12.5 people (this is called the number needed to harm).

@mistakes
- Treating a high sensitivity as proof that a positive result is probably correct. The probability that a positive result is right also depends on the specificity and on the base rate.
- Concluding that a neuroscientific finding settles a moral question. The is–ought gap means that an argument also needs a premise about values.
- Treating one principle as always decisive. Autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence and justice can conflict, and applying them takes judgment about the case.

@practice num
prompt: A brain-based screening test has sensitivity 85% and specificity 90%. In the group screened, 20% have the condition. A person tests positive. What is the probability that the person has the condition? Give the answer as a percent to one decimal place.
answer: 68.0
tol: 0.1
unit: %
check: 100 * 0.85 * 0.20 / (0.85 * 0.20 + 0.10 * 0.80)
hint: In 1,000 people, count the true positives and the false positives, then divide the true positives by all positives.
solution: Of 1,000 people, 200 have the condition and 170 of them test positive. Of the 800 without it, 80 test positive. So $170/(170+80) = 0.68$, or 68.0%.

@practice self
prompt: A research group has a scan-based score that predicts later dementia, with sensitivity 90%, specificity 80%, and a positive predictive value of one third in the population studied. Using the four principles of autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence and justice, give the strongest case for telling participants their scores and the strongest case for withholding them. Then say which fact about the score matters most to your own judgment and why.
hint: Give each side its best argument before you choose. Consider what a positive score means when two of three positives will not develop the condition.
solution: The case for disclosure rests on autonomy: it is information about a person's own body that they may want for planning, and withholding it treats adults as unable to handle the truth. Beneficence supports it if early planning, lifestyle changes or research enrollment help, and justice supports offering the same option to everyone. The case for withholding rests on nonmaleficence: two thirds of the people with a positive score will not develop the condition, so many would carry anxiety, insurance or employment risks and possible changes in how others treat them for nothing, and no proven treatment may exist to offset this. Autonomy can support withholding too if participants were never told they might receive results and did not agree to hear them. A reasonable resolution might offer results only to those who chose in advance to receive them, with counseling that explains the one-in-three figure. The fact that matters most may be the positive predictive value, because it determines how many people are harmed by false alarms, but a reader could instead stress the lack of an effective intervention. Credit comes from the quality of the reasoning, not from the side chosen.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which of these belong to the standard caution about moving from neuroscience to ethics?
choice*: Facts about which regions are active during a moral judgment do not by themselves show that the judgment is right or wrong.
choice*: Neuroscience can still bear on ethics by correcting empirical assumptions in a moral argument.
choice: Because of the is–ought gap, empirical findings can never be relevant to any ethical argument.
choice: A brain region's activation during a judgment shows that the judgment is an error.
hint: The caution is about what a fact can show on its own, not about whether facts are ever relevant.
solution: The is–ought gap says that value conclusions need value premises, so activation alone does not show a judgment is right or wrong. It does not make facts irrelevant: facts about what a method can show or what an intervention does feed into ethical arguments. Saying that activation shows error is the very move the caution warns against.

@quiz num
prompt: Each of 40 participants in a study has an independent 2% chance of a minor adverse event. What is the probability that at least one participant has an adverse event? Give the answer as a decimal to three decimal places.
answer: 0.554
tol: 0.001
check: 1 - 0.98 ** 40
hint: Compute the probability that nobody has an event, and subtract it from 1.
solution: The probability that nobody has an event is $0.98^{40} \approx 0.446$, so at least one occurs with probability $1 - 0.446 = 0.554$. Small per-person risks add up across many participants.
