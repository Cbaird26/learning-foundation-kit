@article
A headband that records brain activity is sold for focus training. The company stores the recordings. Could they be used to infer a person's moods, health or political views? Could a scanner read a private thought? The honest answer is that current methods are limited, yet the data are sensitive enough that governments have begun to legislate.

### What decoding can and cannot do

Kay and colleagues (2008) showed that from functional MRI responses in early visual cortex, a model could identify which of 120 novel natural images a participant had viewed far above chance, which is 1 in 120. Later work reconstructed approximate pictures or movies from viewing. Tang, Lebel, Jain and Huth (2023) used fMRI responses to reconstruct the gist of continuous language that a person heard, imagined or watched in a silent movie, not the exact words. Their decoders required many hours of scanning for each participant, a decoder trained on one person did not work for another, and participants could reduce decoding by using strategies such as thinking about something else. Taken together, these studies show real but bounded decoding: it needs cooperation and costly data from a person in a scanner, works best on stimuli the person is perceiving, and recovers general content with errors. Consumer EEG devices record far noisier signals and support much more limited inferences about states such as drowsiness or attention. Decoding methods improve, however, and laws made for today's methods may need to cover tomorrow's. Both overstating and understating what can be done are mistakes.

### Neural data

**Neural data** are information about the activity of a person's nervous system, and, in many laws, inferences drawn from it. Reasons for treating them as sensitive: they may reveal health conditions, emotional states or intentions, they are hard to anonymize because they can identify an individual, and a person may not know what is in them. Reasons for caution about special treatment: much information that is as sensitive (genetic data, location, typing patterns) is also collected, and the line between neural data and other physiological data is unclear.

### Proposed neurorights

Ienca and Andorno (2017) proposed four new rights: **cognitive liberty**, **mental privacy**, **mental integrity** and **psychological continuity**. Yuste and colleagues (2017) set out ethical priorities that include privacy and consent, agency and identity, augmentation and bias. Two broad views:

**New rights are needed.** Defenders argue that existing rights were written before technologies that could record or alter mental processes, that a distinct right makes protection clearer and harder to erode, and that the special relation of the brain to the self warrants a distinct category.

**Existing rights are enough.** Critics argue that freedom of thought, privacy, bodily integrity and data protection already cover these cases, that adding rights risks confusion and lowers the value of existing ones, and that the better course is to interpret and apply the rights we have to brain data.

### Current measures (check the current text)

Laws and standards change quickly in this area, so check the current text of each.

- **UNESCO.** On 12 November 2025 the General Conference adopted the Recommendation on the Ethics of Neurotechnology, the first global standard on the topic. It is not legally binding. It treats neural data as sensitive and calls for protecting mental privacy and freedom of thought, with special care for children and for non-medical uses.
- **OECD.** On 11 December 2019 the OECD Council adopted the Recommendation on Responsible Innovation in Neurotechnology, which the OECD calls the first international standard in this field. It sets out nine principles and is not legally binding either.
- **Chile.** In October 2021 Chile amended its constitution (Law 21.383) to protect brain activity and the information drawn from it. In August 2023 Chile's Supreme Court ordered a company to delete a former senator's brain data recorded by a consumer headset.
- **United States.** There is no federal neural data law. By April 2026 four states had enacted protections treating neural data as sensitive in their privacy laws: Colorado (2024), California (2024; in force 1 January 2025), Montana (2025; in force 1 October 2025) and Connecticut (2025; in force 1 July 2026). A federal bill, the MIND Act, introduced in the Senate in September 2025, would direct the Federal Trade Commission to study neural data. More state bills are pending.

### Numbers in decoding

If a decoder chooses among $N$ equally likely options, chance accuracy is $1/N$. If it is correct on $k$ of $n$ independent trials when chance is $p$, the probability of doing at least this well by luck is $\sum_{j=k}^{n}\binom{n}{j}p^j(1-p)^{n-j}$.

@example
title: Could a decoder be lucky?
problem: A decoder chooses which of 4 categories a person is thinking about. On 12 independent trials it is right 9 times. Chance accuracy is $p = 0.25$. What is the probability of getting 9 or more correct by chance?
step: The number correct under chance is binomial with $n = 12$ and $p = 0.25$.
step: Compute each term: $P(9) = \binom{12}{9}0.25^9 0.75^3$, $P(10)$, $P(11)$ and $P(12)$ in the same way.
step: $P(9) \approx 0.000\,354$, $P(10) \approx 0.000\,035$, $P(11) \approx 0.000\,002$, and $P(12) \approx 0.000\,00006$.
step: The sum is about $0.000\,39$, so a result this good by luck alone is very unlikely.
answer: About 0.0004, so the decoder's performance is very unlikely to be due to chance (the test does not show that its inferences are useful or accurate beyond this).

@mistakes
- Treating all brain data as equivalent to thought reading. Current decoding needs cooperation and costly data, recovers gist, and works best on perceived content.
- Treating UNESCO's and the OECD's recommendations as binding laws. They are not legally binding.
- Stating the legal situation without a date. This area changes quickly, so say as of when, and check the current text.
