@article
"We use only ten percent of our brains." Many people believe it; many films assume it. Yet the claim is false. How does a false claim about the brain spread, and what should scientists, journalists and teachers do about it?

### Common neuromyths

**The ten percent myth.** Imaging shows activity throughout the brain over a day, damage to almost any region has consequences, and the brain uses a large share of the body's energy for its weight (roughly a fifth of resting energy use, a figure commonly cited). There is no unused ninety percent.

**Learning styles.** Pashler and colleagues (2008) reviewed the evidence for the idea that people learn better when teaching is matched to their preferred style (visual, auditory, kinesthetic). They found that people do report preferences, but the studies needed to show a benefit of matching, which assign people to matched or mismatched instruction and test them on the same material, did not support it. Surveys of teachers (Dekker and colleagues, 2012) found that belief in matching was widespread.

**Left-brain and right-brain people.** The hemispheres differ in some functions (language is often left-lateralized), but Nielsen and colleagues (2013) analyzed resting-state fMRI from over a thousand participants and found no evidence that individuals are generally dominated by one hemisphere's networks.

**The Mozart effect.** Rauscher, Shaw and Ky (1993) reported that college students did slightly better on a spatial reasoning task right after listening to a Mozart sonata than after other conditions. Later studies found small or no effects and suggested explanations such as arousal and mood, and the claim that Mozart raises children's intelligence is not supported.

**Brain training.** Owen and colleagues (2010) had more than 11,000 people take part in six weeks of online brain-training tasks. Performance improved on the trained tasks but there was no evidence of improvement on untrained tasks, so claims of general transfer were not supported. Research on other training methods continues, and the question of transfer under other conditions remains debated.

### Why neuroscience persuades

**Seductive allure.** Weisberg and colleagues (2008) found that adding irrelevant neuroscience information made poor explanations of psychological phenomena more satisfying to novices and students, though not to experts. McCabe and Castel (2008) reported that brain images made reasoning in a short article seem more convincing. Later studies found that the effect of images alone is small or absent in several attempts, so the safest summary is that neuroscience wording can lend persuasive weight to weak claims and that the role of images in particular is debated.

**Exaggeration in the news.** Sumner and colleagues (2014) examined health-related press releases and news and found that exaggerated advice or causal claims in news stories were strongly associated with similar exaggeration in the press release from the university. Stories matched the claims of the release, not just the paper, so responsibility is shared.

**Statistical traps.** Many of the problems in earlier weeks reappear: correlation presented as causation, reverse inference ("the pleasure center lit up"), group differences presented as individual traits, and uncorrected multiple comparisons. A well-known demonstration by Bennett and colleagues (2009) found apparent "activity" in a dead salmon when thousands of voxels were tested without correction. If $m$ independent tests are each made at significance level $\alpha$ when no true effect exists, the expected number of false positives is $m\alpha$, and a Bonferroni correction uses $\alpha/m$ for each test to keep the chance of any false positive at most $\alpha$.

### Communicating well

Good practice for scientists, press officers and writers: state the study design and sample; separate correlation from cause; give effect sizes and uncertainty, not only "significant"; say what the study cannot show; avoid headlines that name a brain region as the "center" of a complex trait; avoid language that implies that a brain explanation removes responsibility; and give the date of any law, guideline or product claim, with a reminder to check it. A debate remains. One view holds that simplification is needed to reach the public, and that insisting on every caveat leaves the field to those who make bolder claims. Another holds that scientists have a duty to resist distortion, because public trust, policy and individual choices (about education, health and the law) rest on what is said. Both can agree that a clear message can be accurate if it is chosen with care.

### A policy brief

Many readers of neuroscience are policy makers. A **policy brief** is a short document for a decision maker: it states the problem, summarizes the evidence with its limits, describes options and their trade-offs, and recommends one, with the main objection. Brevity does not excuse inaccuracy.

@example
title: Expected false positives without correction
problem: A brain-imaging analysis tests 60,000 voxels, each at $\alpha = 0.001$ with no true effect anywhere. How many false-positive voxels are expected, and what per-voxel threshold would a Bonferroni correction use to keep the chance of any false positive at 0.05?
step: Expected false positives: $m\alpha = 60{,}000 \times 0.001 = 60$.
step: Bonferroni threshold: $\alpha/m = 0.05/60{,}000 \approx 8.33 \times 10^{-7}$.
step: Interpret: without correction, a map with dozens of "active" voxels can arise from noise alone; with correction, the threshold is far stricter, at a cost in power.
answer: About 60 false positives are expected, and the corrected threshold is about $8.3 \times 10^{-7}$ per voxel.

@mistakes
- Believing that a claim is true because it is stated in neuroscientific language or accompanied by a brain image.
- Treating a finding that one training task improved performance on that task as evidence that general intelligence improved.
- Treating one failed or one successful study as the final word. Evidence on contested effects, such as brain training, is judged across studies.

prompt: Write a policy brief of about 300 words for a legislator who is deciding whether to regulate consumer EEG headsets that collect brain data. Include: the problem, what the science can and cannot infer from such data, the current law as of the course date (UNESCO, OECD, Chile, US states and the MIND Act, with the reminder to check the current text), at least two options with their trade-offs, a recommendation and the strongest objection to it.
hint: Keep claims accurate and dated, state limits of decoding, and give the strongest objection to your recommendation.
solution: Problem: consumer headsets record brain activity and companies store the data; users may not know what can be inferred or how it may be used. Science: consumer EEG is noisy and supports limited inferences about states such as drowsiness or attention; research decoding with fMRI has recovered the gist of language and identified images, but required cooperation and many hours of data per person; methods improve, so rules should not rest on today's limits alone. Law as of the course date: UNESCO's Recommendation on the Ethics of Neurotechnology (12 November 2025) and the OECD's Recommendation on Responsible Innovation in Neurotechnology (11 December 2019) are not legally binding; Chile amended its constitution in October 2021 (Law 21.383) to protect brain activity and information drawn from it; in the United States there is no federal neural data law, four states had enacted protections by April 2026 (Colorado, California, Montana, Connecticut) and the MIND Act, introduced in September 2025, would direct the Federal Trade Commission to study neural data; more bills are pending, so check the current text. Options: (1) a statute treating neural data as sensitive, with consent and use limits, giving certainty but risking definitional problems; (2) clarifying that existing privacy and consumer protection law covers headsets, which is faster but may leave gaps. Recommendation: a narrow statutory definition with limits on high-risk uses, such as employment decisions, and periodic review. Strongest objection: definitions of neural data may be too broad or too narrow and may burden beneficial research; reply: build in an exemption for consented research and review the definition regularly.
