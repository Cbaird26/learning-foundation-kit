@article
A defendant's lawyer shows a jury a scan: activity in part of the prefrontal cortex is lower than the average of a comparison group. The lawyer argues that the defendant "could not help it." What does the scan show? What would it have to show to matter? Courts increasingly meet such evidence, and the questions it raises mix neuroscience, law and ethics.

### What the law asks

Criminal law generally asks about an **act** (*actus reus*) and a **mental state** (*mens rea*, such as intent or knowledge), and then whether the person had the capacities that responsibility requires. Legal tests for conditions such as insanity or incompetence to stand trial differ among jurisdictions and are phrased in terms of understanding, rationality and control. A brain scan does not measure intent or knowledge; at most it provides evidence relevant to a capacity. The gap between what the scan measures (activity in a region) and what the law asks (what this person understood or could control at the time) must be bridged by argument.

### Uses of brain evidence

Brain evidence appears in several settings: to show brain injury or disease (a tumor or traumatic injury) that bears on capacity; at sentencing, as mitigation; in questions of competence to stand trial; in claims of pain; in attempts to detect deception or recognition (functional MRI studies of lying, and EEG methods based on a response to recognized details of an event); and in policy about the culpability of young people. In *Roper v. Simmons* (2005) the United States Supreme Court held that executing people who were under 18 when they committed their crimes violates the Eighth Amendment, and in *Graham v. Florida* (2010) it held that sentencing a juvenile to life without parole for a crime other than homicide violates the Eighth Amendment. Scientific findings on adolescent brain development were presented to the Court in briefs; commentators disagree about how much they influenced the reasoning, which also rested on other considerations.

### Admissibility

Courts decide whether scientific evidence may be presented. Under the standard from *Frye v. United States* (1923), a technique had to be generally accepted in the relevant scientific community. In *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals* (1993) the Supreme Court held that federal judges act as gatekeepers who must assess the reliability and relevance of expert evidence; the factors it mentioned include whether the technique can be and has been tested, peer review, the known or potential error rate, standards for its use, and general acceptance. States follow one or the other approach, or their own variants, and other countries have different rules.

### Three problems

**Group to individual.** Most brain findings are differences between group averages. If two groups differ by an effect size $d$ with equal spread, and the people are classified by the best single cutoff, the share classified correctly (with equal numbers in each group) is $\Phi(d/2)$. For $d = 0.5$ that is only about 60%. A difference between groups that is real and statistically reliable can be useless for deciding about one person.

**Reverse inference.** Observing that a region is active during a mental process does not show that the process occurred when the region is active, because regions take part in many processes. Inferring a mental state from activity is a probabilistic argument whose strength depends on how selective the activation is.

**Base rates and error.** A test's usefulness depends on its sensitivity, its specificity and how common the condition is in the group tested. If few of those tested are lying, even an accurate test produces many false alarms. In laboratory studies of lie detection, participants are often told to lie, stakes are low, and no one tries to defeat the test; real settings differ.

### Two views

**The skeptical view.** Law presumes a folk-psychological picture of persons as agents who respond to reasons, and responsibility turns on whether the defendant had the relevant mental capacities, which are established by behavior, history and testimony. Morse has criticized "brain overclaim syndrome", the habit of claiming that neuroscience undermines responsibility when it does not meet the legal question, and warns that scans can mislead because they look objective.

**The reformist view.** Greene and Cohen (2004) argued that neuroscience will gradually change intuitions about responsibility by showing mechanisms behind behavior, leading toward forward-looking approaches to punishment and away from retribution. Others argue more modestly that brain evidence can legitimately show injury, or capacities of groups such as adolescents, and that excluding it would discard relevant information.

Both agree that the quality of the evidence matters and that jurors and judges need accurate presentation.

@example
title: How good is a test that is 90% sensitive and 85% specific?
problem: A hypothetical brain-based lie-detection test flags 90% of people who are lying (sensitivity) and clears 85% of people who are telling the truth (specificity). In a group tested, 10% are lying. If the test flags a person, what is the probability the person is lying?
step: Take 1,000 people: 100 are lying and 900 are truthful.
step: True positives: $0.90 \times 100 = 90$. False positives: $0.15 \times 900 = 135$.
step: Probability that a flagged person is lying: $90/(90 + 135) = 0.40$.
step: Interpret: despite high sensitivity and specificity, a flag is wrong 60% of the time here because lying is uncommon in the group tested.
answer: The probability is 0.40, or 40%.

@mistakes
- Treating a brain-based group difference as a way to classify an individual. The accuracy of classification depends on the size of the difference relative to the spread.
- Treating activity in a region as proof of a mental state (reverse inference) or a defect in a region as proof that the person could not control their behavior.
- Assuming that a test's accuracy in a laboratory carries over to a courtroom, where stakes, base rates and motivation to defeat the test differ.

prompt: Two groups differ on a brain measure by $d = 1.0$ standard deviations, with equal spread and equal numbers in each group. If people are classified using the best single cutoff, what percent are classified correctly? Give the answer to one decimal place.
answer: 69.1
tol: 0.1
unit: %
check: 100 * 0.5 * (1 + erf((1.0 / 2) / sqrt(2)))

@practice self
prompt: A defendant accused of fraud offers fMRI "lie-detection" results, showing a pattern said to match truthful answers when he denies the charge. Evaluate whether the court should admit this evidence. Use the Daubert factors, the problem of applying group results to an individual, base rates, and the difference between laboratory and real conditions. Give the strongest argument for admitting it and the strongest argument for excluding it, then say which you find more persuasive and why, or what further facts you would require.
hint: Consider testing and error rates, standards, general acceptance, the effect of high stakes and countermeasures, and what a jury might make of a brain image.
solution: The strongest argument for admission is that the method is a testable measurement, that peer-reviewed laboratory studies report accuracy above chance, and that excluding it keeps from the jury information that may be more objective than the testimony of an interested party. The strongest argument for exclusion is that the Daubert factors are not met for individual cases: laboratory studies use instructed lies in low-stakes tasks with cooperative participants, the error rate in real, high-stakes settings is unknown, there are no accepted standards for how a test should be run, countermeasures and memory distortion may affect results, and group-level accuracy does not give the probability that this person is truthful. Base rates matter: a test with 80% sensitivity and 90% specificity gives a probability of lying of only about 30% for a flagged person when 5% of those tested lie (per 1,000 people, 40 true and 95 false flags). A jury may also give brain images undue weight. A reasoned conclusion might be to exclude the evidence until error rates under realistic conditions are known, or might admit it with limits; either is acceptable if tied to the factors. Credit comes from the quality of the reasoning, not from the side chosen.
