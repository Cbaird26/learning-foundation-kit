@article
Much of what is known about the brain comes from experiments on animals: the action potential in the squid axon, receptive fields in the cat and monkey visual cortex, place cells in the rat. Today, mice, rats, zebrafish, fruit flies, monkeys and other species are used to study circuits, disease and treatments. The same research raises one of the most disputed questions in bioethics: may we cause pain, distress or death to sentient animals for the benefit of human knowledge and health, and if so, under what conditions?

### The 3Rs

Russell and Burch (1959) proposed three principles for humane experimental technique, now called the **3Rs**. **Replacement** means using methods that do not use animals, or using animals whose capacity to suffer is thought to be lower, where the scientific aim can still be met (computer models, human cell cultures, studies in volunteers). **Reduction** means using the smallest number of animals that can answer the question, through good design and statistics. **Refinement** means changing procedures to reduce pain and distress and improve welfare (better anesthesia and analgesia, enriched housing, training animals to cooperate with procedures, and humane endpoints that end an experiment before suffering becomes severe). The 3Rs are accepted in almost all regulatory systems, including by people who disagree on whether animal research is justified.

### Oversight

In the United States, research with vertebrate animals is reviewed by an **institutional animal care and use committee** (IACUC) at each institution, under the Animal Welfare Act and the Public Health Service policy that applies to federally funded work. The committee reviews each protocol: is the research justified, is there no suitable alternative, are the numbers minimized, is pain managed? The regulations under the Animal Welfare Act do not cover purpose-bred mice and rats, which the Public Health Service policy does cover when the work is federally funded. In the European Union, animal research is regulated by **Directive 2010/63/EU**, which requires authorization of projects, a harm–benefit assessment, application of the 3Rs and classification of the severity of procedures (non-recovery, mild, moderate or severe). Rules differ across countries and change, so check the current text that applies to a project. Journals and funders also ask for transparent reporting of animal experiments, so that others can judge the quality of the work.

### The main positions

**The interests view.** Many philosophers hold that what matters morally is the capacity to suffer. If a mouse and a human have a comparable interest in avoiding pain, the interest counts equally, whatever the species. The weighing of interests (associated with Peter Singer's utilitarian argument) can allow some animal research if the benefits are very large and there is no alternative, but it rejects a blanket priority of human interests and forbids much trivial or poorly designed research.

**The rights view.** Others hold that animals that are subjects of a life have rights that forbid treating them merely as resources, whatever the benefits (associated with Tom Regan). On this view the aggregate benefit to humans does not justify the harm to an individual animal, and the right response is to end the use of animals in research.

**The benefit view.** A third position holds that important medical and scientific knowledge cannot yet be gained in other ways, that humans have stronger moral claims than other animals (or at least a duty to protect other humans first), and that regulated research with high welfare standards is justified. Defenders point to the use of animal research in the development of treatments and vaccines, and in understanding the brain.

**The scientific question.** A separate and empirical issue is **validity**: whether a finding in one species predicts what happens in humans. Critics note that many treatments that worked in animal models failed in human trials, and that poor design (small samples, no blinding, no randomization) wastes animals. Defenders reply that failure does not show that all models are useless, but that each model must be judged for the question asked. Both sides agree that research that cannot answer its question is the hardest to justify.

These positions also depend on uncertain facts about **sentience**. As of 2026 there is no scientific consensus on which animals are conscious or how much they suffer, and evidence for sentience is assessed differently for primates, mammals, fish and invertebrates.

### Reduction in numbers

Statistics is an ethical tool. For comparing two group means with a standardized effect size $d$ (the difference in means divided by the standard deviation), a common normal approximation for a two-sided test with significance level 0.05 and power 0.80 gives the number per group

$$n \approx 2\left(\frac{1.96 + 0.84}{d}\right)^2 ,$$

rounded up. For $d = 0.8$ this is $2 \times 3.5^2 = 24.5$, so 25 animals per group. Too few animals gives an underpowered experiment that wastes them, and too many uses animals needlessly.

@example
title: Group size and a repeated-measures design
problem: A lab expects an effect of size $d = 1.25$ between two conditions. Using the approximation in the article, how many animals per group does a two-group experiment need, and how many animals in total?
step: Substitute into $n \approx 2\left(\frac{1.96 + 0.84}{d}\right)^2$: the sum is $2.8$, and $2.8 / 1.25 = 2.24$.
step: Square and double: $2 \times 2.24^2 = 2 \times 5.0176 \approx 10.04$.
step: Round up, because a fraction of an animal is not possible: 11 animals per group.
step: With two groups the total is $2 \times 11 = 22$ animals.
answer: 11 animals per group, 22 in total.

@mistakes
- Treating the 3Rs as a position in the debate. They are a shared method that people with very different views accept.
- Saying that the failure of some animal-tested treatments in humans shows that animal research never works. It shows that validity must be judged model by model.
- Assuming that statements about which animals suffer are settled. Evidence differs by species and there is no consensus on sentience.

@practice num
prompt: An experiment compares two conditions with a between-groups design that needs 16 animals per group. A within-animal (crossover) design that tests each animal in both conditions needs only 11 animals in all. By what percent is the total number of animals reduced? Give the answer to one decimal place.
answer: 65.6
tol: 0.1
unit: %
check: 100 * (2 * 16 - 11) / (2 * 16)
hint: The between-groups design uses two groups of 16.
solution: The between-groups design uses $2 \times 16 = 32$ animals, and the crossover design uses 11. The reduction is $(32 - 11)/32 = 0.656$, or 65.6%. Whether a crossover design is valid depends on carry-over effects between conditions.

@practice self
prompt: A lab proposes an experiment in which a few macaque monkeys will have electrodes implanted in the cortex to study attention, with the long-term aim of understanding disorders of attention. Write a balanced harm-benefit analysis of the proposal: give the strongest argument that it is justified, the strongest argument that it is not, and say which scientific and welfare facts you would want to know before judging it.
hint: Include the 3Rs, validity of the model, and the possibility that reasonable people weigh the same facts differently.
solution: The strongest argument for the study is that high-resolution recordings in a species with a visual and attention system much like ours cannot be obtained from humans in this way, that a few animals can yield data on circuits that may inform treatments of attention disorders, and that welfare is protected by review, anesthesia and analgesia, enrichment and training. The strongest argument against is that monkeys are highly cognitive and social animals whose suffering may be considerable, that the path from basic findings to treatment is long and uncertain, and that on the rights view their use as tools cannot be justified by any benefit; on the interests view, the expected benefit must clearly exceed the harm, and a loose connection to disease is not enough. Facts to find out: whether the question can be answered with human imaging, smaller species or models (replacement); whether the sample size is justified by a power calculation (reduction); how housing, training and humane endpoints reduce distress (refinement); how the severity of the procedures is classified; and whether earlier work in this model has predicted human findings. A reader may reasonably weigh the same facts differently, so a good analysis states the weighing and its strongest objection.
rubric: States the strongest argument for the study and the strongest argument against it, without caricature.
rubric: Applies replacement, reduction and refinement to the proposal.
rubric: Identifies the validity of the model as an empirical question and names facts that would change the judgment.
rubric: Does not treat one view as the only reasonable one.
