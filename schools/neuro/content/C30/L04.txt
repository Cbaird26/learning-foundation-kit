@article
A person with advanced paralysis imagines moving a hand, and a cursor moves across a screen. How can a thought turn into an action, and what do we owe the person who volunteers to carry the device that makes it possible?

### What a brain–computer interface is

A **brain–computer interface** (BCI) is a system that records signals from the central nervous system, decodes them, and converts them into outputs that replace, restore or supplement a natural output, with feedback to the user. Its parts are signal acquisition, feature extraction, a decoder or classifier, an output device (a cursor, a speller, a robotic arm, a speech synthesizer) and feedback. Recording can be **non-invasive** (scalp electroencephalography, EEG), **semi-invasive** (electrodes on the surface of the cortex, electrocorticography) or **invasive** (microelectrode arrays that record from neurons inside the cortex). Invasive recording gives signals with better resolution and a higher information rate but requires neurosurgery and brings long-term device risks. A related class of devices stimulates instead of recording: cochlear implants, in wide clinical use for decades, deliver electrical stimulation to the auditory nerve.

### Research systems in their historical context

Wolpaw and colleagues (1991) showed that people could learn to control the vertical movement of a cursor with changes in scalp EEG rhythms. Hochberg and colleagues (2006) implanted a microelectrode array in the motor cortex of a person with tetraplegia and reported that neural activity could control a computer cursor and simple robotic devices; Hochberg and colleagues (2012) later reported reach and grasp with a robotic arm in two participants with tetraplegia. Willett and colleagues (2021) reported that decoding the neural activity of attempted handwriting in a person with paralysis produced text at about 90 characters per minute, approaching typical smartphone typing speeds for people of the participant's age. Moses and colleagues (2021) reported a speech neuroprosthesis based on cortical surface recordings that decoded words from the attempted speech of a person unable to speak because of brainstem stroke. These are research systems, with small numbers of participants, close technical support and a limited duration. They show what is possible in research, not what any current device can do.

### Measuring performance

A BCI's usefulness is often summarized by its **information transfer rate**. For a selection among $N$ equally likely targets made correctly with probability $P$ and errors spread evenly over the other targets, the Wolpaw formula gives the bits per selection:

$$B = \log_2 N + P\log_2 P + (1-P)\log_2\frac{1-P}{N-1}.$$

At chance accuracy $P = 1/N$, $B = 0$. At $P = 1$, $B = \log_2 N$. Multiplying by the number of selections per minute gives bits per minute. The formula assumes equal target probabilities and evenly distributed errors, which real systems only approximate, and it ignores the benefit of language models that correct errors.

### Ethical questions

**Risk and benefit.** Surgery carries risks such as infection and bleeding, and long-term risks include device failure, loss of signal over time and the need for replacement or removal. Participants with severe paralysis may accept risk because the possible benefit (communication) is large, which makes the quality of consent and the avoidance of the therapeutic misconception especially important.

**Obligations after the study.** What happens when a trial ends, a sponsor withdraws or a device is no longer supported? A participant may depend on a device that no one is responsible for maintaining, or may have a device that must be removed. Many ethicists argue that sponsors and investigators should plan in advance for long-term follow-up, device support and removal, and should tell participants what they can expect.

**Agency and responsibility.** If a decoder interprets signals and a robotic arm acts, who is the author of the action? Some argue that, as with a tool, the user is the agent when the device is faithfully controlled by the user's intention. Others note that machine learning decoders and shared-control systems add their own contributions, so that responsibility for mistakes may be shared, which requires thought in law and design.

**Identity and the self.** Some philosophers argue (the extended mind thesis of Clark and Chalmers, 1998) that a reliably coupled device can be part of a person's cognitive system, so that its removal or hacking harms the person. Others hold that a device remains an external tool, and that the thesis confuses use with constitution. Participants themselves report a range of attitudes, and these should be studied rather than assumed.

**Privacy and security.** Neural signals and decoders are data about a person and a connected device can be attacked. We take up neural data in Week 6.

**Justice.** Who has access to the technology, who pays and who is included in trials are questions of fairness.

@example
title: Information per selection in a four-target speller
problem: A BCI speller offers $N = 4$ equally likely targets. A user selects the intended target with probability $P = 0.8$, and errors are spread evenly over the other three targets. How many bits does each selection convey, and how many bits per minute at 12 selections per minute?
step: Compute $\log_2 4 = 2$.
step: Compute $P\log_2 P = 0.8 \log_2 0.8 = 0.8 \times (-0.3219) = -0.2575$.
step: Compute $(1-P)\log_2\frac{1-P}{N-1} = 0.2 \log_2 (0.2/3) = 0.2 \times (-3.9069) = -0.7814$.
step: Add: $B = 2 - 0.2575 - 0.7814 = 0.961$ bits per selection.
step: Multiply by 12 selections per minute: $0.961 \times 12 \approx 11.5$ bits per minute.
answer: About 0.961 bits per selection and 11.5 bits per minute.

@mistakes
- Treating a research demonstration as a statement about devices in general use. Published systems involved few participants, researchers' support and limited durations.
- Computing information transfer with accuracy alone. Accuracy must be considered with the number of targets: 90% correct among 2 targets conveys much less than 90% among 8.
- Assuming that an implant's risk ends at surgery. Replacement, removal, support and data protection are continuing issues.

hint: Compute the probability of no complication in all three procedures.
solution: The probability of none is $0.95^3 = 0.857$, so the probability of at least one is $1 - 0.857 = 0.143$. Repeated procedures add risk, which is why device lifetime is an ethical question.
