@article
An **argument** in writing is not a fight. It is a **claim** (a statement the author wants you to accept) supported by **reasons** and **evidence**. Your job as a reader is to decide whether the support is strong enough.

### The parts of an argument

- **Claim**: the position. "Our school should start later."
- **Reason**: why the claim is true. "Teenagers need more sleep."
- **Evidence**: facts, statistics, examples, expert testimony or research that back the reason. "A study of 9,000 students found that those who started later slept about an hour more."
- **Counterclaim**: an opposing view. A strong writer names it and responds with a **rebuttal**.

### Testing evidence

Ask three questions:

1. **Relevant?** Does the evidence actually connect to the claim? A statistic about school lunches does not support a claim about start times.
2. **Sufficient?** Is there enough of it? One example or one story is rarely enough to prove a broad claim.
3. **Credible?** Does it come from a reliable, qualified source (a scientific study, a recognized expert, official data) rather than an unnamed rumor, or a source that would profit from your agreement?

Also check **recency** (is the data up to date?) and **bias** (does the source have a reason to be one-sided?).

### Types of evidence

Statistics, expert opinions, research studies, firsthand accounts and examples are all evidence, but they differ in strength. A large study is generally stronger than a single anecdote. An expert speaking about their own field is stronger than a celebrity speaking outside it.

### Reasoning errors (fallacies)

- **Hasty generalization**: a conclusion from too little evidence. "Two of my friends hate the cafeteria, so the whole school does."
- **False cause**: assuming that because B came after A, A caused B. "I wore my lucky socks and we won, so the socks caused the win."
- **Appeal to emotion** that replaces evidence entirely.
- **Either/or** reasoning: pretending there are only two options.
- **Bandwagon**: "Everyone does it, so it must be right."

Spotting these does not mean the claim is false. It means the author has not proven it.

@example
title: Judging evidence
problem: Claim: "Our town should build a skate park." Evidence offered: "My cousin says skate parks make cities fun." Another writer gives: "A survey of 600 local teens found 410 want a skate park, and the city's parks office reports that vandalism fell in two nearby towns after they built one." Which evidence is stronger?
step: The cousin's statement is one person's opinion. It is not sufficient (a single voice) or clearly credible (no data).
step: The survey is relevant (it measures local demand), reasonably sufficient (600 teens) and the percent is $410/600 \approx 68\%$.
step: The parks office report is a credible source with data on a related effect, so it supports the claim that a park can be beneficial.
step: Conclusion: the survey and report are far stronger. They are relevant, specific and from credible sources, while the cousin's comment is an unsupported opinion.
@mistakes
- Treating any fact as good evidence even when it does not connect to the claim.
- Accepting a single example or a personal story as proof of a broad claim.
- Assuming a source is credible because it sounds confident, without checking who wrote it and why.

@practice self
prompt: A writer claims, "Teenagers should have a later school start time." Write one reason and one piece of evidence (a type of source you would look for) that would support it, and one question you would ask to test the evidence.
hint: Ask why teenagers might need a later start, then name a kind of source (a study, official data, an expert) that could show it, and a question about its size, date or author.
solution: Reason: Teenagers' sleep schedules shift later, so early classes leave them tired. Evidence: A sleep-medicine study comparing students' sleep and grades at schools with early and later start times. Question: Was the sample large, recent and from a credible organization, and did it compare similar schools?
rubric: The reason explains why the claim is true.
rubric: The evidence is a specific, relevant type of source (such as a study, data or expert).
rubric: The testing question asks about relevance, sufficiency or credibility.
