@article
An **argumentative essay** tries to convince readers that a position is reasonable. It is built from a **claim**, **reasons**, **evidence** and **reasoning**, and it takes opposing views seriously.

### The claim

Your claim must be **arguable**: reasonable people could disagree. "Sleep is important" is a fact most people accept. "Middle schools should start no earlier than 8:30 a.m." can be argued. A good claim is specific and often includes *should*.

### Structure

- **Introduction**: a hook, background and a clear claim.
- **Body paragraphs**: each focuses on a reason. Use the pattern **claim of the paragraph (topic sentence), evidence, explanation (reasoning), link back to the thesis**.
- **Counterclaim and rebuttal**: name the strongest opposing view fairly, then respond with evidence or logic. Phrases: "Some argue that... However, this overlooks..."
- **Conclusion**: restate the claim in fresh words, summarize the strongest reasons and, if appropriate, call readers to act.

### Evidence and reasoning

Evidence is facts, statistics, expert opinions, research and examples. **Reasoning** (also called commentary or warrant) is your explanation of *why* the evidence supports the reason. Without reasoning, a quotation or statistic just sits on the page. After each piece of evidence, write at least one sentence that begins to explain: "This shows that... because..."

Example: Reason: later start times improve attendance. Evidence: "One district reported that tardiness fell by 20 percent after the change." Reasoning: "Fewer late arrivals suggest students are better able to arrive on time when they have extra time in the morning."

### Tone and style

Use a formal tone; appeal to reason first, and to emotion only when it is relevant and honest. Avoid insults, exaggerations and absolute words like "everyone" or "never" unless supported. Do not use fallacies. Transitions such as *for instance, furthermore, in contrast, therefore* connect reasons.

### Counterclaims make you stronger

Ignoring the other side makes an argument look weak or uninformed. Fair treatment of a counterclaim shows credibility. A good rebuttal might show the opposing view is outdated, less important, or can be solved in another way.

### Revising

Is the claim arguable? Does every reason have evidence and reasoning? Did you include and answer a counterclaim? Do sources come from credible places? Is the conclusion more than a copy of the introduction?

@example
title: Claim, evidence, reasoning and rebuttal
problem: Build one body paragraph skeleton for the claim "Schools should provide free breakfast to every student."
step: Reason: Free breakfast helps students focus. Topic sentence: "First, providing breakfast helps students concentrate during the morning."
step: Evidence: "Teachers in one study reported fewer morning trips to the nurse after a school began serving breakfast."
step: Reasoning: "This suggests that students who eat before class feel better and can pay attention to lessons."
step: Counterclaim and rebuttal: "Some argue that the program is too costly. However, many districts offset costs with federal funding, and the benefits to learning justify the investment."
@mistakes
- Using a claim that is simply a fact, so there is nothing to argue.
- Dropping in evidence without any explanation of why it proves the reason.
- Ignoring the counterclaim, or describing it unfairly so it is easy to dismiss.

@practice self
prompt: Write a claim about a school policy you have an opinion on (for example, homework limits). Add one reason, one piece of evidence you could look for, one sentence of reasoning, and one counterclaim with a rebuttal.
hint: Make the claim a "should" statement, then ask what evidence would convince a skeptic.
solution: Claim: Middle schools should limit homework to one hour per night. Reason: Too much homework reduces sleep. Evidence to find: a study linking late-night homework with fewer hours of sleep. Reasoning: If students sleep less, they may focus less in class, so the extra work can defeat its own purpose. Counterclaim: Some argue homework builds responsibility. Rebuttal: Shorter, focused assignments can build responsibility without costing sleep.
rubric: The claim is arguable and specific.
rubric: The reason is supported by relevant evidence and a sentence of reasoning.
rubric: The counterclaim is fair and the rebuttal answers it.
