@article
**Meiosis** is the special division that produces **gametes** (sperm and egg) with **half** the chromosome number. Gametes are **haploid** ($n$); fertilization joins two gametes to restore the **diploid** ($2n$) number. In humans, $n = 23$ and $2n = 46$.

### Homologous chromosomes

A diploid cell has pairs of **homologous chromosomes**: one from each parent, carrying the same genes in the same order, though possibly different **alleles**.

### Two divisions after one replication

DNA is replicated once (S phase), then the cell divides twice.

**Meiosis I (reductional division)**
- **Prophase I:** homologs pair up (**synapsis**) to form tetrads, and **crossing over** exchanges segments between non-sister chromatids at points called **chiasmata**.
- **Metaphase I:** homologous pairs line up at the plate; each pair orients randomly (**independent assortment**).
- **Anaphase I:** homologs are separated; sister chromatids stay together.
- **Telophase I and cytokinesis:** two haploid cells, each chromosome still with two chromatids.

**Meiosis II (like mitosis)**
- Sister chromatids separate in anaphase II.
- Result: **four haploid cells**, each with unreplicated chromosomes, genetically different from each other.

### Sources of genetic variation

1. **Crossing over** produces recombinant chromosomes.
2. **Independent assortment** of homologs: for $n$ chromosome pairs there are $2^n$ possible gamete combinations (without crossing over). For humans, $2^{23} \approx 8.4$ million.
3. **Random fertilization:** any sperm can fuse with any egg.

### Errors: nondisjunction

**Nondisjunction** is failure of chromosomes to separate. If it happens in meiosis I, all four resulting gametes are abnormal (two have an extra chromosome, two lack one); in meiosis II, two gametes are abnormal and two are normal. Fertilization with an abnormal gamete gives **trisomy** (such as Down syndrome, trisomy 21) or **monosomy** (such as Turner syndrome, 45,X).

### Mitosis versus meiosis

| Feature | Mitosis | Meiosis |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | growth, repair, asexual reproduction | making gametes |
| Divisions | 1 | 2 |
| Daughter cells | 2 | 4 |
| Chromosome number | stays $2n$ | halves to $n$ |
| Genetic makeup | identical | different |
| Synapsis and crossing over | no | yes (prophase I) |

### Cell-cycle errors and cancer

Mitosis relies on checkpoints and tumor suppressors. Mutations in oncogenes or tumor suppressor genes remove these controls, so cells divide without limit, and unrepaired DNA damage accumulates. In meiosis, errors in chromosome separation cause aneuploidy rather than cancer, but both are failures of accurate chromosome distribution.

@example
title: Counting gamete combinations
problem: A species has $2n = 8$. How many different gametes can one individual make by independent assortment alone, and how many chromosomes does each gamete have?
step: $2n = 8$ means $n = 4$ pairs of homologous chromosomes.
step: Each pair orients independently, giving $2^4 = 16$ combinations.
step: Each gamete is haploid with $n = 4$ chromosomes.

@mistakes
- Saying homologous chromosomes separate in meiosis II; homologs separate in meiosis I and sister chromatids in meiosis II.
- Thinking that meiosis II halves the chromosome number again; the number is halved only in meiosis I.
- Confusing homologous chromosomes with sister chromatids.
- Saying crossing over occurs between sister chromatids; it occurs between non-sister chromatids of homologs.
