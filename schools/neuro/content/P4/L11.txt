@article
By now you can fit encoding models, compute RDMs and train networks. This week asks what the resulting numbers mean. A correlation of $0.4$ between a network and a brain region could be excellent or poor, an artifact or a discovery. Four ideas let you read such numbers: a ceiling, a null, a set of rivals, and an inference ladder.

### The noise ceiling

Neural measurements are noisy, so no model, even the true one, can predict a single noisy measurement perfectly. The **noise ceiling** estimates the best score an ideal model could obtain on the data you have, and it comes from the repeatability of the data. Suppose each stimulus was presented several times. A standard estimate is the **split-half reliability**: divide the repeats into two halves (for example, odd-numbered and even-numbered repeats), average the responses over the repeats in each half, and compute the Pearson correlation $r_{\text{half}}$ across stimuli between the two half-averages. Each half has only half of the data, so the correlation underestimates the reliability of the full average. The **Spearman–Brown correction** for doubling the data is

$$\rho = \frac{2\,r_{\text{half}}}{1 + r_{\text{half}}}.$$

For two repeats of four stimuli, $(4, 6, 5, 9)$ and $(5, 5, 6, 8)$, the correlation between the repeats is $r_{\text{half}} = 0.873$, so $\rho = 1.746/1.873 = 0.932$. In general, with $k$ times as much data, $\rho_k = k\rho_1/(1 + (k-1)\rho_1)$. The reliability $\rho$ is a squared-correlation-like quantity: the largest correlation a model of the noiseless signal could have with the noisy full-average data is roughly $\sqrt{\rho}$, here $\sqrt{0.932} = 0.965$. A model's score divided by this ceiling is its **normalized predictivity**. Several things matter in practice: the ceiling is itself an estimate with uncertainty, different splits give different values, and estimates can place a good model's normalized score above 1; that signals noise in the estimates, not a better-than-perfect model. For RDMs, analogous ceilings (a lower bound from predicting each subject with the others, an upper bound including everyone) are standard.

### Beyond the ceiling: rivals and flexibility

A score near the ceiling tells you the model leaves little explainable variance, not that it is right. Three more issues follow.

**Underdetermination.** Many models fit the same data. If networks with different architectures and objectives reach similar scores, the data cannot tell you which is right. Evidence for a model needs stimuli on which candidate models *disagree*: stimuli synthesized, or chosen from a large pool, so that the models' predictions differ, with the experiment deciding between them.

**Mapping flexibility.** A comparison that includes a fitted mapping (a regression with thousands of weights) lets a rich feature set fit nearly anything, so a high score may reflect the mapping, not the model. Remedies: held-out evaluation, comparison with untrained or shuffled-weight networks of the same size, simpler mappings (for example, a fixed one-to-one correspondence or a constrained mixture), and complexity-matched controls.

**Stimuli.** Fits can reflect low-level properties of the image set, such as color or contrast, instead of the abstract property the authors name. Decorrelating candidate properties by design is better than controlling afterward.

### The inference ladder

A comparison can support claims at different levels of strength.

1. *Descriptive*: this model's features predict these responses beyond these baselines.
2. *Representational*: the model and the brain share a geometry for these stimuli.
3. *Computational*: the brain solves a task, or optimizes an objective, like the model's.
4. *Mechanistic*: the model's components and learning rule correspond to biological ones.

Prediction supports the first rung and, with RSA, the second. The upper rungs need more: perturbations of the brain and the model with matched predictions (the model analogue of a double dissociation: disabling one component should reproduce the pattern of loss that lesioning one region does, and not another), converging behavioral evidence, and independent biological data. The reverse-inference problem has a model version: observing that the brain activity resembles the model's does not show that the brain does what the model does, because other computations might produce similar activity.

### Reading the literature

Standard failures to look for: selection of voxels, layers or models on the evaluation data (circularity, Kriegeskorte and colleagues, 2009); no noise ceiling; training and test data that are not independent (leakage); inference with flawed cluster-level corrections, which produced inflated false-positive rates in a major evaluation of fMRI methods (Eklund, Nichols and Knutsson, 2016); small samples with low power (Button and colleagues, 2013); one stimulus set, one model, one region, with a broad claim; and verbal conclusions stronger than the effect sizes warrant. Good papers state the strongest alternative explanation and test it.

@example
title: Normalizing a model score by the noise ceiling
problem: A region was measured twice for each of four stimuli, giving the responses $(4, 6, 5, 9)$ and $(5, 5, 6, 8)$. A model's predictions correlate with the average of the two repeats at $r = 0.72$. Compute the split-half reliability and the normalized predictivity.
step: Correlate the two repeats: means are $6$ and $6$; deviations $(-2, 0, -1, 3)$ and $(-1, -1, 0, 2)$; the sum of products is $2 + 0 + 0 + 6 = 8$; the sums of squares are $14$ and $6$; $r_{\text{half}} = 8/\sqrt{84} = 0.873$.
step: Apply the Spearman–Brown correction: $\rho = 2(0.873)/(1.873) = 0.932$.
step: Convert to a ceiling for a correlation: $\sqrt{0.932} = 0.965$.
step: Normalize: $0.72/0.965 = 0.746$. The model explains most, but not all, of what is explainable, and with four stimuli the estimate has a very wide uncertainty.
answer: Reliability $0.93$, ceiling $r = 0.97$, normalized predictivity about $0.75$.

@mistakes
- Using the correlation between two half-averages as the reliability of the full data, without the Spearman–Brown correction.
- Treating a score near the ceiling as proof that the model is correct: other models might reach it too.
- Choosing the layer, region or mapping that maximizes the score on the same data used to report it.
- Expecting normalized values never to exceed 1: ceiling estimates are noisy and values slightly above 1 occur.

model:
```python
import math


def _mean_over_repeats(rows):
    return [sum(col) / len(rows) for col in zip(*rows)]


def split_half_reliability(trials):
    """Odd-even split-half reliability with Spearman-Brown correction."""
    a = _mean_over_repeats(trials[0::2])
    b = _mean_over_repeats(trials[1::2])
    n = len(a)
    ma, mb = sum(a) / n, sum(b) / n
    num = sum((x - ma) * (y - mb) for x, y in zip(a, b))
    den = math.sqrt(sum((x - ma) ** 2 for x in a)
                    * sum((y - mb) ** 2 for y in b))
    r = num / den
    return 2 * r / (1 + r)
```
hint: Slicing with `trials[0::2]` and `trials[1::2]` gives the even-index and odd-index repeats; `zip(*rows)` groups the responses by stimulus.
hint: Do not split into first and second halves of the session: the split here is by index parity.
solution: Average each half over its repeats for every stimulus, correlate the two averages across stimuli, and apply the correction for the doubled amount of data. The second test has a different value under a first-half versus second-half split, so a version that splits the session in two fails it.

prompt: Write the main paragraph of a referee report for the following (invented) abstract: "We trained a deep network on object categorization and compared its layers with fMRI responses of 12 participants to 60 images. A linear mapping fitted on all 60 images from layer 6 explained 41% of the variance in a visual cortex region (p < 0.001), more than any other layer. We conclude that visual cortex implements the computations of this network and that backpropagation shapes cortical representations." State what the evidence supports, give the three most serious problems, and the analyses or experiments you would require.
hint: Look for where the fit was computed, how the layer was chosen, and which claim (descriptive, computational, learning rule) each conclusion belongs to.
solution: The evidence supports at most a descriptive claim: layer-6 features are related to responses in this region for these 60 images, and, if the evaluation is genuinely held out, better than other layers of this network. The three most serious problems are as follows. First, the mapping was fitted on all 60 images and the variance explained was reported on the same images, so the 41% is a training fit, which with a high-dimensional layer and 60 images can reach large values on noise alone; the best layer was also chosen on the same data (circularity). I would require cross-validation over images (with the layer and regularization chosen in the training folds only), with a noise ceiling from the repeats or from other participants, so that the score can be read as a fraction of what is explainable, and a comparison with an untrained network of the same architecture and with baseline models of low-level features. Second, the conclusion that cortex implements the network's computations is a computational or mechanistic claim, and the evidence is at the descriptive rung: other architectures might fit as well, and the study includes one network and one stimulus set. I would require several networks with different architectures and objectives, stimuli chosen to make them disagree, and representational comparison across participants with uncertainty over both stimuli and participants (12 participants and 60 images give wide intervals). Third, backpropagation concerns the learning rule, which the study never examines: the network's training procedure says nothing about how cortex learned, and the objection from weight transport and the alternatives (Week 8) are not discussed. I would ask the authors to delete the claim or support it with a test that distinguishes learning rules, such as comparing networks trained by different rules on the same neural data. I would also note that the statistic, $p < 0.001$, tests whether the fit differs from zero, which is not the question of interest, and that the sample of participants and images should be justified by a power analysis.

```python
import math


def _pearson(a, b):
    n = len(a)
    ma, mb = sum(a) / n, sum(b) / n
    num = sum((x - ma) * (y - mb) for x, y in zip(a, b))
    den = math.sqrt(sum((x - ma) ** 2 for x in a)
                    * sum((y - mb) ** 2 for y in b))
    return num / den


def _mean_over_repeats(rows):
    return [sum(col) / len(rows) for col in zip(*rows)]


def ceiling_normalized(pred, trials):
    """Model-data correlation divided by the square root of reliability."""
    r_half = _pearson(_mean_over_repeats(trials[0::2]),
                      _mean_over_repeats(trials[1::2]))
    rho = 2 * r_half / (1 + r_half)
    return _pearson(pred, _mean_over_repeats(trials)) / math.sqrt(rho)
```
hint: Compute the correlation of the predictions with the average over all repeats, then divide by the square root of the corrected reliability.
hint: The result may exceed 1 and may be negative; do not clip it.
solution: The ceiling comes from the reliability of the data, and the model's correlation with the full average is divided by its square root. Values above 1 occur because both the numerator and the ceiling are noisy estimates, and a version that clips to 1 fails the third test.
