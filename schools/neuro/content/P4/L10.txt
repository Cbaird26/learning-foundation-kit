@article
A **language model** assigns probabilities to sequences of words or word pieces. The models used in neuroscience are trained on large text collections to predict the next piece of text from the preceding context; they are neural networks (this week treats them generically, without reference to any product), and their internal activations can be read out for each word of a text. The question of the week: if a person hears or reads the same text, do those activations predict the person's brain responses, and what does it mean if they do?

### The quantities

The model's probability for the actual next word $w_t$ given the context is $p(w_t \mid w_{<t})$. The **surprisal** of the word is $-\log_2 p(w_t \mid w_{<t})$ bits: a word with probability $1/8$ has a surprisal of 3 bits. Averaged over a text, the mean surprisal $\bar{s}$ is the model's cross-entropy in bits per word, and the **perplexity** is $2^{\bar{s}}$, the effective number of equally likely choices; it equals the inverse geometric mean of the probabilities given to the actual words. A lower perplexity means better prediction. Human reading times and the amplitude of an event-related brain response associated with unexpected words (the N400) increase with surprisal, which is a long-standing connection between prediction models and language processing.

### The language network

In most people, language processing relies on a set of regions in left lateral frontal and temporal cortex, extending from the inferior frontal region associated with Broca (1861) to the posterior temporal region associated with Wernicke (1874). Imaging studies describe a **language network**: regions that respond more strongly to sentences than to lists of unconnected words or to nonwords, and that are separable from regions supporting arithmetic, music or general cognitive control. How finely the network divides into components (syntax versus meaning, words versus sentences), and how selective it is, are active debates.

### Encoding models with language-model features

The method is the one of Week 3. For each word (or each time point, once word features are aligned to the recording) take the activations of one layer of the language model as the feature vector $\mathbf{x}_t$, fit a regularized linear mapping to the response of a voxel, an electrode or a source, and test on held-out text with held-out folds. Studies using fMRI, MEG and intracranial recordings have reported that such models predict language-network responses to natural stories and sentences better than older models, with predictivity above that of simple baselines, and several reported that models that predict the next word better also predict neural responses better, with intermediate layers often predicting best. These are consistent findings across several research groups; their meaning is the issue.

### What a claim of "predicts brain activity" needs

A claim of this kind can be made well or badly, and the difference lies in the controls.

- **Baselines.** Word rate, word length, word frequency, position in the passage and simple syntactic features all predict responses, and all correlate with language-model features. The relevant quantity is the extra variance explained beyond them, and also beyond static word embeddings.
- **Autocorrelation and blurring.** fMRI responses are slow, and each volume covers many words. Features must be aligned with a hemodynamic delay and cross-validation must use blocks of text, not random time points, or neighbors leak between training and test. Significance tests must preserve autocorrelation, for example by block permutation of the features relative to the data.
- **Noise ceiling.** A correlation of $0.2$ means one thing if the data's reliability limits it to $0.25$ and another if the limit is $0.8$ (Week 11).
- **Region and layer selection.** Choosing the best layer for each region on the evaluation data, or reporting only the regions that worked, is circular (Week 3).
- **Model comparison.** Compare against a model with the same number of features but untrained weights, and against models of different sizes and objectives, so that the effect of training and of next-word prediction can be separated from the sheer number of features.
- **Interpretation.** Predicting activity shows that the features carry information related to the responses. It does not show that the brain predicts the next word, that it uses the model's computations, or that the model understands language, and a model trained on far more text than a child sees is unlike language learning. Which property of the model drives the fit (prediction, syntax, meaning, word identity) can be tested with controlled sentences and by ablating information in the features.

Open questions include whether prediction of upcoming words is the brain's language objective or a byproduct, what makes the intermediate layers fit best, how far fits depend on the amount of text, and how to test model–brain relations with stimuli designed to make models disagree.

@example
title: Surprisal and perplexity of a short text
problem: A language model gives the probabilities $0.2$, $0.5$ and $0.1$ to the three actual words of a short text. Compute each word's surprisal in bits, the mean surprisal and the perplexity.
step: Surprisals: $-\log_2 0.2 = 2.322$, $-\log_2 0.5 = 1$ and $-\log_2 0.1 = 3.322$ bits.
step: Mean surprisal: $(2.322 + 1 + 3.322)/3 = 2.215$ bits per word.
step: Perplexity: $2^{2.215} = 4.64$. Check with the geometric mean: $(0.2 \times 0.5 \times 0.1)^{-1/3} = 100^{1/3} = 4.64$.
step: Interpret: on this text the model is as uncertain as if it were choosing among about 4.6 equally likely words at each step.
answer: Surprisals $2.32$, $1.00$, $3.32$ bits; mean $2.21$ bits; perplexity $4.64$.

@mistakes
- Mixing logarithm bases: surprisal in bits uses $\log_2$, while the natural logarithm gives nats, and perplexity must use the matching base.
- Reporting that model features "predict the brain" without baselines for word frequency, length, rate and position.
- Splitting training and test at random time points for slowly varying data, so that neighboring words appear in both.
- Concluding from a good fit that the brain predicts upcoming words, or that the model processes language as people do.

@practice self
prompt: A paper reports: "Layer 8 of the language model predicts electrocorticography responses to sentences in left temporal electrodes with a held-out correlation of $r = 0.25$ ($p < 0.001$), so the brain predicts upcoming words like the model." The data's noise ceiling is $r = 0.40$. A baseline using word frequency, word length and position gives $r = 0.20$, and the same model with shuffled weights gives $r = 0.15$. Evaluate the claim: say what the numbers support, what they do not, and what additional analyses you would request.
hint: Express each correlation as a fraction of the noise ceiling, then ask which comparison the p-value actually addresses.
solution: The numbers support a modest, specific claim: in left temporal electrodes, layer-8 features predict held-out responses at $r = 0.25$, which is $62.5\%$ of the noise ceiling and above both the baseline ($0.20$, or $50\%$ of the ceiling) and the shuffled-weights network ($0.15$), so the trained features carry some information that simple word properties and the architecture alone do not. They do not support the stated conclusion. First, the gain over the baseline is $0.05$ in correlation, small compared with the baseline's own performance, and no confidence interval or comparison of the two models is reported, while $p < 0.001$ speaks to whether $r$ differs from zero, not to whether the model beats the baseline. Second, nothing here tests prediction of upcoming words: predictivity could come from lexical, syntactic or semantic information in the features, and the claim that the brain predicts words needs an analysis in which the variance explained is linked to the model's next-word probabilities (for example, surprisal as a regressor, or a comparison of layers and models by perplexity). Third, "like the model" goes beyond correlation: a different model with a different mechanism could give the same fit. I would request: a paired comparison of the full model against the baseline with confidence intervals across electrodes and held-out sentences (with block-wise resampling); a comparison with static word embeddings and with models of different sizes and perplexities; the selection of layer and electrodes on data independent from the test; analyses of which information drives the fit (for example, by removing the syntactic or lexical components from the features); and a test on sentences chosen to make models disagree.
rubric: Computes or states what the numbers show relative to the ceiling, baseline and shuffled control, and notes the gain over baseline is modest.
rubric: Notes that significance against zero does not test the model against the baseline, and asks for a paired comparison with uncertainty.
