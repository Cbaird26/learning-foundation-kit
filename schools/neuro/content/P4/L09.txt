@article
Sensory input is ambiguous, and cortex is full of connections running from higher to lower areas. **Predictive coding** puts the two facts together: the brain maintains a generative model of the causes of its input, higher areas send predictions down, and lower areas send up only what the predictions failed to explain. This week covers the idea in its simplest mathematical form, the ideas it grew from, and the state of the evidence.

### Precursors: efficient and sparse coding

**Efficient coding** (Barlow, 1961) proposed that sensory neurons should remove the redundancy of natural inputs so that the limited capacity of neurons is used well; retinal receptive fields that decorrelate natural images are a classic application. **Sparse coding** (Olshausen and Field, 1996) made it concrete. They asked for a dictionary of basis functions such that a natural image patch is a combination of a few of them, and a learning algorithm that minimized reconstruction error plus a sparsity penalty found basis functions that were localized, oriented and bandpass, resembling the receptive fields of simple cells in primary visual cortex (Hubel and Wiesel, 1959). That is a well-established result and the model's main success: a statistical principle applied to natural images produced a known cortical receptive-field structure.

### One layer of predictive coding

Let $\mathbf{x}$ be the input and $\mathbf{r}$ the latent causes. The generative model predicts the input as $\mathbf{W}\mathbf{r}$ with weight matrix $\mathbf{W}$ (columns are basis functions). The **prediction error** is $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} = \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{W}\mathbf{r}$. Inference finds the $\mathbf{r}$ that minimizes the energy

$$E(\mathbf{r}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{W}\mathbf{r}\|^2 + \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\|\mathbf{r}\|^2,$$

in which the second term is a Gaussian prior that keeps the causes small (use $\lambda\sum_i|r_i|$ instead for sparse coding, with the sparsity penalty of Olshausen and Field). Gradient descent on $E$ gives the update

$$\mathbf{r} \leftarrow \mathbf{r} + \eta\,(\mathbf{W}^\top\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} - \lambda\mathbf{r}).$$

The error unit computes the mismatch, and the cause unit changes in the direction that reduces it, weighted by how much each cause contributes to each input. Learning adjusts $\mathbf{W}$ by $\Delta\mathbf{W} = \eta_W\,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\,\mathbf{r}^\top$, a product of the error and the cause activity that is local to the synapse. At equilibrium without a prior, $\mathbf{W}^\top\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} = 0$: the errors have nothing left to explain in the directions the model can represent.

Rao and Ballard (1999) built a hierarchical version, in which each level predicts the activity of the level below and receives its errors, and showed that it could account for extra-classical receptive-field effects in visual cortex such as end-stopping, as consequences of the predictions of a higher level canceling the drive from a lower one. The **free-energy principle** (Friston, 2010) is a broader framework in which perception and action minimize a bound on surprise; its predictive-coding implementation is a particular proposal about neural message passing, and the principle is general enough that the question of what it forbids is itself debated.

### Evidence, and what would count as evidence

Observations consistent with predictive coding include reduced responses to expected stimuli, larger responses to unexpected ones (mismatch responses), and the anatomy of feedback and feedforward pathways. But each has alternative explanations: reduced responses to predicted stimuli can be produced by neural **adaptation** to recent stimulation, with no prediction involved; a mismatch response can reflect a change of stimulus statistics. Stronger tests include (a) *omission responses*, in which an expected stimulus is withheld and there is no bottom-up input to adapt to, yet activity changes; (b) independently manipulating *expectation* and *repetition*, so that an expected stimulus is not the most recent one; (c) identifying neurons whose activity scales with the signed or unsigned mismatch, and showing that top-down signals carry the prediction; and (d) perturbing feedback and testing whether the error signals change as the model says. Evidence from these directions exists but is mixed, and which cells carry prediction errors is not settled. Analyses have shown that, under certain assumptions, predictive-coding networks can approximate the weight updates of backpropagation with local rules, which links this week to the previous one; how closely biological circuits meet those assumptions is open.

@example
title: One inference step in a linear generative model
problem: A model has $\mathbf{W} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. It receives the input $\mathbf{x} = (3, 1)$, currently has $\mathbf{r} = (1, 1)$, and uses $\eta = 0.1$ and $\lambda = 0$. Compute the prediction error and the updated $\mathbf{r}$.
step: Predict the input: $\mathbf{W}\mathbf{r} = (2 \times 1,\; 1 \times 1) = (2, 1)$.
step: Compute the error: $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} = \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{W}\mathbf{r} = (3 - 2,\; 1 - 1) = (1, 0)$.
step: Project the error back: $\mathbf{W}^\top\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} = (2 \times 1,\; 1 \times 0) = (2, 0)$.
step: Update: $\mathbf{r} \leftarrow (1, 1) + 0.1\,(2, 0) = (1.2, 1.0)$. The next prediction is $(2.4, 1.0)$, closer to the input, and only the first cause moves because only it has a prediction error.
answer: $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} = (1, 0)$ and the new $\mathbf{r} = (1.2, 1.0)$.

@mistakes
- Equating predictive coding with Bayesian inference in general: predictive coding is one proposed neural implementation, and other implementations exist.
- Treating reduced responses to expected stimuli as proof of prediction: adaptation predicts it too, so expectation and repetition must be separated.
- Forgetting the transpose: the error is sent back through $\mathbf{W}^\top$, so each cause is changed according to how its basis function overlaps the error.
- Calling the free-energy principle a tested neural theory: the principle is a general framework, and tests concern specific implementations.

After one inference update $\mathbf{r} \leftarrow \mathbf{r} + \eta(\mathbf{W}^\top\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} - \lambda\mathbf{r})$, what is the second component of $\mathbf{r}$?
answer: 0.45
tol: 0.001
check: 0.5 + 0.2 * (2 * (1 - 2 * 0.5) - 0.5 * 0.5)
hint: Compute $\mathbf{W}\mathbf{r} = (1, 1)$ and the error $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} = (1, 0)$ first. The second component of $\mathbf{W}^\top\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ is $2\,\varepsilon_2$.

prompt: A sparse-coding model has $\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{I}$ (the identity), input $\mathbf{x} = (1, 2)$, causes $\mathbf{r} = (0.5, 1.5)$ and sparsity weight $\lambda = 0.3$. What is the cost $E = \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{W}\mathbf{r}\|^2 + \lambda\sum_i |r_i|$?

check: 0.5 * ((1 - 0.5) ** 2 + (2 - 1.5) ** 2) + 0.3 * (0.5 + 1.5)
hint: Add the reconstruction term (half the squared error) and the penalty on the sum of absolute values.
solution: The error is $(0.5, 0.5)$, so the first term is $\frac{1}{2}(0.25 + 0.25) = 0.25$; the penalty is $0.3 \times 2.0 = 0.6$; the total is $0.85$.

@practice self
prompt: A study shows that the response of a visual area to a grating is smaller when the grating was predicted by a preceding cue than when it was not. The authors conclude that the area computes prediction errors. Design a follow-up experiment that distinguishes a prediction-error account from stimulus-specific adaptation. State the conditions, the key comparison, the prediction of each account and one limitation.
hint: Make sure an expected stimulus is not also the most recently seen one, and think about what an account based on adaptation predicts when nothing is shown.
solution: Design: use cue-grating pairs in which a cue predicts the orientation of the upcoming grating with 75% validity, and construct the conditions so that expectation and recent stimulation are decoupled. The frequency of each orientation is equal across the session, and the orientation shown on the previous trial is balanced across conditions, so that an expected grating is no more likely to repeat the preceding one than an unexpected grating is. Conditions: expected grating (cue valid), unexpected grating (cue invalid), and omission trials (cue followed by a blank, 10% of trials). Key comparisons: the response to expected versus unexpected gratings with matched recent stimulation, and the response during the omission interval, recorded with population imaging or electrophysiology so that stimulus-specific population activity can be decoded. A prediction-error account predicts a smaller response to expected than to unexpected gratings even when adaptation is matched, and it predicts activity during omission, carrying orientation-specific information about the predicted grating or a signed error, although no stimulus was shown. A pure adaptation account predicts no effect of the cue once recent stimulation is matched, and no stimulus-related activity at omission, beyond what the cue itself evokes (controlled by a cue-only block with no expectation). A cue-evoked response can mimic an omission response, so the cue must be counterbalanced. Limitation: omission activity could reflect a top-down prediction without being an error (prediction rather than error units), and an expected-versus-unexpected difference could reflect attention to surprising stimuli, so attention manipulations and a task that holds attention constant are needed; a result would constrain, not prove, a hierarchical generative model.
rubric: Decouples expectation from repetition or recent stimulation, and includes omission trials or an equivalent control.
rubric: States different predictions for the prediction-error and adaptation accounts on the key comparison.
rubric: Names a limitation or alternative, such as attention or a prediction signal that is not an error.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain the difference between the efficient coding hypothesis and predictive coding. State what each says is transmitted by neurons, give one observation each can explain, and say how one study could favor one over the other.
hint: Think about what each hypothesis says gets removed from the signal: redundancy in the stimulus, or what a model of the world already predicts.
solution: Efficient coding says neurons should transmit a representation of the input that removes redundancy, so that a limited-capacity channel is used well: it explains why retinal and early cortical receptive fields whiten or sparsify natural-image statistics (center-surround and oriented filters). It makes no commitment to feedback or to expectations about specific events. Predictive coding says that neurons transmit the part of the input not predicted by a model held at a higher level, so the transmitted signal depends on context, expectation and feedback and not only on the stimulus statistics: it explains context-dependent suppression such as responses that shrink when a stimulus is predicted by a cue, and extra-classical receptive-field effects. A study could favor predictive coding over efficient coding by holding the stimulus and its long-term statistics fixed while manipulating a learned expectation (for example, through a cue), and showing that responses change as the prediction changes, including an activity change when the stimulus is omitted. If responses depend only on the stimulus and its adapted statistics, efficient coding suffices. The two are not exclusive, since a predictive system also removes redundancy.
rubric: Correctly describes what each hypothesis says is transmitted (redundancy-reduced code versus unpredicted residual).
rubric: Gives an observation each explains, such as natural-image receptive fields versus expectation-dependent suppression.
rubric: Proposes a study that manipulates expectation with the stimulus fixed, and notes the hypotheses are not exclusive.
