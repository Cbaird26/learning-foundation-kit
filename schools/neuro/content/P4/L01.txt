@article
Neuroscience and artificial intelligence have been borrowing from each other for eighty years, and that exchange is the subject of this course. Before asking whether a deep network is a model of the brain, you need the history, because each idea in the lineage carried a claim about brains and a claim about machines, and the two were often confused.

### Threshold units and the perceptron

McCulloch and Pitts (1943) showed that networks of idealized binary threshold units can compute logical functions. A **perceptron** (Rosenblatt, 1958) added learning. It takes inputs $\mathbf{x}$, weights $\mathbf{w}$ and a bias $b$, and outputs

$$\hat{y} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b > 0,\\ 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

Given a labeled example $(\mathbf{x}, y)$ with $y \in \{0, 1\}$, the **perceptron learning rule** with learning rate $\eta$ is

$$\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} + \eta\,(y - \hat{y})\,\mathbf{x}, \qquad b \leftarrow b + \eta\,(y - \hat{y}).$$

Nothing changes when the unit is right. When it answers 0 but should answer 1, the weights move toward $\mathbf{x}$; when it answers 1 but should answer 0, they move away. In code the unit itself is two lines:

```python
def predict(w, b, x):
    """Perceptron output (0 or 1) for inputs x."""
    z = sum(wi * xi for wi, xi in zip(w, x)) + b
    return 1 if z > 0 else 0
```

The **perceptron convergence theorem** says that if some weight vector separates the two classes, the rule reaches one after finitely many updates. The rule resembles Hebb's postulate (1949) only loosely: it is error driven, needs a teacher signal, and uses the postsynaptic error rather than correlated firing.

### The limit and the way past it

The data are **linearly separable** if a single line (a plane, a hyperplane) puts all the 1s on one side. Of the 16 Boolean functions of two inputs, 14 are separable. The two that are not are exclusive-or (XOR) and its complement: no line separates $\{(0,1),(1,0)\}$ from $\{(0,0),(1,1)\}$. Minsky and Papert (1969) analyzed what single-layer perceptrons can and cannot compute, XOR being the standard example. Historians debate how much the book changed funding and interest; as science, its point is mathematical and correct, and it applies only to a single layer.

Adding a **hidden layer** removes the XOR limit, and the question becomes how to train the hidden weights. **Backpropagation** (Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams, 1986) applies the chain rule to compute how the error depends on every weight, layer by layer, and then descends that gradient; the idea had earlier independent precedents. Its lasting contribution to cognitive science was a demonstration: in the parallel distributed processing program (Rumelhart and McClelland, 1986), networks trained this way developed useful internal representations that nobody had specified. Whether the brain computes anything like these gradients is a separate, still open question that occupies Week 8.

### Convolution and depth

The **neocognitron** (Fukushima, 1980) was a layered network built from Hubel and Wiesel's picture of simple and complex cells (1959, 1962): alternating layers that detect local features and pool over position. Trained with backpropagation, networks of this kind became **convolutional networks**. Krizhevsky, Sutskever and Hinton (2012) trained a large deep convolutional network on a large labeled image set and reduced the error on a standard image-classification benchmark far below the competing methods. That result, more than any single idea, brought deep networks into neuroscience, because their units resembled the visual hierarchy and their performance was suddenly useful as a scientific yardstick (Week 2).

### Borrowing versus modeling

Distinguish three claims. (1) *Inspiration*: an architecture borrowed an idea from the brain, such as thresholds, local receptive fields or error-driven learning. That implies nothing about the brain. (2) *Functional model*: a network performs a task and its internal representations resemble neural ones. (3) *Mechanistic model*: its components and learning rule correspond to biological ones. In Marr's terms (1982), the first two live at the computational and algorithmic levels, and only the third commits to the implementation level. A great deal of confusion in this field comes from supporting the second claim and stating the third.

The traffic also runs the other way. Temporal-difference learning (Sutton, 1988) came from machine learning and then gave neuroscience the account of dopamine you will meet in Week 7; the Hopfield network (1982) came from physics and became a model of memory. The exchange is productive precisely when each side states what it takes from the other.

@example
title: One perceptron update, step by step
problem: A perceptron with two inputs has $\mathbf{w} = (0.4, -0.3)$, bias $b = 0.2$ and learning rate $\eta = 0.5$. It sees $\mathbf{x} = (2, 1)$ with target $y = 1$. Compute the new weights and bias.
step: Compute the net input: $z = 0.4 \times 2 + (-0.3) \times 1 + 0.2 = 0.7$. It is positive, so the output is $\hat{y} = 1$.
step: Compare with the target: $y - \hat{y} = 1 - 1 = 0$. The unit is already correct.
step: Apply the rule: every change is a multiple of $y - \hat{y} = 0$, so $\mathbf{w}$ and $b$ stay at $(0.4, -0.3)$ and $0.2$. Now suppose the target had been $y = 0$: then $y - \hat{y} = -1$, and the new weights would be $(0.4 - 0.5 \times 2,\; -0.3 - 0.5 \times 1) = (-0.6, -0.8)$ with bias $0.2 - 0.5 = -0.3$.
answer: No change when $y = 1$; if the target were $y = 0$ the update gives $\mathbf{w} = (-0.6, -0.8)$ and $b = -0.3$.

@mistakes
- Updating after a correct answer, or using $\hat{y}$ in place of $y - \hat{y}$: the error term is zero when the unit is right, so nothing may change.
- Forgetting the bias update, or treating the bias as a weight on an input that is not constant: the bias moves by $\eta(y - \hat{y})$, exactly as a weight on a constant input of 1.
- Reading Minsky and Papert as proving that multilayer networks cannot learn: their analysis concerned single-layer perceptrons.
- Treating inspiration by the brain as evidence that a network models the brain: that needs a comparison with neural data.

model:
```python
def train_perceptron(data, lr, max_epochs):
    """Train a perceptron with the error-driven rule; return (w, b, epochs)."""
    w = [0.0] * len(data[0][0])
    b = 0.0
    used = 0
    for _ in range(max_epochs):
        updates = 0
        for x, y in data:
            z = sum(wi * xi for wi, xi in zip(w, x)) + b
            err = y - (1 if z > 0 else 0)
            if err != 0:
                w = [wi + lr * err * xi for wi, xi in zip(w, x)]
                b += lr * err
                updates += 1
        if updates == 0:
            break
        used += 1
    return w, b, used
```
hint: Keep a counter of updates in each epoch; if it is zero at the end, break out of the loop.
hint: At the start, every net input is exactly 0, which must count as output 0.
solution: The loop applies the rule example by example and counts updates per epoch. The first test checks the exact trajectory, the exclusive-or test checks that the epoch cap is respected when no separator exists, and the last test, where the net input is exactly 0, fails if the threshold is written as `>=`.

@practice self
prompt: A colleague says that because a perceptron has inputs, weights and a threshold, it "is" a model of a neuron. Using the three claims in the article (inspiration, functional model, mechanistic model), list three properties of a real neuron that the perceptron keeps or abstracts and three that it omits, and say for which kind of scientific question the abstraction is nonetheless useful.
hint: Sort features into those the perceptron keeps (weighted sum, threshold, plasticity) and those it leaves out (time, dendrites, neuromodulation), then ask at which of Marr's levels each abstraction is harmless.
solution: What it keeps or abstracts: synaptic inputs are summed with weights of either sign (excitatory and inhibitory synapses become positive and negative weights); a threshold turns the summed input into an all-or-none output (the spike, with the membrane potential and its threshold removed); learning is a change of the weights as a function of experience (synaptic plasticity). What it omits: time (real neurons integrate and leak, with a membrane time constant, and fire in trains whose timing may matter); dendritic structure (inputs are not summed linearly at one point, and dendrites can perform nonlinear operations of their own); neuromodulators, cell types and metabolic and anatomical constraints, and the fact that the error signal the rule needs is not obviously available at a synapse. The abstraction is useful for questions at the computational and algorithmic levels, such as what a population of threshold units can in principle represent or learn, or which classes of function need a hidden layer; the perceptron gives exact answers there. It is a poor guide to implementation-level questions, such as how a particular neuron's dendrites shape its responses, and the rule's resemblance to Hebb's postulate is loose, since it needs an error signal. So it counts as inspiration, and as a functional idealization at best, until compared with data.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain why the claim "a deep network was trained to do the task and its units resemble neurons, so the network is a model of the brain" mixes two of the three claims in the article. State what additional evidence would be needed to support each claim separately.
hint: Which claim needs only a comparison of representations, and which needs a correspondence of mechanism?
solution: Showing that a network performs a task and that its units resemble neurons supports at most the functional-model claim: the network and the brain solve a similar problem with similar representations. It says nothing about whether the brain's components or learning rule correspond to the network's, which is the mechanistic claim. For the functional claim you need quantitative comparison with neural data under cross-validation, with a noise ceiling and with competing models, to show that the match is better than alternatives and not an artifact of the stimuli or of a flexible mapping. For the mechanistic claim you need independent evidence about the biology: that the relevant cell types, connectivity, timescales and plasticity could implement the proposed computation, ideally with perturbation experiments whose predictions differ from those of rival mechanisms. Resemblance of units is also a weak guide to either, because many different networks produce similar tuning.
