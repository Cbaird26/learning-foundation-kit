@article
This course has taught concepts, methods and well-established results. The field itself moves fast: methods that are standard today may be replaced, and claims in recent papers will be revised. What lasts is the set of questions and the habits of evaluation. This lesson lists the questions that remain open, describes what each would take to resolve, and ends with the practice that lets you contribute.

### The open questions

**What does the brain optimize?** Task-optimized networks predict neural responses (Week 2), but "categorize objects" is a convenient task, not a known objective. Candidates include prediction (Week 9), reward (Week 7), efficient coding, and combinations. Different objectives can yield similar representations, so the question needs stimuli and measurements that separate them, and it may have no single answer: different areas may optimize different things.

**How does learning happen?** We do not know whether cortex follows gradients, with what approximations and through what circuits (Week 8). Evidence that could change this includes measurements of how feedback synapses relate to forward ones, identification of neurons that carry error signals, and learning experiments with controlled perturbations of those signals. The question of rules is not settled by task performance.

**Development and data efficiency.** Models trained on very large datasets differ from infants in the amount, kind and structure of input and in embodiment. Whether this gap reflects different learning algorithms, built-in structure, or richer data (action, multisensory, social) is disputed and open.

**Recurrence, feedback and time.** Most successful models of perception are feedforward, while cortex is recurrent and responses unfold over time (Weeks 5–6). How much of the late response, of attention and of context depends on feedback, and which network classes capture it, is unresolved.

**Embodiment and action.** Brains evolved to control bodies. Comparing sensory models without closed-loop behavior may miss what the representations are for. How to build models and experiments that include action and the animal's own influence on its input is active work.

**Wiring and cell types.** Connectomes of small nervous systems exist (the nematode *C. elegans*, White and colleagues, 1986), and large reconstructions of bigger circuits exist. Whether wiring diagrams plus physiology constrain function enough to predict behavior, and how to use them to constrain models, is open; knowing the wiring of the nematode has not by itself yielded a full model of its behavior.

**Metrics, benchmarks and measurement.** Scores are only as good as the stimuli, the noise ceilings and the rival models behind them (Week 11). Public benchmarks speed progress but invite overfitting to the benchmark; the field needs held-out stimuli, held-out data and comparisons designed to discriminate.

**Machine and animal minds.** There is no scientific or philosophical consensus on how consciousness arises, on which animals are conscious, or on whether any artificial system is or could be conscious. Researchers disagree on what would settle these questions. This course treats the question as open and asks that claims about it be tied to a stated theory and to evidence.

### Governance of neural data

Work at this border uses neural recordings from people. Two international instruments are current at the time of writing. The OECD Council adopted its Recommendation on Responsible Innovation in Neurotechnology on 11 December 2019, with nine principles; UNESCO's member states adopted the Recommendation on the Ethics of Neurotechnology at the General Conference on 12 November 2025, the first global standard on the topic. Neither is legally binding. UNESCO's text treats neural data as sensitive and calls for protecting mental privacy and freedom of thought, with special care for children and for non-medical uses. Chile amended its constitution in 2021 to protect brain activity and the information drawn from it. In the United States there is no federal neural-data law; several states have added neural data to their privacy laws, and federal proposals are pending. These laws and guidelines change, so check the current versions that apply to you.

### Practice that lets you contribute

Credible work in this area shares habits. Pre-register the analysis plan or use a registered report, in which the question and methods are reviewed before the data exist (introduced at the journal *Cortex* in 2013). Hold out stimuli and test sets you do not touch. Report noise ceilings, rival models, untrained and shuffled baselines, and uncertainty over stimuli and participants. Share data, code and models under the FAIR principles (Wilkinson and colleagues, 2016) as far as consent and privacy allow; in the United States the NIH Data Management and Sharing Policy has applied to funded applications since 25 January 2023, and other funders and journals set their own rules, so check the ones that apply to you. Write the strongest alternative explanation in your own paper.

### From an open question to an aim

A good aim names a comparison, a control and a result that would count against the idea: "We test whether a network trained with objective A explains held-out responses in region R beyond a network trained with objective B on stimuli chosen to make their predictions differ; if the two do not differ by more than a stated margin, the data do not distinguish the objectives."

@example
title: Is a difference between two models large enough to report?
problem: Two models were evaluated on 16 participants' held-out data. Model A's normalized predictivity exceeded Model B's by a mean of $0.06$, and the standard deviation of the 16 per-participant differences was $0.12$. Compute the paired $t$ statistic, and say what it does and does not tell you.
step: The standard error of the mean difference is $s/\sqrt{n} = 0.12/\sqrt{16} = 0.03$.
step: The statistic is $t = 0.06/0.03 = 2.0$, with 15 degrees of freedom.
step: Interpret: a $t$ of 2.0 with 15 degrees of freedom is near the conventional significance boundary (the two-sided critical value is about 2.13). The result is suggestive, not decisive.
step: Add what it does not tell you: the test treats participants as the sampled units, so it says nothing about generalization to other stimuli. A fuller analysis would also resample the stimuli, and the effect size ($0.06$ relative to the participant-level spread of $0.12$, a standardized effect of $0.5$) matters more than the threshold.
answer: $t = 2.0$ (15 degrees of freedom): suggestive but not conclusive, and silent about generalization over stimuli.

@mistakes
- Treating a question as settled because a recent paper reported a result: replications, rival models and new stimuli often change the picture.
- Presenting benchmark scores as evidence of a mechanism: scores need ceilings, rivals and held-out stimuli.
- Treating the UNESCO or OECD recommendations as binding law, or assuming the same neural-data rules apply everywhere: check the current local rules.
- Stating that an AI system is, or is not, conscious as an established fact: there is no consensus.

@practice self
prompt: Write a one-paragraph "specific aim" for a proposal on the open question of whether a prediction objective or a categorization objective better explains responses in a high-level visual region. Include the comparison, the stimuli, the control, the analysis, and the result that would count against your hypothesis.
hint: Write the comparison first, then the control that isolates the objective from architecture and data, then the result that would count against you.
solution: Aim 1. We test whether a network trained to predict future video frames explains neural responses in human ventral temporal cortex better than a network of the same architecture and size trained for object categorization, once both are evaluated on stimuli selected to make their predictions differ. From a large pool of images and short video clips, we select 80 stimuli on which the two networks' representational dissimilarities disagree most, and add 80 random images to anchor results to natural stimuli. For 20 participants, fMRI responses are measured in two sessions, so each stimulus has repeats and a split-half noise ceiling. For each network we compute held-out encoding predictivity (blocked cross-validation by run) and the RDM correlation with the region's RDM, with the best layer chosen in training folds only, reported as a fraction of the ceiling, with confidence intervals from resampling stimuli and participants. Controls: an untrained network of each architecture, a low-level image-feature model, and a third network trained with both objectives, so that the effect of the objective is not confused with architecture or training data. Hypothesis: the prediction-trained network will exceed the categorization-trained one on the discriminating stimuli by a difference whose confidence interval excludes zero. The result that would count against it is a difference no larger than a prespecified margin of 0.05 in normalized predictivity (a smaller effect would not distinguish the objectives), or a reversal in sign on the discriminating stimuli, in which case the data favor categorization or neither. The analysis plan will be preregistered, and the data and code shared under the applicable consent and policy rules.
rubric: States a comparison between objectives with matched architecture and training data and stimuli chosen to make the models disagree.
rubric: Specifies held-out evaluation, a noise ceiling, controls and uncertainty over stimuli and participants.
rubric: States a result that would count against the hypothesis, with a prespecified margin, and mentions preregistration or data sharing.

prompt: In a paired comparison of two models on 9 participants, the mean difference in normalized predictivity is $0.05$ and the standard deviation of the per-participant differences is $0.10$. What is the paired $t$ statistic?
answer: 1.5
tol: 0.01
check: 0.05 / (0.10 / sqrt(9))

solution: The standard error is $0.10/3 = 0.0333$, so $t = 0.05/0.0333 = 1.5$ with 8 degrees of freedom, below the conventional threshold of about 2.3.

@quiz self
prompt: Suppose a lab trains a network by a biologically motivated learning rule and reports that its representations match visual cortex as well as a network trained by backpropagation. Write a short assessment: what the result would show, what it would not show, and which two further results would most increase your confidence that the cortex uses something like the rule.
hint: Distinguish matching representations from matching the learning process, and think about measurements of the biology itself.
solution: The result would show that the rule is sufficient to produce representations that predict the cortical data as well as an alternative, on the stimuli and region tested, and so that matching those data does not require the exact gradient. It would not show that cortex uses the rule: representations are produced by the architecture, data, and objective as well as the rule, and several rules can reach similar end points. Equal fits also do not identify the rule, because the comparison is with one rival at one point in training. Two results would raise my confidence most. First, evidence from the biology that the rule's required signals exist: for instance, a measured neuromodulatory or dendritic signal whose properties (timing, sign, targets) match the global or local error the rule needs, and synaptic plasticity that follows the rule in vitro or in vivo. Second, a distinguishing prediction about learning dynamics, tested on data: the rule and backpropagation should differ in the trajectory of representations over learning, in the effect of perturbing the error signal, or in how fast the network adapts to a changed task, so recording neural activity across learning and perturbing the candidate error signal, with the outcome predicted before the experiment, would separate them. The match at the endpoint alone is weak evidence.
rubric: States what the match shows (sufficiency of the rule for these data) and what it does not (that cortex uses it).
rubric: Names evidence from the biology for the required signals or plasticity.
rubric: Names a distinguishing prediction about learning dynamics or perturbation, specified before the experiment.
