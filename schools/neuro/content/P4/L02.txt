@article
The primate **ventral visual stream** runs from the retina through the thalamus to primary visual cortex (V1), then V2, V4 and **inferior temporal cortex** (IT). Along it, receptive fields grow, selectivity moves from oriented edges (Hubel and Wiesel, 1959, 1962) toward complex shapes and object-related features, and responses become more tolerant to changes in position and size. Within roughly a tenth of a second of seeing an image, IT activity supports object recognition. For decades models of this hierarchy were designed by hand. The new move, around 2012 to 2014, was to optimize a network for a task and ask whether its internal representations resemble cortical ones.

### Convolutional hierarchies in numbers

A convolutional layer applies the same small filter at every position. For an input of width $n$, a filter of width $k$ and stride $s$ with no padding, the output width is $\lfloor (n - k)/s \rfloor + 1$. Pooling over $2 \times 2$ blocks halves each dimension. Take a $64 \times 64$ input: a $5 \times 5$ filter at stride 1 gives $60 \times 60$ and pooling gives $30 \times 30$.

The **receptive field** of a unit is the region of the input that can influence it. It grows layer by layer. Track the field size $r$ and the spacing in input pixels between neighboring units, the jump $j$ (starting at $r = 1$, $j = 1$). A layer with filter width $k$ and stride $s$ updates $r \leftarrow r + (k - 1)\,j$ and then $j \leftarrow j\,s$. Two $3 \times 3$ filters at stride 1 give $r = 3$ then $r = 5$. Pooling counts as a layer with $k = 2$, $s = 2$. Growth of field size with depth mirrors the growth from V1 to IT, which is the first reason the analogy is attractive.

### The goal-driven approach

The approach has three steps: choose an architecture family loosely constrained by anatomy (convolution, pooling, nonlinearity, depth); optimize its parameters for a behaviorally relevant task, usually object categorization, and *not* to match neurons; then ask how well the trained network predicts neural responses to images it was not trained on. Yamins and colleagues (2014) found that among many models, those that categorized objects better also predicted IT responses better, and that for the best network the top layer predicted IT responses and an intermediate layer predicted V4 responses; the neural predictions came from a held-out linear mapping from model units to recorded sites. Khaligh-Razavi and Kriegeskorte (2014) used representational similarity analysis (Week 4) on many models and found that a supervised deep network explained the geometry of IT responses better than the unsupervised models they tested, although fitting weights over layers was needed to approach the best fits. A graded pattern, with early layers resembling earlier visual areas better than late layers do, is the usual finding in this literature.

### What was shown, and the limits

These are well-established qualitative results: task optimization can yield internal representations that predict held-out cortical responses far better than earlier hand-built models, and across networks, task performance and neural predictivity are positively related over a range. Several points are disputed or open, and a careful reader keeps them in view.

- **Layer-to-area maps depend on the method.** The mapping of layers to areas comes from a regression or similarity measure with its own flexibility. A better-fitting later layer means its features are more linearly related to the neural responses; it does not mean that the area is that layer.
- **Feedforward only.** The networks are mostly feedforward, while the ventral stream has dense feedback and recurrence, and responses evolve over time. Late response components may need recurrence.
- **Underdetermination.** Many different architectures give similar predictivity on a limited image set. A good fit does not single out one model, and fit saturates below the noise ceiling (Week 11) or at it for the particular image set.
- **Training is not development.** Supervised training on large labeled sets is unlike what an animal experiences. That the end state matches says little about how the cortex gets there.
- **Behavior and robustness.** A network can match neural responses on natural images yet differ from humans in how it fails on distorted or adversarial ones, so predictivity is one test among several.

The right conclusion is modest and still significant: a task-optimized hierarchy is a better functional model of ventral-stream representations than its predecessors, and it supplies hypotheses about what the stream is optimized for.

### Comparing a layer with neural data

For your first deliverable, the simplest comparison is a correlation. Let $x_i$ be a model unit's response to stimulus $i$ and $y_i$ a neuron's response to the same stimulus. The Pearson correlation is $r = \sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y}) / \sqrt{\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2 \sum_i (y_i - \bar{y})^2}$. A single unit rarely matches a neuron, which is why population regression is used; and correlation fitted and evaluated on the same stimuli overstates the match, which is Week 3's subject.

@example
title: How well does one unit match one neuron?
problem: A network unit gives the responses $x = (1, 3, 2, 5, 4)$ to five images. A neuron gives $y = (2, 6, 3, 8, 6)$ spikes per second to the same five images. Compute the Pearson correlation.
step: Compute the means: $\bar{x} = 15/5 = 3$ and $\bar{y} = 25/5 = 5$.
step: Compute the deviations. For $x$: $(-2, 0, -1, 2, 1)$. For $y$: $(-3, 1, -2, 3, 1)$.
step: Compute the sums: $\sum (x - \bar{x})(y - \bar{y}) = 6 + 0 + 2 + 6 + 1 = 15$; $\sum (x - \bar{x})^2 = 10$; $\sum (y - \bar{y})^2 = 24$.
step: Divide: $r = 15/\sqrt{10 \times 24} = 15/15.49 \approx 0.968$. Five images are far too few to trust this number, but it shows the computation.
answer: $r \approx 0.97$.

@mistakes
- Treating a high correlation on the stimuli used to choose the unit, the layer or the weights as evidence of a match: that needs held-out stimuli.
- Reading "layer 7 predicts IT best" as "IT is layer 7": the mapping reflects a regression's flexibility and the stimulus set.
- Forgetting that the receptive-field update uses the current jump: each stride multiplies the spacing between units for all later layers.
- Assuming a network matches the brain's behavior because it matches neural responses on one image set.

@practice self
prompt: Write the paragraph of a referee report on a paper that claims that "layer 4 of our network is area V4 and layer 7 is IT" because the layers' held-out linear predictions of recorded sites peak in the matching layers. State what the evidence supports, what it does not, and two analyses or controls you would request.
solution: The evidence supports a functional claim: representations in the intermediate layers are more linearly related to V4 responses than those of other layers, and deeper layers are more related to IT, on the image set used and with the regression chosen, and the use of held-out images makes this more than curve fitting. It does not support the stronger claim that layers are areas. The peak layer depends on the regression (its regularization and the number of units), on the image set (natural images versus synthetic), and on the network's design, such as where pooling occurs; a layer is a stage in one architecture, while an area contains recurrent circuits, cell types and feedback, and its response evolves in time. I would ask for the following. First, the predictivity of each layer as a fraction of the noise ceiling, with confidence intervals across sites and images, so the authors can say how much explainable variance is captured, and a comparison with alternative architectures and with an untrained network of the same architecture, which controls for what the architecture alone gives. Second, a test on a new image set with different statistics (for example, images from a different category distribution), and an analysis of whether late response components, which may depend on feedback, are fit as well as the early ones. I would ask the authors to rewrite the claim as correspondence in representations.

@quiz self
prompt: Design a control experiment, using the idea of an untrained network, that tests whether the neural predictivity of a trained convolutional network comes from learning or from the architecture alone. State the comparison, what each outcome would mean and one limit on the inference.
hint: Keep the architecture, the regression and the image set fixed, and change only the training.
solution: Compare the held-out neural predictivity of the trained network with that of the same architecture with random weights, using the identical image set, layers, regression, regularization and cross-validation folds, and report both as fractions of the noise ceiling. If the trained network is much better, learning on the task contributed representations that the architecture alone does not supply; if the two are close, the architecture (local filters, pooling, depth) and the statistics of the images account for most of the predictivity, and the claim that task optimization explains the match is weakened. Intermediate results are the common case, so the quantity of interest is the difference and its confidence interval across sites. One limit: random networks of this kind still extract useful features from natural images, so a small gap may reflect how much structure the architecture imposes, not that training is irrelevant, and a large gap does not show that the learning rule resembles the brain's, only that learning something about the task helps.
