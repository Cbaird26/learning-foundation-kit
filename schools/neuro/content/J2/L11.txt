@article
**Probability** measures how likely an event is. It is a number from $0$ to $1$: $0$ means impossible, $1$ means certain, and $\frac{1}{2}$ means as likely as not. Probability can be written as a fraction, decimal or percent. A value below $0$ or above $1$ is never a probability.

### Theoretical probability

When all outcomes are **equally likely**,

$$P(\text{event}) = \frac{\text{number of favorable outcomes}}{\text{total number of outcomes}}.$$

A bag with $3$ red and $5$ blue marbles has $8$ equally likely outcomes when you draw one, so $P(\text{red}) = \frac{3}{8}$. The probability of an event **not** happening is $1 - P(\text{event})$, so $P(\text{not red}) = \frac{5}{8}$.

### Experimental probability

Run the experiment many times and count:

$$P(\text{event}) \approx \frac{\text{times it happened}}{\text{number of trials}}.$$

If a spinner lands on green $18$ times in $60$ spins, the estimate is $\frac{18}{60} = \frac{3}{10}$. More trials usually bring this estimate closer to the theoretical value.

### Expected counts

If $P(\text{green}) = \frac{3}{10}$, then in $500$ spins you expect about $\frac{3}{10} \cdot 500 = 150$ greens. The actual count will be near this, but rarely exactly it.

### Compound events

A **compound event** has more than one stage, such as flipping two coins or rolling two dice. List the **sample space** (all outcomes). Two coins give $HH$, $HT$, $TH$, $TT$, so $P(\text{two heads}) = \frac{1}{4}$. Two dice give $6 \cdot 6 = 36$ outcomes; six of them have sum $7$, so $P(\text{sum } 7) = \frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6}$. For independent stages, multiply the probabilities: $\frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{4}$.

### Simulations

A **simulation** uses a simple device to imitate a real situation: a die, coins, or random digits. Match each outcome of the real situation to outcomes of the device with the same probability, run many trials, and count. To simulate an event with probability $\frac{1}{4}$, roll a die, count a $1$ as "yes", count $2$, $3$ or $4$ as "no", and roll again on a $5$ or $6$.

### Comparing with theory

Compare your simulated fraction with the theoretical probability. They should be close, though not identical, and the gap shrinks with more trials.

@example
title: A bag of marbles
problem: A bag has $5$ yellow, $4$ blue and $3$ green marbles. One marble is drawn at random. Find $P(\text{not blue})$ and the number of non-blue draws you would expect in $60$ draws with replacement.
step: There are $5 + 4 + 3 = 12$ marbles, so $P(\text{blue}) = \frac{4}{12} = \frac{1}{3}$.
step: The complement: $P(\text{not blue}) = 1 - \frac{1}{3} = \frac{2}{3}$.
step: Expected count in $60$ draws: $\frac{2}{3} \cdot 60 = 40$.
step: Check by counting favorable marbles: $5 + 3 = 8$ of $12$ is $\frac{8}{12} = \frac{2}{3}$.

@mistakes
- Using the number of different colors, rather than the total number of marbles, as the denominator.
- Adding probabilities for independent stages instead of multiplying them.
- Expecting the experimental result to equal the theoretical probability exactly, especially after few trials.

@practice num
prompt: A fair six-sided die is rolled once. What is the probability of rolling a number greater than $4$? Give an exact fraction.
answer: 1/3
check: 2 / 6
hint: List the numbers greater than $4$.
solution: Only $5$ and $6$ qualify, so $\frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{3}$.

@practice num
prompt: Two fair six-sided dice are rolled. What is the probability that the sum is $7$? Give an exact fraction in lowest terms.
answer: 1/6
check: sum(1 for a in range(1, 7) for b in range(1, 7) if a + b == 7) / 36
hint: There are $36$ equally likely outcomes. List the pairs that add to $7$.
hint: The pairs are $(1,6)$, $(2,5)$, and so on.
solution: Six pairs, $(1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1)$, give sum $7$, so $\frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6}$.

@practice self
prompt: A cereal box contains one of $4$ equally likely prizes. Describe a simulation using a six-sided die to estimate the probability of getting prize $1$ at least once in $5$ boxes.
hint: Decide what one roll of the die will stand for, and how to make the chance of the prize exactly $\frac{1}{4}$.
solution: Let each roll of the die stand for one box: rolling a $1$ means prize 1, rolling $2$ or $3$ or $4$ means another prize, and ignore (reroll) a $5$ or $6$, so each box has probability $\frac{1}{4}$ of prize $1$. One trial is five valid rolls. Record whether any roll was a $1$. Repeat for many trials, such as $50$. The estimate is the number of trials containing at least one $1$ divided by the number of trials. (Theoretically it is $1 - (\frac{3}{4})^5 \approx 0.76$.)
rubric: Each box is matched with one roll and the probability of the prize is exactly $\frac{1}{4}$.
rubric: One trial is clearly defined as five boxes, and the result recorded is whether prize $1$ appeared at least once.
rubric: Many trials are repeated and the estimate is the fraction of trials with a success.

@quiz num
prompt: A spinner landed on blue $14$ times in $40$ spins. Based on this, about how many blue results should you expect in $800$ spins?
answer: 280
check: 14 / 40 * 800
hint: First find the experimental probability of blue.
solution: $\frac{14}{40} = 0.35$, and $0.35 \cdot 800 = 280$.

@quiz num
prompt: Three fair coins are flipped. What is the probability of exactly two heads? Give an exact fraction.
answer: 3/8
check: comb(3, 2) / 2**3
hint: List all outcomes. There are $8$ of them.
hint: Count the outcomes with exactly two $H$: $HHT$, $HTH$, $THH$.
solution: Three of the $8$ outcomes have exactly two heads, so the probability is $\frac{3}{8}$.
