# Writing one course for the Neuroscience Learning Foundation

The Neuroscience Learning Foundation is a Khan Academy–style self-study site for a 70-course pathway from kindergarten to research in neuroscience and cognitive science: elementary, middle and high school (shared with the AI Learning Foundation, with its programming track), a complete college degree in neuroscience and cognitive science, and graduate study and research. Each course follows a 14-week syllabus. You write the lessons for ONE course: weeks 1–12 (one lesson per weekly topic) plus a project/review/final-exam file for weeks 13–14. Your course JSON says who the learner is (`audience`) and the course `level`; write for that learner. Address the learner as "you". Assume the course prerequisites, but briefly remind key prerequisite ideas when you use them. Use the standard names of structures, quantities, methods and results (the action potential, the Nernst equation, the Hodgkin–Huxley model, long-term potentiation, the hippocampus, signal detection theory and $d'$, the BOLD signal, the general linear model, the drift-diffusion model, the Rescorla–Wagner rule, the temporal-difference error, place cells, working memory, double dissociation): learners need them to use the readings, read papers and talk with other researchers. Attribute findings and methods to their authors where the attribution is standard (see Content standards), but do not write biographies or anecdotes about real people, and never mention the curriculum's author.

The site runs Python in the learner's browser, so learners write and run real code inside lessons, and the site grades it by running your tests. The browser's Python has **no NumPy, SciPy, pandas, scikit-learn or neuroscience packages**, so every graded exercise builds the idea from scratch in plain Python on small data. That is a feature: simulating a neuron, computing a peristimulus time histogram, fitting a psychometric function or a learning model in twenty lines is the surest way to understand it. Articles may show how researchers write the same thing with NumPy, SciPy or specialized packages.

## Files you write (only these)

- `/home/claude/neuro/content/<CODE>/L01.txt` … `L12.txt` — week N lesson = the syllabus topic for week N.
- `/home/claude/neuro/content/<CODE>/project.txt` — the week 13–14 project brief, review guide and cumulative exam items.

Your course data (audience, level, profile, topics, block deliverables, project, primary reading) is in `/home/claude/neuro/toolkit/courses/<CODE>.json`. Before writing anything, read it and `/home/claude/neuro/toolkit/example_neuro_lesson.txt`: a complete, valid lesson that shows the exact format, the conventions for math, units, data and code, and every common item type. The example lesson is a format sample: never copy its examples, data, functions or items. C12 (the membrane as an RC circuit, weeks 3–4) and C25 (the leaky integrate-and-fire neuron, week 2) overlap the example's topic and must use entirely new functions, parameters, numbers and exercises.

## File format

Sections start with `@name` at the start of a line. Fields are `key: value`; a line that does not begin with a known key continues the previous field, so multi-line values are fine. Text inside ``` fences is kept exactly as written: inside a fence, lines that look like `key: value` or `@name` are just code, so decorators and dictionaries are safe there. Write TeX directly; inline math is `$...$`, display math is `$$...$$`.

**Dollar signs.** A `$` outside code starts math. Write a literal dollar sign as `\$` (a grant of `\$2,000`). Put anything else with `$` inside backticks or a fence.

**Math notation and units** (use them consistently): scalars in italics ($V$, $I$, $\tau_m$); vectors bold lowercase ($\mathbf{x}$, $\mathbf{w}$) written `\mathbf{x}`; matrices bold uppercase ($\mathbf{X}$, $\mathbf{W}$); the transpose $\mathbf{x}^\top$; expectation $\mathbb{E}$, probability $P(\cdot)$ for events and $p(\cdot)$ for densities. Neuroscience conventions: membrane potential $V$ in millivolts (mV), currents $I$ in nanoamperes (nA) or picoamperes (pA), conductances $g$ in nanosiemens (nS) or mS/cm², resistance $R$ in megaohms (MΩ), capacitance in picofarads (pF) or µF/cm², time constants $\tau$ and times in milliseconds (ms), firing rates $r$ in hertz (Hz, spikes per second); sensitivity $d'$ and criterion $c$ in signal detection; reaction times (RT) in ms. Always state units, and convert carefully (MΩ × nA = mV; an interval in ms gives a rate of $1000/T$ Hz). **$\log$ means the natural logarithm** unless you write $\log_2$ or $\log_{10}$; say "nats" or "bits" when it matters. Data tables have one trial, subject or time point per row.

**Algorithms.** Give an algorithm or a simulation as runnable Python in a ```python fence whenever you can (the site adds a Try it button to code that runs the same in the browser), or as numbered steps in prose. Use ```text for pseudocode or for NumPy, SciPy or package code you show for reference (it does not run in the browser).

**The site shows no images.** Describe what a picture would show in words and tables: a voltage trace or a learning curve as a table of times and values, a raster as lists of spike times, a tuning curve as a table of stimuli and rates, a brain structure by its location and connections in words, a circuit or a network as a list of nodes and edges, an image or a receptive field as a small grid of numbers.

Code in prose: inline code in single backticks; blocks in fences with a language (```python, ```text, ```bash). Keep code lines under 80 characters where you can (the hard limit is 100), and indent with 4 spaces, never tabs.

Lesson file sections, in order:
- `@lesson` — `title:` (the week's topic as a clear title), `summary:` (one sentence: what the learner will be able to do), `objective:` ×2–4, `reading:` (see below).
- `@article` — markdown: paragraphs, `###` subheadings, **bold**, lists, tables, code blocks, `$math$`. Length by level (see below).
- `@example` ×1–2 — `title:`, `problem:`, `step:` ×2–6 (each step is revealed one at a time, Khan-style; a step may contain a table or a code block), optional `answer:`.
- `@mistakes` — 2–4 lines starting with `- `.
- `@practice <type>` ×5 — ordered easy → hard, each with its own data and numbers: never reuse a worked example, the article's own data or its numbers (also in code items' example calls and tests), so learners must apply the idea rather than recognize an answer they have seen. A code item never asks for a function the article already gives in full: ask for a variant, an extension or the next step (a different separator, a second dimension, a stopping rule, an edge case the article's version mishandles).
- `@quiz <type>` ×2–3 — new problems used only in unit quizzes, the midterm and the final. Never near-copies of practice items, and never the data of a worked example.

## Item types

Item fields: `prompt:`, `answer:`, `check:`, `hint:` ×1–3 (progressive; the last hint gets the learner to the method but not the final answer), `solution:` (complete and short), plus the fields each type needs.

| type | learner does | you give | verified by |
|---|---|---|---|
| `num` | one number | `answer:`, usually `tol:`, and `unit:` when it helps | `check:` expression |
| `expr` | an expression in `vars` (a derivative, a gradient component) | `answer:`, `vars:` | `check:` lambda |
| `mc` | one choice | mark the correct `choice*:` | — |
| `ms` | select all that apply | mark every correct `choice*:` | — |
| `self` | derives, proves, designs, writes or builds, then compares with a model | `solution:` (model answer) and `rubric:` ×2–4 | — |
| `code` | writes Python in an editor, runs it, presses Check | `starter:`, `tests:` and/or `stdout:`, `model:` | the validator runs `model` against your tests in CPython **and** in the browser's Python, and checks that `starter` fails them |
| `text` | types an exact short answer, usually what a program prints | `answer:`, optional `accept:` ×n | `check:` that computes the same text |
| `list` | several values, any order | `answer:` (`-2, 3` or `none`) | `check:` |
| `tuple` | ordered values | `answer:` (`(2, -1)`) | `check:` |

`mc`/`ms` use `choice:` lines (3–5 total) with the correct ones written `choice*:`. **The site shuffles the choices**, so never use "all/none of the above" or "both A and B", and in hints and solutions name a choice by its content, never by its position ("the second choice", "option B"). Each `mc` item has exactly one best answer. Distractors come from real misconceptions: confusing the equilibrium potential with the resting potential, depolarization with hyperpolarization, an EPSP with an action potential, a neuron's threshold with its rheobase, sensitivity ($d'$) with response bias, a lesion's effect with the function of the damaged region, the BOLD signal with spiking, correlation with causation, forward with reverse inference, a single with a double dissociation, a p-value with an effect size, statistical with practical significance, fixed with random effects, or one memory system with another. `ms` items suit "which of these findings support the claim?" and "which of these controls are needed?". Use `self` for derivations, designs, critiques, case analyses, lab-report sections and short essays; give a complete model `solution:` and 2–4 specific `rubric:` lines.

**What an auto-graded item may test.** An `mc`, `ms`, `num`, `text`, `list` or `tuple` item must have an answer that is correct whatever view the learner holds: what a structure does according to the evidence, what an experiment found, what a model or a method computes, what a theory claims, what a law or a guideline says, what follows from given numbers, or a computed value. Never make a contested claim (which theory of consciousness is right, whether the mind is physical, whether free will exists, how much of a trait is innate, whether an animal or an AI system is conscious, whether a form of enhancement or an animal experiment is justified) the correct answer, and never mark a reasonable view wrong. Questions about what the learner thinks go in `self` items, whose model answers argue one view well and give the strongest reply, and whose rubrics reward the quality of the reasoning, never agreement with a position.

### Numbers (`num` items)

`num` items are the backbone of the math: a membrane or equilibrium potential, a time constant, a conduction delay, a firing rate, a $d'$ or a threshold, a reaction-time effect, a test statistic, an effect size or a sample size, a probability, an information value, one step of a simulation or a learning rule, or a corrected significance threshold.
- `answer:` is a plain number: `3`, `0.95`, `-0.25`, `3/4`, `2.83`, `1.5e9`. No `%`, commas or words in the answer field. Learners may type `1,234` or `7.25%`: the site removes thousands separators, and a `%` typed into an item whose unit is `%` is ignored.
- **Percentages**: answer as a percent with `unit: %` ("Give the accuracy as a percent to one decimal place" → `answer: 87.5`, `unit: %`). A probability or a rate asked for as a decimal has no unit.
- **Other units**: `unit: mV`, `unit: ms`, `unit: Hz`, `unit: nA`, `unit: MΩ`, `unit: µm`, `unit: m/s`, `unit: bits`, `unit: trials`, `unit: participants`.
- **Tolerance**: whenever the prompt asks for rounding, set `tol:` to one unit of the last place asked for (two decimal places → `tol: 0.01`); `tol: 1%` gives a relative tolerance for large numbers. A `num` item without `tol:` must have an exact answer (an integer, or a terminating decimal such as `0.95` that needs no rounding).

`check:` is ONE Python expression that computes the expected result independently from the problem statement — never retype your answer. Redo the arithmetic (`-log(0.7)`, `sum(p * log2(1 / p) for p in [0.5, 0.25, 0.25])`, `2 * 0.8 * 0.6 / (0.8 + 0.6)`), use the program itself (`float(run("...program..."))`), or use the helpers: `math`, `np` (NumPy), `exp`, `log` (natural), `log2`, `log10`, `sqrt`, `tanh`, `erf`, `comb`, `perm`, `factorial`, `floor`, `ceil`, `Fraction`, `pi`, `e`, `inf`, `mat(A)` (a NumPy array, so `mat(A) @ mat(B)` works), `dot`, `norm`, `det`, `inv`, `eig`, `lin(A, b)`, `rank`, `nderiv(f, x)`, `pderiv(f, point, i)`, `root`, `quad(f, a, b)`, `nsum`, and builtins such as `sum`, `range`, `min`, `max`, `round`, `len`, `sorted`, `zip`, `enumerate`, `abs`, `all`, `any`, `int`, `float`. Check expressions run only in the validator (in CPython with NumPy), never in the learner's browser. Only when an item truly cannot be computed, write `nocheck: <reason>` (at most six per course).

`expr` items: `answer:` in calculator syntax (`2*w*x^2 - 2*x*y`, `e^(-z)/(1+e^(-z))^2`, `ln(x)`), `vars: w, x, y`, and a `check:` lambda giving the correct value (`lambda w, x, y: 2 * w * x**2 - 2 * x * y`). Use them for derivatives and gradient components; optional `domain: x: 0.5..3` sets the sampling range.

### `code` items

```
@practice code
prompt: Write `mse(preds, targets)` that returns the mean squared error of two equal-length lists of numbers. For example, `mse([1, 2], [1, 4])` returns `2.0`.
starter:
```python
def mse(preds, targets):
    pass
```
tests:
```python
assert abs(mse([1, 2], [1, 4]) - 2.0) < 1e-9
assert abs(mse([0.5], [0.5])) < 1e-9
assert abs(mse([1, 2, 3], [2, 2, 2]) - 2 / 3) < 1e-9
assert abs(mse([-1, 1], [1, -1]) - 4.0) < 1e-9
```
model:
```python
def mse(preds, targets):
    """Mean of the squared differences between predictions and targets."""
    return sum((p - t) ** 2 for p, t in zip(preds, targets)) / len(preds)
```
hint: Square each difference, add them up, and divide by the number of pairs.
solution: Pair each prediction with its target, square the differences, and average them; dividing by the count, not by the count minus one, makes it a mean.
```

- `starter:` (optional) is the code the editor starts with: usually the function header and `pass`. It must compile and must **fail** the tests. `model:` is your complete reference solution. Each of `starter`, `tests`, `model`, `stdin`, `stdout` holds exactly one fenced block.
- `tests:` is Python run after the learner's code, in the same namespace. Each top-level `assert` is one test: the learner sees a ✓ or ✗ beside each one, and a failing `assert expr == expected` automatically reports what the code gave and what was expected. Write 3–6 asserts: the main case, edge cases (one trial, no spikes, a tie, a current exactly at threshold, an empty bin) and one case a typical wrong solution gets wrong (dividing by n − 1 instead of n or the reverse, a sign error in the leak term, mixing milliseconds and seconds in a rate, an off-by-one bin, counting a spike exactly on a bin edge twice, updating values in place where a copy is needed, forgetting to center the data).
- **Data and representations.** State the representation and the units in the prompt and keep to them within a lesson. A spike train is a sorted list of spike times (say whether in ms or s); several trials are a list of such lists; a sampled signal (a voltage trace, an LFP, a BOLD time series, a calcium trace) is a list of floats with a stated sampling interval; trial data are lists of dictionaries or parallel lists (stimulus, response, reaction time in ms, correct as 0 or 1); a stimulus image or a receptive field is a small list of rows of numbers; a network's weights are a list of rows; a graph or a circuit is a dictionary of adjacency lists. Data sets are tiny and invented: a handful to a few dozen trials, neurons or time points.
- **Floating point.** Compare computed floats with a tolerance, never with `==`: `assert abs(f(x) - 0.5) < 1e-6`, or for lists `assert all(abs(a - b) < 1e-6 for a, b in zip(got, want))`. `math.exp`, `math.log` and `math.tanh` can differ in the last digit between the browser and CPython, so always allow a tolerance (1e-6 relative to the size of the numbers is usually right). Integer results, shapes, counts, argmax indices and labels may be compared exactly.
- **Randomness.** Noise and stochastic spiking are central to neuroscience, but tests never check specific random values. For simulations with noise (a diffusion model, a Poisson spike generator, a noisy neuron), pass the random numbers in as a list argument (`noise`, `us`) or take a `random.Random` instance and test only properties (the output has the right length, values are in range, calling twice with the same seed gives the same result). Prefer deterministic simulations in graded items: fixed initial conditions, fixed inputs, supplied noise.
- **Keep the work small.** The model plus the tests must finish in well under a second in the browser, which runs Python about 20–50 times slower than CPython: aim for at most about 100,000 arithmetic operations per check (simulating one neuron for 1,000 Euler steps or fitting a model by a grid search over a few hundred values is fine; simulating a network of hundreds of neurons for thousands of steps is not). No timing-based tests.
- `stdout:` (optional) is the exact text the program must print; trailing spaces and trailing blank lines are ignored, everything else must match. `stdin:` (optional) holds the lines `input()` returns. A program that reads input must be checked on two or three different inputs: repeat `stdin:` and `stdout:` as pairs; the first pair is shown to the learner, the others are hidden checks. Prefer functions that return values over programs that print.
- Helpers available to learner code and tests: `output()` returns the text printed since the tests began or since the last `output()` call; `write_file(name, text)` and `read_file(name)` set up and inspect files (the learner's `open()` works on these in-memory files in text mode, so CSV-reading exercises work); `raises(ExcType, func, *args)` is True when the call raises that exception.
- `solution:` explains the approach in words. **The site shows your `model:` code right after the solution**, so do not paste the whole model again.
- The prompt must specify everything the tests check: the function name and parameters, the representation, the return value (returned, not printed, unless the exercise is about printing), how to treat ties and edge cases, and anything not allowed. Give one example call in the prompt when it helps.
- Good code: idiomatic, readable, PEP 8, docstrings for public functions, small helper functions where they clarify; type hints welcome in model solutions (tests must not depend on them).
- **What the browser's Python can run.** It is Brython, implementing Python 3.14, and it also has to pass under CPython 3.13, so use features common to both. Available: the whole core language (classes, dataclasses, generators, comprehensions, `match`, f-strings, exceptions, `with`, `enum`, `typing`) and most of the standard library: `math`, `random`, `statistics`, `itertools`, `functools`, `collections`, `heapq`, `fractions`, `string`, `re`, `json`, `csv`, `copy`, `operator`, `textwrap`. **Not available in the browser**: `numpy`, `pandas`, `scipy`, `sklearn`, `torch` and every other third-party package; `sqlite3`; threads and `asyncio`; networking. Graded code is plain Python.
- **The built-in `sum` of floats differs**: CPython 3.12 and later add floats with compensated summation, so `sum([0.1] * 10)` is `1.0` there but `0.9999999999999999` in the browser. Never show or test the exact printed result of `sum` over floats; use an explicit loop when rounding is the point, and integers in `text` items.
- **Tests must give the same result in any Python**: never test specific random values, set iteration order or the printed form of a set, default object reprs, object ids, the exact wording of a built-in error message (test the exception type with `raises`), or anything that depends on the date or time.
- **How many.** In a P (programming and modeling) course (C12, C15, C16, C17, C24, C25, C26, C27), every lesson has at least two `code` items among its practice and quiz items, usually three or four, plus `text` items that trace code and `num` items worked by hand. In T courses (C11, C13, C14, C18, C19, P1, P2, P4), add a `code` item in about half the lessons where the topic is naturally programmed (a membrane computation, a reaction-time analysis, a learning rule, a simple model or statistic); C13 may have fewer. The W courses (C20, C30, P3) and the E courses (P5, P6) have no `code` items; their computations are `num` items.

### `text` items

For "what does this program print?" and other short exact answers:

```
@practice text
prompt: What does this program print?
```python
rates = [12.0, 31.5, 8.0]
print(rates.index(max(rates)))
```
answer: 1
check: run("rates = [12.0, 31.5, 8.0]\nprint(rates.index(max(rates)))")
hint: `max` finds the largest firing rate; `index` gives its position, counting from 0.
solution: The largest rate is 31.5 Hz, at position 1, so the program prints `1`: the neuron's preferred stimulus is the one at index 1.
```

- The learner's text is compared with `answer:` line by line after trimming each line and treating any run of spaces as one space; capital letters and punctuation matter. Multi-line answers go in a fence. `accept:` lines give other forms that are also correct, but keep answers unambiguous: say exactly what to type.
- `check:` must reproduce the answer independently: `run(src)` runs Python (CPython 3.13) and returns what it prints, and `run(src, stdin)` feeds input lines; copy the program exactly from the prompt into the check as a Python string with `\n` line breaks.
- Prefer `mc` for names and terms (which structure, which method, which researcher): spellings vary, and a learner who knows the answer should not be marked wrong for a hyphen.

## Writing for each level

- **College (C11–C30).** Articles 450–850 words, with university-level precision, as in a good undergraduate neuroscience or cognitive science course. Teach in this order: a puzzle, an observation or a classic experiment with numbers the learner can follow, then the precise concept, mechanism or equation with every step shown, then the evidence for it and its limits, then (in P courses) code that implements or analyzes it. State results with their conditions and their evidence (the Nernst equation for a membrane permeable to one ion; what a double dissociation does and does not show; a test's assumptions). Distinguish clearly what is well established, what is supported but debated, and what is unknown. In P courses the code items are the backbone: simulate, fit or analyze the week's idea from scratch on tiny data, with `text` items that trace code and `num` items worked by hand. In T courses: mechanisms, computations and evidence, `num` items, `mc`/`ms` on concepts, findings and the logic of experiments, `self` items for explanations, designs and critiques, and code where natural. W courses (C20, C30) teach with `mc`/`ms` on concepts, findings and what laws and guidelines say, `num` items for computations, and `self` items for balanced analyses and designs with model answers.
- **Graduate (P1–P6).** Articles 500–900 words, written as a faculty member coaching a graduate student: the shape of the current research, the main results with accurate attributions, the arguments and evidence behind them, open problems, and how to read and contribute to the literature. `self` items are research work (a derivation, a critique paragraph, an experimental design, a referee report paragraph, a section of a proposal, a timed exam answer) with model answers a faculty reader would admire and rubrics that name what earns credit. P1 is rigorous: complete derivations and exact statements. P2 and P4 cover fast-moving areas: teach the concepts, the methods and the well-established results with their dates, say plainly which questions are open, and never describe the current state of the art as fact or any company's current products. P3 presents each theory of consciousness in its strongest form, with its evidence and the strongest objections, and takes no side. P5 and P6 teach research practice accurately: programs, journals and institutions differ, so describe common practice and tell learners to check the rules that apply to them.

- **Balance on contested questions.** Where informed researchers disagree (the mind–body problem and the hard problem; which theory of consciousness is right; whether animals, infants or machines are conscious; free will and responsibility; how much of language or cognition is innate; whether psychiatric categories carve nature at its joints, and how much brain-based explanations of mental illness can deliver; the costs and benefits of animal research; cognitive enhancement; neuroscience evidence in court; how to interpret the replication crisis), present the strongest version of each main view, with the evidence and arguments behind it, and do not tell the learner what to believe. - **Current facts to teach as current** (verified; use them where your course touches them, and tell learners that laws, guidelines and classifications change, so they should check the current versions):
  - **Neural data and neurorights.** UNESCO's member states adopted the Recommendation on the Ethics of Neurotechnology at the General Conference on 12 November 2025, the first global standard on the topic; it is not legally binding, and it treats neural data as sensitive and calls for protecting mental privacy and freedom of thought, with special care for children and for non-medical uses. Earlier, on 11 December 2019, the OECD Council adopted the Recommendation on Responsible Innovation in Neurotechnology, which the OECD calls the first international standard in this field (nine principles; OECD recommendations are not legally binding either). Chile amended its constitution in October 2021 (Law 21.383) to protect brain activity and the information drawn from it, and in August 2023 Chile's Supreme Court ordered a company to delete a former senator's brain data recorded by a consumer headset. In the United States there is no federal neural data law: by April 2026 four states had enacted protections that treat neural data as sensitive in their privacy laws (Colorado, 2024; California, 2024, in force 1 January 2025; Montana, 2025, in force 1 October 2025; Connecticut, 2025, in force 1 July 2026), and a federal bill, the MIND Act, introduced in the Senate in September 2025, would direct the Federal Trade Commission to study neural data; more state bills are pending, so check the current list.
  - **Research ethics.** The Declaration of Helsinki was last revised on 19 October 2024 by the World Medical Association's General Assembly in Helsinki (the first revision since 2013; it now speaks of research participants rather than subjects and stresses community engagement and vulnerability). In the United States, research with people is governed by the Common Rule (45 CFR 46; the revised rule's general compliance date was 21 January 2019) and reviewed by institutional review boards; research with animals is reviewed by institutional animal care and use committees under the Animal Welfare Act and Public Health Service policy; the European Union regulates animal research under Directive 2010/63/EU. The 3Rs (replacement, reduction, refinement) come from Russell and Burch (1959).
  - **Data sharing, public access and reporting.** In the United States, the NIH Data Management and Sharing Policy took effect on 25 January 2023 (applications include a plan for managing and sharing the scientific data), and NIH's 2024 Public Access Policy, which requires papers from NIH-funded research to be publicly available on the date of publication with no embargo, took effect on 1 July 2025. Other funders, countries and journals set their own rules, so tell learners to check the ones that apply to them. The FAIR principles (findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable) come from Wilkinson and colleagues (2016); registered reports were introduced at the journal Cortex in 2013; reporting guidelines include ARRIVE 2.0 for animal research (2020) and CONSORT for randomized trials (updated as CONSORT 2025, published in April 2025).
  - **Psychiatric and medical classification.** The DSM-5-TR was published in 2022, and the ICD-11 came into effect on 1 January 2022. Death determined by neurologic criteria (brain death) is accepted in most countries, with procedures set by national guidelines and law; in the United States a 2023 consensus guideline from several medical societies covers adults and children.
  - **Minds and moral status.** As of 2026 there is no scientific or philosophical consensus on how consciousness arises, on which animals are conscious, or on whether any AI system is or could be conscious, or on what would settle these questions. Present the arguments and the evidence; never state these as settled fact. On 30 April 2025 the Cogitate Consortium published in Nature the first large adversarial collaboration between two theories (256 participants; fMRI, MEG and intracranial EEG), testing preregistered predictions of integrated information theory and global neuronal workspace theory: the results challenged key predictions of both (no sustained synchronization within posterior cortex, which IIT predicted; no ignition at stimulus offset and only partial representation of conscious content in prefrontal cortex, which GNWT predicted), and what they show for each theory is still debated. In September 2023 an open letter signed by more than a hundred researchers called IIT pseudoscience, and many others rejected that label. On 19 April 2024 the New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness, signed by many researchers, stated that there is strong scientific support for conscious experience in mammals and birds and at least a realistic possibility of it in all vertebrates and many invertebrates (such as cephalopods, decapod crustaceans and insects); it is a statement of expert opinion, not a consensus of all researchers.
