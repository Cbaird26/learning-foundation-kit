Corporate law is state law; **securities law** is predominantly federal. The Securities Act of 1933 regulates the **issuance** of securities, and the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 regulates **trading** and created the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC). This lesson covers the antifraud rules most likely to appear in a business associations course.

### What is a security, and the Acts' structure

An **investment contract** is a security under *SEC v. W.J. Howey Co.* (1946): an investment of money in a common enterprise with an expectation of profits derived from the efforts of others. The 1933 Act makes it unlawful to offer or sell a security unless a **registration statement** is in effect (§ 5), unless an **exemption** applies (such as the private-offering exemption of § 4(a)(2) and Regulation D). **Section 11** imposes liability for material misstatements in a registration statement; issuers are strictly liable, and a plaintiff must trace shares to the registration statement (*Slack Technologies v. Pirani* (2023)). The 1934 Act requires periodic reports and contains the antifraud rules below.

### Section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5

Section 10(b) of the Exchange Act and Rule 10b-5 prohibit, in connection with the purchase or sale of any security, employing a device to defraud, making an untrue statement of a material fact or **omitting** a material fact necessary to make statements not misleading, or engaging in a course of business that operates as a fraud. The SEC enforces the rule; courts have implied a **private right of action**, with these elements:

1. A **material misrepresentation or omission.** Information is material if there is a substantial likelihood that a reasonable investor would view it as significantly altering the "total mix" of information (*TSC Industries v. Northway* (1976)); for contingent events such as merger talks, materiality weighs probability against magnitude (*Basic Inc. v. Levinson* (1988)). In *Macquarie Infrastructure Corp. v. Moab Partners* (2024) the Court held that a **pure omission** (silence about information a regulation requires to be disclosed) is not actionable under Rule 10b-5(b); only **half-truths**, statements made misleading by what they leave out, are.
2. **Scienter**: intent to deceive, manipulate or defraud (*Ernst & Ernst v. Hochfelder* (1976)); negligence is not enough, and the courts of appeals accept recklessness.
3. **Connection with the purchase or sale** of a security, and the plaintiff must be an actual purchaser or seller (*Blue Chip Stamps v. Manor Drug Stores* (1975)).
4. **Reliance.** Under the **fraud-on-the-market** theory, plaintiffs who trade in an efficient market are presumed to rely on the market price, which reflects public material misstatements (*Basic*); the defendant may rebut by showing the misstatement had no **price impact** (*Halliburton Co. v. Erica P. John Fund* (2014)).
5. **Economic loss** and **loss causation**: the misrepresentation, not other factors, caused the loss (*Dura Pharmaceuticals v. Broudo* (2005)).

The Private Securities Litigation Reform Act of 1995 requires a complaint to state with particularity facts giving rise to a **strong inference** of scienter, which is one that is cogent and at least as compelling as any opposing inference (*Tellabs, Inc. v. Makor Issues & Rights* (2007)). The limitations period is two years after discovery and five years after the violation (28 U.S.C. § 1658(b)). When the SEC seeks civil penalties for securities fraud, the Seventh Amendment gives the defendant a jury trial in federal court (*SEC v. Jarkesy* (2024)).

### Insider trading

Insider trading is a form of Rule 10b-5 fraud with three theories. There is no general duty to disclose or abstain from trading on material nonpublic information (MNPI); the duty arises from a relationship of trust (*Chiarella v. United States* (1980)).

- **Classical theory.** Corporate insiders (officers, directors, controlling shareholders and temporary insiders such as outside lawyers and accountants) owe a duty to the corporation's shareholders to **disclose or abstain** from trading on MNPI.
- **Tipper-tippee liability.** A tippee is liable if the tipper breached a duty by disclosing the information for a **personal benefit** and the tippee knew or should have known of the breach (*Dirks v. SEC* (1983)). A personal benefit includes a pecuniary gain, a reputational benefit, or a **gift** of information to a trading relative or friend (*Salman v. United States* (2016)).
- **Misappropriation theory.** A person who trades on MNPI in breach of a duty owed to the **source** of the information, by deceiving the source, violates § 10(b) even though she owes no duty to the shareholders of the traded company (*United States v. O'Hagan* (1997)).

Rule 10b5-1 treats a trade as "on the basis of" MNPI if the trader was aware of it, with an affirmative defense for trading under a binding plan adopted in good faith before becoming aware, subject to cooling-off periods under the 2022 amendments. Rule 10b5-2 deems certain family relationships to create duties of trust.

### Section 16(b)

**Section 16(b)** requires **directors, officers and 10% beneficial owners** to disgorge to the issuer any profit from a purchase and sale (or sale and purchase) of the issuer's equity securities within a period of **less than six months**. It is **strict liability**: intent and use of inside information are irrelevant. Profit is computed by matching the **lowest-priced purchase** with the **highest-priced sale** within six months, regardless of the order of trades, and losses are not offset against profits from other matches.

### Applying the rules

For a fraud claim, run the five elements. For insider trading, identify the trader, the information (material and nonpublic?), the source and duty, and the theory. For short-swing profit, check the person's status and the time period, then match trades.
@example
title: A friend gets a tip
problem: Dan, a director of Zeta Corp., knows privately that Zeta will announce a failed drug trial tomorrow. He tells his close friend Ben, "Sell your Zeta stock before the news." Dan receives nothing. Ben sells 1,000 shares at \$50 and avoids a loss when the price falls to \$30. Liability?
step: Rule: tippee liability requires that the tipper breached a duty by disclosing MNPI for a personal benefit and the tippee knew or should have known of the breach (*Dirks*; *Salman*).
step: Dan, a director, owes a duty to Zeta's shareholders. The information is material and nonpublic.
step: Personal benefit: Dan gave a gift of valuable information to a close friend. Under *Salman*, that satisfies the test even without money.
step: Ben knew Dan was a director and that the news was confidential, so he knew or should have known of the breach.
step: Conclusion: Dan is liable as tipper and Ben as tippee. Ben avoided a \$20,000 loss, a measure often used for disgorgement.
@mistakes
- Treating negligence as enough for a Rule 10b-5 claim; scienter is required.
- Saying anyone with MNPI must disclose or abstain; the duty must arise from a relationship of trust (Chiarella), and the misappropriation theory runs to the source.
- Requiring that a tipper be paid; a gift to a trading friend or relative is a personal benefit.
- Computing Section 16(b) profit by matching trades in the order made instead of lowest-in, highest-out.
