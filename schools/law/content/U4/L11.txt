@article
An acquirer can buy a business in four basic ways, and the choice drives who must vote, who is liable and who can dissent. Once a bid arrives, the target's board faces its own duties: what it may do to resist, and what it must do once a sale is inevitable.

### Structures

- **Statutory merger.** One corporation merges into another by operation of law; the survivor acquires all the assets and liabilities, and target shareholders receive cash, stock or other consideration. A **consolidation** creates a new entity.
- **Triangular merger.** The acquirer forms a subsidiary. In a **forward** triangular merger the target merges into the subsidiary and disappears. In a **reverse** triangular merger the subsidiary merges into the target, which **survives** as the acquirer's subsidiary. The reverse form preserves the target's contracts, licenses and permits, so it is common when they are valuable and hard to assign, and the acquirer's own shareholders generally do not vote.
- **Stock purchase / tender offer.** The acquirer buys shares directly from shareholders, which avoids a target shareholder vote but requires each holder to sell, so holdouts remain unless a second-step merger follows.
- **Asset purchase.** The acquirer chooses assets and liabilities. The general rule is that the buyer **does not assume the seller's liabilities**, except where the buyer expressly or impliedly assumes them, the transaction is a **de facto merger**, the buyer is a **mere continuation** of the seller, or the sale is **fraudulent** to escape creditors. A minority of states (for example, California and New Jersey) add a **product-line** exception for product-liability claims.

### Approvals and appraisal

A merger requires approval by the **board of each constituent corporation** and then a **shareholder vote** of the target: under DGCL § 251, a majority of the **outstanding** shares entitled to vote; under MBCA § 11.04, a majority of all votes entitled to be cast unless the articles require more. A parent holding at least 90% of a subsidiary's stock may use a **short-form merger** with no shareholder vote (DGCL § 253). In an asset sale the **seller** needs a shareholder vote only if it sells **all or substantially all** of its assets (DGCL § 271; MBCA § 12.02); the buyer's shareholders usually do not vote.

**Appraisal rights** (DGCL § 262; MBCA chapter 13) let dissenting shareholders of the target demand a judicial determination of **fair value** of their shares, excluding value arising from the merger itself, in place of the merger price. The shareholder must follow the statutory procedure, including not voting for the merger. Delaware's **market-out exception** denies appraisal for shares listed on a national exchange or widely held, unless the shareholders must accept something other than stock of the surviving or another listed corporation (cash in lieu of fractional shares aside), so appraisal is available in an **all-cash** merger even for listed stock. Courts treat the deal price in an arm's-length sale as persuasive evidence of fair value (*Dell, Inc. v. Magnetar Global Event Driven Master Fund* (Del. 2017); *DFC Global Corp. v. Muirfield Value Partners* (Del. 2017)).

### Federal and state takeover regulation

The **Williams Act** requires anyone who acquires more than 5% of a class of registered equity securities to file a **Schedule 13D** (Exchange Act § 13(d)), now within five business days. Tender offers are regulated by § 14(d) and (e) and the rules under them, including a minimum 20-business-day offer period (Rule 14e-1). State **anti-takeover statutes** survive preemption challenges: *CTS Corp. v. Dynamics Corp. of America* (1987) upheld an Indiana control-share statute. **DGCL § 203** bars a business combination for three years with an **interested stockholder** (one owning 15% or more) unless the board approved the transaction or the acquisition beforehand, or the interested holder reaches 85% of the voting stock outstanding (excluding shares held by directors who are also officers and certain employee plans) in the transaction that makes it interested, or the board and two-thirds of the voting stock not owned by the interested stockholder approve the combination.

### Takeover defenses: Unocal

A **poison pill** (shareholder rights plan) lets existing holders buy discounted shares if a bidder crosses a threshold, so a bidder cannot proceed without board approval. *Moran v. Household International, Inc.* (Del. 1985) held that the board could adopt a pill. Because the board has a self-interest in resisting, defensive measures get **enhanced scrutiny** under *Unocal Corp. v. Mesa Petroleum Co.* (Del. 1985): the board must show (1) **reasonable grounds** for believing that the bid posed a danger to corporate policy and effectiveness (shown by good faith and a reasonable investigation, with independent directors) and (2) that the response was **reasonable in relation to the threat**. *Unitrin, Inc. v. American General Corp.* (Del. 1995) adds that a response may not be **preclusive or coercive**; if it is not, it must fall within a range of reasonableness. The BJR then applies to the board's choice. The board does not need to accept a bid it reasonably views as inadequate, but must deal in good faith.

### Sale of control: Revlon

In *Revlon, Inc. v. MacAndrews & Forbes Holdings, Inc.* (Del. 1986), when a break-up of the company is inevitable or the board decides to sell for cash, the directors' role changes from defenders of the corporate entity to **auctioneers**: they must seek the **best value reasonably available** to shareholders, and enhanced scrutiny applies. *Paramount Communications v. QVC Network* (Del. 1994) extends Revlon duties to a sale that results in a **change of control** (for example, a cash sale, or a stock sale to a company with a controlling holder), but a **stock-for-stock merger** with a widely held acquirer does not trigger them (*Paramount v. Time* (Del. 1989)). There is no single blueprint; the board must act reasonably, informed, and in good faith. **Deal protections** such as no-shop clauses and termination fees are allowed in moderation, but they cannot be so preclusive that they lock up the deal without a **fiduciary out** (*Omnicare, Inc. v. NCS Healthcare, Inc.* (Del. 2003)).

### Applying the rules

For a structure question, list the client's needs (preserving contracts, avoiding liabilities, speed, holdouts) and match them to a structure. For a defense, ask whether the board is selling or resisting, then apply Revlon or Unocal in turn.

title: A poison pill against an inadequate bid
problem: Hostile bidder Rex offers \$40 per share cash for Tal Corp., which has traded at \$31. Tal's independent board, advised by counsel and a banker who values Tal at \$55, concludes the bid is inadequate and coercive, and adopts a poison pill with a 15% trigger. Tal has no plan to sell. Rex sues. Result?
step: Issue: the standard of review and whether the pill is valid. Rule: defensive measures get Unocal enhanced scrutiny; a pill is permissible under *Moran* if the board reasonably perceives a threat and responds proportionately.
step: Revlon is not triggered: Tal is not selling or breaking up, so the board is defending the entity rather than conducting an auction.
step: Prong 1: a majority independent board, with counsel and a banker, reasonably found the bid inadequate relative to a \$55 value and coercive. That is reasonable grounds.
step: Prong 2: a pill with a 15% trigger is not preclusive, since Rex can win a proxy contest and replace the board, and it is not coercive. It is a proportionate response.
step: Conclusion: the pill is likely upheld. The board may keep it in place while pursuing its strategy, subject to continuing duties.
@mistakes
- Assuming the buyer in an asset sale inherits all the seller's liabilities; the general rule is the opposite, with four exceptions.
- Applying the business judgment rule directly to a takeover defense, skipping Unocal's enhanced scrutiny.
- Treating any sale of the company as triggering Revlon; a stock-for-stock merger with a widely held buyer does not.
- Denying appraisal in an all-cash merger because the stock is listed.
