@article
A **general partnership** is the default form of a for-profit business owned by two or more people. If you and a friend start a business together and never sign anything, the law may already treat you as partners, with unlimited personal liability for the business's debts. This is the sharpest lesson in the course: a partnership can be formed by accident.

### Formation

RUPA § 202(a) defines a partnership as the association of two or more persons to carry on as **co-owners** a business for profit, whether or not they intend to form a partnership. No writing, filing or fee is required. The test is conduct: do the parties act as co-owners? Evidence includes sharing profits and losses, joint control, contribution of capital, and holding themselves out as partners. The parties' label does not control, and a person who intends to be only a lender but in fact co-owns the business is a partner.

**Profit sharing.** Under RUPA § 202(c)(3), receipt of a share of the profits is **prima facie evidence** of partnership, but no inference arises when the share is received as repayment of a debt, wages or other compensation, rent, an annuity or retirement benefit, interest, or consideration for the sale of goodwill. In *Martin v. Peyton* (1934), lenders to a brokerage who received a share of profits as extra compensation for the loan, with limited oversight rights, were held not to be partners. Control, rather than profit sharing alone, is the more reliable sign of co-ownership.

### Default rules

RUPA treats the partnership as an **entity** distinct from its partners (§ 201), while the partners remain individually liable. Unless the partnership agreement says otherwise:

- Each partner has **equal rights in management** (§ 401(f)); ordinary-course matters are decided by a majority of partners, while acts outside the ordinary course and amendments to the agreement require unanimity (§ 401(j)).
- Profits are shared **equally**, regardless of capital contributions, and **losses are shared in proportion to profits** (§ 401(b)).
- No partner is paid for services, except for winding up (§ 401(h)).
- Each partner is an **agent** of the partnership for apparently carrying on its business in the ordinary course, and the partnership is bound unless the partner lacked authority and the third party knew it (§ 301(1)). The agency rules from Lesson 1 therefore carry over.
- Partnership property belongs to the partnership, not to the partners (§ 501). A partner's creditor reaches only the partner's transferable interest, ordinarily by a **charging order** (§ 504).

### Liability

Under RUPA § 306(a), all partners are **jointly and severally liable** for all obligations of the partnership, in contract and in tort. The older UPA made partners jointly liable on contracts and jointly and severally liable for torts; most states now follow RUPA. A creditor may sue the partnership and partners together, but under RUPA § 307(d) a judgment creditor generally must first obtain a judgment against the partnership and exhaust partnership assets before levying on a partner's personal assets, with exceptions such as partnership insolvency. A **new partner** is liable for obligations incurred before admission only out of partnership property (§ 306(b)).

**Partnership by estoppel** (§ 308): a person who represents herself, or consents to being represented, as a partner, is liable to a third party who reasonably relies on the representation and extends credit, even though no partnership exists in fact.

### Fiduciary duties

RUPA § 404 gives partners duties of **loyalty** and **care** and an obligation of good faith and fair dealing. The duty of loyalty is limited to accounting for profits from partnership business or property, refraining from dealing adversely to the partnership, and refraining from competing. The duty of care is limited to avoiding grossly negligent or reckless conduct, intentional misconduct or a knowing violation of law. In *Meinhard v. Salmon* (1928), co-venturers owed each other a duty stricter than the morals of the marketplace, "the punctilio of an honor the most sensitive"; a managing co-venturer who took a new lease opportunity growing out of the venture without telling his co-venturer breached that duty. Under RUPA the agreement may adjust but not eliminate the duties.

### Applying the rules

Ask in order: (1) Is there an association of co-owners for profit? Look at profit sharing, control and capital, and check the exceptions to the profit-sharing inference. (2) If so, what do the agreement and default rules say about shares and authority? (3) Who is liable to the third party, and has the creditor satisfied the exhaustion rule? (4) If no partnership exists, is there estoppel?
@example
title: Silent agreement, equal split
problem: Ana contributes \$80,000 and Ben \$20,000 to open a bike shop. They never discuss profit shares or sign anything. In year one the shop earns \$60,000 profit. In year two it loses \$40,000. Using RUPA defaults, find Ben's share of the year-two loss and say whether a partnership exists.
step: Formation: Ana and Ben carry on a for-profit business as co-owners. No writing is needed, so a general partnership exists.
step: Profits default to equal shares regardless of capital, so each partner's share of profits is one half.
step: Losses follow profits, so each partner bears one half of the loss.
step: Ben's share of the \$40,000 loss is $\frac{1}{2} \times 40{,}000 = 20{,}000$ dollars, even though he contributed only 20% of the capital.
@mistakes
- Dividing profits in proportion to capital contributions; RUPA's default is equal shares.
- Saying a partnership requires a written agreement, a filing, or an intent to be called partners.
- Treating any profit share as conclusive proof of partnership, ignoring the exceptions for debt repayment, wages and rent.
- Letting a judgment creditor go straight to a partner's house without addressing the exhaustion rule.
