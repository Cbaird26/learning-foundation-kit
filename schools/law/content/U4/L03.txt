@article
General partnership exposes every partner to unlimited personal liability. Lawyers and legislatures responded by creating forms that keep partnership flexibility but cap owners' liability. Both forms are creatures of **statute**: they exist only if someone files the right document with the state.

### The limited partnership (LP)

A **limited partnership** is formed by filing a **certificate of limited partnership** with the secretary of state. It must have at least one **general partner**, who manages the business and is personally liable for the partnership's obligations, and at least one **limited partner**, who is an investor. A limited partner's exposure is generally limited to the capital she contributed or promised to contribute.

**The control rule.** Under the older Revised Uniform Limited Partnership Act (1985), a limited partner who takes part in the **control of the business** is liable as a general partner, but only to persons who transact business with the partnership reasonably believing, based on the limited partner's conduct, that she is a general partner. Under the Uniform Limited Partnership Act (2001), the control rule was abolished: a limited partner is not personally liable for the partnership's obligations merely because she participates in management. Many states have adopted the 2001 approach, and some retain the older rule, so identify the jurisdiction's statute. Limited partners also do not have the same management rights as general partners, and a limited partner who also serves as an officer of a corporate general partner is commonly shielded.

A general partner owes fiduciary duties of loyalty and care to the partnership and limited partners. A **limited liability limited partnership (LLLP)** extends the shield to general partners as well, in states that authorize it.

### The limited liability company (LLC)

An **LLC** is an unincorporated entity that combines a liability shield for all owners (**members**) with partnership-style flexibility and, by default, **pass-through taxation**. It is formed by filing **articles of organization** (called a certificate of formation in Delaware). Without the filing there is no LLC; the owners who simply start a business together are most likely general partners and face unlimited liability. The members' **operating agreement** (written, oral or implied, depending on the statute) governs relations among the members and the company.

An LLC can be **member-managed**, in which the members manage, or **manager-managed**, in which designated managers manage. Under older statutes each member of a member-managed LLC is an agent of the company; the Revised Uniform LLC Act instead provides that a member is not an agent solely by being a member, so authority comes from agency law and the operating agreement. Under the Revised Uniform LLC Act, in a member-managed LLC each member has **equal rights in management** (a per capita rule), ordinary matters are decided by a majority of members, and matters outside the ordinary course or amendments to the operating agreement need unanimity. The uniform acts also default to **equal distributions** among members, while Delaware's statute allocates profits and losses by the agreed value of contributions. Because defaults differ, a well-drafted operating agreement states allocation, voting, transfer and exit rules expressly.

### Liability: what the shield covers

A member or manager is not personally liable for the LLC's debts solely because of that status. The shield does **not** protect a member from liability for her **own torts** or for obligations she personally guarantees, and courts can pierce an LLC's veil in the same circumstances as a corporation's, though the Revised Uniform LLC Act provides that failure to observe formalities is not a ground for piercing. Professional LLCs shield owners from the entity's debts but never from their own malpractice.

### Fiduciary duties and freedom of contract

Under the Revised Uniform LLC Act, managers and members in a member-managed LLC owe duties of **loyalty and care**, and the agreement may adjust them but may **not eliminate** them or the obligation of good faith and fair dealing; it may identify specific categories of conduct that do not violate the duty if not manifestly unreasonable. Delaware goes further: its statute permits the agreement to expand, restrict or even **eliminate** fiduciary duties, but not the implied contractual covenant of good faith and fair dealing. This is why the choice of governing law matters to investors.

### Other features

A member's **transferable interest** (the right to distributions) can usually be assigned freely, but the assignee does not become a member without consent. A judgment creditor of a member typically has only a **charging order** against distributions. An LLC with two or more members is by default taxed as a partnership, a single-member LLC is disregarded, and either may elect corporate treatment under the check-the-box regulations; Lesson 4 returns to tax.

### Applying the rules

For a liability problem, identify who is sued, whether the entity was validly formed, and whether the claim is the entity's obligation or the person's own conduct. For a governance problem, start with the operating agreement, then the statute's default, then fiduciary law.

title: Does the limited partner lose her shield?
problem: Lena is a limited partner in Harbor Ltd., a limited partnership in a state that has adopted the Uniform Limited Partnership Act (2001). Lena regularly attends planning meetings and votes on which tenants to accept. Harbor defaults on a \$300,000 loan and has only \$100,000 in assets. The lender sues Lena. Is she liable?
step: Issue: whether Lena's participation in management makes her liable for Harbor's debts. Rule: under the 2001 Act, a limited partner is not liable for the partnership's obligations solely because she participates in the control of the business.
step: Contrast the older control rule: under the 1985 act, a limited partner who participates in control is liable only to persons who reasonably believed she was a general partner. Here the state follows the 2001 Act, so that rule does not apply.
step: Apply: Lena's votes and attendance are participation in management. They do not make her a general partner, and the lender's claim against her fails unless she has not yet paid capital she promised.
step: Conclusion: Lena is not personally liable; the general partner is liable for the \$200,000 shortfall.
@mistakes
- Applying the 1985 control rule in a state that has adopted the 2001 Act (or vice versa) without checking the statute.
- Saying an LLC exists once the members sign an operating agreement; the filing creates the entity.
- Assuming the LLC shield protects a member from her own torts or personal guarantees.
- Assuming that an LLC agreement may eliminate fiduciary duties everywhere; the uniform act forbids it, while Delaware allows it.
