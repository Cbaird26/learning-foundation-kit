@article
No form is best for every business. A lawyer advising a client who is starting a business first asks what the client wants, then maps the goals onto the forms. The usual factors are **liability**, **taxation**, **management and control**, **ability to raise capital**, **transferability of ownership**, **continuity** and **formalities and cost**.

### The forms compared

- **Sole proprietorship.** One owner, no filing. No liability shield; income is taxed to the owner. Simple and cheap, but all personal assets are exposed.
- **General partnership.** Two or more co-owners, no filing needed. Partners are jointly and severally liable. Pass-through taxation. Flexible management, but each partner is an agent who can bind the others.
- **Limited partnership.** Requires a filing. General partners are liable; limited partners are protected. Pass-through taxation. Useful for passive investors, with the cost of unlimited liability for the general partner (often solved by making the general partner an entity).
- **LLC.** Requires a filing. Shields all members; extremely flexible governance under an operating agreement; by default taxed as a partnership (or disregarded if a single member), with the option to elect corporate taxation.
- **C corporation.** Requires articles of incorporation. Full shield for shareholders, centralized management in a board, free transferability, easy to issue multiple classes of stock. Taxed as a separate entity.
- **S corporation.** Not a separate state-law form but a **tax election** by a qualifying corporation under I.R.C. § 1361 and following. It keeps the corporate shield and, by passing income through to shareholders, avoids a corporate-level tax.

### Taxation

A **C corporation** is a taxpayer. Under I.R.C. § 11 the corporation pays tax on its income at the corporate rate, and when it distributes after-tax profits as **dividends**, the shareholders pay tax again. This is the classic **double taxation**. A **pass-through** entity (partnership, LLC taxed as a partnership, S corporation) pays no entity-level income tax; instead the owners report their shares of income on their own returns, whether or not cash is distributed. Pass-through treatment often costs owners tax on income they have not received, so agreements usually require tax distributions.

An **S corporation** election requires that the corporation be a domestic corporation with no more than **100 shareholders**, only **eligible shareholders** (individuals who are U.S. citizens or residents, certain trusts and estates; not partnerships, corporations or nonresident aliens) and only **one class of stock** (differences in voting rights alone are permitted). An LLC may elect to be taxed as a corporation, and may then elect S status if it qualifies, under the "check-the-box" regulations.

### Matching form to goals

- **Seeking venture capital or an eventual public offering.** Investors typically require preferred stock, which an S corporation may not issue, and many funds cannot hold pass-through interests, so a **C corporation**, usually incorporated in Delaware, is the norm.
- **Small group wanting liability protection, pass-through tax and flexible governance.** An **LLC** is the usual choice.
- **Passive investors plus an active sponsor.** An **LP** or an LLC with manager management and investor members.
- **Professional practice.** A professional corporation or professional LLC, noting that licensed professionals stay liable for their own malpractice.
- **Mission-driven businesses.** Some states offer benefit corporations and similar forms that permit or require directors to weigh public benefits.

### Where to organize

The **internal affairs doctrine** applies the law of the state of incorporation to a corporation's internal governance, so a company can incorporate in one state and operate in another. Delaware attracts many businesses because of its detailed statute, its specialized Court of Chancery and a deep body of case law. Operating in another state requires a foreign-qualification filing there.

### How to advise

A good recommendation is specific. It identifies the client's goals, names the recommended form, explains the main benefit and the main cost, and flags what could change the answer (for example, taking outside investment, adding foreign owners, or expecting early losses that owners want to deduct). Always add that the client should have an attorney and an accountant confirm the tax consequences, because the numbers change from year to year.
@example
title: Double tax versus pass-through
problem: Assume a corporate income tax rate of 21% and a shareholder tax rate on dividends of 20%. A C corporation earns \$100,000 before tax and distributes all of its after-tax income to its sole shareholder as a dividend. How much does the shareholder keep, and how does that compare with a pass-through entity taxed to its owner at 24% on the same income?
step: Corporate-level tax: $0.21 \times 100{,}000 = 21{,}000$, leaving \$79,000 to distribute.
step: Shareholder-level tax on the dividend: $0.20 \times 79{,}000 = 15{,}800$, leaving $79{,}000 - 15{,}800 = 63{,}200$ dollars.
step: Pass-through: the owner is taxed once, $0.24 \times 100{,}000 = 24{,}000$, and keeps \$76,000.
step: Under these assumed rates the pass-through form leaves \$12,800 more with the owner. That does not make it better in every case: investors, reinvested earnings (no dividend tax until distribution), and preferred stock can favor a C corporation.
@mistakes
- Saying an S corporation is a state-law entity type rather than a federal tax election by an eligible corporation.
- Recommending an S corporation to a company that plans to issue preferred stock or take investment from funds or foreign investors.
- Believing the LLC or corporate shield protects a professional from her own malpractice.
- Treating pass-through taxation as meaning the owners pay no tax; they pay on allocated income even without distributions.
