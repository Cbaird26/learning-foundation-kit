@article
Directors and officers are fiduciaries. They owe the corporation and its shareholders a **duty of care** and a **duty of loyalty**, which includes good faith. This lesson covers care; Lesson 8 covers loyalty. The central tension is that courts want directors to take risks on behalf of shareholders, so they do not second-guess business decisions that turn out badly.

### The duty of care

The standard is that of an ordinarily prudent person in a like position under similar circumstances; MBCA § 8.30(b) states the director must act in good faith and with the care a person in a like position would reasonably believe appropriate. The duty has two parts: a duty to **make informed decisions** (to read, ask and deliberate) and a duty of **oversight** (to monitor the business). Directors may rely in good faith on reports and opinions of officers, employees, committees and outside experts (such as lawyers and bankers) whom they reasonably believe competent (DGCL § 141(e); MBCA § 8.30(d)). Blind reliance without inquiry is not good faith. A passive director who never reads financial statements or attends meetings can be liable if a reasonable director would have prevented the loss, but the plaintiff must show causation (*Francis v. United Jersey Bank* (1981)).

### The business judgment rule

The **business judgment rule** (BJR) is a presumption that, in making a business decision, the directors acted on an **informed basis**, in **good faith** and in the honest belief that the action was in the corporation's best interests (*Aronson v. Lewis* (1984)). The rule has three consequences. It insulates the decision from judicial review, it allocates the burden to the plaintiff, and it protects directors from personal liability. A plaintiff rebuts the presumption by showing a breach of one component: the directors had a **conflict** (the duty of loyalty is implicated), acted in **bad faith**, were **uninformed** (gross negligence in the decision process in Delaware), or committed fraud or illegality. If the plaintiff rebuts the presumption in a loyalty case, the burden shifts to the defendants to prove the transaction was **entirely fair**; in a care case the question is whether the directors were grossly negligent. The BJR does not shield **waste**, a transaction so one-sided that no business person of ordinary judgment could conclude the corporation received adequate consideration. Courts review the decision-making **process**, not the substance, of the outcome.

### Smith v. Van Gorkom

In *Smith v. Van Gorkom* (1985), the Delaware Supreme Court held that directors who approved a cash-out merger after a brief meeting, relying on the chairman's oral summary, without reading the merger agreement and without a valuation or inquiry into the price, were grossly negligent and had not made an informed decision. They could not rely on the BJR. The decision alarmed directors and led the Delaware legislature to adopt DGCL § 102(b)(7) in 1986.

### Exculpation

**DGCL § 102(b)(7)** permits a corporation's charter to eliminate or limit directors' **monetary liability** for breach of the duty of care. It cannot protect against liability for breach of the duty of loyalty, acts or omissions not in good faith or involving intentional misconduct or a knowing violation of law, improper personal benefit, or unlawful dividends and repurchases (§ 174). An effective exculpation clause does not bar injunctive relief. Effective August 1, 2022, § 102(b)(7) was amended to allow exculpation of certain **officers** as well, but only for **direct claims** by shareholders, not for claims brought by or in the right of the corporation. The MBCA has a similar provision (§ 2.02(b)(4)). Because nearly all Delaware public companies have such clauses, a care claim for money damages is typically dismissed, and plaintiffs plead loyalty or bad faith instead.

### Oversight: Caremark

In *In re Caremark International Inc. Derivative Litigation* (1996) the Court of Chancery recognized that a board's failure to monitor can breach fiduciary duty. *Stone v. Ritter* (2006) held that oversight liability is a part of the duty of loyalty (good faith) and requires either (1) an **utter failure to implement** any reporting or information system, or (2) a conscious failure to **monitor** it, ignoring **red flags**. The standard is demanding: bad faith, not mere negligence. *Marchand v. Barnhill* (2019) held that boards must make a good-faith effort to implement oversight of the company's **mission-critical** compliance risks, such as food safety for a food producer. The Court of Chancery in *In re McDonald's Corp. Stockholder Derivative Litigation* (2023) held that officers also owe an oversight duty within their areas of responsibility.

### Applying the rules

Start by asking whether the decision involved a conflict. If not, the BJR presumption applies. Then ask whether the directors were informed, acted in good faith and had a rational purpose. If the plaintiff rebuts the presumption on care alone, check the charter for exculpation. For a failure-to-monitor claim, ask whether any system existed and whether red flags were ignored.
@example
title: The rushed merger vote
problem: The board of Dalton Co. meets for two hours. The CEO tells the board orally that a buyer offered \$55 per share cash, which he calls "fair." No director has read the merger agreement, no valuation exists, and the board votes to approve the sale. Dalton's charter has no exculpation provision. A shareholder sues the directors for money damages. Result?
step: Issue: whether the directors breached the duty of care, and whether the BJR protects them. Rule: the BJR presumes directors were informed, in good faith and acting in the corporation's interest; a plaintiff rebuts by showing the directors were uninformed, which in Delaware means gross negligence in the process.
step: Apply: the directors made the decision after two hours, relied on an oral summary, did not read the agreement, and had no valuation. Following *Smith v. Van Gorkom*, that is gross negligence in the decision process, so the presumption is rebutted.
step: Reliance defense: DGCL § 141(e) protects good-faith reliance on reports of competent officers, but a bare oral conclusion from the CEO about a price is not a report supporting an informed judgment.
step: Remedy: the charter lacks an exculpation clause, so the directors may be liable for damages. With such a clause, the claim for money damages would be barred because it rests on care alone.
@mistakes
- Saying directors are liable simply because a decision turned out badly; the BJR protects reasonable, informed processes.
- Assuming exculpation under § 102(b)(7) covers breaches of loyalty, bad faith or improper personal benefit.
- Treating oversight claims as ordinary negligence; Caremark liability requires bad faith, shown by an utter failure to implement a system or by ignoring red flags.
- Forgetting that the 2022 amendment lets a charter exculpate officers only for direct claims, not derivative claims.
