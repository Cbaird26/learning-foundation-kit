@article
When a director steals from the corporation, the injured party is the **corporation**. But the board that would normally decide whether to sue may be the wrongdoer's colleagues. The **derivative suit** lets a shareholder sue **on behalf of the corporation** to enforce the corporation's claim. Any recovery goes to the corporation, and the shareholder benefits indirectly.

### Direct versus derivative

*Tooley v. Donaldson, Lufkin & Jenrette, Inc.* (Del. 2004) asks two questions: (1) **who suffered the alleged harm**, the corporation or the shareholder individually, and (2) **who would receive the benefit of the recovery**, the corporation or the shareholder? A claim for **diversion of corporate assets** or mismanagement, whose harm to shareholders is only a fall in the share price, is derivative. A claim that the shareholder's own rights were impaired, such as denial of the right to vote or inspect, or a claim of a contractual right to dividends, is direct. A harm to all shareholders proportionally does not make a claim direct. Some claims can be both, and a state may classify a closely held corporation's claim differently under a statute or the court's equitable powers.

### Procedural requirements

Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 23.1 and the state analogues impose these requirements:

- **Standing: contemporaneous ownership.** The plaintiff must have been a shareholder at the time of the transaction complained of (or have acquired by operation of law shares from such a person) (Rule 23.1(b)(1); DGCL § 327). Most states also require **continuous ownership** through the litigation, so a shareholder who loses her shares, for example by a cash-out merger, generally loses standing, except where the merger itself is alleged to be fraudulent.
- **Verified complaint** pleading with particularity the plaintiff's efforts to get the directors to act, or the reasons for not making the effort (Rule 23.1(b)(3)).
- **Fair and adequate representation** of the shareholders' interests (Rule 23.1(a)).
- **Court approval** of any voluntary dismissal or settlement, with notice to shareholders (Rule 23.1(c)).
- The corporation is a **necessary party** (nominal defendant). Because the suit is for the corporation, a **security-for-expenses** statute applies in a few states.

### The demand requirement

The board decides whether the corporation sues, so a shareholder must first make a **demand** on the board to bring the suit, or show that demand would be **futile** and so is **excused**.

**Universal demand (MBCA § 7.42).** The MBCA and a number of states require a written demand in **every** case, and the plaintiff must wait **90 days** before filing, unless the demand is rejected earlier or the corporation would suffer irreparable injury from waiting. There is no futility exception. Then the court reviews whether the corporation's independent directors or a committee made a good-faith, informed decision to dismiss (§ 7.44).

**Delaware.** Demand is required unless excused. In *Zuckerberg v. Sandridge* (Del. 2021) the Supreme Court adopted a single test that replaces the older *Aronson* and *Rales* tests. For each director on the **demand board** (the board when the complaint is filed), the court asks:

1. Did the director receive a **material personal benefit** from the alleged misconduct?
2. Does the director face a **substantial likelihood of liability** on the claim?
3. Does the director **lack independence** from someone who answers yes to the first or second question?

If the answer is yes for **at least half** of the directors on the demand board, demand is **excused**. A showing that a minority is conflicted is not enough, because the board majority could still consider a demand impartially. Because of the exculpation clauses discussed in Lesson 7, a director faces a substantial likelihood of liability only for a non-exculpated claim, such as loyalty or bad faith.

**Making demand concedes independence.** A shareholder who makes a demand concedes that the board is capable of acting impartially, so the board's refusal is reviewed under the business judgment rule and is overturned only for bad faith or a failure to inform itself (*Spiegel v. Buntrock* (Del. 1990)). That is why plaintiffs often argue futility instead.

**Federal law.** Under *Kamen v. Kemper Financial Services* (1991), the law of the state of incorporation governs whether demand is excused in a federal-law derivative claim, unless it is inconsistent with the federal policies of the statute.

### Special litigation committees

After a demand or a suit, the board may appoint a **special litigation committee** (SLC) of independent directors to investigate and decide whether the suit should proceed. In *Zapata Corp. v. Maldonado* (Del. 1981), where demand was excused and the board had lost control of the claim, the court applies a **two-step review**: first it asks whether the committee was independent and acted in good faith after a reasonable investigation, with the burden on the corporation; second, the court may apply its **own business judgment** to decide whether to dismiss. *Auerbach v. Bennett* (N.Y. 1979) applies the business judgment rule to the committee's decision with no review of the merits of the decision. The MBCA relies on the independent-director determination in § 7.44.

### Recovery and fees

Any recovery belongs to the **corporation**. The successful plaintiff's attorney is paid a fee out of the recovery under the **common fund** doctrine, or under the **substantial benefit** doctrine when the suit confers a nonmonetary benefit.

### Applying the rules

Classify the claim (direct or derivative). If derivative, check standing, then the demand rule of the jurisdiction. In Delaware, count the directors on the demand board and apply the three questions. If the board forms an SLC, address independence and good faith.
@example
title: Is demand on the board excused?
problem: Vox Corp. has eight directors. The shareholder alleges that the CEO, Cy, who is also a director, caused the company to overpay for a company he owns. Cy received a material personal benefit. Directors Dee and Eli are Cy's direct reports and depend on him for their jobs. The other five directors are outside directors with no ties to Cy or the target, and the complaint pleads no facts showing a substantial likelihood of liability against any of them. The shareholder did not make a demand. Under Delaware law, is demand excused?
step: Rule: under *Zuckerberg*, demand is excused if for at least half of the directors on the demand board the answer is yes to (1) material personal benefit, (2) substantial likelihood of liability or (3) lack of independence from someone who is yes to (1) or (2).
step: Cy: yes to question 1. Dee and Eli: yes to question 3, because they lack independence from Cy.
step: The other five: no on all three questions. So three of eight directors are conflicted.
step: Three is less than half of eight (which is four). A majority of the demand board can impartially consider a demand, so demand is not excused.
step: Conclusion: the shareholder had to make a demand first, so the suit is subject to dismissal; by making demand, she would concede independence and the board's refusal would be reviewed under the business judgment rule.
@mistakes
- Treating a claim as direct because the shareholder lost value; harm to the corporation that reduces the share price is derivative.
- Excusing demand because some directors are conflicted without counting whether at least half of the demand board is conflicted.
- Applying the futility exception in a universal-demand state.
- Forgetting that the recovery belongs to the corporation and that the court must approve any settlement.
