@review
### Week by week

- **Week 1, agency.** Agency requires assent, action on the principal's behalf and the principal's right of control. A principal is bound by actual authority, apparent authority (the principal's manifestations to the third party) or ratification. A disclosed principal alone is liable; a partially disclosed or undisclosed principal and the agent are both liable. Respondeat superior reaches torts within the scope of employment; independent contractors usually do not create vicarious liability. Agents owe loyalty and must disgorge secret profits.
- **Week 2, partnerships.** A partnership forms from co-ownership of a for-profit business, with no formalities. Profit sharing is prima facie evidence, subject to exceptions. RUPA defaults: equal shares, losses follow profits, equal management. Partners are jointly and severally liable, with an exhaustion rule for personal assets; partnership by estoppel exists.
- **Week 3, LPs and LLCs.** Both need a filing. Limited partners are shielded; the 1985 control rule was repealed in the 2001 Act. An LLC shields all members but not their own torts; operating agreements may restrict, but under the uniform act may not eliminate, fiduciary duties (Delaware permits elimination).
- **Week 4, choosing a form.** Compare liability, tax, control, capital and transferability. C corporations face double tax; S corporations need at most 100 eligible shareholders and one class of stock.
- **Week 5, formation.** Articles, promoter liability, the MBCA's knowledge rule for defective incorporation, board management and limits on distributions.
- **Week 6, veil piercing.** Unity of interest plus injustice; undercapitalization, commingling, siphoning; passive owners are safe; *Bestfoods* for parents.
- **Week 7, care.** The business judgment rule, *Van Gorkom*, § 102(b)(7) (including officers for direct claims since 2022) and Caremark oversight.
- **Week 8, loyalty.** Safe harbors under § 144 (as amended in 2025) and MBCA subchapter F, corporate opportunity, entire fairness, *Sinclair*, *Weinberger*, *M&F Worldwide*.
- **Week 9, shareholders.** Plurality voting, cumulative voting, proxies, removal, § 220 and close-corporation duties.
- **Week 10, derivative suits.** *Tooley*, Rule 23.1, universal demand, *Zuckerberg*, special litigation committees.
- **Week 11, M&A.** Merger structures, appraisal, *Unocal*, *Revlon*, § 203.
- **Week 12, securities.** *Howey*, 10b-5 elements, *Macquarie*, insider-trading theories, Section 16(b).

### Common errors

Confusing apparent authority with the agent's own claims; assuming partnership needs a writing; treating an LLC as formed without a filing; applying the business judgment rule to a conflicted transaction; excusing demand when fewer than half the directors are conflicted; applying Revlon to every sale; treating negligence as enough under Rule 10b-5.

@exam self
prompt: Write an IRAC analysis. Pace Corp., a Delaware corporation, has seven directors. Director Dina owns Vista Services, which sells consulting to Pace at 40% above market. Dina told the board only that she had "a connection" to Vista, and four directors approved the contract. A shareholder who has owned shares since before the contract sues derivatively for the overpayment without making demand. Dina and the two directors she controls are conflicted; the other four directors approved the contract but have no personal interest and Pace's charter exculpates directors to the extent allowed by § 102(b)(7). Address the standard for the contract and whether demand is excused.
solution: Issue: whether the contract is protected, and whether demand on the board is excused. Rule: a conflicted transaction is protected if approved by informed disinterested directors after full disclosure, or by disinterested shareholders, or if it is entirely fair; without a safe harbor, entire fairness (fair dealing and fair price) applies, and loyalty claims cannot be exculpated. Demand is excused under Zuckerberg if at least half of the demand board received a material personal benefit, faces a substantial likelihood of liability or lacks independence from someone who does. Application: Dina disclosed only a vague connection, so the disinterested directors approved without knowing of her ownership or the markup, and the approval does not cleanse the transaction; there was no shareholder approval. Entire fairness applies, and a 40% markup is not a fair price, so the shareholder has a strong claim against Dina. For demand, Dina benefited and the two directors she controls lack independence, so three of seven are conflicted, which is fewer than half (3.5). The four other directors approved with incomplete information; because of the exculpation clause, they face a substantial likelihood of liability only if their approval involved bad faith, which the facts do not show. Conclusion: the contract is likely unfair and recoverable from Dina, but demand is probably not excused, so the shareholder should make demand (which concedes board independence and invites business-judgment review of any refusal) or plead particularized facts showing bad faith by at least one more director.
hint: Count the conflicted directors and remember the exculpation clause limits who faces a likelihood of liability.
rubric: States the safe-harbor routes and the disclosure requirement, and applies entire fairness.
rubric: States the three Zuckerberg questions and the at-least-half rule.
rubric: Counts three of seven conflicted, explains why the exculpation clause matters for the other four, and concludes demand is probably not excused.
