@article
Almost every business act is done through someone else: an employee signs a purchase order, a manager hires a driver, a partner makes a promise. **Agency** is the body of law that decides when one person's act binds or makes liable another. Business associations courses begin here because partnerships and corporations are built on it.

### The rule and its elements

Under the Restatement (Third) of Agency, **agency** is the fiduciary relationship that arises when a **principal** manifests assent that an **agent** will act on the principal's behalf and subject to the principal's control, and the agent manifests assent or otherwise consents. The elements are therefore (1) assent by both sides, (2) action on the principal's behalf, and (3) the principal's right of control. No writing and no payment is needed; a gratuitous agent is still an agent. An **independent contractor** is not an agent for tort purposes when the hiring party does not control the manner and means of the work.

### Authority: when the principal is bound by a contract

A principal is bound by the agent's contract only if the agent had authority or the principal ratifies.

- **Actual authority** is what the agent reasonably believes the principal wants, based on the principal's manifestations to the agent. It may be **express** or **implied** (reasonably necessary to carry out the express grant, or arising from custom and prior dealing).
- **Apparent authority** exists when a third party reasonably believes the agent is authorized, and that belief is traceable to the **principal's** manifestations to the third party. The agent's own claims cannot create it. Apparent authority can bind a principal even when the agent exceeded actual authority, so a secret instruction to the agent ("never sign above \$10,000") does not protect the principal against a third party who knew nothing of it. Apparent authority lingers after actual authority ends until the third party has notice.
- **Ratification** is the principal's affirmance of an unauthorized act, by words or by conduct such as knowingly accepting benefits. It must cover the whole transaction, be made with knowledge of material facts, and relates back to the time of the act.
- **Estoppel** and, in the Restatement (Second) of Agency, **inherent agency power** fill remaining gaps; the Third Restatement does not adopt inherent power as a separate doctrine, so treat it as a minority and older concept.

### Disclosed, partially disclosed and undisclosed principals

A principal is **disclosed** if the third party knows the agent acts for an identified principal, **partially disclosed** if the third party knows there is a principal but not who, and **undisclosed** if the third party does not know there is a principal. When an authorized agent contracts for a disclosed principal, the principal is the contracting party and the agent is not liable unless the agent agrees to be. With a partially disclosed or undisclosed principal, both the principal and the agent are liable to the third party, though the third party gets one satisfaction. The agent also can sue on the contract; an undisclosed principal, when discovered, may generally step in as well, subject to exceptions such as an express exclusion of agents.

### Tort liability: respondeat superior

An employer (the master) is **vicariously liable** for torts an employee commits **within the scope of employment**. An act is within scope if it is of the kind the employee is hired to perform, occurs substantially within the work time and place, and is motivated at least in part by a purpose to serve the employer. A minor **detour** remains in scope; a substantial departure for the employee's own purposes is a **frolic**. Intentional torts can be within scope when they grow out of the job and are motivated in part to serve the employer, but a purely personal grudge falls outside. An employer generally is **not** vicariously liable for an independent contractor's torts, with exceptions such as nondelegable duties and inherently dangerous activity. The employer may also be directly liable for negligent hiring, retention or supervision.

### The agent's fiduciary duties

An agent owes the principal duties of **loyalty**, **obedience** and **care** (and to disclose material information). The agent may not take a **secret profit** or deal for a competing interest without consent. The principal can recover the profit through disgorgement (an accounting or constructive trust), even if the principal suffered no loss, and may also discharge the agent and rescind the tainted transaction. The policy is deterrence: the principal cannot easily detect self-dealing, so the law removes the incentive.

### Applying the rules (IRAC)

For a contract problem, state the issue (was the agent authorized?), run actual, then apparent, then ratification, and conclude; then ask who is liable (principal, agent or both) based on disclosure. For a tort problem, first decide employee versus independent contractor, then scope. Always tie each element to a specific fact.
@example
title: The purchasing manager who exceeded her limit
problem: Acme Corp. makes Ana its purchasing manager and privately instructs her not to sign any contract above \$10,000. For two years Ana has signed Acme's supply contracts, and Acme has paid them. Ana signs a \$50,000 contract with Vendor, who has dealt with Ana before and knows nothing of the limit. Acme refuses to pay. Is Acme bound?
step: Issue: was Ana authorized to bind Acme to the \$50,000 contract? Rule: a principal is bound by actual authority, apparent authority or ratification.
step: Actual authority: Ana's express instruction capped her at \$10,000, so she reasonably understood she lacked actual authority for this contract.
step: Apparent authority: Acme placed Ana in the purchasing manager role and let her sign and pay supply contracts for two years. Those are Acme's manifestations, and Vendor, unaware of the limit, reasonably believed Ana was authorized.
step: Conclusion: Acme is bound through apparent authority. The secret limit does not defeat a third party who had no notice of it.
step: Remedy for Acme: it may seek damages from Ana for breaching her duty of obedience, but that does not release Acme from Vendor.
@mistakes
- Basing apparent authority on what the agent said or on the agent's title alone, when it must rest on the principal's manifestations to the third party.
- Treating a secret limit on the agent's authority as effective against a third party who had no notice of it.
- Holding an employer liable for an independent contractor's tort simply because the employer hired the contractor.
- Forgetting that an agent who contracts for an undisclosed or partially disclosed principal is personally liable too.
