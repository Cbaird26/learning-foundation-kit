@article
The starting point of corporate law is **limited liability**: a shareholder risks her investment, not her personal assets. **Piercing the corporate veil** is the equitable exception, under which a court disregards the entity and holds the shareholder directly liable. It is invoked in a small share of cases and is rarely granted, but it is a staple of exams.

### The rule and its elements

Courts use somewhat different words, but most apply two parts:

1. **Unity of interest and ownership (alter ego).** The corporation is a mere instrumentality of the shareholder, so that separate personalities no longer exist in fact.
2. **Injustice or fraud.** Respecting the corporate form would sanction a fraud or promote injustice. This is more than the mere fact that the creditor was not paid.

Both parts are needed in most jurisdictions. Some states state the test as a single balancing inquiry with the same factors.

### Factors

No single factor is conclusive. Courts look at:

- **Undercapitalization**: capital that is grossly inadequate in light of the business risks at the time of formation, often measured against expected liabilities and insurance.
- **Commingling** of corporate and personal funds, or paying personal expenses from the corporation.
- **Siphoning** of corporate assets through excessive salaries or distributions that leave the corporation unable to pay creditors.
- **Failure to observe formalities**: no meetings, minutes or separate records.
- **Nonfunctioning officers and directors**, and domination by one shareholder who treats corporate property as her own.
- Use of the corporation as a **facade** for the dominant shareholder's personal dealings, and misrepresenting who the contracting party is.

Observing formalities is the weakest factor on its own. Close corporation statutes in many states relax the formal requirements for small corporations.

### Contract versus tort

Courts are generally more willing to pierce for **tort** creditors, who did not choose to deal with the corporation and could not bargain for protection, than for **contract** creditors, who could investigate the company's finances and demand guarantees or security. Empirical studies show courts pierce in both settings and the distinction is not absolute. In *Walkovszky v. Carlton* (1966), a pedestrian injured by a taxi sued the individual who owned shares in a group of corporations, each of which owned two cabs and carried the minimum insurance. The New York Court of Appeals held the complaint insufficient to pierce, because it did not allege that the owner was conducting business in his individual capacity; the court said the law permits businesses to incorporate to limit liability, and it allowed an amended complaint to try to show use of the corporations as a conduit for personal dealings. A dissent would have found the inadequate capital and insurance enough. Compare *Kinney Shoe Corp. v. Polan* (4th Cir. 1991), where severe undercapitalization and ignoring of formalities by the sole shareholder supported piercing in a contract setting under the two-part test.

### Who can be reached

Only shareholders who **dominated** and used the corporation in the abusive way are liable; a passive investor who did not control the corporation generally is not. In some jurisdictions courts also look to individuals who exercised control without owning shares. **Reverse piercing** reaches corporate assets to satisfy a shareholder's personal debt; some states allow it in limited circumstances and others reject it.

### Parents and subsidiaries

A parent corporation is not liable for a subsidiary's debts merely because it owns the stock, shares directors and officers, or is part of the same corporate family. In *United States v. Bestfoods* (1998), the Supreme Court held that a parent is liable for a subsidiary's environmental cleanup costs under CERCLA only if the veil is pierced under ordinary common-law principles or if the parent itself operated the facility; officers may hold positions in both companies and the presumption is that they act for the entity they are serving.

### LLCs

Courts apply the veil-piercing doctrine to LLCs, but the Revised Uniform LLC Act states that the failure to observe particular formalities is not a ground for imposing liability on members or managers. Because LLCs have few formal requirements, courts focus on undercapitalization, commingling and misuse of the entity. The shield never covers the owner's own conduct: she remains liable for her own torts and personal guarantees regardless of piercing.

### Applying the rules

State the issue and who the plaintiff, defendant and entity are. Run both parts of the test, using facts from the record for each factor. Say whether the plaintiff is a contract or tort creditor and whether the defendant dominated the entity. Then conclude, including how much the plaintiff may recover: a pierced shareholder generally answers for the entity's unpaid liability, after the entity's own assets are applied.

title: A one-person contractor's unpaid supplier
problem: Dev is the sole shareholder, director and officer of Dev Builders, Inc. He keeps no minutes, deposits customer payments into a personal account, and pays his mortgage from the corporate account. When Supplier demands payment of \$80,000, Dev moves the remaining corporate funds to his personal account and Dev Builders has no assets. Supplier seeks to hold Dev personally liable. Result?
step: Issue and rule: whether to pierce the veil. Most courts require (1) unity of interest and ownership and (2) that respecting the entity would sanction fraud or injustice.
step: Unity of interest: Dev commingled funds, paid personal expenses from the corporation and observed no formalities. He dominated the corporation completely, so the entity was his alter ego.
step: Injustice: Dev moved the remaining funds out after the demand, leaving Dev Builders unable to pay a debt it owed. That is siphoning that frustrates a creditor, which is more than an unpaid bill.
step: Conclusion: Supplier is likely to pierce the veil and recover the \$80,000 from Dev. Supplier is a contract creditor, so the injustice showing carries more weight; the formalities lapse alone would not have sufficed.
@mistakes
- Piercing based on failure to hold meetings alone, without the injustice or fraud element.
- Treating a corporation's failure to pay a debt as enough injustice; some wrongdoing beyond non-payment is required.
- Reaching passive shareholders who did not control the corporation.
- Holding a parent liable for its subsidiary's debts solely because it owns the subsidiary's stock.
