@article
The **corporation** is the form that separates ownership from management and gives owners limited liability. It is a creature of state statute, so the first questions are how it is created, what happens when people act before or without creation, and who holds which powers once it exists.

### Formation

A corporation is formed when the **incorporator** files **articles of incorporation** (called a certificate of incorporation in Delaware) with the secretary of state and pays the fee. MBCA § 2.02 requires the articles to state the corporation's **name**, the number of **authorized shares**, the street address of its **registered office and agent**, and the name and address of each incorporator. Corporate existence begins on filing (MBCA § 2.03), and the filing is conclusive proof that the incorporation requirements were met, except in a proceeding by the state. After incorporation the organizers adopt **bylaws**, which are internal rules and are not filed, elect directors if the articles did not name them, and issue shares.

Corporations have the powers of natural persons. An **ultra vires** act (outside the stated purposes) is largely a dead doctrine: under MBCA § 3.04, the validity of an act may not be challenged on that ground except in limited proceedings by a shareholder, the corporation or the state.

### Promoters and pre-incorporation contracts

A **promoter** is a person who, before the corporation exists, arranges its formation and enters contracts for it. A promoter is **personally liable** on a pre-incorporation contract she signs, even if she signs "on behalf of" the future corporation, unless the other party agrees to look only to the corporation. The corporation is not liable merely because it later exists; it becomes liable when it **adopts** the contract, expressly (board resolution) or impliedly (knowingly accepting its benefits). Adoption does not release the promoter unless the third party agrees to a **novation**. Promoters also owe fiduciary duties to each other and to the corporation, so a promoter who secretly profits on a sale to the corporation must account for the profit.

### Defective incorporation

If the formation steps fail, three doctrines may preserve the shield:

- **De jure corporation**: the incorporators substantially complied with mandatory statutory requirements, so the corporation is valid against everyone.
- **De facto corporation** (common law): there was a relevant statute, a **good-faith, colorable attempt** to comply, and actual use of corporate privileges. It shields owners against private creditors but not against the state.
- **Corporation by estoppel**: a third party that dealt with the business as a corporation may be estopped from denying the corporation's existence when the contract was in the corporate name and the party relied on it. It applies mostly in contract cases.

The MBCA drops de facto and estoppel in favor of a statutory rule: under MBCA § 2.04, persons who act as or on behalf of a corporation **knowing that no corporation has been formed** are jointly and severally liable for resulting liabilities. Someone who does not know of the defect (a good-faith belief) is generally not liable.

### Who holds power

Under DGCL § 141(a) the business and affairs of the corporation are managed **by or under the direction of the board of directors**, and MBCA § 8.01(b) is to the same effect. **Shareholders** do not run the corporation; their rights are to **elect and remove** directors, vote on fundamental changes (charter amendment, merger, sale of substantially all assets, dissolution), and enforce fiduciary duties through litigation. The board appoints **officers** who run day-to-day operations; officers have actual and apparent authority under agency law (Lesson 1) within the usual scope of their offices. Directors act as a body at a meeting with a **quorum** (by default a majority of the directors) and decisions require the vote of a majority of directors present, unless the articles or bylaws say otherwise (MBCA § 8.24).

### Stock and distributions

**Authorized** shares are the maximum the articles allow, **issued** shares are those sold, and **outstanding** shares are issued shares not repurchased (Delaware treats repurchased shares as treasury stock, which is issued but not outstanding; the MBCA eliminates the concept). **Common stock** carries the residual claim and ordinarily the vote; **preferred stock** has priority on dividends or liquidation as the articles provide. Under the MBCA, shareholders have **no preemptive rights** unless the articles provide them.

A board may not authorize a **distribution** (a dividend or repurchase) if, after it, the corporation could not pay its debts as they come due (the **equity insolvency test**) or its total assets would be less than its total liabilities plus any liquidation preferences senior to the distributing shares (the **balance-sheet test**) (MBCA § 6.40(c)). Directors who vote for an unlawful distribution can be personally liable to the corporation (MBCA § 8.33). Delaware uses a surplus test (DGCL § 170).

### Applying the rules

For a pre-incorporation problem, ask who signed, in what capacity, whether the corporation adopted the contract, and whether there was a novation. For a defective-incorporation problem, ask whether the jurisdiction follows the MBCA or the common law and whether the defendant knew of the defect.

title: Promoter's lease
problem: Pia signs a five-year lease for retail space "on behalf of Greenleaf, Inc., a corporation to be formed." Greenleaf, Inc. is later formed, uses the space and pays rent for six months, then stops paying. Landlord sues Pia and Greenleaf. Who is liable?
step: Issue: liability on a pre-incorporation contract. Rule: a promoter is personally liable on a contract she signs before the corporation exists unless there is a novation, and the corporation is liable only if it adopts the contract.
step: Pia: she signed as promoter and Landlord never agreed to release her. She is liable.
step: Greenleaf: it was not a party when the lease was signed, but it adopted the lease by impliedly accepting its benefits, occupying the premises and paying rent. It is liable too.
step: Conclusion: Landlord may recover from both Pia and Greenleaf, once. Adoption by Greenleaf does not discharge Pia without a novation.
@mistakes
- Releasing the promoter automatically when the corporation is formed or adopts the contract; only a novation does that.
- Holding the corporation liable on a pre-incorporation contract it never adopted.
- Forgetting that the MBCA shields persons who believed in good faith that the corporation existed, and punishes those who knew it did not.
- Saying shareholders manage the corporation; under DGCL § 141(a) the board does, and shareholders mainly elect and remove directors and vote on fundamental changes.
