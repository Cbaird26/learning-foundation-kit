@article
The **duty of loyalty** requires directors, officers and controlling shareholders to put the corporation's interests ahead of their own. Where the duty of care asks whether the process was careful, loyalty asks whether the fiduciary has a **conflict** and, if so, whether the transaction is protected or fair. Loyalty claims cannot be exculpated under DGCL § 102(b)(7).

### Interested-director transactions

At common law, a contract between a corporation and one of its directors was voidable at the corporation's option. The modern rule is that such a **conflicting-interest transaction** is not voidable merely because of the conflict if it satisfies a **safe harbor** or is fair. The statutes are DGCL § 144 and MBCA subchapter F (§§ 8.60 to 8.63). The three routes are:

1. **Approval by disinterested directors** after full disclosure of the director's interest and the material facts (MBCA § 8.62 refers to "qualified directors").
2. **Approval by disinterested shareholders** after full disclosure (MBCA § 8.63).
3. **Fairness**: the transaction was fair to the corporation, with the burden on the fiduciary (MBCA § 8.61).

Delaware amended § 144 in March 2025 (Senate Bill 21). Under the amended statute, as generally taught in 2025 to 2026, a transaction involving a director or officer is protected from equitable relief or damages if approved by a majority of the disinterested directors (or a committee of at least two disinterested directors when a majority are not disinterested), or by an informed, uncoerced vote of a majority of the votes cast by disinterested shareholders, or if it is fair to the corporation. The amendment also defines **controlling stockholders** and provides safe harbors for their transactions, which is an area to verify against the current statute. Valid disclosure of the conflict and the facts is essential to every route. A director who conceals her interest or the material facts loses the protection.

### Corporate opportunities

A fiduciary may not take for herself a business opportunity that belongs to the corporation. Under *Guth v. Loft* (1939) and *Broz v. Cellular Information Systems* (1996), Delaware asks whether (1) the corporation is **financially able** to take the opportunity, (2) it is **within the corporation's line of business**, (3) the corporation has an **interest or expectancy** in it, and (4) taking it would put the fiduciary in a position **inconsistent with her duties** to the corporation. A fiduciary may take the opportunity if she first **presents it to the board** and the corporation rejects it. The **ALI** approach (Principles of Corporate Governance § 5.05), adopted in some states such as in *Northeast Harbor Golf Club v. Harris* (Maine 1995), requires senior executives to offer the opportunity to the corporation and obtain disinterested approval before taking it, and defines the category more broadly for executives than for outside directors. The remedy is a **constructive trust** over the opportunity or disgorgement of the profits, even if the corporation suffered no loss.

### Entire fairness

If a conflicted transaction is not cleansed, the court applies **entire fairness**, with the burden on the fiduciary to prove **fair dealing** (timing, structure, negotiation, disclosure and approvals) and **fair price** (*Weinberger v. UOP, Inc.* (1983)). The test is unitary, but both parts are scrutinized. The remedy can be rescission or damages equal to the difference between a fair price and the price paid.

### Controlling shareholders

A **controlling shareholder** (one who owns a majority of the votes or controls the board) owes fiduciary duties to the minority. In *Sinclair Oil Corp. v. Levien* (1971), the court applied **intrinsic fairness** only to transactions in which the parent receives a benefit to the exclusion and at the expense of the subsidiary's minority; a parent's pro rata dividends that treated minority holders equally were reviewed under the business judgment rule. In *Kahn v. M&F Worldwide Corp.* (2014), the Delaware Supreme Court held that a **controller buyout** is reviewed under the business judgment rule rather than entire fairness if, from the outset, it is conditioned on both (1) approval of a fully empowered, independent special committee that satisfies its duty of care and (2) an informed, uncoerced vote of a **majority of the minority** shareholders. If either protection is missing, entire fairness applies. In 2025 Delaware's amendments to § 144 modified these frameworks for many controlling-stockholder transactions, so check the current statute before relying on the older cases for a Delaware controller transaction.

### Other loyalty issues

Directors and officers may not compete with the corporation, use confidential information for personal profit, or set their own compensation without disinterested approval. Executive compensation set by a conflicted board can be reviewed under entire fairness.

### Applying the rules

Identify the fiduciary and the conflict. Ask whether a safe harbor applies: was the interest disclosed, and who approved (disinterested directors or shareholders)? If not, apply entire fairness: fair dealing and fair price. For an opportunity, run the four-part test and ask whether the board rejected it after disclosure.
@example
title: A director sells land to her corporation
problem: Dina is one of seven directors of Pace Corp. She owns a parcel she offers to sell to Pace for \$400,000. She discloses her ownership to the board, leaves the room, and the other six directors, none of whom has an interest in the parcel, review an independent appraisal showing a fair value of \$310,000, but approve the purchase at \$400,000 without discussion. A shareholder challenges the deal. Result?
step: Issue: whether the conflicted transaction is protected. Rule: a conflicting-interest transaction is protected by approval of disinterested directors after full disclosure, or by fairness; but approval must be informed and the directors must act in good faith.
step: Safe harbor: the disinterested directors approved with disclosure of Dina's interest. But they approved at \$90,000 above the appraisal without discussion, so the approval may not have been informed or in good faith. A rubber stamp does not cleanse.
step: Fairness: if the safe harbor fails, entire fairness applies. The appraisal shows the price was \$90,000 above fair value, so the price is not fair, and the lack of negotiation undermines fair dealing.
step: Conclusion: Pace may rescind or recover the \$90,000 overpayment from Dina. Approval that merely rubber-stamps a known overpayment does not satisfy the safe harbor.
@mistakes
- Saying any interested-director transaction is automatically void; modern statutes protect it if disclosed and approved by disinterested decision-makers or if fair.
- Treating approval by interested directors, or approval after incomplete disclosure, as a safe harbor.
- Assuming that a fiduciary who used her own money, rather than corporate funds, to take an opportunity cannot be liable for usurping it.
- Applying the business judgment rule to a controller buyout that was not conditioned from the outset on both a special committee and a majority-of-the-minority vote.
