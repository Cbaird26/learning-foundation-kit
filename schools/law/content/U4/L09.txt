@article
Shareholders are the corporation's residual owners, but they hold few direct powers. This lesson covers what they can do (vote, remove directors, inspect records) and how courts protect minority owners of closely held firms.

### Meetings, notice and quorum

A corporation must hold an **annual meeting** to elect directors. The corporation sets a **record date** to decide who may vote, and gives notice of the meeting (under MBCA § 7.05 and DGCL § 222, generally not less than 10 nor more than 60 days before the meeting). Unless the articles say otherwise, a **quorum** is a majority of the shares entitled to vote. Shareholders may act by unanimous written consent in most states, and Delaware permits written consent by the number of votes needed at a meeting unless the charter prohibits it.

### Voting thresholds

Directors are elected by a **plurality** of the votes cast under the MBCA default (§ 7.28) and under Delaware law absent a bylaw (§ 216), so the nominees with the most votes win. Many public companies adopt a **majority voting** bylaw with a director resignation policy. For other matters, the MBCA default is that the action is approved if the votes cast in favor exceed those cast against (§ 7.25(c)), while Delaware's default for non-election matters is a majority of the shares present and entitled to vote. **Fundamental changes** (charter amendments, mergers, sales of substantially all assets, dissolution) need board approval and then a vote of a majority of all the shares entitled to vote in Delaware and, generally, under the MBCA, unless the articles require more.

### Straight and cumulative voting

Under **straight voting**, each share gets one vote per seat, so a shareholder with a majority can elect every director. Under **cumulative voting**, a shareholder multiplies her shares by the number of seats and may cast all the votes for one candidate or split them. It helps minorities win representation. Most statutes, including the MBCA and Delaware, make cumulative voting **optional**, available only if the articles provide it; a few states mandate it. To elect $N$ of $D$ directors, a holder needs at least

$$\left\lfloor \frac{S \cdot N}{D + 1} \right\rfloor + 1$$

shares, where $S$ is the number of shares voting. For example, with $S = 1{,}000$ shares and $D = 5$ seats, one seat takes $\lfloor 1000/6 \rfloor + 1 = 167$ shares. Staggered (**classified**) boards and cumulative voting both limit a majority's control.

### Proxies and voting agreements

A **proxy** is a written authorization for another person to vote a shareholder's shares. A proxy is **revocable** at will unless it states it is irrevocable and is **coupled with an interest** (for example, a pledgee or a purchaser of the shares). A later proxy revokes an earlier one, and attending and voting in person revokes a proxy. **Voting agreements** among shareholders are generally enforceable, and a **voting trust** (a written agreement transferring voting rights to a trustee) is permitted if its statutory formalities are followed.

### Removal of directors

Under DGCL § 141(k), a majority of the shares entitled to vote may remove directors **with or without cause**, except that with a **classified board** removal is only for cause (unless the charter says otherwise), and with cumulative voting a director cannot be removed if the votes against removal would have been enough to elect her. The MBCA likewise allows removal with or without cause unless the articles provide cause only (§ 8.08). **Vacancies** are filled by the board or the shareholders as the statute and bylaws provide.

### Information and inspection rights

Under **DGCL § 220**, a shareholder who makes a written demand under oath has the right to inspect the corporation's stock ledger, list of stockholders and books and records for a **proper purpose**, that is, a purpose reasonably related to her interest as a shareholder, such as investigating mismanagement. To investigate wrongdoing the shareholder must show a **credible basis** from which a court could infer wrongdoing (*Seinfeld v. Verizon Communications* (2006)). Amendments in 2025 (Senate Bill 21) narrowed what counts as "books and records" to specified categories and require the demand to state the purpose with reasonable particularity, so read the current text. Under the MBCA, shareholders have inspection rights for specified records (§ 16.02). In public companies, SEC **Rule 14a-8** gives qualifying shareholders a right to put proposals in the company's proxy statement, subject to eligibility and subject-matter exclusions.

### Close corporations

A **close corporation** has few shareholders, no public market and owners who often also work as employees and directors. A minority shareholder can be trapped and **frozen out**: fired, removed from the board and denied dividends, while holding stock that cannot be sold. *Donahue v. Rodd Electrotype Co.* (Mass. 1975) held that close-corporation shareholders owe each other a duty of **utmost good faith and loyalty**, comparable to partners, and must give equal opportunity to sell shares back to the corporation. *Wilkes v. Springside Nursing Home, Inc.* (Mass. 1976) refined the test: the majority may show a **legitimate business purpose** for its action, and the minority may then show that the purpose could have been achieved by a **less harmful alternative**. Delaware rejects special close-corporation fiduciary rules, expecting minority holders to protect themselves by contract (*Nixon v. Blackwell* (Del. 1993)), though Delaware has a statutory close-corporation subchapter. Many states give a statutory remedy for **oppression**, such as **judicial dissolution** when the controlling shareholders act oppressively (MBCA § 14.30(a)(2)), often with a buyout alternative. Parties protect themselves with shareholder agreements, supermajority requirements, buy-sell agreements and deadlock provisions.

### Applying the rules

For a vote, identify the matter, the voting method, the quorum and the threshold, and compute. For a minority complaint, ask first whether the corporation is close, then whether the jurisdiction follows the Massachusetts, Delaware or statutory-oppression approach.
@example
title: Electing a minority director with cumulative voting
problem: Rho Corp. has 1,200 shares voting and will elect 6 directors. Its articles provide cumulative voting. A shareholder group holds 400 shares. How many directors can the group be sure of electing?
step: Compute how many shares are needed to elect one director: $\lfloor 1200 \cdot 1 / 7 \rfloor + 1 = \lfloor 171.4 \rfloor + 1 = 172$.
step: Compute for two directors: $\lfloor 1200 \cdot 2 / 7 \rfloor + 1 = \lfloor 342.9 \rfloor + 1 = 343$.
step: Compute for three directors: $\lfloor 1200 \cdot 3 / 7 \rfloor + 1 = \lfloor 514.3 \rfloor + 1 = 515$. The group's 400 shares fall short of 515.
step: Conclusion: the group can elect two directors (343 shares needed) but not three. Under straight voting it could elect none.
@mistakes
- Using the cumulative-voting formula when the articles do not provide for cumulative voting; in most states it is optional.
- Treating all proxies as irrevocable; a proxy is revocable unless it states it is irrevocable and is coupled with an interest.
- Applying Massachusetts's *Wilkes* test in Delaware, which does not recognize special close-corporation duties.
- Forgetting that a classified board changes the removal rule to for-cause only.
