@article
The scope-of-review question is how closely a court may examine the agency's decision once it is reviewable. The APA gives the answer in § 706. A reviewing court decides all relevant questions of law, interprets constitutional and statutory provisions, and determines the meaning or applicability of agency action. It must set aside agency action found to be:

- **(A)** arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law;
- **(B)** contrary to constitutional right, power, privilege or immunity;
- **(C)** in excess of statutory jurisdiction, authority or limitations, or short of statutory right;
- **(D)** without observance of procedure required by law;
- **(E)** unsupported by substantial evidence in a case subject to §§ 556 and 557 or otherwise reviewed on the record of an agency hearing provided by statute; or
- **(F)** unwarranted by the facts to the extent that the facts are subject to trial de novo by the reviewing court.

The court reviews "the whole record or those parts of it cited by a party," and must take "due account" of "the rule of prejudicial error," which means harmless errors do not require reversal.

### Substantial evidence

Section 706(2)(E) applies mainly to formal adjudication and formal rulemaking. **Substantial evidence** is "such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion." The court looks at the whole record, including evidence that detracts from the agency's finding (*Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB* (1951)), but it does not reweigh evidence. A finding can be supported even if a different conclusion was also reasonable.

### Arbitrary-and-capricious review

Section 706(2)(A) applies to informal rulemaking and informal adjudication. Review is deferential but not toothless. *Citizens to Preserve Overton Park v. Volpe* (1971) requires a "thorough, probing, in-depth" review of the facts, though the standard of review is narrow and a court may not substitute its judgment for the agency's. *Motor Vehicle Manufacturers Ass'n v. State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co.* (1983) provides the leading framework: an agency rule is arbitrary and capricious if the agency

1. relied on factors that Congress did not intend it to consider,
2. entirely failed to consider an important aspect of the problem,
3. offered an explanation that runs counter to the evidence before it, or
4. offered an explanation so implausible that it could not be ascribed to a difference in view or the product of agency expertise.

In *State Farm*, the agency rescinded a passive-restraint requirement without adequately considering the alternative of requiring airbags alone. The Court held the rescission arbitrary and capricious because the agency did not explain why it rejected an obvious alternative within the ambit of the existing standard.

The result is **reasoned decision-making**: the agency must examine the relevant data and articulate a satisfactory explanation with a rational connection between facts found and the choice made. It must respond to significant comments.

### Chenery: the agency's own reasons

Under *SEC v. Chenery Corp.* (1943), a court reviewing agency action may uphold it only on the grounds the agency invoked. Lawyers' **post hoc rationalizations** offered in litigation do not count. In *Department of Commerce v. New York* (2019), the Court held that a stated reason for adding a citizenship question to the census was contrived and could not be accepted because the stated rationale did not match the actual one.

### Changes in policy

A new policy need not be shown to be better than the old one, but the agency must acknowledge the change, show that the new policy is permissible, believe it to be better, and provide good reasons. If its new policy rests on factual findings that contradict earlier findings, or the old policy engendered serious reliance interests, it must provide a more detailed justification (*FCC v. Fox Television Stations, Inc.* (2009)). In *Department of Homeland Security v. Regents of the University of California* (2020), the Court held that the rescission of the DACA program was arbitrary and capricious because the agency did not consider the reliance interests of recipients or alternatives short of full rescission. *FCC v. Prometheus Radio Project* (2021) confirms that the court asks whether the agency's decision was reasonable and reasonably explained. In *Seven County Infrastructure Coalition v. Eagle County* (2025), the Court emphasized substantial deference to agencies' judgments in environmental reviews under NEPA.

### Legal questions versus judgment calls

The deference described here applies to the agency's factual and policy judgments. Questions of law are for the court (Week 9).

### Applying the standards

Identify the type of action. If it was reviewed on a formal record, use substantial evidence. If informal, ask the State Farm questions in order, and use the agency's own explanation. Note possible harmless error.

@example
title: Failure to consider an alternative
problem: The hypothetical Highway Agency rescinds a rule requiring side-impact beams in trucks. Its explanation addresses the cost of beams but does not mention a cheaper alternative, reinforced doors, that commenters raised and that would meet the safety goal. Is the rescission arbitrary and capricious?
step: Rule: under *State Farm*, an agency must consider important aspects of the problem and the obvious alternatives within the ambit of the existing rule.
step: Application: the agency identified cost but did not address the reinforced-door alternative raised in comments.
step: Conclusion: the rescission is likely arbitrary and capricious. The usual remedy is remand for the agency to reconsider and explain, not an order to restore the old rule.

@example
title: Choosing the standard of review
problem: A statute requires the Agency to revoke licenses only "on the record after opportunity for an agency hearing." The Agency revokes a license after an ALJ hearing. The licensee argues the finding of misconduct was not supported by the evidence. What standard applies?
step: Rule: § 706(2)(E) provides the substantial-evidence standard for cases subject to §§ 556 and 557 or reviewed on a statutory hearing record.
step: Application: the hearing was on the record under the statute, so § 706(2)(E) applies.
step: Conclusion: the court reviews the whole record to see whether a reasonable mind could accept the evidence as adequate. It does not reweigh the evidence.

@mistakes
- Using substantial-evidence review for informal rulemaking; that is arbitrary-and-capricious review.
- Defending the agency with reasons that appear only in its litigation brief; *Chenery* limits review to the agency's own grounds.
- Reading *State Farm* as letting courts substitute their judgment for the agency's.
- Treating any change in policy as automatically arbitrary; the agency must only acknowledge the change and give good reasons.

solution: Issue: whether the repeal is arbitrary and capricious under § 706(2)(A).
