- § 201(a): "The Secretary of the Hypothetical Agency for Heater Safety (the Agency) may issue rules establishing safety standards for residential heating products to protect against an unreasonable risk of injury."
- § 201(b): In setting a standard, the Agency shall consider the likely benefits, the costs to manufacturers and consumers, and technological feasibility.
- § 202: "Residential heating product" means a product that is sold or offered for sale to consumers and designed to heat living space, including portable electric heaters, portable gas heaters and fixed space heaters. It does not include a furnace or boiler installed in a building as part of the building's central heating system.
- § 301: The Agency may issue civil penalties of up to a stated amount per violation.
- § 401: Any person adversely affected by a final rule under this Act may seek review in a United States court of appeals. The Act contains no exhaustion requirement.
material: **Source 2: Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (hypothetical), published March 3 in the Federal Register.**

The notice cites § 201(a) and describes the problem as 1,200 residential fires a year linked to portable electric heaters. It sets a 45-day comment period and says the Agency is "considering whether to extend the requirement to portable gas heaters" but proposes no text for gas heaters. The notice says the rule would take effect 90 days after publication of a final rule. It says nothing about furnaces, boilers or landlords.

@review
### Week by week

1. **Structure.** Agencies exist by statute under Article I; the President supervises them under Article II. Know rule versus order (§ 551), *Chadha*, *Bowsher*, *Youngstown* and *Jarkesy*.
2. **Delegation.** The intelligible-principle test (*J.W. Hampton*); only *Panama Refining* and *Schechter* invalidated statutes; *Mistretta*, *Whitman*, *Gundy*, *Consumers' Research* (2025); private delegation (*Carter Coal*).
3. **Appointments and removal.** Officer or employee (*Buckley*, *Lucia*), principal or inferior (*Edmond*, *Arthrex*); *Humphrey's Executor* was overruled by *Trump v. Slaughter* (2026); *Seila Law*, *Collins*; the Federal Reserve and *Trump v. Cook* left open.
4. **Due process.** Protected interest first (*Roth*, *Loudermill*), then the three *Mathews* factors; *Londoner* versus *Bi-Metallic*.
5. **Rulemaking.** § 553 notice, comment and basis and purpose; exemptions; good cause; 30-day rule; logical outgrowth; *Vermont Yankee*.
6. **Adjudication.** "On the record" triggers §§ 554, 556, 557; separation of functions; *Withrow*; *Chenery* and *Bell Aerospace*.
7. **Availability of review.** Cause of action, final agency action (*Bennett*), standing, zone of interests, *Heckler v. Chaney*, ripeness, exhaustion (*Darby*), *Corner Post*, *Trump v. CASA*.
8. **Scope of review.** § 706(2): substantial evidence versus arbitrary and capricious; *State Farm*, *Fox*, *Regents*; *Chenery* limits post hoc reasons.
9. **Questions of law.** *Loper Bright* overruled *Chevron*; best reading; *Skidmore*; express delegations; statutory stare decisis; *Kisor*.
10. **Major questions.** *West Virginia*, *Biden v. Nebraska*, *Learning Resources* (2026), stated no more broadly than the holdings.
11. **Enforcement.** Subpoenas (*Morton Salt*), warrants (*Camara*, *Barlow's*), *Burger*, *Patel*, *Jarkesy*, *Axon*, § 2462.
12. **FOIA.** 20 working days, nine exemptions, burden on the agency, segregability, foreseeable harm.

### Common errors

- Teaching *Chevron* or *Humphrey's Executor* as current law.
- Skipping the protected-interest step before *Mathews*.
- Defending agency action with reasons that the agency never gave.
- Saying that *Jarkesy*, *CASA* or *Learning Resources* decided more than they did.
- Using calendar days where the statute counts working days.

@exam self
prompt: The hypothetical Agency for Transit Safety issues, without notice and comment, a "guidance" requiring every city bus operator nationwide to install cameras on all buses within one year. The agency relies on a 1970s statute authorizing rules "to promote the safe operation of public transit." An operator sues in federal court. (a) Was notice and comment required? (b) After *Loper Bright*, how would a court review the agency's claim of authority? (c) What role could the major questions doctrine play? (d) What review gates must the operator clear? Answer in organized IRAC form.
solution: (a) The guidance is a rule, since it is of general applicability and future effect (§ 551(4)). It creates a new binding duty, so it is legislative, not interpretive or a policy statement, and § 553(b)-(c) required notice and comment unless an exception applies. The agency claims none, and nothing shows good cause. (b) The court decides the best reading of "promote the safe operation of public transit" using text, structure and context, giving the agency's view only *Skidmore* respect depending on its thoroughness, reasoning and consistency; it will not defer because the phrase is general. If the statute expressly delegated discretion, the court would police the limits of the delegation and the agency's reasoning. (c) A nationwide mandate with large costs, resting on a general 1970s provision the agency has not used this way, invites the major questions doctrine, which requires clear congressional authorization for a power of vast economic and political significance. Supporters see this as protecting Congress's role; critics see it as vague and judge-made. A court may reach the same conclusion on ordinary statutory reading. (d) Final agency action (a binding guidance with legal consequences, under *Bennett*), standing (compliance costs are a concrete injury traceable to the guidance and redressable), zone of interests (an operator regulated by the statute), ripeness (a binding duty with penalties), no required exhaustion absent a statute or rule (*Darby*), and timing within six years of injury (*Corner Post*). Relief would be limited to what is needed for complete relief to the plaintiff after *Trump v. CASA*, with the vacatur question left open.
rubric: Classifies the guidance as a legislative rule and concludes that notice and comment was required.
rubric: States the post-*Loper Bright* approach (best reading, *Skidmore* respect, express delegation) without applying *Chevron*.
rubric: Applies the major questions doctrine neutrally and describes the review gates.
rubric: Addresses remedy under *CASA* without overstating it.
hint: Work through the four parts in order and apply a rule to each.

prompt: A hypothetical statute lets an agency revoke a license "after notice and opportunity for hearing." The agency staff investigated a broker, the same staff attorney then advised the agency head on the decision, and the head revoked the license after an informal conference. The broker sues. Which is the best analysis?
choice: The revocation is invalid, because § 554(d) bars the attorney from advising in every adjudication.
choice*: The formal adjudication provisions are likely not triggered because the statute does not require a hearing on the record, so § 554(d) does not apply of its own force; the broker's remaining claim is due process under *Mathews*, and combining functions does not by itself violate due process.
choice: The revocation is valid, because license holders have no protected interest and receive no process.
choice: The revocation is invalid, because agencies may never combine investigation and adjudication.
hint: Start with whether the statute triggers formal adjudication, then consider what remains.
solution: Without the words "on the record," the formal provisions, including § 554(d), are not triggered (*Florida East Coast*). Due process still applies to a license, and *Withrow* holds that combining investigative and adjudicative functions is not itself a violation, so the broker would need to show unfairness under *Mathews*.
