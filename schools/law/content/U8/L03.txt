@article
Two questions about personnel come up constantly in administrative law. **Appointments**: who may choose the people who exercise federal power? **Removal**: who may fire them? Both are about presidential control over the executive branch, and both have changed substantially in recent years.

### The Appointments Clause

Article II, § 2, cl. 2 provides that the President nominates, and with the Senate's advice and consent appoints, "Officers of the United States." But Congress may by law vest the appointment of **inferior officers** in the President alone, in the courts of law, or in the heads of departments. The sorting questions are:

1. Is the person an **officer** at all? *Buckley v. Valeo* (1976) holds that an officer is someone who exercises significant authority pursuant to the laws of the United States. *Lucia v. SEC* (2018) applied this to hold that SEC administrative law judges, who preside over hearings, take testimony, rule on evidence and issue decisions, are officers and not mere employees. The remedy for a successful challenge was a new hearing before a different, properly appointed official.
2. If an officer, is the person **principal** or **inferior**? *Edmond v. United States* (1997) holds that an inferior officer is one whose work is directed and supervised at some level by a principal officer. In *United States v. Arthrex* (2021), the Court held that patent judges whose decisions could not be reviewed by any principal officer exercised too much unsupervised power for inferior officers. The fix was to let the agency head review their decisions.

An official who is a mere employee, performing ministerial or advisory work, need not be appointed under the Clause. Another limit is timing: under *NLRB v. Noel Canning* (2014), the President's recess-appointment power is available during an intra-session recess, but a recess of less than ten days is presumptively too short, and the Senate may hold pro forma sessions that prevent a recess.

### Removal

The Constitution does not mention removal. Reading the Vesting and Take Care Clauses, *Myers v. United States* (1926) held that the President may remove executive officers without Senate consent. *Humphrey's Executor v. United States* (1935) carved out an exception for multi-member expert commissions exercising quasi-legislative and quasi-judicial powers, upholding for-cause protection for FTC commissioners.

That exception was gradually narrowed. *Free Enterprise Fund v. PCAOB* (2010) invalidated two layers of for-cause protection between the President and an inferior officer. *Seila Law LLC v. CFPB* (2020) held that for-cause protection for the single Director of the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau violated the separation of powers. *Collins v. Yellen* (2021) reached the same conclusion for the Director of the Federal Housing Finance Agency, and held that an unconstitutional removal restriction does not by itself void the official's actions; a plaintiff must show the restriction caused harm.

In *Trump v. Slaughter* (June 2026, 6–3), the Court overruled *Humphrey's Executor*. It held that the FTC Act's for-cause removal protection for commissioners is unconstitutional, so FTC commissioners may be removed at will. The majority reasoned that executive power is vested in the President, who must be able to control those who exercise it. The dissent argued that Congress long designed independent expert commissions and that the decision erodes that design and the Court's own precedent. The Court left open the Federal Reserve and offices outside the traditional executive branch. In *Trump v. Cook* (2026), the Court refused to allow the removal of a Federal Reserve governor to take effect while that case continues; the merits remain unresolved.

Do not teach *Humphrey's Executor* as current law. Statutes that protect other officials, such as administrative law judges, who are removable only for good cause under 5 U.S.C. § 7521, raise further questions the Supreme Court has not resolved.

### Applying the framework

For a personnel question, first decide whether the person is an officer, then whether principal or inferior and who appointed them. For removal, decide what the person does, how the removal restriction operates (one layer or two, single head or multi-member), and whether it is controlled by *Slaughter*, *Seila Law*, *Collins* or the open question about the Federal Reserve.

@example
title: An unappointed hearing officer
problem: The hypothetical Securities Review Agency employs hearing officers who preside over enforcement hearings, take testimony, and issue initial decisions that become final unless the agency reviews them. The officers were hired by agency staff, not by the agency head. A respondent objects. What is the analysis?
step: Issue: are the hearing officers officers of the United States, and if so, were they properly appointed?
step: Rule: under *Buckley* and *Lucia*, a person who exercises significant authority, such as presiding at hearings and issuing decisions, is an officer.
step: Application: these hearing officers hold hearings, take testimony and issue decisions, so they are officers. Hiring by staff does not satisfy the Clause, which requires appointment by the President, a court, or a department head.
step: Conclusion: the respondent should win a new hearing before a properly appointed official, the remedy used in *Lucia*.

@example
title: A removal restriction
problem: A statute says the five members of the hypothetical Trade Board may be removed by the President only for "inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance." The President removes a member for policy disagreement and the member sues. How should a court analyze this after 2026?
step: Rule: *Trump v. Slaughter* overruled *Humphrey's Executor* and held that the FTC's for-cause protection was unconstitutional.
step: Application: the Trade Board is a multi-member commission exercising executive power like the FTC, so the same reasoning applies.
step: Conclusion: the restriction is likely unconstitutional and the removal stands. A court would ask whether the Board is more like the FTC or like the Federal Reserve, which the Court left open.

@mistakes
- Teaching *Humphrey's Executor* as current law; it was overruled in *Trump v. Slaughter* (2026).
- Saying that *Slaughter* resolved the status of the Federal Reserve or of offices outside the executive branch; the Court left those open.
- Treating every unconstitutional removal restriction as voiding the agency's past actions; *Collins* requires a showing of harm.
- Calling every government worker an "officer"; the question is significant authority under federal law.
