@article
A court can review an agency action only if the plaintiff clears several gates. Think of availability of review as a checklist: (1) a cause of action, (2) a reviewable, final agency action, (3) Article III standing, (4) ripeness, (5) exhaustion, and (6) timing. Missing any gate ends the case before the merits.

### The APA cause of action

Section 702 gives a right of review to a person "suffering legal wrong because of agency action, or adversely affected or aggrieved by agency action within the meaning of a relevant statute." Section 702 also waives sovereign immunity for non-monetary relief. Section 703 identifies the forum and form of proceeding.

### Two limits on reviewability

Section 701(a) says the chapter does not apply where (1) a statute precludes judicial review, or (2) agency action is **committed to agency discretion by law**. The second is a narrow exception applied where there is "no law to apply." The leading example is nonenforcement: *Heckler v. Chaney* (1985) holds that an agency's decision not to take enforcement action is presumptively unreviewable, because it involves a balance of factors within the agency's expertise, though Congress can rebut the presumption by giving guidelines in the statute. In *United States v. Texas* (2023), the Court held that states lacked standing to challenge federal immigration enforcement priorities, because a litigant generally has no judicially cognizable interest in the prosecution or non-prosecution of another.

### Final agency action

Section 704 makes reviewable "final agency action for which there is no other adequate remedy in a court." Under *Bennett v. Spear* (1997), an action is final if (1) it marks the consummation of the agency's decision-making, not a tentative or interlocutory step, and (2) it determines rights or obligations or has legal consequences. A proposed rule and a staff recommendation are generally not final.

### Article III standing

The plaintiff must show an **injury in fact** (concrete, particularized, actual or imminent), **causation** (fairly traceable to the defendant's conduct) and **redressability** (a favorable decision is likely to remedy the injury) (*Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife* (1992)). A concrete injury can be economic, physical or other tangible harm; bare statutory violations without concrete harm do not suffice (*TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez* (2021)). A speculative chain of events or a plaintiff's disagreement with policy is not enough. In *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* (2024), the Court held that doctors and medical organizations lacked standing to challenge FDA's regulation of a drug that they did not prescribe, because they had not shown they would be injured. Regulated parties challenging a rule that directly burdens them usually have standing; third parties often must show a more complicated causal path.

### The zone of interests

Under § 702, the plaintiff's interest must also be arguably within the zone of interests protected or regulated by the statute at issue (*Association of Data Processing Service Organizations v. Camp* (1970)). The test is not especially demanding under the APA: the plaintiff loses only if its interests are so marginally related to or inconsistent with the statute's purposes that Congress cannot reasonably be assumed to have authorized the suit (*Match-E-Be-Nash-She-Wish Band of Pottawatomi Indians v. Patchak* (2012)).

### Ripeness and exhaustion

**Ripeness** asks whether a dispute has matured enough for review. *Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner* (1967) considers (1) the fitness of the issues for judicial decision and (2) the hardship to the parties of withholding review. A pre-enforcement challenge to a final rule that forces a party to change its behavior at the risk of penalty is typically ripe.

**Exhaustion** requires a litigant to use available administrative remedies first. Under the APA, however, *Darby v. Cisneros* (1993) holds that a court may not require exhaustion of further administrative appeals unless a statute or agency rule requires it and the agency action is made inoperative pending the appeal. Statutes may require exhaustion, and courts enforce those.

### Timing and remedy

Most APA suits are subject to the six-year statute of limitations in 28 U.S.C. § 2401(a). In *Corner Post, Inc. v. Board of Governors* (2024), the Court held that an APA claim accrues when the plaintiff is injured by the rule, not when the rule was issued. A business formed after a rule was issued can therefore challenge the rule within six years after it is first injured.

On remedies, *Trump v. CASA, Inc.* (2025) held that universal injunctions likely exceed the equitable authority Congress gave federal courts, and that relief is limited to what is needed to give the plaintiffs complete relief. The Court did not decide whether the APA's authorization to "set aside" agency action permits vacatur of a rule for everyone; that question remains contested.

### Applying the checklist

For each gate, state the rule and the facts. A strong answer notes the best argument against the plaintiff at each gate and ends with a conclusion on whether the court can reach the merits.

@example
title: A challenge to a staff memo
problem: The hypothetical Dairy Agency's staff circulates a draft memo proposing to ban certain milk additives. A producer sues to enjoin the memo. The memo states it is subject to change and has no effect until the Agency's board votes. Is it reviewable?
step: Issue: is the memo final agency action under § 704?
step: Rule: under *Bennett*, the action must mark the consummation of decision-making and determine rights or obligations.
step: Application: the memo is a draft that may change and has no legal effect until the board votes, so neither prong is satisfied.
step: Conclusion: not final agency action; the suit should be dismissed as premature, and the producer may challenge the final rule if one is adopted.

@example
title: Timing under Corner Post
problem: In 2015 an agency issues a rule. Ben begins operating a business covered by the rule in 2022 and is injured by it that year. Assuming the six-year period in § 2401(a) applies, what is the last calendar year in which Ben may file an APA challenge?
step: Rule: after *Corner Post*, the claim accrues when the plaintiff is injured, not when the rule was issued.
step: Application: Ben's claim accrued in 2022.
step: The six-year period runs for six years after accrual, so $2022 + 6 = 2028$ is the end date. If the injury occurred in, for example, March 2022, the deadline falls in March 2028.
step: Conclusion: the last calendar year to file is 2028. (Under the older accrual-at-issuance view, the period would have expired in 2021.)

@mistakes
- Treating a proposed rule or draft as final agency action.
- Assuming a person who disagrees with an agency has standing; injury in fact, causation and redressability are all required.
- Saying that the six-year period always runs from publication of the rule; after *Corner Post*, it runs from injury.
- Overreading *CASA* to decide whether the APA allows vacatur; the Court left that question open.
