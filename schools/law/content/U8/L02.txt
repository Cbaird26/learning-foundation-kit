@article
Article I, § 1 provides that "All legislative Powers herein granted shall be vested in a Congress." The **nondelegation doctrine** asks whether a statute transfers too much of that power to someone else. Everyone agrees Congress cannot hand over its entire lawmaking power; the dispute is over how much discretion is too much. Because modern agencies fill in the details of broad statutes, this question underlies much of administrative law.

### The test: an intelligible principle

*J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States* (1928) states the modern test: Congress may confer discretion on another branch if it lays down an **intelligible principle** to which the recipient must conform. The statute need not decide every detail. It must tell the agency what it is to accomplish and give courts a way to tell whether the agency stayed within bounds. The usual questions are: what is the general policy, which agency applies it, and what are the boundaries of its authority?

### The only two invalidations

The Court has struck down statutes for excessive delegation twice, both in 1935. *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan* involved a provision letting the President prohibit certain oil shipments with no standard guiding the decision. *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States* involved a provision authorizing "codes of fair competition," which the Court viewed as giving the President virtually unlimited discretion across the economy. Since then the Court has upheld a long list of broad delegations.

### Modern cases

In *Mistretta v. United States* (1989), the Court upheld the delegation to the Sentencing Commission to write sentencing guidelines, noting that Congress gave goals and factors to guide it. In *Whitman v. American Trucking Ass'ns* (2001), the Court upheld the Clean Air Act's instruction to set air quality standards "requisite to protect the public health" with an adequate margin of safety, and observed that the degree of discretion that is acceptable varies with the scope of the power conferred: the broader the consequences, the more guidance is expected.

In *Gundy v. United States* (2019), a plurality upheld the delegation to the Attorney General to specify how the sex-offender registration law applied to offenders convicted before the statute was enacted, reading the statute as giving enough guidance. One Justice concurred in the judgment but signaled willingness to reconsider the approach in a future case. Three dissenters argued for a stricter test: Congress must make the policy decisions, and the executive may only fill in details, find facts or exercise authority within a stated rule.

In *FCC v. Consumers' Research* (2025), the Court (6–3) rejected a nondelegation challenge to the Universal Service Fund, the program funded by contributions collected from telecommunications carriers. The majority applied the intelligible-principle framework and concluded that the statute gave the FCC adequate guidance and that the arrangement did not unconstitutionally delegate. The dissent argued that the delegation lacked meaningful limits. The Court did not announce a new, stricter test.

### Private delegation

A separate rule concerns delegations to private parties. In *Carter v. Carter Coal Co.* (1936), the Court invalidated a provision letting a majority of coal producers set wages and hours binding on the rest. Private actors may advise or assist an agency that retains final authority; they may not wield binding regulatory power over competitors.

### Related tools

Courts often avoid the constitutional question by interpreting broad statutes narrowly. Another doctrine, the major questions doctrine, reaches similar concerns through statutory interpretation (Week 10). Some view it as a way to enforce nondelegation values; others view it as an ordinary canon of interpretation. In foreign-affairs settings, Congress has traditionally been allowed broader delegations (*United States v. Curtiss-Wright Export Corp.* (1936)).

### Applying the doctrine

Issue: does the statute give an intelligible principle? Rule: *J.W. Hampton* and its progeny. Application: identify the policy, the agency, the limits and any factors, then compare with *Panama Refining* and *Schechter* at one extreme and *Whitman* and *Mistretta* at the other. Conclusion: because the Court has upheld nearly all statutes, a challenge to a statute with any standards is likely to fail under current precedent; the arguments for a stricter test remain contested.

@example
title: Comparing two delegations
problem: Under the hypothetical Water Quality Act, Statute A directs the Water Agency to set discharge limits "as the Administrator deems wise." Statute B directs it to set discharge limits "to the extent necessary to protect fish and drinking water, considering technological feasibility and cost." Which is more vulnerable to a nondelegation challenge?
step: Issue: does each statute supply an intelligible principle?
step: Statute B names a goal (protect fish and drinking water) and factors (feasibility, cost), as in *Whitman* and *Mistretta*.
step: Statute A states no goal and no factors, so it resembles the standardless delegation in *Panama Refining*.
step: Conclusion: Statute A is more vulnerable, though a court might still avoid the issue by finding a purpose in the structure of the Act. Statute B likely survives.

@example
title: A private delegation
problem: The hypothetical Dairy Act gives a board elected by the largest dairies the power to fix prices that bind all dairies, with no agency review. A small dairy challenges the arrangement. Analyze the claim.
step: Rule: *Carter Coal* invalidated a statute that allowed private parties to set binding rules over competitors.
step: Application: the board is composed of the largest competitors and its decisions bind others with no agency approval.
step: Conclusion: the delegation is likely invalid. If the agency could approve or reject each price, the arrangement would resemble ordinary private advice and would likely be valid.

@mistakes
- Believing the Court strikes down delegations regularly; only two statutes have been invalidated, both in 1935.
- Saying that *Consumers' Research* adopted a stricter nondelegation test; it applied the intelligible-principle framework and upheld the program.
- Treating "broad" as the same as "standardless": a broad statute with a stated goal and factors usually satisfies the test.
- Forgetting that a delegation to a private party is analyzed differently from a delegation to an agency.
