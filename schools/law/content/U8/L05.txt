@article
Most federal regulations are made through **informal rulemaking**, also called notice-and-comment rulemaking, governed by 5 U.S.C. § 553. It is the main way agencies make binding law, and it is the reason many agency rules are challenged: skipping a required step is a common basis for invalidation.

### The three requirements

1. **Notice** (§ 553(b)). The agency publishes a notice of proposed rulemaking in the Federal Register. It must include the time, place and nature of the public rulemaking proceedings, a reference to the legal authority under which the rule is proposed, and either the terms or substance of the proposed rule or a description of the subjects and issues involved.
2. **Opportunity to comment** (§ 553(c)). The agency must give interested persons an opportunity to participate through written submissions, with or without oral presentation.
3. **Statement of basis and purpose** (§ 553(c)). After considering the relevant matter presented, the agency must incorporate in the rules adopted a concise general statement of their basis and purpose.

Under § 553(d), a rule must be published at least 30 days before its effective date, with exceptions for substantive rules that grant an exemption or relieve a restriction, for interpretive rules and policy statements, and for good cause stated by the agency.

Courts have also required the agency to disclose the data and technical studies it relies on, so the public can comment meaningfully, and to respond to significant comments in the final rule.

### Exceptions to notice and comment

Section 553(a) excludes rules involving a military or foreign-affairs function, and (a)(2) excludes matters relating to agency management or personnel or to public property, loans, grants, benefits or contracts (many agencies voluntarily use notice and comment for these). Section 553(b)(A) exempts:

- **Interpretive rules**, which state the agency's reading of a statute or regulation without changing legal rights and duties;
- **General statements of policy**, which announce how the agency will exercise its discretion in the future without binding itself; and
- **Rules of agency organization, procedure or practice.**

Section 553(b)(B) exempts rules for which the agency for **good cause** finds, and incorporates the finding and brief statement of reasons, that notice and public procedure are impracticable, unnecessary, or contrary to the public interest. Courts read good cause narrowly. An emergency, such as an imminent safety threat, can qualify; administrative convenience does not.

### Legislative versus nonlegislative rules

A **legislative rule** has the force of law because Congress delegated power to make such rules, and it must go through notice and comment unless an exception applies (*Chrysler Corp. v. Brown* (1979)). An interpretive rule or policy statement does not bind private parties or the agency in the same way, so it can be issued without comment. The test asks whether the rule changes legal rights and duties and whether the agency treats it as binding. In *Perez v. Mortgage Bankers Ass'n* (2015), the Court held that the APA does not require an agency to use notice and comment to amend or repeal an interpretive rule. A statute outside the APA can add its own requirement: in *Azar v. Allina Health Services* (2019), the Medicare statute's requirement of notice and comment for rules that establish or change a substantive legal standard applied to a policy that the agency had labeled as non-binding.

### Logical outgrowth

The final rule may differ from the proposal, but it must be a **logical outgrowth** of the proposal: interested parties should have been able to anticipate the change from the notice. If the final rule is so different that commenters had no fair chance to respond, the agency must give new notice.

### Limits on courts: *Vermont Yankee*

In *Vermont Yankee Nuclear Power Corp. v. NRDC* (1978), the Court held that reviewing courts may not impose procedural requirements on agencies beyond those in the APA and other statutes. Courts review rulemaking for compliance with the statute and for reasoned decision-making (Week 8), but they may not add extra procedures.

### Formal rulemaking

If another statute requires rules to be made "on the record after opportunity for agency hearing," then §§ 556 and 557 apply and the process is trial-like. Courts do not infer this requirement from vague language (*United States v. Florida East Coast Railway Co.* (1973)); the statute must require a hearing on the record.

### Applying § 553

Ask: Is the action a rule (§ 551(4))? Is it legislative or nonlegislative? Is a § 553(a) exclusion or § 553(b) exception available, and has the agency met its requirements (for example, a good-cause finding)? If notice and comment was required, was the notice adequate, were comments considered, and does the final rule follow logically from the proposal?

@example
title: A rule without comment
problem: The hypothetical Food Labeling Agency, under a statute authorizing "rules to carry out this Act," announces without notice that all juice labels must state sugar content in grams, effective immediately. The agency says the rule merely "interprets" the Act. Did it need notice and comment?
step: Issue: is the action an interpretive rule exempt under § 553(b)(A), or a legislative rule that required comment?
step: Rule: an interpretive rule states what the agency thinks the statute already means; a legislative rule creates new binding duties.
step: Application: the Act does not mention sugar disclosure, and the agency creates a new, binding obligation backed by penalties. That is the exercise of delegated lawmaking power.
step: Conclusion: it is a legislative rule that required notice and comment. No good-cause finding was made, and "effective immediately" also conflicts with the 30-day rule in § 553(d), so the rule is likely invalid.

@example
title: Counting the 30 days
problem: A final rule is published in the Federal Register on June 12. It is a substantive rule with no exemption from § 553(d). Assume the 30 days are counted starting the day after publication. What is the earliest permissible effective date?
step: Day 1 is June 13, so day 30 is the 30th day after June 12.
step: June has 30 days, so 18 days remain after June 12 (days 1 through 18 end on June 30).
step: The remaining 12 days run into July, so day 30 is July 12.
step: Conclusion: the earliest effective date is July 12.

@mistakes
- Labeling a rule "interpretive" or "policy" to avoid comment even though it creates new, binding obligations.
- Reading the good-cause exception broadly; convenience or delay is not good cause.
- Forgetting the separate 30-day requirement of § 553(d), which has its own exceptions.
- Having a court require extra procedures beyond the APA; *Vermont Yankee* forbids that.
