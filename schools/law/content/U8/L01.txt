@article
Administrative law is the law about the bodies that actually run most federal programs: the departments, commissions, boards and bureaus that write regulations, decide benefit claims, license industries and bring enforcement actions. The Constitution never mentions them by name. They exist because Congress, using its Article I powers together with the Necessary and Proper Clause, creates them by statute and gives them jobs, and because the President, who holds "the executive Power" under Article II and must "take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed," supervises the officials who staff them. A constant theme of this course is that agencies sit where those two sources of power overlap, and that the courts police the boundaries.

### The APA vocabulary

The Administrative Procedure Act (APA), 5 U.S.C. §§ 551–559 and 701–706, is the general statute that governs federal agency procedure and judicial review. Its definitions drive everything else.

- **Agency** (§ 551(1)): each authority of the federal government, whether or not it is within or subject to review by another agency, with listed exceptions. Congress and the federal courts are not agencies. The President is not an agency either (*Franklin v. Massachusetts* (1992)), so presidential action is not reviewable under the APA, though the officials who carry it out are.
- **Rule** (§ 551(4)): an agency statement of general or particular applicability and future effect designed to implement, interpret or prescribe law or policy. **Rulemaking** (§ 551(5)) is the process of formulating, amending or repealing a rule.
- **Order** (§ 551(6)): the final disposition of an agency in a matter other than rulemaking, including licensing. **Adjudication** (§ 551(7)) is the process of formulating an order.

The practical test is functional. A rule looks forward and speaks generally, like a legislature. An order resolves a particular matter, like a court. Later weeks show why the label matters: rules usually require notice and comment, and orders may require a trial-type hearing.

### Separation-of-powers limits on congressional control

Congress may create agencies and set their duties, but it must exercise its own power through the lawmaking process. In *INS v. Chadha* (1983), the Court held that a one-House legislative veto over an executive decision violated Article I, § 7: when Congress takes action with the force of law, it must pass both Houses (bicameralism) and be presented to the President. In *Bowsher v. Synar* (1986), the Court held that Congress may not keep removal power over an officer who executes the law; an official removable by Congress cannot be given executive duties such as ordering budget cuts. By contrast, Congress may shape agencies through statutes, appropriations, hearings and oversight.

### Presidential power and Youngstown

In *Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer* (1952), the Court held that the President lacked power to seize steel mills without statutory authority. The concurrence of Justice Jackson supplies the standard framework: (1) the President's authority is at its maximum when he acts with express or implied congressional authorization; (2) in the "zone of twilight," where Congress has been silent, authority is uncertain and turns on the circumstances; (3) when the President acts against the express or implied will of Congress, his power is at its lowest ebb and he must rely on his own constitutional powers alone.

### Agencies and Article III

Agencies adjudicate, but only Article III courts exercise "the judicial Power." Under the **public rights** line of cases, Congress may assign matters involving public rights to non-Article III adjudicators. But in *SEC v. Jarkesy* (2024), the Court held that the Seventh Amendment requires a jury when the SEC seeks civil penalties for securities fraud, because the claim resembles a common-law action and the public-rights exception did not apply. The case did not hold that every agency penalty needs a jury.

### Applying the framework

Organize any structural question as IRAC. Issue: which structural principle is at stake (lawmaking process, removal, judicial power, presidential power)? Rule: the case that states it. Application: compare the facts, noting what Congress did, whom the action binds and who controls the official. Conclusion: state the result and a reason.

@example
title: A one-House veto of a regulation
problem: Congress enacts the hypothetical Pipeline Safety Act, which authorizes the Pipeline Agency to issue safety regulations but provides that any regulation "shall not take effect if the House of Representatives, by simple resolution, disapproves it within 60 days." The House disapproves a regulation by resolution. Is the veto valid?
step: Issue: does a one-House veto of an executive action with legal effect comply with Article I, § 7?
step: Rule: under *Chadha*, action by Congress that alters legal rights and duties must satisfy bicameralism and presentment. A legislative veto by one House does neither.
step: Application: the regulation has legal effect, and the House resolution would nullify it without Senate passage or presentment to the President.
step: Conclusion: the veto is invalid and the regulation takes effect. Congress could instead pass a statute that repeals or limits the regulation, which would go through both Houses and the President.

@example
title: Rule or order?
problem: The hypothetical Federal Marine Agency (a) announces that all commercial ferries must carry a new type of radio starting next year, and (b) denies one ferry company's application for a route license. Classify each action under § 551.
step: Look at generality and timing. Action (a) applies to all commercial ferries and operates prospectively, so it fits the definition of a rule.
step: Action (b) is a final disposition of a particular application, and the definition of an order expressly includes licensing.
step: Conclusion: (a) is a rule produced by rulemaking, and (b) is an order produced by adjudication. Different APA procedures attach to each.

@mistakes
- Treating the President or Congress as an "agency" under the APA; § 551(1) excludes Congress and the courts, and *Franklin* excludes the President.
- Classifying by the agency's label rather than by function: a document called a "decision" can be a rule if it states general policy for the future.
- Saying that *Chadha* bars all congressional control of agencies; it bars lawmaking outside bicameralism and presentment, not statutes, appropriations or oversight.
- Reading *Jarkesy* as requiring a jury for every agency penalty.
