@article
**Adjudication** is the process for formulating an order (§ 551(7)). The APA creates two tracks. **Formal adjudication** is governed by §§ 554, 556 and 557 and resembles a trial. **Informal adjudication** is every other adjudication; the APA says very little about it.

### When formal procedures apply

Section 554(a) applies "in every case of adjudication required by statute to be determined on the record after opportunity for an agency hearing," subject to listed exceptions (for example, matters subject to a subsequent trial de novo in court, the selection or tenure of employees, and military or foreign affairs functions). The trigger is statutory. In *United States v. Florida East Coast Railway Co.* (1973), a rulemaking case, the Court held that a statute that merely required a "hearing" did not trigger the formal procedures, because it did not say "on the record"; courts and commentators generally read the same words the same way for adjudication, though the case itself concerned rulemaking. Most agency adjudications, such as many benefit determinations, grants and licensing decisions, are therefore informal unless Congress said otherwise.

### Features of formal adjudication

- **Notice** of the time, place, nature of the hearing, the legal authority and the matters of fact and law asserted (§ 554(b)).
- **A neutral presiding officer**: the agency itself, one or more members of the body, or an administrative law judge (§ 556(b)).
- **Burden of proof**: the proponent of a rule or order has the burden (§ 556(d)).
- **Evidence**: any oral or documentary evidence may be received, but the agency must provide for exclusion of irrelevant, immaterial or unduly repetitious evidence, and a sanction may be imposed only on "reliable, probative, and substantial evidence." The Federal Rules of Evidence do not apply strictly, so hearsay can be admitted.
- **Cross-examination** as required for a full and true disclosure of the facts (§ 556(d)).
- **Decision on the exclusive record**, with findings and conclusions on all material issues of fact, law or discretion, and an opportunity to submit proposed findings (§ 557(c)).

### Separation of functions and ex parte contacts

Section 554(d) provides that an employee engaged in investigating or prosecuting a case may not, in that or a factually related case, participate or advise in the decision, except as a witness or counsel in public proceedings. This separation applies to adjudications under § 554 but does not apply to the agency heads themselves. Under § 557(d), once a proceeding is under way, interested persons outside the agency and agency decision-makers may not engage in ex parte communications about the merits, and such communications must be placed on the public record. The constitutional baseline is lower: in *Withrow v. Larkin* (1975), the Court held that combining investigative and adjudicative functions in the same agency does not by itself violate due process.

### Informal adjudication

Informal adjudication is governed by § 555 (for example, the right to counsel, prompt notice of denial with a brief statement of the grounds, and the agency's duty to act within a reasonable time), by any statute or regulation applicable to the agency, and by constitutional due process (Week 4). Courts review informal adjudications on the administrative record under the arbitrary-and-capricious standard (*Citizens to Preserve Overton Park, Inc. v. Volpe* (1971)); a court may require the agency to explain its decision when the record is inadequate.

### Rulemaking versus adjudication

An agency with both powers may usually choose how to proceed. In *SEC v. Chenery Corp.* (1947), the Court held that an agency may announce new principles in an adjudication, and need not use rulemaking. *NLRB v. Bell Aerospace Co.* (1974) held that the choice lies primarily within the agency's discretion, though abuse of discretion is possible, for example where retroactive application of a new standard would cause serious hardship. The tradeoff is that adjudication applies the law case by case and may operate retroactively, while rulemaking provides notice and public participation. A court reviewing an adjudication also considers only the grounds the agency invoked (*SEC v. Chenery Corp.* (1943)).

### Applying the APA

Ask: Is the proceeding an adjudication? Does a statute require a hearing on the record? If so, check each formal requirement, the separation of functions and the ex parte rules. If not, apply § 555, the agency's own regulations and due process.

@example
title: Formal or informal?
problem: A hypothetical statute provides that the Agency may revoke a broker's license "after notice and opportunity for hearing." The Agency revokes a license after a brief meeting at which the broker could present documents. Must the Agency follow §§ 556 and 557?
step: Issue: does the statute require a hearing "on the record," triggering § 554?
step: Rule: *Florida East Coast* held that a statutory hearing requirement without the words "on the record" did not trigger the formal procedures.
step: Application: the statute says "notice and opportunity for hearing," not "on the record," so the formal provisions are not triggered by their terms. The broker may still be entitled to due process, since a license is likely a protected interest.
step: Conclusion: the proceeding is informal. Section 555 and due process, not §§ 556–557, apply.

@example
title: Prosecutor advising the decision-maker
problem: In a formal adjudication, an agency's enforcement attorney who prosecuted a case against Acme Corp. also meets privately with the ALJ's staff to discuss how the ALJ should decide it. Is there an APA violation?
step: Rule: § 554(d) bars an employee engaged in investigating or prosecuting a case from participating or advising in the decision, except as a witness or counsel in public proceedings.
step: Application: the attorney is a prosecutor and advises the decision-maker in private.
step: Conclusion: this violates § 554(d). (The ex parte rules of § 557(d) address communications between interested persons outside the agency and the decision-makers, so § 554(d) is the operative provision for an agency's own prosecutor.) Acme may seek to set aside the order.

@mistakes
- Assuming every agency hearing is formal; the trigger is a statute requiring a hearing on the record.
- Thinking the Federal Rules of Evidence apply fully in formal adjudication.
- Treating the combination of investigation and adjudication within one agency as a per se due process violation; *Withrow* says it is not.
- Ignoring that § 554(d) does not apply to the agency head.

@practice self
prompt: Explain, in a short paragraph, the difference between formal and informal adjudication under the APA, including what triggers each and one procedural protection that applies to each.
solution: Formal adjudication is triggered when a statute requires an adjudication to be determined on the record after an opportunity for an agency hearing (§ 554(a); *Florida East Coast*). It follows §§ 554, 556 and 557: notice, a neutral presiding officer, burden on the proponent, a hearing record and a decision based on it, separation of investigative and prosecuting functions (§ 554(d)) and limits on ex parte contacts (§ 557(d)). Informal adjudication covers all other adjudications. The APA imposes little beyond § 555 (for example, prompt notice of denial with a brief statement of grounds), plus any applicable statute or regulation and constitutional due process; courts review the result on the administrative record under the arbitrary-and-capricious standard (*Overton Park*).
