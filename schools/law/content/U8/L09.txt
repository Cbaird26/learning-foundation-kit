@article
For forty years, administrative law students learned **Chevron** deference: if a statute was ambiguous, a court deferred to the agency's reasonable interpretation. That is no longer the law. In *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo* (2024), the Court overruled *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council* (1984). You still need to know the *Chevron* framework, because earlier opinions are written in its terms, but it is history, not a rule you apply.

### The holding

The Court held that the APA, in § 706, requires courts to exercise independent judgment on questions of law and not to defer to an agency's reading merely because a statute is ambiguous. The majority reasoned from the text of § 706, which says the reviewing court "shall decide all relevant questions of law," from the long tradition that interpreting statutes is the judicial role (going back to *Marbury v. Madison*), and from the view that *Chevron*'s premise, that ambiguity is an implicit delegation to the agency, was a fiction. It also observed that the APA's reference to the court deciding "all relevant questions of law" contrasts with its more deferential language for facts and policy judgments. The dissent argued that agencies have expertise on technical statutory questions, that Congress often leaves gaps expecting the agency to fill them, and that the decision shifts power from the agencies to the courts and would invite instability. Both sides agree the question is about the allocation of interpretive authority between courts and agencies.

The practical rule: a court determines the **best reading** of the statute using the traditional tools of interpretation (text, structure, context, canons), and then holds the agency to that reading. The statute can be difficult to interpret, but difficulty does not authorize deference.

### What remains for agencies

*Loper Bright* did not abolish all weight for agency views. Courts may give the agency's interpretation **Skidmore respect** (*Skidmore v. Swift & Co.* (1944)): the weight it deserves depends on the thoroughness of the agency's consideration, the validity of its reasoning, its consistency with earlier and later positions, and the other factors that give it "power to persuade." That respect is persuasive, not binding, and a court that disagrees follows its own best reading. Agency interpretations that are contemporaneous with the statute and long-standing may carry more weight.

The Court also explained that some statutes **expressly delegate discretionary authority** to the agency, for example by empowering it to regulate as "appropriate" or "reasonable," or to define terms. In those cases the court's task is to identify the boundaries of the delegation, to hold the agency within them, and to ensure that the agency's exercise of discretion is reasoned (for which arbitrary-and-capricious review applies; Week 8). The difference from *Chevron* is that the court decides whether Congress delegated, rather than presuming a delegation from ambiguity.

### Prior decisions stay

The Court said that holdings in earlier cases that relied on *Chevron* remain in force: the decision does not call those holdings into question, and reliance on *Chevron* alone is not a special justification for overruling them under **statutory stare decisis**. A court asked in 2026 to revisit a 2010 decision upholding an agency's reading must follow it, unless some independent ground such as an intervening statutory change or an ordinary reason for overruling applies.

### Agency interpretations of regulations

A related question is how courts treat an agency's interpretation of its own regulation (*Auer v. Robbins* (1997)). In *Kisor v. Wilkie* (2019), the Court limited that deference: it applies only if the regulation is genuinely ambiguous after the court exhausts the traditional tools of construction, the agency's reading is reasonable, and the interpretation is authoritative, implicates the agency's expertise, and reflects fair and considered judgment. *Loper Bright* addressed statutes, and how it affects *Kisor* is debated.

### Applying the framework

Issue: what does the statute mean? Rule: courts decide the best reading (§ 706; *Loper Bright*); agency views get *Skidmore* weight; where the statute delegates discretion, the court polices the limits and the reasoning. Application: read the text and structure first, then ask whether the statute grants discretion in express terms, and then ask how persuasive the agency's reasoning is. Conclusion: predict the outcome and say what would change it.

@example
title: Ambiguous term, no express delegation
problem: The hypothetical Wetlands Act regulates "navigable waters." The Agency issues a rule defining the term to include seasonal ditches. A landowner challenges the rule. How does a court review the definition after *Loper Bright*?
step: Rule: a court decides the best reading of the statute using traditional tools. Ambiguity alone does not trigger deference.
step: Application: the court asks what "navigable waters" means by text, history and structure. It may consider the agency's view, giving *Skidmore* respect depending on the rule's reasoning and consistency.
step: Check: does the Act expressly delegate authority to define the term? If it said the Agency "shall define the term," the court would instead ask whether the definition falls within the delegated boundary and is reasoned.
step: Conclusion: with no express delegation, the court adopts its own best reading, and the Agency wins only if that reading matches the rule.

@example
title: A prior Chevron holding
problem: In 2012, a court of appeals upheld an agency's reading of a statute under *Chevron*. In 2026, a challenger argues that the holding must be revisited because *Chevron* was overruled. How should the court respond?
step: Rule: *Loper Bright* stated that holdings based on *Chevron* are still subject to statutory stare decisis, and that reliance on *Chevron* alone is not a special justification for overruling them.
step: Application: the earlier case is a precedent about the meaning of the statute, and the challenger identifies nothing but the change in *Chevron*.
step: Conclusion: the court follows the earlier holding unless the challenger shows a separate ground for overruling it.

@mistakes
- Teaching *Chevron* two-step as current law.
- Saying that agency views no longer matter; they receive *Skidmore* respect and statutes may expressly delegate discretion.
- Believing that every earlier *Chevron*-based holding was overruled; those holdings stay in force.
- Confusing an express delegation of discretion with mere ambiguity.
