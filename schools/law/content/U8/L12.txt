@article
The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA), 5 U.S.C. § 552, gives any person a right of access to federal agency records. The default is disclosure; the agency may withhold only what falls within a statutory exemption. FOIA applies to federal agencies (not Congress, the courts or the President's immediate staff) and does not apply to state agencies, which have their own open-records laws.

### The request

Under § 552(a)(3), an agency must make records available to any person on a request that **reasonably describes** the records and follows published procedures. The requester need not give a reason or show a need. Fees vary by requester category, and fees can be reduced or waived if disclosure is in the public interest.

### Deadlines

Under § 552(a)(6)(A), the agency must determine within **20 working days** (excluding Saturdays, Sundays and legal holidays) after receipt whether to comply and notify the requester. In "unusual circumstances," the agency may extend the deadline by up to **10 working days** by written notice (§ 552(a)(6)(B)). A requester who is denied may appeal administratively, and the agency has 20 working days to decide the appeal. If the agency fails to meet the deadlines, the requester is deemed to have exhausted administrative remedies and may sue.

### Judicial review

A requester may sue in federal district court. The court reviews **de novo**, and the **agency bears the burden** of showing that a withholding is justified (§ 552(a)(4)(B)). Agencies often submit a **Vaughn index** (from *Vaughn v. Rosen* (1973)) describing withheld documents and the exemption claimed for each. An agency may sometimes issue a **Glomar response**, refusing to confirm or deny that records exist, where even acknowledging their existence would reveal protected information.

### The nine exemptions (§ 552(b))

Exemptions are narrowly construed. The most tested:

1. **Exemption 1**: properly classified national-defense or foreign-policy information.
2. **Exemption 2**: records related solely to internal personnel rules and practices of the agency.
3. **Exemption 3**: matters specifically exempted from disclosure by another statute that meets statutory criteria.
4. **Exemption 4**: trade secrets and commercial or financial information obtained from a person and privileged or confidential. In *Food Marketing Institute v. Argus Leader Media* (2019), the Court held that information is "confidential" at least where it is customarily and actually treated as private by its owner and the government provided an assurance of privacy.
5. **Exemption 5**: inter-agency or intra-agency memoranda or letters that would not be available by law to a party in litigation with the agency. This incorporates privileges such as the **deliberative process privilege** (protecting predecisional, deliberative documents), the attorney-client privilege and the work-product doctrine. Final decisions and adopted reasoning are not protected, while drafts and recommendations may be. In *U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service v. Sierra Club* (2021), draft biological opinions that were predecisional and deliberative were protected.
6. **Exemption 6**: personnel, medical and similar files, the disclosure of which would constitute a clearly unwarranted invasion of personal privacy. It requires balancing privacy against the public interest in learning what the government is up to.
7. **Exemption 7**: records compiled for law-enforcement purposes, but only to the extent disclosure, for example, could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings (7(A)), could reasonably be expected to constitute an unwarranted invasion of personal privacy (7(C)), or could reasonably be expected to disclose a confidential source (7(D)).
8. **Exemption 8**: matters relating to the regulation of financial institutions.
9. **Exemption 9**: geological and geophysical information concerning wells.

### Segregability and foreseeable harm

The agency must release any **reasonably segregable** portion of a record after deleting the exempt portions (§ 552(b)). Since the FOIA Improvement Act of 2016, § 552(a)(8)(A) allows an agency to withhold information only if it reasonably foresees that disclosure would harm an interest protected by an exemption, or disclosure is prohibited by law.

### Related open-government laws

The **Privacy Act** (5 U.S.C. § 552a) governs agencies' collection and disclosure of records about individuals and gives individuals access to their own records. The **Government in the Sunshine Act** (5 U.S.C. § 552b) requires that meetings of multi-member agencies be open to the public, with exceptions. The **Federal Advisory Committee Act** requires openness for certain advisory committees. These laws have different coverage and exemptions from FOIA.

### Applying FOIA

Draft the request with a specific description of the records, a fee statement and an agency contact. Then analyze any withholding: identify the exemption, apply its elements to the record, ask whether any portion is segregable, and test foreseeable harm.

@example
title: A deliberative memo
problem: A requester seeks (a) a staff draft memo recommending that the Agency deny a permit and (b) the Agency's final decision denying it, which adopts the memo's reasoning. The Agency withholds both under Exemption 5. Analyze.
step: Rule: Exemption 5 protects predecisional, deliberative inter-agency or intra-agency documents. A final decision, and reasoning the agency adopts as its own, is not protected.
step: Application: the staff draft memo recommending a result is predecisional and deliberative, so it fits unless the agency adopted it as its reasoning.
step: The final decision is the agency's decision, so Exemption 5 does not cover it.
step: Conclusion: the Agency may withhold the draft (subject to the foreseeable-harm requirement and to adoption of its reasoning in the final decision), but must release the final decision.

@example
title: Counting 20 working days
problem: An agency component receives a FOIA request on Monday, March 2, 2026. Assume no holidays and count the day after receipt as day 1 and weekdays only. On what day of March must the agency make its determination?
step: Rule: § 552(a)(6)(A)(i) gives 20 working days, excluding weekends and holidays.
step: Count: working days run Tuesday March 3 through Friday March 6 (4 days), then 5 days in each of the next three weeks (15 days), for 19 days ending Friday March 27.
step: The 20th working day is Monday, March 30.
step: Conclusion: the determination is due March 30. If the agency invokes unusual circumstances for 10 more working days, the deadline moves to mid-April.

@mistakes
- Using calendar days instead of working days for FOIA deadlines.
- Applying Exemption 5 to final decisions or adopted reasoning.
- Withholding an entire record when only part is exempt; segregable portions must be released.
- Assuming FOIA applies to state agencies, Congress or the courts.
