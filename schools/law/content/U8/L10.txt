@article
The **major questions doctrine** (MQD) is a rule of statutory interpretation: in "extraordinary cases" where an agency claims power of vast economic and political significance, a court will not read general or ambiguous statutory language to confer that power. The agency must point to clear congressional authorization.

### Origins and cases

The roots lie in *FDA v. Brown & Williamson Tobacco Corp.* (2000), where the Court rejected the FDA's claim that it could regulate tobacco, reasoning that Congress does not hide fundamental changes in the regulatory scheme in vague terms, a point the Court later put with the image of Congress not hiding "elephants in mouseholes" (*Whitman v. American Trucking Ass'ns* (2001)). The modern doctrine took form in:

- *Alabama Ass'n of Realtors v. Department of Health & Human Services* (2021): the CDC lacked authority under a public-health statute to impose a nationwide eviction moratorium.
- *NFIB v. OSHA* (2022): OSHA lacked authority to impose a vaccine-or-test mandate on large employers under its workplace-safety statute.
- *West Virginia v. EPA* (2022): the Court named the doctrine and held that the Clean Air Act's general language did not clearly authorize EPA to restructure the national energy mix through generation-shifting. The Court stressed the agency's claim of power over a significant portion of the economy under a long-extant provision it had rarely used.
- *Biden v. Nebraska* (2023): the HEROES Act authority to "waive or modify" student-loan provisions did not clearly authorize cancellation of roughly \$430 billion in debt.

### The test in practice

Courts look at whether (1) the question is of vast economic and political significance, (2) the agency claims a newly discovered power in an old or general statute, or one that departs from its traditional role, and (3) Congress has clearly authorized it. If the first two are satisfied, the agency needs clear authorization; broad words such as "waive or modify," "necessary," or "public health" may not suffice. The context of the statute and the history of congressional treatment of the issue also matter.

### Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump (2026)

In *Learning Resources* (February 2026, 6–3), the Court held that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorize tariffs. The majority relied on the text of the statute. A plurality also applied the major questions doctrine. Learn the case at this level: an emergency-powers statute that does not mention tariffs was not read to confer a power to impose them. Do not say more than the holding about how many Justices joined the MQD analysis.

### Arguments about the doctrine

Supporters say the doctrine protects the separation of powers and democratic accountability: major policy choices belong to Congress, and ordinary readers would not expect a transformational power to be conferred through vague words. Some also say it works as a clear-statement rule or as a way of enforcing nondelegation values (Week 2). Critics say the doctrine is not found in statutory text, that "major" is vague and gives courts discretion to reject policies they disfavor, and that it ignores the way Congress often legislates broadly and leaves agencies to adapt to new problems. In *Biden v. Nebraska*, one concurring opinion described the doctrine as part of ordinary context-sensitive reading, not a free-standing rule, and dissenters argued that it intrudes on Congress's choice to delegate. Courts continue to debate whether it is a substantive canon or a reading of ordinary meaning.

### Putting MQD, Skidmore and *Loper Bright* together

After *Loper Bright*, the court decides the best reading on its own. In a major-questions setting, it requires clear authorization first, and the agency's view carries no special weight. In an ordinary setting, the agency's view receives only *Skidmore* respect, and where the statute expressly delegates discretion, the court polices its boundaries. The agency's strongest position is a specific grant in the statute's text.

### Predicting review

Ask: (1) What power does the agency claim, and is it economically and politically significant? (2) Is the claimed power new, or does it rest on general language? (3) Is there a clear statement or a specific grant? (4) If not major, what does the best reading say, and how persuasive is the agency's view under *Skidmore*?

@example
title: A claim of sweeping power
problem: The hypothetical Housing Agency relies on a 1960s statute allowing it to "take such action as is necessary to protect public welfare" to ban all rent increases above 3 percent nationwide. The agency has never used the statute for rent regulation. Evaluate the agency's authority.
step: Issue: does the statute clearly authorize nationwide rent control?
step: Significance: a nationwide cap on rent would affect a huge part of the economy and is politically contested.
step: Novelty: the agency claims a power it never used in an old statute with general words.
step: Conclusion: the MQD applies and the general phrase "necessary to protect public welfare" is unlikely to count as clear authorization, so the rule is likely invalid.

@example
title: Ordinary statutory question
problem: A statute directs an agency to regulate "toys intended for children under 12." The agency's rule covers a game sold with a warning label that it is for ages 13 and up but that is marketed to younger children. A court reviews the interpretation. Does the MQD apply?
step: Issue: is this a major question?
step: Significance: a rule about one type of product has limited economic and political significance and is not a claim of new transformative power.
step: Conclusion: no. The court decides the best reading of "intended for" using text and context, giving the agency's view only *Skidmore* respect.

@mistakes
- Applying the MQD to every large or important rule; it requires an extraordinary claim of power that goes beyond clear congressional authorization.
- Saying that *Learning Resources* held more than that IEEPA does not authorize tariffs; the majority relied on the statute's text and only a plurality applied MQD.
- Treating the MQD as a constitutional rule; it is a canon of statutory interpretation, though scholars debate its basis.
- Forgetting that a specific, clear statutory grant satisfies the doctrine.
