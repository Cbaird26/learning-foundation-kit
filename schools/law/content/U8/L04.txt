@article
The Fifth Amendment (federal government) and the Fourteenth Amendment (states) forbid deprivations of life, liberty or property "without due process of law." In administrative law, the question is usually procedural: when an agency terminates a benefit, fires an employee or revokes a license, what process must it provide? Answering requires two steps: (1) is there a protected interest, and (2) if so, what process is due?

### Step 1: protected interests

A **property** interest requires a legitimate claim of entitlement, not just a unilateral expectation (*Board of Regents v. Roth* (1972)). The entitlement comes from independent sources such as statutes, regulations or contracts. A tenured public employee who can be fired only for cause has a property interest in the job (*Cleveland Board of Education v. Loudermill* (1985)); an at-will employee does not. Welfare benefits for eligible recipients are property (*Goldberg v. Kelly* (1970)). Someone with only a hope of a discretionary benefit has no entitlement; nor does a person have a property interest in police enforcement of a protective order (*Town of Castle Rock v. Gonzales* (2005)). A **liberty** interest includes bodily freedom, certain reputational harms accompanied by loss of a right or status, and state-created liberty interests.

Federal law defines the minimum process, but state or federal law defines the entitlement. Under *Loudermill*, the government cannot define the procedure as the limit of the right: state law creates the entitlement, but the Constitution decides what process is due.

### Step 2: *Mathews v. Eldridge* (1976)

*Goldberg v. Kelly* required an evidentiary hearing before welfare benefits are terminated. *Mathews v. Eldridge* then set the general test for deciding how much process is due. The Court weighed three factors:

1. **The private interest** affected by the official action.
2. **The risk of an erroneous deprivation** through the procedures used, and the **probable value** of additional or substitute safeguards.
3. **The government's interest**, including the fiscal and administrative burdens of additional procedures.

In *Mathews*, a Social Security disability recipient challenged termination of benefits without a prior evidentiary hearing. The Court held that a post-termination hearing was enough. It reasoned that the decision turned mainly on written medical records, so the risk of error was lower than in *Goldberg*, where credibility was often at stake; that disability recipients, unlike welfare recipients, were not necessarily left without income; and that the government had a legitimate interest in conserving resources.

### Typical outcomes

Process is flexible. In *Loudermill*, the Court required, before a tenured employee is fired, notice of the charges, an explanation of the employer's evidence and an opportunity to respond, with a full hearing available afterward. In *Goss v. Lopez* (1975), a student facing a short suspension was entitled to notice and an informal opportunity to give his side. The more serious and irreversible the loss and the more it turns on disputed facts, the more process is likely to be required before the loss.

### Adjudication versus general rules

*Londoner v. Denver* (1908) held that a few property owners singled out for a special tax assessment were entitled to be heard. *Bi-Metallic Investment Co. v. State Board of Equalization* (1915) held that when a rule applies to a large number of people, the process is political, not individualized hearings. This is why due process hearings attach to adjudications but not to rules of general application.

### Applying *Mathews*

For each factor, state the facts, the direction it points (more or less process) and why. Then weigh the factors and state what process, at what time, is required. The structure is: interest? entitlement? factor 1, factor 2, factor 3, balance. *Mathews* is an approach to balancing, not a formula, but a numerical illustration can clarify the second factor: if a procedure reduces the error rate from 8 percent to 2 percent over 10,000 terminations, it prevents 600 erroneous deprivations, and the question is whether that benefit is worth its cost.

@example
title: Termination of housing assistance
problem: A hypothetical state agency terminates a family's housing assistance, to which they are entitled by statute, after a computer match suggests unreported income. The agency sends notice and offers a hearing only after the benefit ends. The family argues for a pre-termination hearing. Apply *Mathews*.
step: Protected interest: statutory entitlement makes the assistance property, so due process applies.
step: Factor 1: the family may lose housing, a severe and hard-to-reverse harm, which favors prior process.
step: Factor 2: computer matches often misidentify income and the family can easily show documents, so a pre-termination chance to respond could reduce errors significantly.
step: Factor 3: the agency's cost of a brief informal review before termination is modest, while a full trial-type hearing before every termination would be costly.
step: Conclusion: the family is likely entitled to notice and an informal opportunity to respond before termination, with a full hearing available promptly afterward.

@example
title: An at-will worker
problem: A hypothetical agency dismisses an employee who has no contract or statute limiting dismissal to cause. She demands a hearing under *Loudermill*. How should the claim be analyzed?
step: Rule: due process protects property interests created by statute, regulation or contract (*Roth*).
step: Application: nothing gives her a claim of entitlement to continued employment, so she has only an expectation.
step: Conclusion: no protected property interest, so no *Mathews* balancing is needed. A liberty claim might exist if the agency publicly stigmatized her and altered her legal status, which these facts do not show.

@mistakes
- Skipping the protected-interest question and going straight to *Mathews*.
- Treating *Mathews* as always allowing post-deprivation hearings; the factors can require pre-deprivation process.
- Assuming that a statute that grants a benefit also controls what process is due; the Constitution does.
- Applying *Londoner*-style hearing rights to general rules, which *Bi-Metallic* places in the political process.

solution: Protected interest: statutory benefits are property. Balance: notice with the reasons and a chance to submit written evidence before termination, plus a prompt evidentiary hearing afterward, is likely sufficient under *Mathews*. If credibility or sharply disputed facts were involved, the balance would shift toward a hearing before termination.
