@article
Agencies investigate, inspect, prosecute and punish. Each stage is limited by the Constitution and by statute. This lesson follows an enforcement matter from investigation to penalty.

### Investigations and subpoenas

Section 555(c) says a process, requirement of a report, inspection or other investigative act or demand shall not be issued, made or enforced except as authorized by law. Section 555(d) says agency subpoenas authorized by law shall be issued to a party on request and, when required by procedural rules, on a showing of general relevance and reasonable scope of the evidence sought. An agency usually cannot enforce its own subpoena: it must ask a court, and the recipient may defend by challenging the demand.

Under *United States v. Morton Salt Co.* (1950) and *Oklahoma Press Publishing Co. v. Walling* (1946), a court will enforce an administrative subpoena if (1) the investigation is within the agency's authority, (2) the demand is not too indefinite, and (3) the information sought is reasonably relevant. The agency need not show probable cause; it may investigate on suspicion or simply to confirm compliance. The Fourth Amendment still forbids a demand that is unreasonably broad or burdensome.

### Inspections: the warrant rule

The Fourth Amendment applies to civil inspections of private premises. *Camara v. Municipal Court* (1967) held that a housing inspector needs a warrant to inspect a home over the occupant's objection, and *See v. City of Seattle* (1967) extended this to commercial premises. In *Marshall v. Barlow's, Inc.* (1978), the Court held that OSHA's authority to inspect workplaces without a warrant was unconstitutional. A warrant for an administrative inspection can issue on a showing less than traditional probable cause: it is enough that the inspection is based on reasonable legislative or administrative standards, such as a neutral schedule, or on specific evidence of a violation.

### Closely regulated industries

There is an exception for **closely regulated industries**, where the owner's expectation of privacy is reduced (*Colonnade Catering Corp. v. United States* (1970); *United States v. Biswell* (1972)). Under *New York v. Burger* (1987), a warrantless inspection of such a business is reasonable if (1) there is a substantial government interest in the regulatory scheme, (2) warrantless inspections are necessary to further that scheme, and (3) the inspection program, in terms of its certainty and regularity, provides a constitutionally adequate substitute for a warrant. In *City of Los Angeles v. Patel* (2015), the Court held that a city ordinance letting police inspect hotel guest records on demand was facially unconstitutional because it gave no opportunity for precompliance review before a neutral decision-maker, and it said hotels are not closely regulated businesses.

### Enforcement discretion

An agency decides whom to investigate and charge. Under *Heckler v. Chaney* (1985), a decision not to enforce is presumptively unreviewable (Week 7). *United States v. Texas* (2023) held that states lacked standing to challenge federal enforcement priorities. Selective or retaliatory enforcement may raise constitutional issues, but the bar is high.

### Penalties and the jury right

Agencies often seek civil penalties. In *SEC v. Jarkesy* (2024), the Court held that the Seventh Amendment requires a jury when the SEC seeks civil penalties for securities fraud, because the penalty is a legal remedy and the claim resembles common-law fraud, so the public-rights exception did not permit agency adjudication. Apply the case to its holding: it addressed SEC civil penalties for fraud, and later cases will decide how far its reasoning extends to other agencies and penalties.

A defendant who contends that the agency's structure itself is unconstitutional may sue in district court rather than waiting until the end of the agency process, even where a statute provides a review scheme (*Axon Enterprise, Inc. v. FTC* (2023)).

### Time limits

Under 28 U.S.C. § 2462, an action to enforce a civil fine, penalty or forfeiture must be commenced within five years from when the claim first accrued. In *Gabelli v. SEC* (2013), the Court held that the claim accrues when the fraud occurs, not when the government discovers it.

### Applying the rules

Ask: What power is the agency using (subpoena, inspection, enforcement)? What authorizes it? Does the Fourth Amendment require a warrant, and does an exception apply? If the agency seeks a penalty, is it timely and does the Seventh Amendment require a jury?

@example
title: A warrantless inspection
problem: A hypothetical state inspector arrives at a licensed scrap-metal yard and demands to inspect the yard and its records without a warrant under a statute authorizing regular inspections of licensed yards. The owner refuses. Is the inspection lawful?
step: Rule: administrative inspections generally require a warrant, but a closely regulated business may be inspected without one if the *Burger* requirements are met.
step: Application: if the industry is pervasively regulated through licensing and recordkeeping, the state has a substantial interest, warrantless inspections are needed to catch violations, and the statute defines the time, scope and regularity of inspections.
step: Conclusion: if the industry is closely regulated and the statute limits the inspectors' discretion, the inspection is lawful. If the statute leaves the inspectors unlimited discretion or the industry is not closely regulated, a warrant is required.

@example
title: A stale penalty claim
problem: In 2019 a company commits a violation for which an agency may seek a civil penalty. The agency learns of it in 2022. In what year does the five-year period of 28 U.S.C. § 2462 end?
step: Rule: the period runs five years from accrual, and under *Gabelli*, accrual is when the violation occurs.
step: Application: the claim accrued in 2019, not when the agency learned of it in 2022.
step: Computation: $2019 + 5 = 2024$.
step: Conclusion: the agency must file by the end of the five-year period in 2024.

@mistakes
- Assuming agencies need a warrant for every inspection; closely regulated industries are an exception.
- Saying that an agency may enforce its subpoena by itself; it must go to court.
- Requiring probable cause of a violation for a subpoena; reasonable relevance suffices.
- Reading *Jarkesy* more broadly than its holding about SEC penalties for securities fraud.
