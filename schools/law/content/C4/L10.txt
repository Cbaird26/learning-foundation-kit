@article
An **electoral system** is the set of rules that turns votes into winners. Different systems create different incentives for candidates, parties and voters, and each reflects trade-offs among values such as simplicity, representation and stability.

### Electoral systems

- **Single-member plurality** ("first past the post"): each district elects one winner, the candidate with the most votes, even without a majority. It is simple and links each representative to a district, but it can elect a winner opposed by most voters in a crowded race and tends to produce two major parties (**Duverger's law**).
- **Two-round (runoff)**: if no candidate wins a majority, the top candidates face a second vote. It ensures a majority winner but costs more and requires a second election.
- **Ranked-choice (instant runoff)**: voters rank candidates; the last-place candidate is eliminated and those ballots go to the next choice until someone has a majority. Supporters say it reduces the "spoiler" effect; critics point to complexity, ballot errors and cases where a candidate leading in first choices loses.
- **Proportional representation (PR)**: parties win seats in proportion to votes, usually in multi-member districts with party lists. It gives smaller parties representation but often requires coalition governments.

Most American federal and state offices use plurality. Some states and cities use ranked-choice voting, and the Constitution leaves most details to the states.

### The Electoral College

The president is chosen by **electors** allocated to each state equal to its House seats plus two senators, and the Twenty-Third Amendment gives the District of Columbia three: $435 + 100 + 3 = 538$ electors. A candidate needs a majority, **270**. Almost every state awards all its electors to the winner of the state's popular vote; Maine and Nebraska award some by congressional district. If no candidate has a majority, the Twelfth Amendment sends the choice to the House, with one vote per state delegation. In *Chiafalo v. Washington* (2020), the Court upheld state laws that bind electors to the state's popular vote. Supporters argue that the system gives weight to states, encourages broad coalitions and ends elections with clear winners; critics argue that a candidate can win without a national popular-vote plurality and that it concentrates attention on a few states.

### Redistricting and money

*Reynolds v. Sims* (1964) established "one person, one vote" for legislative districts. In *Rucho v. Common Cause* (2019), the Court held that partisan-gerrymandering claims are nonjusticiable in federal courts. In *Louisiana v. Callais* (2026), the Court held that a congressional map was an unconstitutional racial gerrymander because the Voting Rights Act did not require the second majority-minority district.

Campaign-finance law balances speech and anti-corruption concerns. *Buckley v. Valeo* (1976) treated spending as protected speech and upheld contribution limits to prevent corruption. *Citizens United v. FEC* (2010) held that government may not ban independent expenditures by corporations, and **super PACs** may raise unlimited sums for independent expenditures. In *NRSC v. FEC* (2026), the Court held that limits on political parties' coordinated spending with candidates violate the First Amendment. Defenders emphasize free expression; critics emphasize concerns about unequal influence.

### Parties and interest groups

Parties recruit candidates, organize government, mobilize voters and simplify choices. Primaries select nominees: **closed primaries** limit participation to party members, **open primaries** allow any voter to choose a party's ballot. **Interest groups** organize people with shared goals and influence policy by lobbying, litigation, endorsements and campaign spending. Collective-action problems (the **free rider** problem) make large groups hard to organize, so organized interests are not equally distributed. Madison warned in *Federalist No. 10* that factions are inevitable and argued that a large republic helps check them.

@example
title: Counting ranked-choice ballots
problem: In a three-candidate race with 100 ballots, first choices are A, 40; B, 35; C, 25. Of C's ballots, 20 rank B next and 5 rank A next. Who wins under ranked-choice voting, and under plurality?
step: Under plurality, A wins with 40 votes, the most, although A lacks a majority.
step: Under ranked-choice, no candidate has more than 50 votes at first, so C, with the fewest, is eliminated.
step: Redistribute C's ballots: B gets $35 + 20 = 55$ and A gets $40 + 5 = 45$.
step: B has a majority, 55 of 100, and wins. The two systems pick different winners from the same ballots.

@mistakes
- Saying the winner of the national popular vote always becomes president. The Electoral College decides.
- Confusing plurality with majority. A plurality is the most votes, not more than half.
- Reading *Citizens United* as allowing unlimited direct contributions to candidates. Contribution limits remain.
- Treating ranked-choice voting and proportional representation as the same. RCV elects one winner; PR allocates multiple seats.

@practice self
prompt: Compare single-member plurality with proportional representation in 8 to 10 sentences. State the trade-offs of each system and give the strongest argument its supporters would make, without recommending either.
solution: In single-member plurality, each district elects the candidate with the most votes. Supporters argue that it is simple, gives each district one clear representative, produces stable single-party governments and ties accountability to a local figure. Critics argue that it can leave many votes unrepresented, can elect a winner opposed by a majority and favors two large parties. In proportional representation, parties win seats in proportion to their votes in multi-member districts. Supporters argue that it reflects the full range of opinion, wastes fewer votes and gives smaller parties a voice. Critics argue that it often produces coalition governments, weakens the link between a voter and one representative and can give small parties bargaining power. The choice depends on how much weight one gives to simplicity and local accountability compared with proportionality and breadth of representation.
