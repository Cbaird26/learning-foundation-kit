@article
Article I vests "all legislative powers" in a Congress of two chambers. The two-house design came from the Great Compromise and also serves as an internal check: a bill must pass both chambers in identical form.

### Two chambers

The **House of Representatives** has 435 voting members, a number fixed by statute since 1929 and apportioned among the states by population after each decennial census. Members serve two-year terms, must be at least 25, citizens for seven years, and residents of the state they represent. The **Senate** has 100 members, two per state, six-year terms staggered so about a third face election every two years, and a minimum age of 30 and nine years of citizenship. The Seventeenth Amendment (1913) provides for direct election of senators.

Each chamber has special powers. Only the House may originate revenue bills and impeach (a majority vote accuses an official). The Senate tries impeachments (conviction needs two-thirds of senators present), confirms nominations by majority vote, and consents to treaties by two-thirds of senators present. The Vice President is President of the Senate and votes only to break ties.

### Powers

Article I, Section 8 gives Congress the power to tax, borrow, spend, regulate commerce, coin money, establish post offices, raise armies, declare war and make all laws "necessary and proper" to carry out those powers. Congress also has oversight powers: hearings, subpoenas and the power of the purse. Because tariffs are taxes, the power to impose them is textually assigned to Congress, but Congress has delegated some trade authority to the president by statute. In *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* (February 2026, 6-3), the Court held that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorize tariffs. The majority relied on the statutory text; a plurality also applied the **major questions doctrine** (*West Virginia v. EPA*, 2022; *Biden v. Nebraska*, 2023), under which a claim of vast economic and political significance needs clear congressional authorization.

### Organization and procedure

The **Speaker**, elected by the House, leads the majority party's agenda; the **majority leader** performs a similar role in the Senate. Most work happens in **standing committees** and subcommittees, whose chairs control hearings and markups. Most bills die in committee. In the House the **Rules Committee** sets time and amendment limits for floor debate, and a bill reaches the floor with a simple majority. In the Senate, debate is long by default, and ending it by **cloture** requires three-fifths of the senators sworn, which is 60 of 100 (on most matters). A senator can therefore use a **filibuster** to block a bill unless 60 senators vote for cloture. **Budget reconciliation** is a special procedure that limits debate and permits passage by simple majority for certain fiscal measures.

Supporters of the filibuster say it encourages compromise, protects the minority and slows hasty change. Critics say it lets a minority block majorities, increases gridlock and shifts decisions to the executive branch and the courts. Both sides draw support from the Senate's tradition as a deliberative body.

### Representation and re-election

Members of Congress face incentives to serve their districts or states (casework, projects) and to follow national party positions. Incumbents win re-election at high rates, which scholars attribute to name recognition, fundraising, constituent service and the way district lines are drawn; they disagree about the relative weight of each cause.

@example
title: How many senators can block cloture?
problem: A bill is before the 100-member Senate, and cloture requires 60 votes. What is the smallest number of senators who can prevent cloture, and what does this mean for a majority party with 55 seats?
step: Cloture needs 60 of 100. If 41 senators vote no, at most 59 can vote yes, so cloture fails. Forty senators voting no would leave 60 yes votes.
step: The minimum blocking number is therefore $100 - 60 + 1 = 41$ senators.
step: A party with 55 seats has a simple majority but needs five more votes for cloture, usually from the other party.
step: On a matter eligible for budget reconciliation, the same party could pass the measure with a simple majority and avoid the 60-vote requirement.

@mistakes
- Saying the Senate needs 60 votes to pass every bill. Passage is by majority; 60 votes are generally needed to end debate by cloture.
- Mixing up which chamber does what: the House impeaches and the Senate tries the case.
- Treating the number 435 as written in the Constitution. It is set by statute.
- Describing the filibuster as constitutionally required. It is a product of Senate rules.
