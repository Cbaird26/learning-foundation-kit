@article
**Public opinion** is what people think about public issues, officials and institutions. It matters because elected officials depend on voters, but political scientists debate how closely policy follows opinion and how much opinion itself is shaped by elites, parties and the media.

### How opinions form

**Political socialization** is the process by which people acquire political attitudes, mainly through family, school, peers, religious institutions, work and the media. Age, education, region, religion, race, gender and life experiences are associated with different views, though with many exceptions. **Partisanship** (identification with a party) and **ideology** (a coherent set of beliefs about government's role) often serve as shortcuts when people judge new issues. Opinions on many topics are stable, but they can shift after major events.

### Measuring opinion

A **poll** surveys a **sample** to learn about a **population**. A **random sample**, in which every member of the population has a known chance of selection, allows reliable inference. A sample of volunteers (a call-in or website poll) suffers from **self-selection** and cannot be generalized. The *Literary Digest* poll of 1936 mailed millions of ballots to lists drawn from telephone and car owners and badly missed the presidential result, a classic case of sampling bias; smaller polls with more representative samples did better.

Other problems include **question wording** and order, **nonresponse**, social pressure to give acceptable answers, and sampling frames that miss groups. A **push poll** uses loaded "questions" to persuade rather than measure.

At the 95 percent confidence level, the **margin of error** for a proportion $p$ from a random sample of size $n$ is approximately

$$ \text{MOE} = 1.96 \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}. $$

The value is largest at $p = 0.5$. Because $n$ sits under a square root, cutting the margin of error in half requires four times the sample. The margin of error covers only random sampling error, not bias in wording or sampling.

### The media

The press informs voters, acts as a watchdog and shapes what people think about. Scholars describe **agenda setting** (media attention affects which issues seem important), **framing** (how a story is presented affects interpretation), **priming** (what is emphasized affects the standards by which people judge leaders) and **gatekeeping** (editors and platforms decide what gets through). The market has changed: print and broadcast outlets have lost audiences to online and social media, which let people choose sources that match their views. Researchers disagree about whether this produces "echo chambers" or whether most people still see diverse information.

### Law and the media

The First Amendment protects the press. *Near v. Minnesota* (1931) and *New York Times Co. v. United States* (1971, the Pentagon Papers case) place a heavy burden on government attempts at prior restraint. *New York Times v. Sullivan* (1964) requires actual malice for public officials to win defamation suits. Broadcast media are regulated by the Federal Communications Commission under the idea that the airwaves are a limited public resource; the **equal-time rule** applies to candidates, and the **fairness doctrine** was abandoned in 1987. Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act (47 U.S.C. § 230) generally protects online platforms from being treated as the publisher of content posted by others. Supporters say this protects free expression and innovation; critics say it shields harmful content, and proposals for reform come from across the political spectrum.

@example
title: Computing a margin of error
problem: A random poll of 900 adults finds that 50 percent approve of a proposal. What is the 95 percent margin of error, in percentage points?
step: Use $\text{MOE} = 1.96\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$ with $p = 0.5$ and $n = 900$.
step: Compute $p(1-p) = 0.25$, then $0.25/900 = 0.000278$, and the square root is about $0.01667$.
step: Multiply: $1.96 \times 0.01667 \approx 0.0327$, or about 3.3 percentage points.
step: Interpretation: the true support is probably between about 46.7 and 53.3 percent, assuming a random sample and no bias.

@mistakes
- Believing a large sample fixes a biased sampling method. A huge volunteer poll is still unrepresentative.
- Reading the margin of error as covering all errors, including leading questions and nonresponse.
- Assuming a poll of 2,000 has half the margin of error of a poll of 1,000. It needs four times the sample to halve it.
- Treating the media's agenda-setting power as the power to tell people what to think. It affects what they think about.

@practice num
prompt: A random poll of 400 adults finds 50 percent support for a proposal. Using $1.96\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$, what is the 95 percent margin of error in percentage points? Round to one decimal place.
answer: 4.9
check: 1.96 * math.sqrt(0.5 * 0.5 / 400) * 100
tol: 0.1
hint: Substitute $p = 0.5$ and $n = 400$.
hint: Compute $0.25/400$ and take the square root, then multiply by $1.96$ and by $100$.
solution: $\sqrt{0.25/400} = 0.025$, and $1.96 \times 0.025 = 0.049$, or 4.9 percentage points.

@practice num
prompt: A poll with 600 respondents has a margin of error of about 4 points. A pollster wants to cut the margin of error in half. How many respondents are needed, assuming the same method?
answer: 2400
check: 600 * (1 / 0.5) ** 2
hint: Margin of error varies with $1/\sqrt{n}$.
solution: Halving the margin of error requires multiplying $n$ by $2^2 = 4$, so $600 \times 4 = 2400$.

@practice self
prompt: A group reports that "most Americans" support a policy, based on 5,000 replies to an email sent to its own members, using the question "Do you agree that the harmful, wasteful policy should end?" In 6 to 8 sentences, identify the problems and rewrite the question in neutral form.
solution: The sample consists of the group's own members who chose to reply, so it is self-selected and not random, and the group's members likely share a view. The size of 5,000 does not fix the bias. The wording is loaded because "harmful" and "wasteful" tell respondents what to think, and "agree" invites acquiescence. A neutral version would be: "Do you support or oppose the policy, or are you unsure?", with the answer options offered in randomized order. A sound poll would draw a random sample of adults, weight to match known population characteristics and report the margin of error and the question wording.
rubric: Identifies self-selection and the unrepresentative sample.
rubric: Identifies loaded wording and acquiescence.
rubric: Offers a neutral, balanced rewrite.
hint: Ask who answered and how the question tells them what to think.

@quiz num
prompt: A random poll of 1,600 adults finds 50 percent support for a proposal. What is the 95 percent margin of error in percentage points, using $1.96\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$? Round to two decimal places.
answer: 2.45
check: 1.96 * math.sqrt(0.5 * 0.5 / 1600) * 100
tol: 0.02
hint: Substitute $p = 0.5$ and $n = 1600$.
solution: $\sqrt{0.25/1600} = 0.0125$, so $1.96 \times 0.0125 = 0.0245$, or 2.45 points.
