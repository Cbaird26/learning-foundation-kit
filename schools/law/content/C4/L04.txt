@article
**Civil rights** are guarantees of equal treatment, mainly under the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment ("equal protection of the laws") and under statutes such as the Civil Rights Act of 1964. Civil liberties limit what government may do; civil rights concern whether government treats groups equally.

### The Reconstruction Amendments

The **Thirteenth Amendment** (1865) abolished slavery. The **Fourteenth** (1868) made all persons born or naturalized in the United States citizens and barred states from denying due process or equal protection. The **Fifteenth** (1870) barred denial of the vote on account of race. *Dred Scott v. Sandford* (1857) had held that Black Americans could not be citizens; the Fourteenth Amendment overturned that result. The Court read the amendments narrowly for decades. *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896) upheld "separate but equal" railway cars, and Jim Crow laws spread. *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954) held that state-mandated racial segregation of public schools violates equal protection because separate educational facilities are inherently unequal, and *Brown II* (1955) ordered desegregation "with all deliberate speed."

### Tiers of scrutiny

When a law classifies people, courts choose a level of review:

| Classification | Review | What the government must show |
|---|---|---|
| Race, national origin | Strict scrutiny | Compelling interest, narrowly tailored |
| Sex | Intermediate scrutiny | Important interest, substantially related (*Craig v. Boren*, 1976; *United States v. Virginia*, 1996: an "exceedingly persuasive justification") |
| Most other classifications | Rational basis | Legitimate interest, rationally related |

Equal protection applies to state action; discriminatory *intent* must be shown, not only disparate impact (*Washington v. Davis*, 1976).

### Statutes

The **Civil Rights Act of 1964** bars discrimination in public accommodations (Title II, upheld under the commerce power in *Heart of Atlanta Motel v. United States*), in employment (Title VII) and in federally funded programs (Title VI). The **Voting Rights Act of 1965** targeted voting barriers. In *Shelby County v. Holder* (2013), the Court held the coverage formula that determined which jurisdictions needed federal preclearance unconstitutional, so that preclearance cannot be applied under it unless Congress enacts a new one. *Bostock v. Clayton County* (2020) held that Title VII's ban on sex discrimination covers discrimination based on sexual orientation and gender identity.

### Recent and contested cases

- *Loving v. Virginia* (1967): laws banning interracial marriage violate equal protection and due process.
- *Obergefell v. Hodges* (2015): the Fourteenth Amendment requires states to license and recognize marriages between two people of the same sex.
- *Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard* (2023): race-conscious admissions at Harvard and UNC violate equal protection. The majority emphasized that classifications by race get strict scrutiny and must have limits; the dissents emphasized the continuing effects of past discrimination and the educational value of diversity.
- *United States v. Skrmetti* (2025): Tennessee's ban on puberty blockers and hormones to treat gender dysphoria in minors gets rational-basis review and is upheld.
- *Little v. Hecox* and *West Virginia v. B.P.J.* (2026): Idaho's law limiting girls' and women's teams by biological sex does not violate the Equal Protection Clause, and Title IX allows schools to keep separate male and female teams based on biological sex.
- *Trump v. Barbara* (2026): children born in the United States to parents who are unlawfully or temporarily present are citizens at birth under the Fourteenth Amendment.

In each, supporters and critics advance principled arguments about the meaning of equality, the proper role of courts and the scope of legislative judgment; this course describes the holdings and does not choose among them.

@example
title: Choosing the level of scrutiny
problem: State A offers a scholarship only to women in engineering. State B bans people with red hair from a civil-service job. Which level of review applies to each?
step: State A's rule classifies by sex, so it receives intermediate scrutiny: the state must show an important interest and that the classification is substantially related to it (*United States v. Virginia*).
step: State B's rule classifies by hair color, which is not a suspect or quasi-suspect class, so rational basis applies: a legitimate interest and a rational relationship.
step: State B's rule would almost certainly fail even this lenient test, because hair color has no rational link to civil-service work. State A's outcome depends on whether the state can show a real, non-stereotyped justification.

@mistakes
- Treating all discrimination as unconstitutional under the Equal Protection Clause. It applies only to state action, and Title VII and similar statutes reach private employers.
- Applying strict scrutiny to every classification. Only race, national origin and a few others receive it.
- Saying *Brown* overruled *Plessy* generally. *Brown* rejected "separate but equal" in public education and later cases extended the result.
- Assuming disparate impact alone proves an equal protection violation. Discriminatory intent is required.
