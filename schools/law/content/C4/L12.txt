The Constitution divides foreign-affairs authority between the president and Congress, and the lines between them have been disputed since the founding. State and local governments, by contrast, handle most day-to-day governing. This lesson covers both.

### Constitutional powers in foreign affairs

The **president** is commander in chief, negotiates treaties, nominates ambassadors and receives foreign ambassadors. The Supreme Court held in *Zivotofsky v. Kerry* (2015) that the president alone holds the power to recognize foreign nations and governments. **Congress** declares war, raises and funds the armed forces, regulates foreign commerce and holds the tariff and spending powers. The **Senate** must consent to treaties by two-thirds of senators present, and appoints no ambassadors without confirmation by majority. Many agreements are made as **executive agreements**, which presidents conclude without Senate consent, and as **congressional-executive agreements**, approved by majorities of both houses. States may not make treaties (Article I, Section 10). Under the Supremacy Clause, treaties are the supreme law of the land (*Missouri v. Holland*, 1920), though a treaty that is not self-executing may require legislation (*Medellin v. Texas*, 2008).

*United States v. Curtiss-Wright* (1936) described the president as the main representative of the nation in foreign relations, while *Youngstown* (Lesson 6) makes clear that presidential power is weakest when it conflicts with Congress. In *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* (2026), the Court held that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorize tariffs, showing that statutory text still limits presidential action in foreign commerce. The **War Powers Resolution** of 1973 attempts to check presidential use of force; presidents of both parties have questioned its constitutionality, and Congress has authorized force by statute (for example the 2001 authorization against those responsible for the September 11 attacks).

### Tools and institutions

The main tools are **diplomacy**, **economic measures** (trade policy, sanctions, aid), **military force**, **intelligence** and **international organizations and alliances**. Key institutions include the State Department, the Defense Department, the intelligence agencies and the National Security Council staff, which advises the president. Scholars describe competing outlooks: **realists** stress national interest and power, **liberal internationalists** stress institutions and cooperation, and **restraint** advocates urge a narrower role. These are descriptive labels for debate, not endorsements.

### State government

Each of the 50 states has its own constitution, generally longer and more easily amended than the federal one. Most follow the national pattern of three branches. **Governors** execute the laws and most have a line-item veto. Every state but Nebraska has a two-house legislature; Nebraska's is unicameral. State courts handle the large majority of cases; judges are chosen by election, appointment or merit selection, depending on the state. About half the states allow some form of **direct democracy**: the **initiative** (citizens propose laws), the **referendum** (voters approve or reject laws) and the **recall** (voters remove officials). Supporters say these tools increase participation; critics say they can oversimplify complex issues. Justice Brandeis wrote that a single state may "serve as a laboratory" and try new policies without risk to the rest of the country, which is why states are often called laboratories of democracy.

### Local government

Local governments are created by the state: **counties**, **municipalities**, **townships**, **school districts** and **special districts**. Under **Dillon's Rule**, a local government has only the powers the state expressly gives it; under **home rule**, a locality may act on local matters unless state law forbids it. Local governments raise revenue mainly from property and sales taxes, plus state and federal grants, and are responsible for services such as schools, policing, zoning, water and roads.

@example
title: One policy at three levels
problem: Show how safe drinking water is regulated at federal, state and local levels.
step: Federal: Congress passed the Safe Drinking Water Act, and the Environmental Protection Agency sets national standards for contaminants.
step: State: states usually take responsibility for enforcement under federal oversight, adopt their own rules and may set stricter standards.
step: Local: municipal or regional water utilities treat and deliver water, test it and report results to customers and regulators.
step: Funding and accountability run through all three levels: federal grants, state loans and local rates, with each level answerable to different voters.

@mistakes
- Saying the president needs Senate approval for every international agreement. Executive agreements and congressional-executive agreements do not use the treaty process.
- Believing the president may declare war. Congress has that power, although presidents have used force without a declaration.
- Treating local governments as independent of states. They exist by state law, and the state can change their powers.
- Assuming every state has a ballot initiative. About half do.

@practice self
prompt: Choose one policy (for example drinking water, public health insurance for low-income people, or road safety). In 7 to 9 sentences, describe how it is made and carried out at the federal, state and local levels, naming the institution at each level and how money, rules and enforcement flow among them.
solution: Take Medicaid. At the federal level, Congress created the program by statute, and the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services issue rules and approve state plans and waivers. Federal funds match state spending, with the federal share varying by state. At the state level, each state runs its own program, decides many details such as provider payments and some eligibility rules within federal limits, and contributes its share of the cost. At the local level, county or city agencies often enroll applicants, public hospitals and clinics deliver care, and some counties contribute funding. Money flows from federal matching funds and state revenue to providers; rules flow from federal law and regulation through state agencies to local offices. Each level answers to different voters, and disagreements over cost, eligibility and flexibility run across all three.
