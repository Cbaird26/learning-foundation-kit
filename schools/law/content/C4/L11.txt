@article
**Public policy** is a course of action government takes, or chooses not to take, to address a problem. Domestic policy covers areas such as health, education, income support, housing, the environment and the economy. Because the Constitution divides power among branches and levels of government, almost every domestic policy involves several institutions.

### The policy process

Scholars use stages as a rough guide, not a rigid sequence:

1. **Agenda setting**: a problem gains attention through events, the media, interest groups or officials.
2. **Formulation**: proposals are drafted by Congress, the president, agencies, think tanks and advocacy groups.
3. **Adoption**: a statute, regulation, executive order or court decision makes the choice official.
4. **Implementation**: agencies, states and local governments carry it out, and implementation often shapes outcomes as much as the law does.
5. **Evaluation**: officials, auditors and researchers assess results, which can lead to change.

### Policy tools

Government can **regulate** (set rules, such as emission standards), **tax or subsidize** (including tax credits and deductions), make **grants** to states or individuals, **provide services directly** (such as the post office or national defense) or **inform and persuade** (public campaigns). Choosing among them involves value judgments about the role of government and empirical questions about what works.

### The federal budget

The federal fiscal year begins October 1. The president proposes a budget, but Congress holds the power of the purse and passes appropriations bills. Federal spending falls into three main categories: **mandatory spending**, set by permanent law and flowing automatically to eligible people (Social Security, Medicare and most of Medicaid); **discretionary spending**, set each year by appropriations (defense and most other agencies); and **interest** on the debt. A **deficit** occurs in a year when spending exceeds revenue; the **debt** is the accumulated total of past deficits (net of surpluses).

Taxes are **progressive** if higher incomes pay a higher share, **regressive** if they take a larger share from lower incomes, and **proportional** if the rate is the same. A **marginal rate** applies to the last dollar earned; the **average rate** is total tax divided by income. Debates over taxes, spending and deficits reflect disagreement about priorities. Some argue that lower taxes spur growth and respect individual choice; others argue that public programs deliver needed services and that revenue is required to fund them. Economists disagree about the size of the effects, which is why this course describes the arguments rather than choosing.

### Fiscal and monetary policy

**Fiscal policy** is taxing and spending set by Congress and the president. **Monetary policy** is conducted by the Federal Reserve, whose Federal Open Market Committee sets a target for short-term interest rates. By statute the Fed pursues maximum employment and stable prices. The Fed is designed to be insulated from short-term politics, and the Court has left open how far recent removal decisions reach it (see Lesson 6).

### Contested policy questions

Health care shows how the policy process and federalism interact. The Affordable Care Act of 2010 expanded coverage through subsidies, regulation of insurers and a Medicaid expansion; in *NFIB v. Sebelius* (2012) the Court upheld the minimum-coverage provision under the taxing power and held the Medicaid expansion could not be enforced by threatening to withdraw existing funds. Supporters of broader public coverage emphasize access and financial security; supporters of market-based approaches emphasize choice, innovation and cost control. Education policy, housing, energy and welfare involve comparable debates over who should pay, who should decide and what works.

@example
title: Marginal versus average tax rate
problem: Assume a tax of 10 percent on the first \$30,000 of income and 25 percent on income above that. What is the tax on \$50,000, and what is the average rate?
step: The tax on the first \$30,000 is $0.10 \times 30{,}000 = 3{,}000$.
step: The remaining income is $50{,}000 - 30{,}000 = 20{,}000$, taxed at 25 percent: $0.25 \times 20{,}000 = 5{,}000$.
step: Total tax is $3{,}000 + 5{,}000 = 8{,}000$.
step: The average rate is $8{,}000 / 50{,}000 = 16$ percent, below the 25 percent marginal rate.

@mistakes
- Confusing the deficit with the debt. The deficit is one year's gap; the debt is the accumulated total.
- Applying the highest bracket rate to all income. A marginal rate applies only to income above the threshold.
- Saying the president sets federal spending. The president proposes, but Congress appropriates and sets mandatory programs by law.
- Treating implementation as a minor detail. Agencies and states often shape what a policy does in practice.

solution: Problem: housing costs have risen faster than many incomes in some regions. Approach A uses regulation: loosen local zoning rules so more housing can be built. Supporters argue that more supply lowers prices and respects property owners' choices; the weakness they acknowledge is that new construction may take years and may not help the lowest-income households. Approach B uses subsidies and direct provision: expand rental vouchers and public housing. Supporters argue that this helps low-income households quickly; the weakness they acknowledge is the cost to taxpayers and that demand subsidies can raise rents where supply is limited. The approaches are not mutually exclusive, and the weight each receives depends on views about markets, local control and public spending.
