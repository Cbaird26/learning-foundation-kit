@article
A **constitution** is a society's basic law: it creates institutions, assigns them powers and sets limits. The U.S. Constitution is the oldest written national constitution still in force, and almost every later topic in this course, from federalism to the courts, is an argument about what its text and structure require.

### Why the first government failed

The **Articles of Confederation** (drafted 1777, in force 1781) created a "league of friendship" among sovereign states. Congress was the only national institution, and each state had one vote. Congress could not tax individuals; it could only request money from the states, which often did not pay. It had no independent executive and no national court system, it could not effectively regulate trade among the states, and amending the Articles required the consent of all thirteen states. Shays' Rebellion (1786-87), an armed uprising of indebted farmers in Massachusetts, convinced many leaders that the national government was too weak to keep order.

### The Convention and its compromises

Delegates met in Philadelphia in 1787 to revise the Articles but wrote a new framework instead. Large states favored the **Virginia Plan** (representation by population); small states favored the **New Jersey Plan** (equal state representation). The **Great Compromise** (the Connecticut Compromise) created a House apportioned by population and a Senate with two members per state. The **Three-Fifths Compromise** counted three-fifths of the enslaved population for apportionment and direct taxes; it embedded slavery in the founding text and was later superseded by the Fourteenth Amendment. Other bargains addressed commerce and the slave trade.

### Core design principles

- **Popular sovereignty**: legitimate power comes from the people.
- **Republicanism**: the people govern through elected representatives rather than directly.
- **Separation of powers**: Articles I, II and III vest legislative, executive and judicial power in different institutions.
- **Checks and balances**: each branch can restrain the others (the presidential veto, Senate confirmation, impeachment by the House and trial by the Senate).
- **Federalism**: power is divided between the national government and the states (Lesson 2).
- **Limited government**: enumerated powers and, after 1791, the Bill of Rights.

Madison defended this design in *Federalist No. 10* (a large republic dilutes the power of any single **faction**) and *Federalist No. 51* (ambition must be made to counteract ambition, so each branch needs the means to resist the others).

### Ratification and amendment

Article VII made the Constitution effective once nine of the thirteen states ratified it in special conventions. **Federalists** argued the new government was needed for security, commerce and stability. **Anti-Federalists** feared distant, concentrated power and objected that the document lacked a bill of rights; the promise of amendments helped secure ratification, and ten were added in 1791.

**Article V** sets two routes for proposal (two-thirds of both houses of Congress, or a convention called by two-thirds of the state legislatures) and two routes for ratification (three-fourths of the states, through legislatures or conventions). All twenty-seven amendments so far were proposed by Congress. The high thresholds make formal change rare and push most constitutional change into interpretation by courts and practice.

@example
title: Counting the votes to amend the Constitution
problem: Assume all 435 House members and all 100 senators are present and voting, and there are 50 states. How many House votes, Senate votes and ratifying states does the ordinary congressional route to an amendment require?
step: The proposal needs two-thirds of each house. In the House, $\frac{2}{3} \times 435 = 290$ exactly, so 290 votes.
step: In the Senate, $\frac{2}{3} \times 100 = 66.67$. Votes are whole numbers and the threshold is "two-thirds", so you round up: 67 votes.
step: Ratification needs three-fourths of the states: $\frac{3}{4} \times 50 = 37.5$, which rounds up to 38 states.
step: The pattern is general: compute the fraction, then round up to the next whole vote or state, because a fraction of a vote does not exist.

@mistakes
- Rounding down: 66 senators is less than two-thirds of 100, so it fails.
- Saying the president signs a constitutional amendment. The president has no formal role in Article V.
- Treating the Great Compromise as the Three-Fifths Compromise. The first created the structure of the House and Senate; the second counted enslaved persons for apportionment and direct taxes.
- Assuming the Bill of Rights restricted state governments from the start. Its restraints originally applied to the national government.

solution: The Virginia Plan would have based representation in both houses on population, favoring large states, while the New Jersey Plan would have given each state equal representation, favoring small states. The House is apportioned by population, which gives large states more influence there; the Senate gives each state two members, which gives small states equal weight.
