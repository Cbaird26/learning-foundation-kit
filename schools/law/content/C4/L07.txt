@article
Article III vests "the judicial Power" in one Supreme Court and in lower courts Congress creates. Federal judges are nominated by the president, confirmed by a Senate majority, hold office "during good behavior" (in practice for life, unless impeached) and cannot have their salaries reduced. These protections are meant to insulate judges from political pressure.

### Structure and jurisdiction

There are 94 federal district courts (trial courts), 13 courts of appeals (11 numbered regional circuits, the D.C. Circuit and the Federal Circuit) and the Supreme Court, whose size is set by statute at nine. Most cases are heard in state courts. Federal courts hear cases of two main kinds: those involving a **federal question** (the Constitution, federal statutes or treaties) and those involving **diversity of citizenship**, which requires citizens of different states and an amount in controversy over \$75,000. The Supreme Court has **original jurisdiction** in a few matters, such as disputes between states, and otherwise acts mainly as an appellate court.

### Judicial review

The Constitution does not expressly give courts the power to strike down laws. *Marbury v. Madison* (1803) established it. Marbury sought a writ of mandamus under section 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789. The Court held that section 13 enlarged the Court's original jurisdiction beyond what Article III allows, and that a law conflicting with the Constitution is void: "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is." Later cases extended review to state laws and state-court judgments (*Martin v. Hunter's Lessee*, 1816; *Cohens v. Virginia*, 1821).

### Limits on courts: justiciability

Article III limits courts to **cases and controversies**, so federal courts do not issue advisory opinions. A plaintiff needs **standing**: an injury in fact that is concrete and particularized, fairly traceable to the defendant, and redressable by a favorable decision (*Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 1992). A general grievance shared by all citizens is not enough. A claim must be **ripe** (not premature) and not **moot** (still live). The **political question doctrine** leaves some disputes to the other branches (*Baker v. Carr*, 1962, narrowed it for malapportionment; *Rucho v. Common Cause*, 2019, held partisan-gerrymandering claims nonjusticiable in federal court).

### Remedies: universal injunctions

In *Trump v. CASA, Inc.* (2025), the Court held that universal injunctions likely exceed the equitable authority Congress gave the federal courts; relief is limited to what is needed to give the plaintiffs complete relief. The Court noted that class actions remain available, and it left open whether states may obtain broader relief.

### Interpretation and precedent

Judges differ on method. **Originalists** and **textualists** look to the original meaning of the text; supporters say this constrains judges and honors democratic enactment. Proponents of a **living constitution** approach argue that general terms like "equal protection" and "unreasonable" must be applied to changing circumstances; supporters say this keeps the document workable. **Judicial restraint** advocates urge deference to elected branches; **judicial activism** is a label critics apply, from different directions, to decisions they disagree with. Courts normally follow precedent (*stare decisis*), yet the Court has overruled precedents, such as *Dobbs* (2022) and *Trump v. Slaughter* (2026), and statutory precedents may be kept more firmly than constitutional ones.

@example
title: Does the plaintiff have standing?
problem: A taxpayer sues to stop a federal program she believes is unwise, alleging only that her tax money is spent on it and that she objects. A different plaintiff, a business denied a permit under a new rule, sues to challenge the rule. Who has standing?
step: Standing requires injury in fact, causation and redressability.
step: The taxpayer alleges a generalized grievance shared by all taxpayers, not a concrete and particularized injury, so she likely lacks standing.
step: The business has a concrete injury (the denied permit), caused by the rule, and a favorable decision could redress it, so it likely has standing.
step: Standing does not decide the merits; it decides only whether the court may reach them.

@mistakes
- Saying the Constitution expressly grants judicial review. The power comes from *Marbury* and from the structure of Article III and Article VI.
- Confusing diversity jurisdiction with federal-question jurisdiction. Diversity requires citizens of different states and over \$75,000 in controversy.
- Thinking a favorable political question label means the claim is weak. The doctrine concerns whether courts may decide it.
- Treating *CASA* as banning all broad relief. It limits universal injunctions, while class actions and other tools remain.

@practice self
prompt: In 5 to 7 sentences, explain the reasoning of *Marbury v. Madison*: the statute at issue, what the Court said about the Constitution and ordinary legislation, and how it reached the conclusion that courts may review acts of Congress.
solution: Marbury asked the Court for a writ of mandamus under section 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789, which seemed to authorize the Court to issue such writs as an original matter. The Court first found that Marbury had a right to his commission, but then asked whether the Court could give the remedy. It held that Article III fixes the Court's original jurisdiction and that Congress may not enlarge it, so section 13 conflicted with the Constitution. The Court reasoned that the Constitution is written, superior law and that an ordinary statute contrary to it cannot be law. Because judges must decide cases under the law, they must decide which law controls when two conflict, and in a conflict the Constitution prevails. The Court therefore declined to apply section 13 and established judicial review.

rubric: Explains that the Constitution is superior to ordinary legislation.
rubric: Connects the duty to decide cases with the power of judicial review.
hint: Begin with what the statute allowed and what Article III allows.
