@project
title: Policy brief: one issue, several branches and levels of government
overview: You will write a neutral 1,500-word policy brief on an issue that is handled by several branches and levels of government. The brief explains the constitutional authority, the institutions involved and the strongest arguments on each side, without taking a side. It is the capstone for the course and a model of the neutral analytical writing law schools and the LSAT reward.
goal: Identify the constitutional sources of authority for each institution acting on one issue.
goal: Explain how federal, state and local governments, and Congress, the president, agencies and courts, divide the work.
goal: Present the main arguments on each side fairly enough that a supporter of either side would accept your description.
step: Choose an issue from the issue menu in the material below, or one you propose that involves at least two branches and two levels of government. Write a one-sentence statement of the policy question in neutral wording.
step: Build an authority map: for each institution (Congress, the president, agencies, federal courts, states, local governments) list its source of power (a constitutional clause, statute or doctrine from Lessons 1-12) and what it actually does on the issue.
step: Research the recent legal developments on your issue, using primary sources (statutes, regulations, court opinions) and the course reading. For each court decision, record the holding and the main reasoning of the majority and of any dissent.
step: List the strongest arguments on each side, using the framework in the material below: the values each side prioritizes, the empirical claims it makes and the weakest point its own supporters would acknowledge.
step: Draft the brief in about 1,500 words using the structure in the material below. Keep the language descriptive, and replace loaded words with neutral ones.
step: Run a neutrality check: ask someone with a different view, or imagine one, to read the argument sections and say whether their side is described accurately. Revise.
step: Verify every legal statement (case names, holdings, statutes, vote thresholds) against a primary source, and cut any claim you cannot verify.
material: **Issue menu.** Choose one: (1) regulation of drinking-water quality; (2) the use of tariffs and trade authority; (3) the structure and independence of federal regulatory agencies; (4) public school funding and standards; (5) health-insurance coverage for low-income people (Medicaid); (6) rules on campaign finance; (7) the regulation of online speech platforms; (8) immigration enforcement and state and local roles; (9) disaster response and emergency management; (10) rules on firearms. Each issue involves Congress, the president, agencies, courts, states and localities.
material: **Brief structure.** (1) The question, in neutral terms (about 100 words). (2) Constitutional authority and institutions (about 450 words): the powers of Congress, the president, agencies, courts, states and localities, with the doctrines of the course. (3) The legal landscape (about 300 words): key statutes and cases, with holdings stated accurately and no more broadly than they go. (4) Arguments on the first side, then the second side (about 250 words each). (5) Open questions and what evidence would help resolve them (about 150 words).
material: **Neutrality checklist.** Describe each position in terms its own supporters use. Give each side the same number of arguments and a similar length. State holdings, then the majority's and dissent's reasoning, without calling either right. Distinguish facts from values. Do not end with a recommendation.
deliverable: A policy brief of about 1,500 words following the structure in the material.
deliverable: A one-page authority map listing each institution, its source of power and its role on the issue.
deliverable: A short verification list of each case, statute and number cited, with its source.
rubric: Accuracy: constitutional provisions, cases, statutes and vote thresholds are stated correctly and no more broadly than the source supports.
rubric: Institutional analysis: the brief explains the roles of the branches and levels of government with the correct source of authority for each.
rubric: Neutrality: each side's arguments are fair, balanced in length and stated in language its supporters would accept; no recommendation.
rubric: Evidence and sources: claims rest on primary sources and the course reading, and empirical claims are attributed.
rubric: Organization and clarity: a clear structure, plain language and about 1,500 words.
tutor: Which constitutional provisions and doctrines give each branch and level of government a role in my chosen issue?
tutor: Can you read my description of one side's position and tell me whether its supporters would recognize it as fair?
tutor: Which cases on my issue have a majority and dissent, and what is the strongest statement of each reasoning?

### Week by week

1. **Founding and Constitution.** Weaknesses of the Articles; the Great Compromise; separation of powers and checks and balances; Article V (two-thirds to propose, three-fourths of states to ratify); *Federalist No. 10* and *No. 51*.
2. **Federalism.** Enumerated, implied, reserved and concurrent powers; *McCulloch*; the Supremacy Clause and preemption; *Lopez*, *Wickard*, *NFIB*; *South Dakota v. Dole*; anti-commandeering.
3. **Civil liberties.** Selective incorporation; *Brandenburg*, *Tinker*, *Sullivan*; *Kennedy v. Bremerton*; *Mapp*, *Gideon*, *Miranda*; *Dobbs*; *Heller* and *Bruen*.
4. **Civil rights.** The Reconstruction Amendments; *Plessy* and *Brown*; tiers of scrutiny; the Civil Rights Act of 1964; recent cases described by holding.
5. **Congress.** House and Senate; committees; cloture (60 votes); the House impeaches and the Senate tries; *Learning Resources* and tariffs.
6. **The presidency.** Article II powers; *Youngstown*'s three categories; veto, pocket veto and override (two-thirds); *Trump v. Slaughter* and removal.
7. **Courts.** Article III; *Marbury*; justiciability; *Trump v. CASA*; methods of interpretation.
8. **Agencies.** APA; notice and comment; *Loper Bright*, *Jarkesy*, *Corner Post*, *Consumers' Research*; the path to the Supreme Court.
9. **Opinion and media.** Socialization; random sampling and margin of error; agenda setting, framing and priming; Section 230.
10. **Elections.** Plurality, ranked-choice and PR; the 538-vote Electoral College; *Citizens United*, *NRSC*, *Rucho*, *Callais*; parties and interest groups.
11. **Domestic policy.** Policy stages and tools; mandatory and discretionary spending; deficit and debt; marginal tax rates; fiscal and monetary policy.
12. **Foreign policy and state and local government.** Treaty and war powers; *Zivotofsky*; direct democracy; Dillon's Rule and home rule.

### Self-check

- Can you compute any vote threshold (majority, three-fifths, two-thirds, three-fourths) and round correctly?
- Can you state a holding without overstating it, and give the main reasoning on each side?
- Can you tell which level of government handles a policy and cite the source of its power?
- Can you describe a contested question so that supporters of each side would accept your description?

### Common errors

Rounding down thresholds; confusing preemption with anti-commandeering; saying the Bill of Rights applied to the states in 1791; treating 60 votes as the Senate's passage threshold; assuming the president's veto is final; confusing the deficit with the debt; mixing up plurality with majority; and letting loaded words slip into a "neutral" summary.

@exam num
prompt: A bill passes the Senate 66-34 and the House 280-155, with all 100 senators and all 435 representatives voting. The president vetoes it. To override, each house needs two-thirds. How many additional yes votes are needed in total, across both houses, to reach the thresholds? (Round each threshold up.)
answer: 11
check: (math.ceil(2 * 435 / 3) - 280) + (math.ceil(2 * 100 / 3) - 66)
hint: Find the two-thirds threshold for each house, rounding up.
hint: Subtract each house's current yes votes from its threshold and add the shortfalls.
solution: The House needs $\lceil 2 \times 435 / 3 \rceil = 290$, so 10 more votes. The Senate needs $\lceil 2 \times 100 / 3 \rceil = 67$, so 1 more. The total is $10 + 1 = 11$.

@exam mc
prompt: Congress passes a statute directing that 5 percent of a state's federal highway funds be withheld unless the state sets a minimum age for buying alcohol. The condition is stated clearly in the statute and relates to highway safety. A state sues, arguing the statute violates the Tenth Amendment. How will the Court most likely rule under *South Dakota v. Dole*?
choice*: The statute is likely valid, because the condition is clearly stated, related to the program and not coercive, and Congress is acting under its spending power.
choice: The statute is invalid, because the Tenth Amendment forbids Congress from attaching any condition to federal funds.
choice: The statute is invalid, because the anti-commandeering doctrine bars Congress from using money to influence state policy.
choice: The statute is valid only if the Senate approves it by two-thirds vote, as with a treaty.
hint: Recall the *Dole* factors and how large the penalty is.
solution: *Dole* allows conditions on federal funds that serve the general welfare, are unambiguous, relate to the federal interest in the program and are not coercive. A 5 percent loss is not the kind of overwhelming threat found coercive in *NFIB*. Anti-commandeering concerns commands, not funding conditions.

@exam self
prompt: Write a neutral analysis of about 300 words of this scenario. Congress delegates to a federal agency the power to set air-quality standards. The agency issues a rule that a state and an industry group challenge in court, and the state's legislature passes a stricter state law. Identify (a) the sources of authority for Congress, the agency, the courts and the state; (b) the doctrines that govern review of the rule after *Loper Bright*, the major questions doctrine and nondelegation; (c) how the Supremacy Clause and preemption affect the state law; and (d) the strongest argument each side would make.
solution: Congress acts under its commerce and spending powers and may delegate rulemaking to an agency by statute; the agency's authority is limited to what the statute grants. The APA provides for notice-and-comment rulemaking and judicial review for arbitrary and capricious action. After *Loper Bright* (2024), the court decides the best reading of the statute itself and gives the agency's view *Skidmore* respect only to the extent persuasive. If the agency claims authority of vast economic and political significance, the major questions doctrine demands clear congressional authorization (*West Virginia v. EPA*), and a nondelegation challenge asks whether Congress supplied an intelligible principle (*FCC v. Consumers' Research* (2025) upheld such a delegation). The challengers would argue that the agency exceeded the statute and that agency lawmaking weakens accountability; the agency and its supporters would argue that the statute authorizes the rule and that expertise is needed. The state law is subject to the Supremacy Clause: if the federal statute expressly or impliedly preempts state standards, the state law is invalid; if the statute sets a floor and permits stricter rules, it may stand. The state would argue that its police power allows stricter protection; industry would argue that national uniformity requires a single standard.
rubric: Identifies a source of authority for each of Congress, the agency, the courts and the state.
rubric: Applies Loper Bright, major questions and nondelegation accurately.
rubric: Explains the Supremacy Clause and preemption, noting that a floor-setting statute may allow stricter state law.
rubric: States the strongest argument for each side in neutral language.
hint: Organize by institution first, then by doctrine, then by argument.
