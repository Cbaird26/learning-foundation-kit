@article
**Civil liberties** are protections against government power, mostly found in the Bill of Rights. In *Barron v. Baltimore* (1833) the Court held that the Bill of Rights restrained only the national government. After the Fourteenth Amendment (1868) barred states from depriving anyone of "liberty" without due process, the Court began **selective incorporation**: it applies individual guarantees to the states one at a time through the Due Process Clause, beginning with free speech in *Gitlow v. New York* (1925). Almost every provision has now been incorporated.

### Speech

The First Amendment protects speech, press, assembly and petition. Government may restrict speech only within limits the Court has defined.

- **Incitement**: under *Brandenburg v. Ohio* (1969), advocacy is protected unless it is directed to inciting **imminent lawless action** and is likely to produce it.
- **True threats**: *Counterman v. Colorado* (2023) requires proof that the speaker had at least a reckless mindset about the threatening nature of the statement.
- **Defamation**: *New York Times v. Sullivan* (1964) requires a public official to prove "actual malice", meaning knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for the truth.
- **Students**: *Tinker v. Des Moines* (1969) protects student expression unless school officials can reasonably forecast substantial disruption.
- **Symbolic speech**: *Texas v. Johnson* (1989) protected flag burning as expressive conduct.
- **Content discrimination**: laws that target speech by content or viewpoint receive strict scrutiny. In *Chiles v. Salazar* (2026), the Court held that a ban on conversion therapy, as applied to talk therapy, regulates speech based on content and viewpoint, and remanded for the correct level of scrutiny.

### Religion

The **Establishment Clause** limits government endorsement or support of religion; the **Free Exercise Clause** protects religious practice. In *Employment Division v. Smith* (1990), a neutral, generally applicable law does not require a religious exemption. For establishment questions, *Kennedy v. Bremerton School District* (2022) abandoned the older *Lemon* test and read the clause by reference to historical practices and understandings. In *Mahmoud v. Taylor* (2025), the Court recognized a parents' free-exercise claim to opt their children out of certain instruction. Supporters of these decisions stress religious liberty and history; critics argue they risk pressure on minority students and weaker separation of church and state.

### Rights of the accused

- **Fourth Amendment**: protects against unreasonable searches; *Mapp v. Ohio* (1961) applied the **exclusionary rule** to the states. *Case v. Montana* (2026) allows warrantless home entry when police have an objectively reasonable basis to believe someone inside needs emergency aid, and *Chatrie v. United States* (2026) treats obtaining a person's location data from Google by a geofence warrant as a search.
- **Fifth Amendment**: *Miranda v. Arizona* (1966) requires warnings before custodial interrogation; *Vega v. Tekoh* (2022) held that a *Miranda* violation alone does not support a § 1983 damages claim.
- **Sixth Amendment**: *Gideon v. Wainwright* (1963) requires appointed counsel for indigent felony defendants in state court.

### Privacy and arms

*Griswold v. Connecticut* (1965) recognized a right of marital privacy in contraception. *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization* (2022) overruled *Roe v. Wade* and *Planned Parenthood v. Casey*, holding that the Constitution does not confer a right to abortion and that the matter returns to the people and their elected representatives; the dissent argued the decision removed a long-recognized liberty. In *District of Columbia v. Heller* (2008) and *McDonald v. Chicago* (2010), the Court recognized an individual Second Amendment right and applied it to states. *Bruen* (2022) requires firearm regulations to fit the Amendment's text and the nation's historical tradition, and *United States v. Rahimi* (2024) upheld the federal ban for persons under domestic-violence restraining orders.

@example
title: Applying the incitement and student-speech tests
problem: A public high school student wears a black armband to protest a policy. Teachers report quiet whispers but no disruption. Separately, an adult tells a rally that "someday the system must be overturned by force." Which tests apply, and what is the likely result?
step: For the student, apply *Tinker*: school officials may restrict student expression only if they can reasonably forecast substantial disruption of school activities.
step: Whispers are not substantial disruption, so the armband is likely protected, and a suspension would likely be unconstitutional.
step: For the speaker, apply *Brandenburg*: protected unless directed to inciting imminent lawless action and likely to produce it.
step: A general statement about a someday action is abstract advocacy, not imminent incitement, so it is likely protected.

@mistakes
- Saying the Bill of Rights applied to the states in 1791. Incorporation came case by case through the Fourteenth Amendment.
- Confusing advocacy of lawbreaking with incitement. Abstract advocacy is protected; only imminent, likely lawless action is not.
- Treating *Dobbs* as banning abortion nationwide. It returned the question to elected governments; it did not itself enact a ban.
- Reading *Vega v. Tekoh* as making *Miranda* warnings optional for police. It limited one civil remedy.

@practice self
prompt: A public university bans all protests within 50 feet of any building. A student group protests peacefully in front of the library and is told to disband. Write a short analysis (6 to 8 sentences) identifying the constitutional issue, the governing doctrines you would use to analyze the rule, and the arguments each side would make, without declaring a winner.
hint: Decide first whether the rule is content-based or content-neutral.
solution: The issue is whether the rule violates the First Amendment's protection of speech and assembly, which applies to the state university through the Fourteenth Amendment. A court would first ask whether the rule regulates speech by its content or viewpoint (strict scrutiny) or is content-neutral, in which case it is judged as a reasonable time, place and manner restriction that serves a significant interest, is narrowly tailored and leaves open alternative channels. Campus open spaces may be treated as public forums. The student group would argue that a blanket ban on peaceful protest near all buildings is overbroad and not narrowly tailored. The university would argue that it has a significant interest in access to buildings, safety and uninterrupted instruction, and that protest remains available elsewhere on campus.
rubric: Identifies the First Amendment speech and assembly issue and incorporation against a public university.
rubric: Distinguishes content-based from content-neutral rules and names time, place and manner analysis.
