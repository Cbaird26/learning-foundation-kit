@article
**Federalism** divides governing authority between a national government and state governments, each with its own institutions and its own relationship to citizens. A resident of Texas, for example, is governed by federal law, Texas law and local ordinances at once. The framers chose this design as a middle path between the weak confederation of the Articles and a single unified government.

### Sources of power

The national government has **enumerated powers**: those listed in the Constitution, mainly in Article I, Section 8 (taxing and spending, regulating commerce among the states, coining money, declaring war, raising armies). The **Necessary and Proper Clause** adds **implied powers**: in *McCulloch v. Maryland* (1819) the Court held that Congress could charter a national bank as a means of carrying out its enumerated powers, and that a state could not tax that bank. The **Tenth Amendment** says powers not delegated to the United States nor prohibited to the states are reserved to the states or the people. States exercise a general **police power** over health, safety, welfare and morals. Some powers, such as taxation, are **concurrent**. The **Supremacy Clause** (Article VI) makes valid federal law supreme over conflicting state law, and **preemption** is the doctrine applying it.

### The commerce power

Congress may regulate commerce "among the several states." *Gibbons v. Ogden* (1824) read commerce broadly. In *Wickard v. Filburn* (1942), the Court upheld regulation of wheat grown for home use because the aggregate effect of such activity substantially affected interstate commerce. The Court later marked outer limits: *United States v. Lopez* (1995) struck down a federal ban on guns near schools, and *United States v. Morrison* (2000) struck down a civil remedy in the Violence Against Women Act, in each case because the activity was not economic in nature. In *NFIB v. Sebelius* (2012), a majority held that the commerce power does not let Congress compel people to buy a product, though it upheld the minimum-coverage provision under the taxing power.

### Money and commandeering

Congress uses grants to shape state policy. *South Dakota v. Dole* (1987) allows conditions on federal funds if they serve the general welfare, are stated clearly, relate to the federal interest in the program, and are not unconstitutionally coercive. In *NFIB*, a majority held that threatening states with the loss of existing Medicaid funds for refusing the expansion was coercive. Grants often require **matching** state spending.

The Court also protects state governments from being directed. Under the **anti-commandeering doctrine** (*New York v. United States*, 1992; *Printz v. United States*, 1997; *Murphy v. NCAA*, 2018), Congress may regulate private parties but may not command state legislatures or officers to enact or enforce federal policy.

### Models of federalism

Scholars describe **dual federalism** (separate spheres, dominant until the 1930s) and **cooperative federalism** (shared programs funded by federal grants and run by states, dominant since the New Deal). Supporters of strong state authority stress local experimentation, closeness to voters and diversity of preferences. Supporters of strong national authority stress uniform rights, spillover effects across state lines and the resources for large problems. Both sides point to the constitutional text and history.

### Mapping policy to levels

Most policy areas involve several levels. National defense, immigration enforcement and monetary policy are mainly federal. Criminal law, education, family law and most land-use rules are mainly state and local. Health care, environmental protection, transportation and welfare are shared.

@example
title: Splitting the cost of a matching grant
problem: A federal program pays 70% of an approved \$40 million highway project under a matching grant; the state pays the rest. What does the state pay, and what condition might the federal government attach?
step: The federal share is $0.70 \times 40 = 28$ million dollars.
step: The state share is the remainder: $40 - 28 = 12$ million dollars (30% of the total).
step: Under *South Dakota v. Dole*, Congress may attach a condition related to the program's purpose, such as highway-safety standards, if it is stated clearly and is not coercive.
step: A condition threatening to withdraw a large share of unrelated existing funding would raise the coercion problem addressed in *NFIB v. Sebelius*.

@mistakes
- Believing the Tenth Amendment gives states a veto over any federal law. A valid federal law within an enumerated power prevails under the Supremacy Clause.
- Confusing preemption with anti-commandeering: preemption displaces conflicting state law, while anti-commandeering stops Congress from forcing states to carry out federal policy.
- Treating all police-power areas as exclusively state. Congress may legislate on health or safety if it is acting under an enumerated power.
- Saying *Lopez* ended the commerce power. It set one outer limit on non-economic activity.

@practice mc
prompt: A state statute requires a product label that directly conflicts with a validly enacted federal labeling statute covering the same product. Which principle most directly resolves the conflict?
choice*: The Supremacy Clause, so the valid federal statute controls and the state label rule is preempted.
choice: The Tenth Amendment, so the state rule controls because labeling is a state police power.
choice: Dual federalism, so each government may enforce its own rule independently.
choice: Anti-commandeering, so Congress may not regulate labels at all.
hint: When valid federal law and state law truly conflict, which is supreme?
solution: Article VI makes valid federal law supreme. The Tenth Amendment does not protect a state rule that conflicts with a valid exercise of an enumerated power, and anti-commandeering concerns commands to state officials, not regulation of private sellers.

@practice mc
prompt: What did the Court hold in *McCulloch v. Maryland* (1819)?
choice*: Congress may charter a national bank under the Necessary and Proper Clause, and a state may not tax it.
choice: States may tax any federal institution located within their borders.
choice: The commerce power cannot reach activity that occurs within a single state.
choice: Federal law is supreme only when it is expressly listed in Article I.
hint: Think of the implied powers and which government's power prevailed.
solution: The Court read the Necessary and Proper Clause to allow means reasonably related to enumerated powers, and held that the state tax on the federal bank was invalid under the Supremacy Clause.

@quiz mc
prompt: Congress passes a law directing state police officers to run federal background checks on gun purchasers. Which doctrine is most directly raised by a state challenge?
choice*: Anti-commandeering, because Congress may not command state officers to carry out a federal program.
choice: Preemption, because the state has already passed a conflicting statute.
choice: The Necessary and Proper Clause, which always allows such commands.
choice: Dual federalism, which treats the gun area as exclusively national.
hint: Who is being commanded: private parties, or state officers?
solution: *Printz v. United States* (1997) held that Congress may not require state officers to administer a federal regulatory program. Preemption requires a conflicting state law, which the problem does not describe.

@quiz num

unit: million dollars
