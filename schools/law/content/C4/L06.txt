@article
Article II vests "the executive Power" in a president. A president must be a natural-born citizen, at least 35, and a resident of the United States for 14 years. The Twenty-Second Amendment (1951) limits a president to two elected terms, and the Twenty-Fifth (1967) governs succession and presidential disability.

### Formal powers

The president is commander in chief of the armed forces, may require the written opinions of department heads, grants reprieves and pardons for federal offenses (except in impeachment), makes treaties with the advice and consent of two-thirds of senators present, and nominates ambassadors, federal judges and other officers with Senate consent. The president gives Congress information on the state of the union, recommends measures and ensures the laws are "faithfully executed." Presidents also issue **executive orders** and proclamations; these must rest on a constitutional or statutory source. Under the **War Powers Resolution** (1973), the president must report to Congress within 48 hours of introducing forces into hostilities and generally withdraw within 60 days, extendable by 30 days for withdrawal, unless Congress authorizes the action; presidents of both parties have questioned its constitutionality.

### Limits on executive action

*Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer* (1952), the steel-seizure case, produced Justice Jackson's concurrence, which sorts presidential action into three categories: (1) with express or implied congressional authorization, where power is at its maximum; (2) where Congress has been silent, a "zone of twilight"; and (3) against Congress's express or implied will, where power is at its lowest ebb. Courts also check the president by statutory interpretation: in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* (2026), the Court held that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorize tariffs.

### The veto and bill-to-law path

After a bill passes both houses in identical text, it goes to the president. The president may sign it, or return it with objections (a **veto**). If the president does nothing for 10 days (Sundays excepted) while Congress is in session, the bill becomes law without signature. If Congress adjourns and prevents return, the unsigned bill dies: a **pocket veto**. To **override** a veto, each house must pass the bill again by a two-thirds vote. Overrides are rare because they require large bipartisan majorities.

### Removal power

Whether the president may remove officials at will has changed. *Myers v. United States* (1926) protected presidential removal of executive officers. *Humphrey's Executor v. United States* (1935) had allowed for-cause protection for FTC commissioners. *Seila Law v. CFPB* (2020) and *Collins v. Yellen* (2021) struck limits for single-director agencies. In *Trump v. Slaughter* (June 2026, 6-3), the Court overruled *Humphrey's Executor*: the FTC Act's for-cause removal protection is unconstitutional, so commissioners are removable at will. The Court left open the Federal Reserve (in *Trump v. Cook*, 2026, it refused to let a Federal Reserve governor's removal take effect while that case continues) and offices outside the traditional executive branch.

Supporters of broad removal power rely on Article II's vesting of executive power in one president, who is accountable to voters. Critics argue that Congress may create independent agencies insulated from politics for expertise and stability, and warn about concentrated power. The decision leaves several structural questions unresolved.

@example
title: Categorizing a presidential action
problem: Congress passes a statute barring the president from sending troops to a named country. The president sends troops anyway, citing commander-in-chief power. Which Youngstown category applies, and how should a court approach it?
step: The statute expresses Congress's will against the action, so the action falls in Jackson's third category, where presidential power is at its lowest ebb.
step: The president can prevail only if the power is exclusive, meaning Congress may not constitutionally limit it.
step: A court would ask whether commander-in-chief power over deploying troops is exclusive or shared with Congress's powers to declare war, fund and regulate the military.
step: Because the powers overlap, the answer is contested; the framework tells you where the burden falls, not the result.

@mistakes
- Saying the president's veto is final. Congress can override with two-thirds of each house.
- Describing the pocket veto as occurring while Congress is in session. It requires adjournment that prevents return of the bill.
- Believing the president can declare war. Congress has that power, though presidents use force on their own authority.
- Reading *Trump v. Slaughter* as settling the Federal Reserve's status. The Court left that question open.

@practice self
prompt: Trace a bill from introduction to law and explain what happens if the president vetoes it. Cover committees, floor action in each chamber, the 10-day rule, the pocket veto and the override votes in 6 to 9 sentences.
solution: A member introduces the bill and it is referred to committee, which may hold hearings and markups; most bills die there. If reported, the House Rules Committee sets terms for floor debate and the House votes by majority. In the Senate, debate may be ended by cloture (60 votes), and the bill passes by majority. Both houses must pass identical text, often resolved through amendments between the chambers or a conference committee. The bill goes to the president, who has 10 days (Sundays excepted) to sign it or return it with objections; if the president does nothing while Congress is in session, it becomes law. If Congress has adjourned and prevents return, an unsigned bill dies by pocket veto. If the president vetoes it, each house may override by a two-thirds vote, and the bill becomes law without presidential approval.
