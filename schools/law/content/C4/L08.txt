@article
The **bureaucracy** is the part of government that carries out the laws. Congress writes statutes in general terms, and agencies fill in details by issuing rules, running programs, investigating violations and adjudicating disputes. Agencies are therefore a major source of law, which raises questions about democratic accountability.

### Kinds of agencies

There are 15 executive departments (such as State, Treasury and Defense), headed by secretaries (the Attorney General heads Justice) who, with other senior officials, form the Cabinet; agencies and commissions outside the departments, often called independent agencies (such as the Securities and Exchange Commission and the Federal Trade Commission, though after *Trump v. Slaughter* (2026) the President may remove FTC commissioners at will); and government corporations. The **Pendleton Act** (1883) began the merit-based **civil service** to replace the spoils system, and the Hatch Act limits partisan activity by federal employees.

### The Administrative Procedure Act

The **Administrative Procedure Act (APA)** of 1946 governs agency procedure. In **notice-and-comment rulemaking** (5 U.S.C. § 553), an agency publishes a proposed rule, accepts public comments and issues a final rule with a statement of its basis. Courts may set aside agency action that is "arbitrary and capricious," for example when the agency ignores an important aspect of the problem or fails to explain its reasoning. Agencies also adjudicate individual disputes in hearings before administrative law judges.

### Judicial review after Loper Bright

For forty years, *Chevron* told courts to defer to a reasonable agency reading of an ambiguous statute. In *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo* (2024), the Court overruled *Chevron*: courts must decide the best reading of a statute themselves and may give an agency's view *Skidmore* respect, depending on the agency's expertise, consistency and the persuasiveness of its reasoning. Prior holdings that rested on *Chevron* remain in force under statutory stare decisis. Related doctrines also constrain agencies. The **major questions doctrine** (*West Virginia v. EPA*, 2022; *Biden v. Nebraska*, 2023) requires clear congressional authorization for decisions of vast economic and political significance. The **nondelegation doctrine** forbids Congress from giving away legislative power, but the Court asks only whether Congress supplied an "intelligible principle"; *FCC v. Consumers' Research* (2025) upheld the Universal Service Fund against a nondelegation challenge. In *SEC v. Jarkesy* (2024), the Seventh Amendment requires a jury when the SEC seeks civil penalties for securities fraud. In *Corner Post v. Board of Governors* (2024), an APA claim accrues when the plaintiff is injured, so a newly formed business may challenge an old rule.

Supporters of these decisions say they restore the separation of powers and ensure judges, not agencies, interpret the law. Critics say agencies have specialized expertise, that Congress cannot foresee every detail and that the decisions increase uncertainty and litigation.

### Presidential control

Congress oversees agencies through appropriations, hearings and statutes, and the president through appointments and executive orders. *Seila Law* (2020), *Collins v. Yellen* (2021) and *Trump v. Slaughter* (2026) hold that limits on the president's power to remove heads of single-director agencies and FTC commissioners are unconstitutional, though the Court left open the Federal Reserve and offices outside the traditional executive branch.

### How a case reaches the Supreme Court

A federal case starts in a district court, or, for many agency actions, goes directly to a court of appeals. The losing party appeals to the court of appeals for its circuit, then may file a petition for a writ of **certiorari**. The Court takes only a small fraction of petitions, usually where circuits disagree or an important federal question is at stake, and grants review when four of nine justices vote (the **rule of four**). After briefs and oral argument, the justices vote and a majority decides. A state case can reach the Court after the state's highest court rules on a federal question.

@example
title: Timing a challenge to an old agency rule
problem: An agency adopted a rule in 2016. A company formed in 2022 is first harmed by the rule in 2023. Under *Corner Post*, and assuming a six-year limitations period for APA claims, when does its claim accrue, and what is its last year to sue?
step: Under *Corner Post*, the claim accrues when the plaintiff is injured, not when the rule was issued.
step: The company was first injured in 2023, so the claim accrued in 2023.
step: Adding six years to 2023 gives 2029 as the last year to file.
step: The 2016 date would have run the clock out in 2022 under the older view, which is why the decision matters to newer businesses.

@mistakes
- Saying *Loper Bright* eliminated all judicial respect for agencies. Courts still give *Skidmore* respect to persuasive agency views.
- Believing that prior *Chevron* cases were all overturned. Their holdings stay in force under statutory stare decisis.
- Treating the rule of four as a requirement that four justices agree on the outcome. It only governs whether the Court takes the case.
- Assuming the president has no control over independent agencies. Removal power is central after *Slaughter*, *Seila Law* and *Collins*.
