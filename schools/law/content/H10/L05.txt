@article
**Rhetoric** is the art of using language effectively to persuade or inform. Aristotle's three **appeals** remain the standard tools of analysis.

### The three appeals

- **Ethos** is the appeal to credibility and character. A speaker builds ethos by showing expertise, honesty, fairness or shared values: "As a nurse for twenty years, I have seen this."
- **Pathos** is the appeal to emotion: vivid stories, loaded words, imagery that makes an audience feel fear, pride, pity or hope.
- **Logos** is the appeal to logic and evidence: statistics, facts, reasoning, clear cause and effect, examples.

Most strong arguments combine all three. Analysis asks not only *which* appeal is used but *how well* and *why*.

### The rhetorical situation

Every text answers a situation: the **speaker** (who is speaking and with what authority), the **audience** (who is being addressed and what they already believe), the **purpose** (what the speaker wants them to think or do), and the **context** (time, place, occasion). A speech to graduating students will use different appeals from a report to a city council.

### Common devices

- **Repetition** and **parallelism** (matching structure) create rhythm and emphasis.
- **Rhetorical question**: a question asked for effect, not for an answer.
- **Anaphora**: repeating a word or phrase at the start of successive clauses.
- **Antithesis**: contrasting ideas in balanced phrases ("ask not what..., ask what...").
- **Analogy** and **anecdote** make an abstract point concrete.
- **Concession and rebuttal** acknowledge an opposing view and answer it, which builds ethos.

### Logical fallacies

A **fallacy** is a flaw in reasoning that can make a weak argument seem strong:

- **Ad hominem**: attacking the person rather than the argument.
- **Straw man**: distorting an opponent's view to refute it easily.
- **False dilemma**: presenting only two options when more exist.
- **Slippery slope**: claiming one step will inevitably lead to an extreme result without evidence.
- **Hasty generalization**: drawing a broad conclusion from too few cases.
- **Bandwagon**: arguing that something is right because many people do it.

### Analyzing a text

Quote a passage, name the appeal, then explain its effect on this audience. "The speaker's story about her own daughter (pathos) makes parents feel the stakes personally" is stronger than "she uses pathos."

@example
title: Labeling appeals
problem: A city official says: "I have managed this district's budget for ten years. Last year, injuries at the unlit intersection rose from 4 to 11. Imagine your child crossing there at dusk. Install the lights." Identify the appeals.
step: "I have managed this district's budget for ten years" establishes experience: ethos.
step: "Injuries rose from 4 to 11" is a statistic: logos.
step: "Imagine your child crossing there at dusk" invites fear and protectiveness: pathos.
step: Judgment: together the appeals persuade; the statistic is checkable, while the emotional image strengthens it without replacing it.

@mistakes
- Naming an appeal without explaining its effect on the audience.
- Treating pathos as bad or illogical; emotion can be legitimate when it fits the evidence.
- Confusing ethos with ego; ethos is about the audience's trust.
- Calling any disagreement a fallacy; a fallacy is a specific flaw in reasoning.
