@article
The **Discussion** is the heart of an office memo, usually about 60 percent of its length. It is where you show the reader how you reached your prediction. Each issue gets its own subsection written in CREAC form: a **conclusion** (thesis), the **rule**, an **explanation** of the rule through cases, **application** to the client's facts, and a closing **conclusion**.

### Start with a thesis (the C in CREAC)

Open each section with a short statement of your conclusion and the main reason. "A court will probably find that Owner knew of the dog's dangerous propensities because the dog had snapped at neighbors on several occasions of which Owner was aware." The reader knows where the section is going. For a **multi-element issue**, open with an **umbrella paragraph**: state the overall rule, list its elements, say which are not seriously disputed and which are contested, and give your overall conclusion. Then analyze each element in its own section or paragraph.

### State the rule (R)

State the rule precisely, with its source (statute section, controlling case). Quote statutory text where the words matter. Make the rule the **synthesized** rule from Week 5; name the jurisdiction's controlling authority, not a paraphrase of a treatise.

### Explain the rule (E)

Show how courts have applied the rule. Choose the **best cases**: controlling authority first, then those closest to your facts. For each case give the court, the **material facts**, the **holding**, and the **reasoning**, in your own words, and cite it. For the principal cases, a paragraph; for supporting cases, a sentence or a parenthetical, such as "(holding that a dog's prior growling gave notice)". Do not summarize an entire opinion; extract only what helps the reader understand the rule's scope. Do not put the client's facts in the explanation; keep that for the application.

### Apply the rule (A)

Apply the rule to the client's facts, element by element. Use the cases: analogize to those that help, distinguish those that do not (Week 6). **Show your reasoning** rather than stating conclusions: tie each fact to the rule's requirement and say why it matters. Add a **counter-analysis**: the best argument against your conclusion and why you think it is weaker. Use only facts that appear in the Statement of Facts.

### Conclude (C)

End with a brief conclusion that repeats the prediction and the main reason, so a reader who skims the last sentence gets the answer.

### Paragraph craft

Give each paragraph one point with a **topic sentence** that states it, a transition from the preceding paragraph, and supporting sentences ordered logically. Use predictive language ("likely," "a court could find") and give reasons. Avoid first-person opinion ("I feel") and avoid filler openers ("It is important to note that").

### Allocating words

A memo is about the issues in dispute. Give an undisputed element a sentence or two ("The first element is met because ...") and spend the words on the contested element, with its rule explanation, application and counter-analysis. The more uncertain the outcome, the more space the analysis deserves. A 2,500-word memo might devote roughly 60 percent to the Discussion.

### Applying it

After drafting, test your discussion by asking: Does each paragraph's topic sentence state a point? Does each case do a job? Did I show how the facts meet or miss each element? Would the supervising lawyer know my confidence?

@example
title: A rule explanation paragraph
problem: Rule (hypothetical Marlow): an owner is liable for a dog bite if the owner knew or should have known of the dog's dangerous propensities. Quill: dog bit a neighbor before; owner liable. Teague: dog snapped at several neighbors, never bit; owner liable because owner knew of aggression. Write a rule explanation.
step: Lead with the rule and what it requires: "An owner is liable for a dog bite if the owner knew or should have known of the dog's dangerous propensities."
step: Give the first case with facts, holding and reasoning: "In *Quill*, the owner was liable because the dog had previously bitten a neighbor, which made its danger known."
step: Add the second case to show scope: "In *Teague*, the owner was liable even though the dog had never bitten anyone because it had snapped at neighbors and the owner knew of its aggression, showing that a prior bite is not required."
step: End with the principle: "Together these cases show that the question is the owner's knowledge of the dog's dangerous propensities, not the number of prior bites."

@example
title: Strengthening an application paragraph
problem: A draft says: "Pat was bitten by Rex. Rex had growled. Owner is probably liable." Revise.
step: Identify the weakness: the draft states facts and a conclusion but does not link them to the rule or a case.
step: Add the rule and the link: "Owner is probably liable because the facts show Owner knew of Rex's dangerous propensities."
step: Use the facts and the case: "Rex had growled at Pat twice and at two other neighbors, and the owner was told of it. As in *Teague*, where snapping at neighbors gave notice without a prior bite, the repeated growling shows aggression the owner knew about."
step: Add counter-analysis: "Owner may argue that growling is not snapping, but the owner's knowledge of repeated threats is the same kind of notice."

@mistakes
- Stating a conclusion without applying the rule to specific facts.
- Putting the client's facts in the rule explanation, or putting new law in the application.
- Spending as many words on an undisputed element as on the contested one.
- Leaving out the counter-analysis, which makes the memo read like advocacy.

@practice self
prompt: Write a Discussion section (about 300–400 words) on one issue. Rule (hypothetical Marlow): an owner is liable for a dog bite if the owner knew or should have known of the dog's dangerous propensities. Cases: Quill (dog bit a neighbor before; owner liable); Sato (dog never bit or threatened anyone; owner not liable); Teague (dog snapped at several neighbors, never bit; owner liable because owner knew of the aggression). Facts: Pat was bitten by Rex, the dog of Pat's neighbor, Lee. Before the bite, Rex had growled at Pat twice and had lunged at a delivery driver, who told Lee. Rex had never bitten anyone. Use CREAC.
solution: Lee is probably liable because Lee likely knew of Rex's dangerous propensities. Marlow holds an owner liable for a dog bite if the owner knew or should have known of the dog's dangerous propensities. In Quill, the owner was liable because the dog had bitten before. In Sato, the owner was not liable because the dog had never bitten or threatened anyone. In Teague, the owner was liable even though the dog had never bitten, because the dog had snapped at several neighbors and the owner knew of the aggression. Together these cases show that a prior bite is not required; what matters is the owner's knowledge of dangerous behavior. Here, Rex had never bitten anyone, which resembles Sato on that point. But Rex had also lunged at a delivery driver, and the driver told Lee, which gave Lee actual knowledge of aggression. As in Teague, where aggressive behavior known to the owner was enough, Lee knew of a threatening incident. The growling at Pat is weaker evidence because there is no indication Lee knew of it. Lee may argue that lunging and growling are not signs of a dangerous propensity and that Sato requires a threat of harm, but Teague treats known aggression as sufficient, and a lunge at a stranger is more serious than growling. A court will probably find that Lee knew of Rex's dangerous propensities and hold Lee liable.
rubric: Begins with a conclusion and the main reason.
rubric: States the rule and explains it through the supplied cases, with facts and holdings.
rubric: Applies the rule to the facts, using Teague and Sato as analogies and distinctions, with reasoning.
rubric: Includes a counter-analysis and ends with a conclusion; uses no facts beyond those supplied.
hint: The deciding fact is what Lee knew, and when and how.

@quiz mc
prompt: In the application of a Discussion, a writer concludes that the client will lose because the facts resemble an adverse case but does not address the client's best counterargument. What is the most accurate criticism?
choice: The writer should have omitted the adverse case.
choice*: The analysis lacks a counter-analysis, so the reader cannot judge how strong the prediction is.
choice: The writer should have used the first person to express confidence.
choice: The writer should have stated the facts again in the rule explanation.
hint: What does an objective memo owe the reader?
solution: An objective memo should address the strongest opposing argument and explain why it is weaker. Omitting adverse authority and adding first-person confidence both reduce objectivity.

@quiz mc
prompt: Which sentence uses the best predictive language?
choice: "Clearly the court will rule for the client."
choice*: "A court will probably rule for the client because the employee gave written notice, which the statute requires."
choice: "I feel the court might possibly rule for the client."
choice: "It is important to note that the court may or may not rule for the client."
hint: Good predictive language states a calibrated conclusion and a reason.
solution: "Probably" plus a reason gives the reader a prediction and its basis. "Clearly" overstates, the first-person sentence is weak and vague, and the sentence beginning "It is important to note" is filler that says nothing.
