@article
Common-law reasoning is **reasoning by analogy**. A court that follows precedent says: this case is like that one in the ways the law cares about, so the result should be the same. A court that departs from precedent without overruling it says: this case differs from that one in a way that matters, so the earlier rule does not control. As a writer, you do the same thing in the **application** part of an analysis.

### Material facts

A fact is **material** if the rule or the court's reasoning makes it relevant. In a spill case the rule turns on notice, so how long the spill sat there, where employees were and whether anyone inspected are material. The spill's color and the customer's shoe size are not. Start every comparison by naming the rule and the facts it makes important.

### Analogizing

An analogy has three parts:

1. The precedent's material facts and result ("In *Ames* (a hypothetical Marlow case), a spill sat for 40 minutes while an employee stood nearby, and the store was liable.")
2. The client's matching material facts ("Here, the spill sat for 30 minutes and a clerk twice walked past it.")
3. The link: **why** the match matters ("In both, the store had the chance to see and fix the hazard, which is the reason the rule imposes liability.")

A claim that two cases are "similar" is not an analogy unless the writer says in what way and why it matters.

### Distinguishing

A distinction identifies a material difference that explains a different result, and ties it to the case's reasoning. "In *Bell* (also hypothetical), the spill had been there two minutes and no employee was nearby; here, a clerk saw the spill. Unlike in *Bell*, the store had actual notice." A difference must be material: that *Bell* involved a supermarket and the client fell in a hardware store is not material unless the rule turns on the kind of store.

### Reasoning, not labels

Courts follow reasons. If the reason behind a precedent applies to the new facts, the cases are alike even if surface facts differ; if it does not, they are different even if the facts look alike. When you state a precedent's reasoning, you can predict where it stops.

### Both sides, honestly

In an **objective memo** you must analyze the facts that help and hurt the client. Present the strongest argument on each side (analogizing favorable cases, distinguishing unfavorable ones), then predict which is more likely to prevail and why. Do not omit a case that is adverse; a supervising lawyer needs it, and in a court filing you must disclose directly adverse controlling authority (ABA Model Rule 3.3(a)(2); see Week 12).

### Synthesis and analogy together

You synthesize first, then analogize to the synthesized rule. In practice, you will group cases: those that favor one side and those that favor the other. Then you apply the rule by running each group against the client's facts.

### Applying it

For each element in dispute: state the rule; give the closest favorable case and compare; give the closest unfavorable case and compare; conclude.

@example
title: Analogizing and distinguishing on one fact pattern
problem: Rule (hypothetical Marlow): a store is liable for a customer's fall on a spill if it had actual notice or the spill existed long enough that reasonable inspection would have found it. Ames: spill 40 minutes, employee nearby, liable. Bell: spill 2 minutes, no employee near, no liability. Client fell on a spill that, per a security video, had been on the floor for 25 minutes while a stocker twice walked past. Write the analysis.
step: Name the material facts: how long the spill sat there, and whether employees were in a position to see it.
step: Analogize to Ames: "As in Ames, an employee was in a position to see the spill. The stocker twice passed it, so the store had the chance to discover and fix it, which is why Ames imposed liability."
step: Distinguish Bell: "Unlike in Bell, where the spill was there for two minutes, this spill was there for 25 minutes, long enough that reasonable inspection would have found it."
step: Conclude: because the duration and the employee's presence both resemble Ames and not Bell, the store likely had constructive notice. (Duration is shorter than in Ames, which is the store's best argument.)

@mistakes
- Asserting that cases are "similar" without saying which material facts match and why they matter.
- Distinguishing on immaterial facts, such as the parties' identities or the kind of building.
- Omitting an adverse case instead of analyzing it.
- Overstating a precedent: the holding cannot be stretched to facts the court never considered.

@practice mc
prompt: In Zed v. Park (a hypothetical case), a tenant was awarded a rent reduction when the heater failed for ten days in winter after the landlord was told. Client's heater failed for twelve days in winter after the landlord was told. Which sentence is the best analogy?
choice: "Zed is similar because both cases involve apartments."
choice*: "As in Zed, the heater failed in winter and the landlord knew; the landlord could have repaired the heater, so a rent reduction is warranted."
choice: "Zed is distinguishable because the tenant in Zed was older."
choice: "Zed controls because it was decided before this dispute arose."
hint: Which choice names matching material facts and explains why they matter?
solution: A good analogy names the material facts (winter failure, landlord's notice, ability to repair) and the reason. "Both are apartments" is too general, age is immaterial, and being earlier does not make a case controlling.

@practice mc
prompt: Rule: a landlord is liable for a tenant's injury from a defect only if the landlord had notice of it. In Pell v. Orr (a hypothetical case) the landlord was told of a broken stair rail and liability was imposed. Client's landlord was never told about the broken stair rail and the rail had broken a day before. Which is the best way to distinguish Pell?
choice: "Pell is distinguishable because it involved a stair rail and this case also involves a stair rail."
choice: "Pell is distinguishable because the tenant in Pell won."
choice*: "Unlike in Pell, where the landlord was told of the defect, here the landlord had no notice, which the rule requires."
choice: "Pell is not binding because it was decided in the past."
hint: Which fact is the rule's required element?
solution: Notice is the element, so the landlord's lack of notice is a material difference that explains a different result. The remaining choices cite immaterial or irrelevant points.

choice*: In the precedent the dog had bitten before; in the client's case it had not.
choice*: In the precedent a neighbor warned the owner about the dog's aggression; in the client's case no one did.
choice: The precedent's dog was brown; the client's dog is black.
choice: The precedent's owner was older than the client.

@practice self
prompt: Synthesize a rule from these four hypothetical State of Marlow noncompete cases. Alder: a five-year, nationwide noncompete for a receptionist was unenforceable. Cole: a one-year, 25-mile noncompete for a sales representative who handled client relationships was enforceable. Evans: a two-year, statewide noncompete for a software engineer who knew trade secrets was enforceable. Gray: a one-year, 25-mile noncompete for a janitor with no access to confidential information was unenforceable. State the rule and test it against each case in one short paragraph.
solution: Rule: a noncompete is enforceable if it protects a legitimate business interest, such as confidential information or client relationships, and is reasonable in duration and geographic reach in light of that interest. Alder fails because the employee had no legitimate-interest connection and the scope (five years, nationwide) was excessive. Cole is enforceable because client relationships are a legitimate interest and one year and 25 miles is reasonable. Evans is enforceable because trade secrets are a legitimate interest and two years statewide is reasonable for that interest. Gray fails because there is no legitimate interest to protect, even though the restriction is modest. The rule explains all four. (This is a hypothetical state; real states vary, and some prohibit most noncompetes.)
rubric: The rule has both the legitimate-interest requirement and the reasonableness requirement.
rubric: Gray is explained by the absence of a legitimate interest, not by scope.
rubric: Alder is explained by both the missing interest and the excessive scope.
rubric: All four cases are tested.
hint: Compare Gray with Cole: restriction is the same, outcome differs. What else differs?

choice: "This case is just like that one."
choice*: "As in the precedent, a clerk walked past the hazard repeatedly, which gave the business the opportunity to discover and remove it."
choice: "The precedent involved a different business, so it is not helpful."

@quiz mc
prompt: Under a rule that a contractor is liable for property damage if it failed to use reasonable care, a precedent held a roofer liable who left a tarp unsecured before an announced storm. Client's roofer left a tarp unsecured, but no storm was forecast. The roofer wants to distinguish the precedent. Which difference is most material?
choice: The precedent involved a roofer in a different city.
choice: The precedent's property was larger.
choice*: Here no storm was forecast, so leaving the tarp unsecured may not have been a failure to use reasonable care.
choice: The precedent was decided by a different judge.
hint: Which fact bears on what reasonable care required?
solution: Foreseeability of the harm is tied to reasonable care, so the missing forecast is material. Location, size and the identity of the judge do not bear on the rule.
