@article
An office memo is written for a lawyer; a **client letter** is written for the person whose problem it is. The client may be a first-time litigant or an experienced executive, but in either case is not a lawyer and needs to know **what you concluded, why, what could go wrong, and what to do next.** A good client letter converts the memo's analysis into a decision-ready explanation.

### Why it matters: the lawyer's duty to communicate

ABA Model Rule 1.4 requires a lawyer to keep the client reasonably informed about the matter, to respond to reasonable requests for information, and to explain a matter to the extent reasonably necessary to permit the client to make informed decisions about the representation. Rule 1.2(a) gives the client the decision on the objectives of the representation, including whether to settle, so a lawyer must promptly communicate settlement offers. Rule 1.1 (competence) requires accurate advice, and Rule 1.6 requires protecting the client's confidential information.

### Structure of a client letter

1. **Heading and label:** date, client's name and address, a clear reference line, and "Privileged and Confidential: Attorney-Client Communication." The label signals confidentiality; it does not itself create privilege.
2. **Bottom line first:** answer the client's question in a sentence or two.
3. **Facts as we understand them:** a short restatement, inviting corrections ("If any of this is wrong, tell us, because it could change our advice").
4. **Explanation:** the rule in plain English, then how it applies to the client's facts.
5. **Risks and uncertainty:** what could change the result, stated honestly.
6. **Options and recommendation:** the choices, the trade-offs, and your recommendation.
7. **Next steps and deadlines:** what you will do, what the client must do, and any deadline (such as a statute of limitations).
8. **Closing:** an invitation to call, and a statement that the advice is based on the facts given.

### Plain English

- Use "you" and "we"; short sentences; active voice.
- Define any legal term you cannot avoid ("a *statute of limitations* is the deadline for filing a lawsuit").
- Replace legalese: "before" instead of "prior to," "ask" instead of "propound."
- Use headings and short lists so the client can scan.
- Explain **why**, not only what; a client who understands the reason can make a better decision.

### Handling uncertainty and expectations

A lawyer cannot promise a result. Say "likely," "more likely than not," "a real risk," and explain what drives the prediction. Avoid false precision (a percentage you cannot support) and avoid both false comfort and alarm. Never guarantee success. Make the letter consistent with the memo: if the memo says "probably," the letter says so, in plain words.

### Professional emails

- Write a **specific subject line** ("Lease renewal: documents needed by Friday").
- Put the **purpose or request in the first lines**; make deadlines and action items explicit.
- Keep paragraphs short; use a list for multiple items; proofread before sending.
- **Check recipients.** Misaddressed emails are a common cause of confidentiality breaches. Be careful with reply-all and with cc and bcc.
- **Protect privilege.** Do not copy people outside the attorney-client relationship on privileged advice, since this can waive privilege. Keep sensitive details out of subject lines, and consider whether a client's work email account is secure and monitored by the employer.
- Be professional in tone. Assume any email may be read by others later.

### Applying it

Write the letter as if reading it to the client across a table. If you would have to stop and define a word, define it in the letter.

title: Turning a memo conclusion into a client sentence
problem: Memo conclusion (hypothetical Marlow law): "Probably yes. Under Marlow's statute, a nonrenewal within 90 days of a good-faith complaint to a housing authority is presumed retaliatory, and the landlord's notice came 50 days after the tenant's complaint. The landlord may rebut with proof that it decided before the complaint." Write the bottom line for the client letter.
step: Identify what the client needs to know: can the landlord lawfully refuse to renew?
step: Translate the legal terms: "presumed retaliatory" means the law starts out assuming the landlord acted in retaliation, unless the landlord proves otherwise.
step: Keep the calibration: "probably" stays as "likely."
step: Write: "Our conclusion is that your landlord's refusal to renew your lease was probably unlawful. Because he told you he would not renew just 50 days after you called the city housing office, the law presumes he was punishing you for complaining. He can still defend himself by proving he decided before you called."

@example
title: Fixing an email
problem: A draft email has the subject "Update" and begins, "I wanted to circle back on a few things. There are some issues. Also please send things when you can." Revise.
step: Fix the subject line: "Lease matter: three documents needed by June 10."
step: Put the purpose first: "To prepare your response, I need three documents from you by June 10."
step: List the action items: (1) your lease, (2) your notice from the landlord, (3) any texts or emails with the landlord about the heater.
step: Add why and how to reach you: "These will let us confirm the dates. Call me with any questions."

@mistakes
- Opening with background and law and burying the answer.
- Using legal terms the client has to guess at, or leaving out why you reached the conclusion.
- Promising success or giving a percentage with no basis.
- Sending a privileged email to the wrong address or copying a third party.

prompt: A client asks whether she can end her commercial lease early. Memo conclusion (hypothetical Marlow law): probably not, because the lease has no early-termination clause and Marlow law does not imply one. Which is the best opening of the client letter?
choice: "Pursuant to our review of the aforementioned lease and applicable authority, we have determined the following."
choice: "Thank you for contacting us. As you know, the firm has many years of experience in lease matters."
choice*: "Our conclusion is that you probably cannot end your lease early, because the lease does not allow it and Marlow law does not give you that right. There are options, which I explain below."
choice: "You absolutely have no way out of the lease."
hint: A client letter leads with the answer, calibrated and in plain words.
solution: The best opening states the answer and the main reason in plain English and signals that options follow. The legalese opening buries the answer, the pleasantry delays it, and the "absolutely no way out" sentence overstates certainty.

@practice self
prompt: Client Jo asks, "Can my landlord keep my \$1,200 security deposit for a stain on the carpet?" Memo conclusion (hypothetical Marlow law): "Probably not. Marlow's statute allows a landlord to deduct only for damage beyond normal wear and tear and requires an itemized statement within 30 days. The landlord sent no statement, and a carpet stain after a three-year tenancy is likely normal wear." Write the bottom line and the next two paragraphs of a client letter in plain English.
solution: Dear Jo: Our conclusion is that your landlord probably cannot keep your \$1,200 deposit. Marlow law lets a landlord deduct only for damage beyond normal wear and tear, and he must send you a written, itemized list of deductions within 30 days. As we understand it, he sent no list, and the stain appeared after you had lived there three years, which a court would likely view as normal wear. Why this matters: the law is designed so that tenants are not charged for ordinary use of a home. There is some uncertainty. If the stain is large or you were negligent, he could argue it is more than normal wear, but his missing list weakens his position. Next steps: we recommend sending him a short letter, which we can draft, asking for the deposit within 10 days. Please send us your lease, the move-out date, and photos of the carpet. If he does not respond, we can discuss filing a claim in small-claims court. This advice is based on the facts you gave us; tell us if any are wrong.
rubric: Opens with the bottom line in plain, calibrated language.
rubric: Explains the rule and why the facts fit it, defining no legal terms without explanation.
rubric: Notes the uncertainty honestly without overpromising.
rubric: Gives concrete next steps and what the client must supply.
hint: Lead with the answer, then the reason, then the risk, then the steps.
