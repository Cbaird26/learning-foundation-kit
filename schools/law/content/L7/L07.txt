@article
An **office memorandum** (or **predictive memo**) is an internal, **objective** document written for a supervising lawyer. It answers a legal question about a client's situation, tells the reader how a court is likely to rule, and explains why. Because the reader will make decisions (settle, file, advise the client) on the basis of your memo, it must report weaknesses as well as strengths. A persuasive brief to a court is a different genre; the memo is an honest assessment. It is usually marked **privileged and confidential, attorney work product**.

### The standard parts

1. **Heading.** To, from, date, and a subject line identifying the client and matter.
2. **Question Presented.** The legal question the memo answers, in one sentence, usually in the "under-does-when" form: **Under** [governing law], **does/is** [legal question] **when** [two to four legally significant facts]?
3. **Brief Answer.** A direct answer (yes, no, probably yes, probably not), the reason in one sentence stating the rule, and the key facts that drive the result. Two to four sentences. State your confidence honestly.
4. **Statement of Facts.** An accurate, organized narrative of the facts that matter: include **unfavorable** facts, background needed to understand the story, and facts that the Discussion will rely on. No legal conclusions or argument; no facts that do not matter; cite the source of facts (interview, document). Chronological order is common.
5. **Discussion.** The analysis, organized by issue, usually with a roadmap or thesis paragraph, then a subsection for each issue or element, written in IRAC or CREAC.
6. **Conclusion.** A short summary of your predictions and, if asked, recommended next steps.

### IRAC and CREAC

**IRAC** is **I**ssue, **R**ule, **A**pplication, **C**onclusion. **CREAC** (also CRAC or TREAT) places the **C**onclusion first and adds an **E**xplanation of the rule: **C**onclusion, **R**ule, **E**xplanation of the rule through cases, **A**pplication to the facts, **C**onclusion. Most legal writing teachers prefer CREAC for each issue because the reader sees the answer first, the rule explanation shows how courts have applied the rule, and the final conclusion ties back to the opening thesis. Exams commonly use IRAC (the "Issue" opens the discussion in lieu of a conclusion); the logic is identical.

### Writing the question presented

A good question presented is **specific** (it names the governing law and the key facts), **neutral** (it does not assume the answer), and **answerable** with yes or no. Avoid "Whether ..." fragments and avoid legal conclusions that smuggle in the answer ("Did the landlord illegally retaliate?").

### Writing the brief answer

Open with the answer. Then give the reason and key facts: "Probably yes. Under the hypothetical Marlow retaliation statute, a nonrenewal within 90 days of a good-faith complaint is presumed retaliatory, and the landlord's notice came 50 days after the tenant's call to the city inspector. The landlord may rebut the presumption only by showing a legitimate reason that it can prove."

### Predicting, not promising

Use calibrated language: "likely," "probably," "a court could conclude," and give the reasons. Avoid certainty you cannot support ("clearly," "definitely") and avoid vague hedging that tells the reader nothing.

### Applying it

Check that the Brief Answer and the Conclusion match the Discussion, that every fact the Discussion relies on appears in the Facts, and that no fact appears in the Facts that never matters.

@example
title: Drafting a question presented
problem: Facts: Buyer bought a used car from Dealer. Dealer knew the car had been in a flood and did not tell Buyer. The hypothetical Marlow statute requires dealers to disclose known flood damage before sale. Draft a question presented.
step: Identify the governing law: Marlow's statute requiring disclosure of known flood damage.
step: Identify the legal question: whether Dealer violated the statute by not disclosing.
step: Select the key facts: Dealer knew of flood damage; Dealer did not disclose before the sale. Leave out the car's model and price unless the statute turns on them.
step: Draft: "Under Marlow's used-car statute requiring dealers to disclose known flood damage before sale, did Dealer violate the statute when it knew the car had been flooded and said nothing to Buyer before the sale?"

@example
title: Spotting problems in a brief answer
problem: A brief answer reads: "The landlord clearly violated the statute because retaliation is wrong." Identify the problems.
step: The answer is overconfident ("clearly") without support and the Discussion has not yet been written.
step: The reason is not a rule: "retaliation is wrong" is a moral assertion, not the statutory test.
step: There are no key facts: nothing tells the reader which facts drive the answer.
step: Repair: "Probably yes. Under the hypothetical Marlow statute, a nonrenewal within 90 days of a good-faith complaint is presumed retaliatory. Here the notice came 50 days after the tenant called the city inspector."

@mistakes
- Writing a question presented that is too general or that assumes the answer.
- Leaving unfavorable facts out of the Statement of Facts.
- Arguing in the Facts section ("the landlord callously ignored ...") or stating conclusions there.
- Writing a Brief Answer that hedges so much it does not answer the question.
