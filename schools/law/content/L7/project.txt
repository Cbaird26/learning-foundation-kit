title: Closed-universe office memo: tenant retaliation
overview: You are a junior associate. Using only the facts, statute and four cases supplied below (a closed universe in the hypothetical State of Marlow), write a 2,500-word objective office memo for your supervising attorney predicting whether the landlord's nonrenewal of a tenant's lease is unlawful retaliation. The memo has a question presented, brief answer, statement of facts, discussion and conclusion.
goal: Synthesize a rule from four cases and apply it to a client's facts, element by element.
goal: Write an objective memo in CREAC form that addresses both strengths and weaknesses.
goal: Cite supplied authority consistently and in a correct, uniform format.
step: Read the statute and brief each of the four cases (Week 2): significant facts, holding, rule and reasoning. Chart them.
step: Map the statute into elements: (1) a good-faith complaint to a governmental housing authority, (2) a nonrenewal, termination or substantial rent increase, (3) the timing that triggers the presumption, and (4) the landlord's rebuttal. Decide which elements are contested.
step: Write a research-style log of your analysis plan: which case speaks to which element, and which facts are favorable or unfavorable. Note facts that are missing and would help.
step: Draft the Statement of Facts first, including unfavorable facts. Then write the Question Presented and Brief Answer last, so they match your conclusion.
step: Write the Discussion in CREAC form: an umbrella paragraph, a short treatment of the undisputed elements, and full rule explanation, application and counter-analysis for each contested element (the complaint and good faith; rebuttal). Budget about 60 percent of the words for the Discussion.
step: Add a short Conclusion with your overall prediction and recommended next steps (for example, facts or documents to obtain).
step: Edit in passes (Week 10): structure, paragraphs, sentences, words, then citations. Cut redundancy and check that every fact in the Discussion is in the Statement of Facts. Cite cases in the form used in the materials (for example, Dorn v. Wick, 210 Marl. 45, 48 (2012)), using the pinpoints given and putting the case name in italics.
material: **The statute (hypothetical).** Marlow Residential Tenancy Act § 12-4. (a) A landlord shall not terminate a residential tenancy, refuse to renew it, or substantially increase the rent, in retaliation for the tenant's good-faith complaint to a governmental housing authority about conditions that violate the housing code. (b) A termination, nonrenewal or substantial rent increase made within 90 days after the tenant's complaint is presumed retaliatory. (c) The landlord may rebut the presumption by proving by a preponderance of the evidence a legitimate reason that was the landlord's actual reason for the action. (d) A tenant must bring an action within 180 days after notice of the landlord's action. A prevailing tenant may recover actual damages and reasonable attorney's fees.
material: **Dorn v. Wick, 210 Marl. 45 (Marlow Supreme Court, 2012).** Facts: Tenant telephoned the city housing inspector to report a broken stair rail and never filed anything in writing. Landlord gave notice of nonrenewal 30 days later and argued that a complaint must be in writing to count. Holding: an oral complaint to a housing authority is a "complaint" under § 12-4; the court noted the statute says "complaint," not "written complaint," and added that tenants with limited resources should not face formal hurdles. 210 Marl. at 48. Disposition: reversed and remanded for trial on the landlord's rebuttal.
material: **Pratt v. Lowell Properties, 225 Marl. App. 310 (Marlow Court of Appeals, 2014).** Facts: Tenant complained repeatedly to Landlord about mold but never contacted any government office. Landlord refused to renew the lease 20 days after the last complaint. Holding: § 12-4 does not apply, because the statute protects only a complaint made "to a governmental housing authority"; a complaint to the landlord alone does not trigger the presumption. 225 Marl. App. at 314. Disposition: affirmed summary judgment for the landlord.
material: **Reyes v. Orton Apartments, 240 Marl. 102 (Marlow Supreme Court, 2017).** Facts: Tenant reported to the city that her apartment had no heat. The inspector found the heater working and cited no violation. Landlord gave notice of nonrenewal 40 days later and argued that the complaint was not in good faith because no violation was found. Holding: "good faith" requires only that the tenant honestly believed a violation existed and had a reasonable basis for that belief; an inspector's later finding of no violation does not defeat good faith. 240 Marl. at 107. Disposition: reversed and remanded.
material: **Sutter v. Hill Management, 255 Marl. 18 (Marlow Supreme Court, 2019).** Facts: Tenant complained to the city about a leaking roof. Landlord gave notice of nonrenewal 55 days later. Landlord produced board minutes dated four months before the complaint, recording a decision to end all leases in the building to convert it to condominiums, and showed that every tenant in the building received a nonrenewal notice. Holding: the landlord rebutted the presumption by proving that it had decided before the complaint and that the earlier decision was its actual reason. 255 Marl. at 24. The court cautioned that a landlord's after-the-fact claim of an earlier decision, without contemporaneous records, would be viewed with skepticism. Disposition: affirmed judgment for the landlord.
material: **Client facts.** Nora Kessler has rented Unit 4B in Elm Court Apartments, a twelve-unit building in the City of Marlow, since September three years ago, on a one-year lease renewed each September. Her current lease expires August 31. Mr. Barlow owns the building. On January 10, Nora texted Mr. Barlow that her heater was not working in a cold snap; he replied, "I'll look at it," and did nothing. On January 24, Nora telephoned the Marlow City Housing Inspection Office and reported that her heater did not work. She filed nothing in writing. On February 3, an inspector visited, found that the heater ran only intermittently but was operating at the time of the visit, and cited no violation. On March 15, Mr. Barlow gave Nora written notice that her lease would not be renewed. Nora always paid rent on time. In October, a neighbor complained to Mr. Barlow about noise from Nora's unit, and Mr. Barlow sent Nora a note asking her to keep it down. Of the twelve units, three other tenants also received nonrenewal notices on March 15, and nine tenants were offered renewals. Mr. Barlow told Nora he "has been thinking about selling the building since last fall." He has produced one document: an email to a real estate broker dated December 2 asking what the building might sell for "if it were empty." Assume it is now March 20 and that February has 28 days.
deliverable: A 2,500-word (plus or minus 10 percent) office memo with a heading, question presented, brief answer, statement of facts, discussion and conclusion.
deliverable: A one-page chart of the four cases (facts, holding, rule) and a short plan showing which case supports which element.
rubric: The question presented and brief answer are specific, neutral and consistent with the discussion (answer first, rule and key facts stated).
rubric: The statement of facts is accurate and complete, including unfavorable facts (the noise note, the oral complaint, the finding of no violation, the broker email), with no argument.
rubric: The discussion is in CREAC form, synthesizes the rule from the four cases, spends most of its words on the contested elements, and includes counter-analysis.
rubric: Application uses analogies and distinctions with explained reasons and states the burden and timing correctly (50 days from the January 24 call to the March 15 notice; presumption; rebuttal by preponderance).
rubric: Writing is concise and precise, citations are in a uniform correct form with pinpoints, and the memo makes honest predictions without overstating certainty.

## Week-by-week review

**Week 1: Sources of law.** Primary authority (constitutions, statutes, regulations, judicial opinions) versus secondary authority (treatises, Restatements, law reviews). The Supremacy Clause (art. VI) and the hierarchy. Mandatory versus persuasive authority; vertical stare decisis; *Erie* in diversity cases. After *Loper Bright* (2024), courts decide the best reading of a statute and give agencies *Skidmore* respect.

**Week 2: Briefing cases.** Citation, significant facts, procedural history, issue, holding, rule, reasoning, disposition. Holding versus dicta. Standards of review. Plurality opinions and *Marks*.

**Week 3: Statutes and regulations.** U.S. Code and C.F.R.; the Federal Register; APA notice and comment (5 U.S.C. § 553). Plain meaning and canons (*ejusdem generis*, *expressio unius*, whole act, lenity). Major questions (*West Virginia v. EPA*; *Learning Resources v. Trump* (2026)). Two-thirds override thresholds.

**Week 4: Research strategy.** Secondary sources first, then primary law, then a citator. Terms-and-connectors syntax. Research plans and logs.

**Week 5: Rule synthesis.** Chart facts and outcomes, find the thread, draft a rule that explains every case, and test it. Avoid over-narrow and over-broad rules; reconcile cases by hierarchy.

**Week 6: Analogy and distinction.** Material facts, analogizing with a reason, distinguishing with a reason, and analyzing adverse authority honestly.

**Week 7: The office memo.** Heading, question presented, brief answer, facts, discussion, conclusion. CREAC and IRAC.

**Week 8: The discussion section.** Thesis, rule, explanation, application, counter-analysis, conclusion. Umbrella paragraphs; word allocation.

**Week 9: Citation.** Case, statute, regulation and rule forms; pinpoints; short forms and *Id.*; signals; parentheticals; block quotes at 50 words or more.

**Week 10: Editing.** Concision, active voice, consistent terms, ambiguity, edit in passes.

**Week 11: Client letters and emails.** Bottom line first, plain English, honest uncertainty, next steps; Rule 1.4; protecting privilege.

**Week 12: Professional responsibility.** Rules 1.1, 1.6, 3.1, 3.3(a)(2), 4.4(b), 5.3; FRCP 11 and its 21-day safe harbor; verifying citations; generative AI (ABA Formal Opinion 512).

### Self-check
- Can I explain which authority binds a given court and why?
- Can I state the holding of a case separately from its dicta?
- Can I build a rule from several cases and test it?
- Does my memo's answer come first, and do the facts include the bad ones?
- Is every citation checked and updated?

### Common errors
Treating persuasive authority as binding; stating the holding too broadly; omitting adverse facts or cases; asserting similarity without a reason; writing a brief answer that hedges without answering; using *Id.* after a string cite; citing AI output without reading the source.

She finds (1) a 2019 Fifth Circuit decision, which relied on Chevron to uphold an agency regulation reading the provision a certain way, (2) a Ninth Circuit decision rejecting the same agency reading, and (3) a law review article criticizing the Fifth Circuit's decision. After Loper Bright (2024), which statement is most accurate?
choice: The 2019 decision no longer binds the district court at all, because Chevron was overruled.
choice*: The 2019 Fifth Circuit holding on the statute's meaning still binds the district court under statutory stare decisis, the Ninth Circuit decision is persuasive only, and the article is secondary authority; the associate should update the 2019 case in a citator.
choice: The Ninth Circuit decision controls, because it rejected a reading that relied on Chevron.
choice: The law review article controls, because it identifies the Chevron problem.
hint: Loper Bright said prior Chevron-based holdings remain in force; which court sits above the district court?
solution: Loper Bright overruled Chevron but held that earlier decisions relying on it keep their statutory stare decisis effect. The Fifth Circuit binds a district court in that circuit; the Ninth Circuit's decision and a law review article are persuasive, and the article is also secondary. The associate should update the case before relying on it.

@exam self
prompt: Use the following closed universe. Statute (hypothetical Marlow): a landlord shall not refuse to renew a tenancy in retaliation for a tenant's good-faith complaint to a governmental housing authority; a nonrenewal within 90 days after the complaint is presumed retaliatory; the landlord may rebut by proving a legitimate actual reason. Case: Dorn v. Wick, 210 Marl. 45, 48 (2012), held that an oral complaint to a housing authority suffices. Facts: Lee telephoned the city housing office on April 2 to report a broken stair rail, and the landlord gave notice of nonrenewal on May 20 (assume April has 30 days). Write (a) a question presented, (b) a brief answer, and (c) one rule-explanation paragraph using Dorn with a correctly formatted citation, for a supervising attorney's memo. Then (d) write two sentences of the plain-English bottom line you would give Lee in a client letter.
solution: (a) Under Marlow's statute presuming retaliatory a nonrenewal made within 90 days after a tenant's good-faith complaint to a housing authority, is the landlord's nonrenewal presumed retaliatory when the tenant reported a broken stair rail to the city by telephone and the landlord gave notice of nonrenewal 48 days later? (b) Probably yes. The statute presumes retaliation for a nonrenewal within 90 days after a good-faith complaint to a housing authority; an oral complaint suffices; and the notice here came 48 days after Lee's call. The landlord may still rebut by proving a legitimate, actual reason. (c) A tenant's oral complaint to a housing authority triggers the statute's protection. In Dorn v. Wick, the tenant telephoned the city inspector about a broken stair rail and filed nothing in writing; the landlord gave notice of nonrenewal 30 days later and argued that a complaint must be written. The court held that the statute protects an oral complaint because it says "complaint," not "written complaint." Dorn v. Wick, 210 Marl. 45, 48 (2012). (d) Your landlord's refusal to renew your lease was probably unlawful, because the law assumes he was punishing you if he refused to renew within 90 days after you complained to the city, and your call was 48 days before his notice. He can still defend himself by proving a real, different reason, so please send us any papers he gave you.

rubric: The rule explanation gives the rule, the case's facts, holding and reasoning, with a citation in correct form including the pinpoint and year.
