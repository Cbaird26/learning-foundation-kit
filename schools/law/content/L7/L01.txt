@article
Every legal question begins with the same two questions: **what is the law, and who says so?** A **source of law** is a place where legal rules come from. Your first research skill is knowing which sources can control your problem and which can only help you think about it.

### Primary and secondary authority

**Primary authority** is the law itself. There are four main kinds in the U.S. system:

- **Constitutions** (the U.S. Constitution and each state's constitution).
- **Statutes** (enacted by Congress or a state legislature), plus court rules such as the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure.
- **Administrative regulations** (rules issued by agencies under authority a legislature delegated).
- **Judicial opinions** (common law and decisions interpreting the other three).

**Secondary authority** describes, analyzes or criticizes the law without being the law: treatises, law review articles, legal encyclopedias such as Am. Jur. 2d, annotations in A.L.R., and the **Restatements** of the American Law Institute. A Restatement is secondary even though courts respect it; it becomes primary only when a court adopts a section as that state's law, and then the court's opinion is the primary authority.

### The hierarchy of law

The **Supremacy Clause** (U.S. Const. art. VI) makes the Constitution, federal statutes made in pursuance of it, and treaties the supreme law of the land, so a valid federal law displaces conflicting state law. Courts may hold statutes unconstitutional (*Marbury v. Madison* (1803)). Within any one jurisdiction the order is: constitution, then statute, then regulation, with common law at the bottom because a legislature can change it by statute. A regulation is valid only if the agency stayed within the authority its statute gave it.

Agency law has shifted. In *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo* (2024) the Supreme Court overruled *Chevron*: a court must itself decide the best reading of a statute and may give an agency's view only *Skidmore* respect, meaning weight proportional to the agency's thoroughness and the persuasiveness of its reasoning. Older holdings that relied on *Chevron* remain in force under statutory stare decisis. Separately, in *Trump v. Slaughter* (2026) the Court overruled *Humphrey's Executor* and held that FTC commissioners are removable at will; it left open the Federal Reserve and offices outside the traditional executive branch.

### Mandatory and persuasive authority

Authority is **mandatory** (binding) when it is primary, comes from the jurisdiction whose law governs, and comes from a court at or above the deciding court in the same system. Everything else is **persuasive**: decisions of other jurisdictions, lower courts, **dicta**, and all secondary sources. **Stare decisis** is the principle that courts follow earlier decisions; it works vertically (lower courts follow higher courts) and, with more flexibility, horizontally (a court follows its own precedent).

The federal system has 94 district courts, 13 courts of appeals (eleven numbered regional circuits, the D.C. Circuit and the Federal Circuit) and the Supreme Court. A district court is bound by its own circuit and the Supreme Court, not by other circuits and not by other district judges. A circuit's published panel decision binds later panels of that circuit. State systems usually have trial courts, an intermediate appellate court and a supreme court; the state supreme court binds every lower state court.

### Which court's law?

On a federal question, the Supreme Court's reading of federal law binds state courts; lower federal court decisions are persuasive there. In a federal diversity case, *Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins* (1938) requires the federal court to apply state substantive law (and federal procedural law). If the state's highest court has decided the question, the federal court follows it; if not, the federal court predicts how that court would rule.

### Applying it (IRAC)

State the **issue** (which court, which law), the **rule** (the hierarchy and binding-versus-persuasive tests), apply them to each authority in turn, and **conclude** with a ranked list. Always ask three questions about a case: same jurisdiction, same or higher court, same area of law.

@example
title: Ranking authorities for a federal district court
problem: A federal district court in the Fifth Circuit is deciding a question under a federal statute. Rank these as mandatory or persuasive: (1) a Supreme Court decision on the statute, (2) a Fifth Circuit published opinion on the statute, (3) a Ninth Circuit opinion on the statute, (4) a law review article.
step: Identify the court and the law. The court is a federal district court in the Fifth Circuit, and the law is federal, so the relevant system is the federal courts.
step: Apply the vertical test. The Supreme Court is above every federal court, and the Fifth Circuit is the court of appeals for this district. Both are mandatory.
step: The Ninth Circuit is a court of equal rank with the Fifth, not a court above this district court, so its opinion is only persuasive.
step: A law review article is secondary authority, so it is persuasive at most. Conclusion: (1) and (2) mandatory; (3) and (4) persuasive.

@example
title: A state statute against a federal statute
problem: A valid federal statute, enacted under Congress's commerce power, forbids conduct that a state statute expressly permits. Which controls?
step: Issue: conflict between federal and state law. The rule is the Supremacy Clause, art. VI.
step: Check validity. The federal statute is within an enumerated power, so it is a valid exercise of federal authority.
step: Apply. A valid federal statute is supreme over a conflicting state statute, so the state statute is preempted to the extent of the conflict. Conclusion: the federal statute controls.

@mistakes
- Treating every appellate decision as binding: a decision from another circuit or another state is persuasive only.
- Calling a Restatement or treatise primary authority because courts quote it.
- Assuming a federal district court's decision binds other judges in the same district; it does not.
- Teaching or citing *Chevron* deference as the current test after *Loper Bright*.
