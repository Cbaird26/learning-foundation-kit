@article
Courts rarely announce a complete rule in one opinion. One case adds an element, another creates an exception, a third explains what a vague word means on particular facts. **Rule synthesis** is the process of reading several cases together and stating, in one rule, the principle that explains all their outcomes. It is **inductive**: you reason from specific results to a general rule. Using that rule on a new problem is **deductive**.

### Why synthesize?

Suppose three cases about a store's liability for spills. In the first, the owner is liable; in the second, not; in the third, not. None states the whole test. Only by comparing facts and outcomes can you see what separated them. In an office memo, your **rule explanation** presents the synthesized rule and then illustrates it with the cases.

### The steps

1. **Brief each case** (Week 2), focusing on the facts that mattered and the outcome.
2. **Chart** the cases in a table: facts across, outcome at the end. Mark facts that appear in winning and losing cases.
3. **Look for the thread:** which facts correlate with outcome? Which facts do the courts themselves call important?
4. **Draft the rule** to explain every outcome, in general terms but no more broadly than the cases support.
5. **Test** it: apply the rule to each case's facts and see whether you get that case's result.
6. **Refine** and reconcile conflicts.

### Common structures

- **Elements:** all of a list of requirements are needed.
- **Factors:** a court weighs considerations, and no single one is required.
- **Rule and exception:** a general rule with defined exceptions.
- **Spectrum or threshold:** a quantity or degree (how long, how much) separates outcomes.

### Reconciling conflicts

Apparent conflicts usually fall into one of these types. Cases from **different jurisdictions** are not synthesized together as one law: synthesize each jurisdiction's cases separately and use others as persuasive. In the **same court**, a later case controls an earlier one it expressly overrules; absent an overruling, try to reconcile them by finding a factual difference or a refinement. A **higher court** controls a lower court. Do not build a rule from **dicta**. Never discard a case simply because it does not fit your rule; if you cannot explain it, your rule is wrong or incomplete.

### Two errors to avoid

An **over-narrow** rule repeats the facts of one case ("a grocery store is liable if a grape was on the floor 40 minutes"). An **over-broad** rule ignores facts that mattered ("a store is liable whenever a customer falls"). The right level of generality is the one that explains the cases and predicts new ones.

### Applying it

State your synthesized rule in a topic sentence, then support it with the cases ("In *A*, ...; in *B*, ..."). Follow it with the rule's reason, the policy it serves.

@example
title: Synthesizing three cases
problem: In the hypothetical State of Marlow: In Ames, a spill was on the floor 40 minutes while an employee stood nearby; the store was liable. In Bell, a spill was there about 2 minutes and no employee was near; no liability. In Cruz, there was no evidence how long the spill had been on the floor; no liability. State the synthesized rule.
step: Chart the facts: time on the floor (40 min, 2 min, unknown), employee nearby (yes, no, unknown), result (liable, not, not).
step: Find the thread: liability tracks whether the store knew or should have known of the spill. An employee nearby gave actual or constructive notice in Ames, and Bell was too brief to expect discovery. Cruz failed because the plaintiff offered no proof of duration.
step: Draft the rule: a store is liable for a customer's fall on a spill if it had actual notice or the spill existed long enough that reasonable inspection would have found it, and the plaintiff must prove that time.
step: Test: Ames meets constructive notice, Bell does not, and Cruz fails the proof requirement. The rule explains all three.

@example
title: Testing an over-narrow rule
problem: A student states: "A store is liable if a spill is on the floor for 40 minutes." Test this against these three hypothetical Marlow cases and say whether it works. Ames: spill on the floor 40 minutes, employee nearby, store liable. Bell: spill about 2 minutes, no employee near, no liability. Cruz: no evidence how long the spill had been there, no liability.
step: Apply it to Ames: 40 minutes, liability. The rule gives the right result.
step: Apply it to Bell: 2 minutes, no liability. Right result, but only because 2 is less than 40.
step: Consider a new fact pattern that the rule does not address: 25 minutes with an employee watching. The cases suggest liability on actual notice, but the rule says no.
step: Conclusion: the rule copies Ames's number and misses the principle (notice), so it is over-narrow.

@mistakes
- Stating the rule so narrowly that it repeats the facts of one case.
- Ignoring a case that does not fit instead of revising the rule to explain it.
- Mixing cases from different jurisdictions into one rule as if all were binding.
- Building the rule from dicta rather than holdings.

@practice mc
prompt: Hypothetical State of Marlow cases: In Dale, a homeowner who hired a contractor was not liable for an injury to the contractor's employee caused by the contractor's chosen methods. In Eng, a homeowner who directed how the work was done was liable. In Gaines, a homeowner who supplied a defective scaffold was liable. Which statement best synthesizes the rule?
choice: A homeowner is always liable for injuries to a contractor's employees.
choice: A homeowner is never liable for injuries to a contractor's employees.
choice*: A homeowner who hires a contractor is not liable for injuries to the contractor's employees unless the homeowner controlled the work or supplied defective equipment.
choice: A homeowner is liable only if the homeowner was present at the work site.
hint: Find what the two liability cases have in common that the no-liability case lacks.
solution: Eng (control) and Gaines (defective equipment) are the exceptions to the general rule from Dale. An always-liable or never-liable rule cannot explain all three outcomes, and a presence requirement would miss Gaines.

prompt: A state's supreme court decided a case in 2005 that appears to require a signed writing for a certain promise. In 2018 the same court decided a similar case enforcing an oral promise on facts where the promisee had relied heavily, without mentioning the 2005 case. What is the best approach in synthesizing the rule?
choice: Rely only on the 2005 case, because it was the first to address the issue.
choice: Rely only on the 2018 case and ignore the 2005 case.
choice*: Try to reconcile them, for example a writing rule with a reliance exception, and note that the later decision controls if they cannot be reconciled.
choice: Combine them with decisions from other states into one rule.
hint: The same court's later decision generally controls, but first look for a way to explain both.
solution: Reconcile if possible; if not, the later decision of the same court generally controls. Ignoring either case or mixing in other states' cases misstates the law.

choice*: It explains the outcome of every case on which it is based.
choice*: It is stated at a level of generality supported by the holdings, not tied to one case's facts.

@practice self
prompt: Synthesize a rule from these hypothetical State of Marlow dog-bite cases. Quill: the dog had bitten a neighbor before; the owner was liable. Sato: the dog had never bitten or threatened anyone; the owner was not liable. Teague: the dog had snapped at several neighbors but never bitten; the owner was liable because the owner knew of the dog's aggressive tendencies. Upton: the dog bit a trespasser who entered a fenced yard posted with a "Beware of Dog" sign; the owner was not liable. State the rule in one or two sentences, chart the cases, and test the rule against each.
solution: Chart. Quill: prior bite, owner knew, liable. Sato: no history, not liable. Teague: prior snapping, owner knew of aggression, liable. Upton: trespasser, warning posted, not liable. Rule: an owner is liable for a dog's bite if the owner knew or should have known of the dog's dangerous propensities, which includes prior aggression and not only a prior bite, unless the victim was a trespasser who had been warned. Test: Quill and Teague satisfy knowledge, so liability. Sato lacks knowledge, so none. Upton falls within the trespasser exception, so none. The rule explains all four.
rubric: The rule uses knowledge of dangerous propensities, not only prior bites.
rubric: The rule includes the trespasser-with-warning exception.
rubric: A chart or list shows each case's facts and outcome.
rubric: The rule is tested against all four outcomes.
hint: What fact separates Quill and Teague from Sato? What explains Upton?

@quiz mc
prompt: In the hypothetical State of Marlow: In Hart, an employer's written offer of a bonus for staying through December was enforced when the employee stayed. In Imes, a manager's remark that "we'll take care of you" was not enforced. In Jett, an oral promise of a stated bonus for staying through the year was enforced when the employee stayed. Which statement best synthesizes the rule?
choice: A bonus promise is enforceable only if it is in writing.
choice: A bonus promise is enforceable whenever an employer says anything about a bonus.
choice*: A bonus promise is enforceable if its terms are definite and the employee performed the stated condition; a writing is not required.
choice: A bonus promise is enforceable only if it states an amount and is in writing.
hint: Compare Jett with Imes: what is different about the oral statements?
solution: The enforced promises were definite and the condition was performed, whether written (Hart) or oral (Jett). Imes failed because the statement was too indefinite. The writing requirement is contradicted by Jett.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which steps help you test a synthesized rule?
choice*: Apply the rule to each case's facts to see whether it produces that case's outcome.
choice*: Check whether any rule element rests on a fact that never affected an outcome.
choice*: Ask whether an earlier and a later case from the same court can be reconciled.
choice: Delete any case that does not fit the draft.
