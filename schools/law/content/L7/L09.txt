@article
A citation tells the reader where your authority comes from so they can find it, check it and weigh it. Two manuals dominate: **The Bluebook: A Uniform System of Citation**, and the **ALWD Guide to Legal Citation**. The Bluebook has two parts that matter for most students: the **Bluepages** (citation in memos, briefs and other practitioner documents) and the **Whitepages** (law review footnotes). Details differ by manual and edition, and a court's local rules control filings in that court. The forms below follow the practitioner (Bluepages) style. Case names are italicized or underlined in documents of this kind.

### Cases

A full case citation has: **case name**, **volume**, **reporter abbreviation**, **first page**, **pinpoint page**, and a **parenthetical with court and year**:

*Int'l Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945).

- Use "v." (not "vs.").
- In a citation, abbreviate the common words listed in the manual (for example "Int'l" for "International" and "R.R." for "Railroad"), but write words out in running text.
- Omit the court from the parenthetical for the U.S. Supreme Court, because the reporter (U.S.) shows it. For a federal court of appeals give the circuit: (9th Cir. 2015). For a federal trial court: (S.D.N.Y. 2020). For a state's highest court, the state abbreviation and year; for an intermediate state court, add the court name.
- The **pinpoint** (the specific page) is required when you cite a case for a proposition; cite the first page alone only when citing the case as a whole.
- Add **subsequent history** (such as *aff'd* or *rev'd*) when it is significant.

### Statutes, regulations, rules and the Constitution

- Federal statute: *Title, U.S.C., §, section*: 42 U.S.C. § 1983. Some forms add the code year; follow your manual.
- Regulation: *Title C.F.R. § section*: 29 C.F.R. § 1630.2.
- Court rule: Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(6).
- Constitution: U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1.

State materials follow each state's abbreviation rules; look them up in your manual's tables.

### Short forms and Id.

After a full cite you may use a **short form**: *Int'l Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 320. **Id.** (italicized or underlined) refers to the immediately preceding authority, and only when that preceding citation cites a single authority: *Id.* (same page) or *Id.* at 320 (different page). If a citation sentence cites two or more authorities, the next citation cannot use *Id.*; use a short form. Do not use *supra* for cases or statutes in practitioner documents; it is for secondary sources.

### Signals

A **signal** tells the reader how the authority relates to the proposition:

- **No signal**: the authority directly states the proposition or is the source of a quotation.
- **See**: the authority clearly supports the proposition but does not directly state it.
- **See also**: additional support.
- **Cf.**: support by analogy.
- **Compare ... with ...**: comparison offers support.
- **But see**: authority clearly supports a contrary proposition. **Contra**: direct contradiction.
- **See generally**: background.
- **E.g.**: one of several authorities that directly support the proposition.

### Parentheticals and quotations

An **explanatory parenthetical** follows the citation and usually begins with a present participle: (holding that a landlord's lack of notice defeats liability). Quote exactly. Use quotation marks for short quotations; use a **block quotation** (indented, no quotation marks) for quotations of 50 words or more. Show alterations in brackets, omissions with ellipses, and indicate added emphasis ("emphasis added").

### Where citations go

In a memo, put the citation in its own **citation sentence** after the sentence it supports. Use a citation clause (inside the sentence) only when the citation supports only part of it. Cite for every proposition drawn from authority.

### Applying it

Before you finish, check every citation: Is the reporter right? Is there a pinpoint? Is the court and year right? Is the signal accurate? Did a citator flag the case?

@example
title: Building a full citation
problem: Cite Erie R.R. Co. v. Tompkins for a proposition on page 78; it appears in volume 304 of the United States Reports at page 64, and was decided in 1938.
step: Case name and v.: abbreviate the common word in "Railroad" to R.R.: Erie R.R. Co. v. Tompkins.
step: Add volume, reporter, first page and pinpoint: 304 U.S. 64, 78.
step: Add the court and year parenthetical, omitting the court for the Supreme Court: (1938).
step: Result: *Erie R.R. Co. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64, 78 (1938).

@example
title: Using Id. and short forms
problem: A memo cites (1) Int'l Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945); (2) the same case at 320; (3) Int'l Shoe at 319 together with Erie R.R. Co. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64, 78 (1938); and (4) Int'l Shoe at 316 again. Write the citations.
step: Citation 1 is the full citation.
step: Citation 2 immediately follows the same single authority: *Id.* at 320.
step: Citation 3 begins with the single authority just cited, so *Id.* may start it. Erie is a new authority and gets a full citation. Authorities of the same court appear in reverse chronological order, so the 1945 case precedes the 1938 case: *Id.* at 319; *Erie R.R. Co. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64, 78 (1938).
step: Citation 4 follows a citation with two authorities, so Id. cannot be used; give the short form: *Int'l Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 316.

@mistakes
- Omitting the pinpoint page when citing a case for a specific proposition.
- Using Id. after a citation sentence that has two or more authorities.
- Using "See" when the authority directly states the proposition (no signal), or using no signal for an inference.
- Writing the court in the parenthetical for a U.S. Supreme Court case or leaving it out for a court of appeals.
