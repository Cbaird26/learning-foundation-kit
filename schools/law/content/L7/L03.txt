@article
Most modern legal problems are governed by a **statute**, a **regulation**, or both. Reading them is a skill separate from reading cases: you start with the words, not with a story.

### Where statutes live

When Congress passes a bill and the President signs it (or two-thirds of those present and voting in each house override a veto, a quorum being present), it becomes a **public law**. Public laws are first published chronologically as **slip laws** and then in the **Statutes at Large**, which is the official session-law record in order of enactment. The **United States Code** rearranges the permanent, general laws by subject into numbered titles (for example 42 U.S.C. § 1983). Annotated versions (U.S.C.A. and U.S.C.S.) add case notes and cross-references, which is why researchers use them. States follow the same pattern with session laws and codes.

### Where regulations live

Agencies make rules under authority a statute delegates. Under the **Administrative Procedure Act**, 5 U.S.C. § 553, informal rulemaking requires notice of a proposed rule, an opportunity for public comment, and a final rule accompanied by a concise statement of basis and purpose. Proposed and final rules appear in the **Federal Register**; the rules in force are codified by subject in the **Code of Federal Regulations (C.F.R.)**. An APA challenge to a rule accrues when the plaintiff is injured (*Corner Post v. Board of Governors* (2024)).

### Reading a statute: a checklist

1. Read the whole section and the **definitions** section; defined terms control.
2. Split the text into **elements** and mark connectors: **and** means every element is required; **or** means any one suffices.
3. Note **modal verbs**: *shall* and *must* are mandatory; *may* is permissive.
4. Find **exceptions** and provisos ("unless," "except," "provided that"), and note who bears the burden of showing them.
5. Check cross-references, effective dates and amendments.
6. Look for cases interpreting the words in the annotated code.

### Interpretation

Courts begin with the **plain meaning** of the text read in context. The textual canons are aids, not rules of law:

- ***Ejusdem generis***: a general term after a list takes its meaning from the list ("cars, trucks, or other vehicles" suggests motor vehicles).
- ***Noscitur a sociis***: a word is known by the company it keeps.
- ***Expressio unius est exclusio alterius***: expressing one thing suggests excluding others.
- **Whole-act and surplusage canons**: read provisions together; avoid readings that make words meaningless.
- **Absurdity doctrine**: avoid a result so absurd that the legislature could not have intended it.
- **Rule of lenity**: ambiguity in a criminal statute that remains after using the other tools is resolved for the defendant.

Legislative history is disputed: purposivists use committee reports, while textualists distrust them and give weight to text first. If text is clear, courts generally stop there.

### Agencies and the courts

After *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo* (2024), courts decide the best reading of a statute and give agency views *Skidmore* respect; *Chevron* is gone, but prior *Chevron*-based holdings stand. Where a statute expressly delegates discretion, the court polices the limits of that delegation. In **major questions** cases (*West Virginia v. EPA* (2022); *Biden v. Nebraska* (2023)) the Court requires clear congressional authorization for actions of vast economic and political significance. In *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* (2026, 6–3) the Court held that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorize tariffs; the majority relied on the statutory text, and a plurality also applied the major questions doctrine. Congress may delegate if it supplies an intelligible principle, and in *FCC v. Consumers' Research* (2025) the Court rejected a nondelegation challenge to the Universal Service Fund. In *Trump v. Slaughter* (2026) the Court held FTC commissioners removable at will, overruling *Humphrey's Executor*.

### Applying it

State the issue, quote the operative text as your rule, apply each element to the facts in order, and say which element decides the case.

@example
title: Using ejusdem generis
problem: An ordinance provides: "No person shall park a car, truck, motorcycle, or other vehicle on the Green." May the city ticket a person who leaves a bicycle on the Green?
step: Issue: does "other vehicle" include a bicycle? The text is a list followed by a general term.
step: Rule: under ejusdem generis, a general term following specific ones is limited to things of the same kind.
step: Apply: car, truck and motorcycle are all motor vehicles, so "other vehicle" plausibly means other motor vehicles. A bicycle is not of that kind.
step: Conclusion: the better reading is that the ordinance does not reach bicycles, though the city may argue a broader plain meaning of "vehicle"; check the definitions section.

@example
title: Parsing elements and connectors
problem: A statute reads: "A seller shall provide a written warranty if the product costs more than \$200 and is sold to a consumer, unless the buyer waives the warranty in writing." Seller sells a \$150 product to a consumer. Must Seller provide a written warranty?
step: Break the text into elements: (1) the product costs more than \$200, and (2) it is sold to a consumer. The word "and" makes both required. The exception is a written waiver by the buyer.
step: Apply element 1: the product costs \$150, which is not more than \$200, so element 1 fails.
step: Because the elements are conjunctive, failure of one is enough. The exception need not be reached.
step: Conclusion: Seller need not provide the warranty under this statute.

@mistakes
- Reading "or" as "and" (or the reverse) without checking whether the statute is a list of alternatives.
- Skipping the definitions section, where a familiar word may have a special statutory meaning.
- Citing *Chevron* deference as current law instead of *Loper Bright* and *Skidmore*.
- Treating canons as binding rules; they are presumptions that give way to clear text and context.

prompt: A statute provides that a will is valid if it is "in writing, signed by the testator, and attested by two witnesses." Testator wrote a will, signed it, and had one person witness it. Is the will valid under the statute as written?
choice: Yes, because two of the three requirements are met, which is substantial compliance.
choice: Yes, because the connector means that any one requirement suffices.

@practice self
prompt: Parse this invented statute into elements and apply it. "A landlord shall return a security deposit within 30 days after the tenancy ends, unless the landlord gives the tenant a written itemized statement of damage within that period." Tenant moved out on March 1. Landlord mailed a written itemized statement of damage on March 20 and kept the deposit. Did Landlord comply? Use IRAC.
solution: Issue: whether Landlord complied with the deposit statute when it kept the deposit after sending a written itemized statement on day 19. Rule: the landlord must return the deposit within 30 days of the end of the tenancy unless it gives the tenant a written itemized statement of damage within that period. Application: the tenancy ended March 1, so the 30-day period ran through March 31. The statement was written and itemized, and it was given March 20, within the period, so the exception applies. The text excuses return of the deposit if the statement is timely given; it does not say the statement must be accurate or that the landlord must return the undamaged portion, so check the definitions and case annotations. Conclusion: on the text given, Landlord complied, subject to any requirement elsewhere that deductions be reasonable.
rubric: States the mandatory duty (shall return) and the 30-day period.
rubric: Identifies the exception and its elements (written, itemized, within the period).
rubric: Applies the dates correctly and reaches a conclusion.
rubric: Notes what to check next (definitions, annotations, any reasonableness requirement).
hint: Underline the duty, the time limit and the exception, and count the days.
