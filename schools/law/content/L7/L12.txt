@article
The research and writing you have learned are not only skills; they are professional obligations. A lawyer who cites a case that does not exist, hides an adverse controlling decision, or exposes a client's secrets has committed a professional wrong, even if the writing is excellent.

### Competence and diligence

**Rule 1.1** requires the knowledge, skill, thoroughness and preparation reasonably necessary for the representation, and its comments say a lawyer should keep abreast of the benefits and risks of relevant technology. For research this means finding the controlling authority, updating it in a citator, and reading what you cite. **Rule 1.3** requires diligence: meet deadlines and do not let a research task lapse.

### Candor and meritorious claims

**Rule 3.1** prohibits bringing or defending a proceeding without a basis in law and fact that is not frivolous (a good-faith argument for extending or changing the law is allowed). **Rule 3.3(a)(1)** forbids knowingly making a false statement of law or fact to a tribunal, and **Rule 3.3(a)(2)** requires a lawyer to disclose legal authority in the controlling jurisdiction that the lawyer knows to be **directly adverse** to the client's position and that opposing counsel has not disclosed. A lawyer may then argue that the authority is distinguishable or wrongly decided. **Rule 4.1** requires truthfulness to others, and **Rule 8.4(c)** forbids dishonesty, fraud, deceit or misrepresentation. Misquoting a case or misstating its holding violates these rules.

**Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 11(b)** applies to anyone who signs, files or later advocates a paper in federal court. By doing so, the person certifies that after an inquiry reasonable under the circumstances, the legal contentions are warranted by existing law or by a nonfrivolous argument for extending, modifying or reversing existing law or for establishing new law, and the factual contentions have evidentiary support. Under Rule 11(c)(2), a motion for sanctions must be served but not filed unless the challenged paper is not withdrawn or corrected within 21 days after service. This is the **safe harbor**. A signature on a brief with unverified or fabricated citations invites sanctions: federal courts have sanctioned lawyers for filing briefs with nonexistent cases generated by AI tools (for example, *Mata v. Avianca, Inc.* (S.D.N.Y. 2023)).

### Confidentiality

**Rule 1.6(a)** forbids revealing information relating to the representation unless the client consents or an exception applies, and **Rule 1.6(c)** requires reasonable efforts to prevent unauthorized disclosure or access. In research, that means being careful with search queries, emails, shared documents and public forums. Do not discuss identifiable client matters in a way that reveals them. **Rule 4.4(b)** requires a lawyer who receives a document or electronic information and knows or reasonably should know it was inadvertently sent to promptly notify the sender.

### Supervision

**Rule 5.1** makes supervising lawyers responsible for reasonable measures that subordinate lawyers comply with the rules, and **Rule 5.3** extends this to nonlawyer assistance, including vendors. A junior lawyer remains responsible for the accuracy of what she submits even when a supervisor reviews it.

### Generative AI tools

ABA Formal Opinion 512 (2024) applies existing rules to generative AI. In short: understand the tool well enough to use it competently (Rule 1.1); do not input information relating to a representation into a tool that may disclose it without evaluating the risk and, where appropriate, getting the client's informed consent (Rule 1.6); consider whether to tell clients about the tool's use and charge fees reasonably (Rules 1.4 and 1.5); supervise its use (Rules 5.1 and 5.3); and **verify every citation and proposition** before relying on it (Rules 1.1, 3.1 and 3.3). The tool's output is a lead, never authority.

### Attribution and honesty

Cite your sources for ideas and language taken from authority and from colleagues' work product. Do not pass off another's analysis as your own, and do not alter a quotation without signaling the change (Week 9).

### Applying it

When faced with a research or writing choice, ask four questions: Is it accurate? Is it candid with the tribunal? Does it protect the client's confidences? Would I be comfortable explaining the choice to a supervising lawyer and to a judge?

@example
title: Disclosing adverse authority
problem: In a federal district court in the Third Circuit, Lawyer finds a published Third Circuit decision that is directly adverse to her client on the controlling legal question. Opposing counsel has not cited it. What should she do?
step: Issue: does Rule 3.3(a)(2) require disclosure? The decision must be (1) legal authority in the controlling jurisdiction, (2) directly adverse, and (3) not disclosed by opposing counsel.
step: Apply: a published Third Circuit decision binds this district court, so it is in the controlling jurisdiction; it is directly adverse; and opposing counsel did not cite it.
step: Conclude that she must disclose it, and may then argue that it is distinguishable on the facts, or that it should be limited or reconsidered where the law allows.

@example
title: Using an AI tool
problem: An associate asks a public chatbot to draft a brief section and to include citations, then files it after fixing the formatting. Several cases do not exist. Identify the rule problems.
step: Competence (Rule 1.1): she did not understand the tool's limits and did not read the cases.
step: Candor and Rule 11: the brief contains false statements of law (Rule 3.3(a)(1)), and the signature certifies a reasonable inquiry (FRCP 11(b)(2)) that she did not make.
step: Supervision and confidentiality: she should have considered whether to put client facts into a public tool (Rule 1.6(c)) and whether a supervisor reviewed the work (Rule 5.1).

@mistakes
- Treating AI output or a database summary as authority without reading the actual source.
- Hiding a directly adverse controlling case instead of disclosing and distinguishing it.
- Citing a case for a proposition it does not support, or misquoting it.
- Putting client confidences into public tools, forums or emails to the wrong recipients.

@practice self
prompt: Write a plain-English client letter (about 250 words) explaining this memo conclusion to Mia, a tenant. Memo conclusion (hypothetical Marlow law): "Probably yes. Marlow's retaliation statute presumes a nonrenewal given within 90 days after a good-faith complaint to a housing authority is retaliatory. The landlord gave notice 50 days after Mia's complaint. The landlord can rebut by proving, with documents, that he decided before the complaint to stop renewing leases. A complaint must be filed within 180 days after the notice." Include the bottom line, why, the risk, and next steps with the deadline.
solution: Dear Mia: Our conclusion is that your landlord's refusal to renew your lease was probably unlawful. Under Marlow law, if a landlord refuses to renew a lease within 90 days after a tenant makes a good-faith complaint to a housing agency, the law starts out assuming the landlord is retaliating. You called the city housing office, and your landlord gave you notice 50 days later, so the law presumption applies in your favor. There is a real risk. Your landlord can win if he proves, with documents, that he decided before your call to stop renewing leases. If he has no such papers, your position is strong. What happens next: Please send us the notice, your lease, and a record of your call to the city. We will ask your landlord to show his documents and, if needed, will file your claim. The law gives you 180 days from the date of his notice to file, so we should act well before that, and we suggest deciding within the next month. This advice is based on the facts you gave us; please tell us if any are wrong. Sincerely, [Name].
rubric: Opens with the bottom line in plain, calibrated words ("probably unlawful").
rubric: Explains the presumption and the 50-day fact without legalese; explains the landlord's rebuttal as the risk.
rubric: States the 180-day deadline and gives concrete next steps, including what Mia should send.
rubric: Does not guarantee a result and invites correction of the facts.
hint: Bottom line, why, risk, next steps, deadline.
