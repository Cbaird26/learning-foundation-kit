@article
Good legal research is not a hunt for one perfect case. It is a **process**: you learn the area, find the controlling primary authority, and confirm it is still law. The usual order starts with secondary sources because they give you vocabulary, structure and citations before you search blindly.

### Secondary sources

- **Treatises and hornbooks**: in-depth, organized treatment of an area (contracts, civil procedure).
- **Legal encyclopedias** (Am. Jur. 2d, C.J.S.): broad overviews with footnotes to cases.
- **A.L.R. annotations**: focused surveys of how courts have decided a narrow question.
- **Restatements** (American Law Institute): restate the common law in black-letter rules with comments and illustrations. They are persuasive, not binding, unless a court adopts them.
- **Law review articles**: deep or critical analysis, often on new problems.
- **Practice guides and form books**: step-by-step help for lawyers in a jurisdiction.

Use a secondary source to learn the issue, pick up key terms and find primary citations. Never cite one when a controlling primary source exists.

### Generating search terms

Turn the facts into a research question and list words from four categories: **parties** (who: landlord, tenant), **the thing or place** (what: heater, apartment), **the claim or defense** (why: retaliation, breach), and **remedy** (what relief: damages, injunction). Add synonyms and the jurisdiction. A common memory aid is TARP: thing, cause of action, party, remedy.

### Searching databases

Databases offer **natural-language** searches and **terms-and-connectors** (Boolean) searches. Westlaw's common connectors: `&` (and), a space (or), `/s` (same sentence), `/p` (same paragraph), `/n` (within n words), `!` (root expander), `*` (one-letter wildcard), and quotation marks (exact phrase). Lexis uses `w/s`, `w/p` and `w/n`. Example: `"adverse possession" /s hostile` finds the phrase in the same sentence as "hostile." Filter by jurisdiction, court and date. Free sources such as Congress.gov, govinfo.gov and court websites cover primary law, and state and federal court websites often publish opinions.

### Validating with a citator

Before relying on any authority, **update** it using a **citator**: KeyCite (Westlaw), Shepard's (Lexis) or BCite (Bloomberg Law). A red flag in KeyCite means the case is no longer good law for at least one point; a yellow flag means some negative history or treatment. The citator also lists later cases that cite yours, which is a fast way to find more authority. Read the citing cases; a flag alone does not tell you how or why the case was treated.

Search results from any AI tool are leads, not authority. Read the actual source and verify that each case exists and says what you claim (see Week 12).

### The research plan and log

A **research plan** is a short document before you start: the question, the jurisdiction, the governing law expected (statute, common law), a list of sources in order, key search terms, and a stopping rule. A **research log** is a running record: for each step, the date, the source or database, the search terms and filters, what you found, and what to do next, including dead ends. The log keeps you from repeating work, lets a supervisor review your path, and makes it easy to pick up the project again.

### When to stop

Stop when searches keep returning the same authorities, your sources agree, you have the controlling statute or case and the leading cases on each element, and every authority has been updated.

@example
title: Building a short plan
problem: Client rented an apartment and was told two weeks after calling the city housing office that her lease would not be renewed. She asks whether the landlord can lawfully refuse. Draft the first three entries of a plan.
step: State the question and jurisdiction: under State law, may a landlord refuse to renew a lease soon after a tenant's complaint to a housing agency? Note the jurisdiction as the state where the apartment is.
step: List sources in order: a state landlord-tenant practice guide or treatise for background, then the state landlord-tenant statute (annotated code) for any retaliation provision.
step: Add search terms: retaliat! /s evict! or "non-renew!" and "housing inspector" or "code enforcement."
step: Add a final step: update every statute and case in a citator, and note the stopping rule.

@example
title: Writing a log entry
problem: You searched an annotated state code. Write a log entry.
step: Record the date, the source (for example, the annotated state code on Westlaw) and the search: `retaliat! /s tenan!` with the jurisdiction set to the client's state.
step: Record the result: one statute, with subsections (a) through (d), and nine annotated cases.
step: Record what you learned: the statute covers non-renewal within 90 days of a complaint; two annotated cases discuss "good faith."
step: Record the next step: read the cases, update the statute in a citator, and check for amendments.

@mistakes
- Starting with an unfocused full-text search instead of a secondary source that gives you the terms and structure.
- Citing a Restatement or a treatise as if it were binding law.
- Not updating authorities in a citator, and so relying on a case that has been overruled or superseded.
- Keeping no log, so you repeat work or cannot show how you reached your answer.

prompt: You want cases in which "adverse possession" appears in the same sentence as "hostile" on Westlaw. Which search is best?

choice: `"adverse possession" /p hostile`, because the connector means within the same sentence.

choice: `adverse possession hostile`, because a space means and.
hint: Which connector means the same sentence?
solution: `/s` means within the same sentence. `&` means and (anywhere in the document), `/p` means the same paragraph, and a space means or on Westlaw.
