@article
A **judicial opinion** is a court's written explanation of its decision. You read opinions not to learn who won but to learn what rule the court announced, why, and how far it reaches. A **case brief** is your own short, structured summary of an opinion. Briefing forces you to find those things before class and before you rely on the case in a memo.

### The parts of an opinion

An opinion usually has a caption (parties and court), the facts, the procedural history, the legal analysis, and a disposition. Read in layers: skim for structure, read for the story, then reread the analysis slowly. Ignore the publisher's **headnotes** and synopsis; they are editorial aids written by the publisher, not law and not the court's words.

### The standard brief

1. **Citation.** Name, court, year.
2. **Facts.** Only the **legally significant facts**: those that affect the outcome under the court's rule. Leave out color that does not matter.
3. **Procedural history and posture.** What the trial court did, and how the case reached this court (for example, an appeal from summary judgment or from dismissal under FRCP 12(b)(6)). The posture fixes the **standard of review**: questions of law are reviewed de novo, findings of fact for clear error, and discretionary rulings for abuse of discretion.
4. **Issue.** A one-sentence question combining the rule and the key facts: "Under [rule], does [key fact] ...?"
5. **Holding.** The court's answer to that question on those facts.
6. **Rule.** The general principle the court states or applies.
7. **Reasoning.** Why: text, precedent, policy.
8. **Disposition.** What the court did with the judgment below: **affirmed** (upheld), **reversed** (overturned), **vacated** (wiped out), **remanded** (sent back for further proceedings), often combined ("reversed and remanded").
9. **Notes.** Concurrences, dissents, and your questions.

### Holding, rule and dicta

The **holding** is the court's decision on the question presented, tied to the material facts and necessary to the result. **Dicta** (obiter dicta) are statements not necessary to the result: hypotheticals, side comments, alternative suggestions. Dicta are persuasive but not binding. A holding is narrower than a rule: the rule is the principle ("damages are limited to what was foreseeable"); the holding applies it ("this miller cannot recover lost profits from this carrier").

When no opinion commands a majority, the result is a **plurality**. Under *Marks v. United States* (1977), courts often treat the position of the justices who concurred on the narrowest grounds as the holding, though courts disagree on how to apply that rule.

### A model brief

In *Hadley v. Baxendale* (1854), a mill's crankshaft broke. The mill owner sent it with a carrier to be copied, but the carrier delayed delivery, and the mill sat idle longer than it should have. The owner sued for lost profits. The carrier had not been told that the mill could not run without the shaft. The court held that lost profits were not recoverable, stating the rule that damages for breach are those arising naturally from the breach or reasonably in the contemplation of both parties when they contracted. The case was sent back for a new trial because the jury had not been properly instructed on the measure of damages.

### Applying it

Briefing is itself an IRAC exercise run backward: you extract the court's issue, rule, application and conclusion. A good brief lets you answer, without rereading, "What would this court do with a new set of facts?"

@example
title: Separating holding from dicta
problem: A court writes: "Tenant's claim is barred because Tenant failed to give the written notice the lease required. Had Tenant given notice but only by email, we might well have excused the defect." What is the holding, and what is dictum?
step: Find the question the court actually had to decide: was Tenant's claim barred by failure to give the notice the lease required?
step: The holding is the answer necessary to the result: the claim is barred because no written notice was given.
step: The second sentence addresses facts that did not occur (notice by email). It is not necessary to the result, so it is dictum.
step: Conclusion: the holding is the bar for failure to give written notice; the email remark is persuasive at most.

@example
title: Writing an issue statement
problem: Compose an issue statement for this: A buyer of a used car sued the dealer, claiming the odometer reading was wrong. The court applied a statute that imposes liability on a seller who knowingly misstates mileage, and found the dealer had no knowledge of the error.
step: Put the rule first: "Under the statute imposing liability for knowingly misstating mileage ..."
step: Add the determinative fact: "... is a dealer liable when it did not know the odometer reading was wrong?"
step: Check that the issue is neither too broad ("Is the dealer liable?") nor too narrow (naming irrelevant facts such as the car's color).

@mistakes
- Copying the headnotes or the publisher's synopsis into the brief as if they were the holding.
- Stating the holding so broadly that it sweeps in facts the court never considered.
- Including every fact instead of only the legally significant ones.
- Treating a concurrence or dissent as the law of the case.

prompt: Buyer sued Seller for breach of a written contract. The trial court dismissed on limitations grounds. The court of appeals held that the limitations period was tolled while Seller was out of the state, so the claim was timely. The court added: "Had the claim been late, we would have been inclined to excuse the delay because Seller's conduct was inequitable." Which statement is the holding?
choice: The court would have excused a late filing because Seller acted inequitably.
choice: The trial court dismissed the claim on limitations grounds.
choice*: The claim was timely because the limitations period was tolled while Seller was out of the state.
choice: Seller's conduct was inequitable.
hint: The holding answers the question the court had to decide to reach its result.
solution: The court had to decide whether the claim was timely, and it held it was because of tolling. The statement about excusing a late claim is dictum about facts that did not occur, and the dismissal is procedural history.

choice: The plaintiff has won and is awarded damages.
choice: The case is over and the defendant has no further exposure.

@practice self
prompt: Brief the following invented case in the standard format (citation, facts, procedural history, issue, holding, rule, reasoning, disposition).

Finch v. Orchard Mutual (State Court of Appeals, 2020). Finch's barn burned. His policy covered fire loss but excluded "intentional acts of the insured." Orchard Mutual denied the claim because a fire investigator thought Finch's hired farmhand set the fire, and the farmhand had acted at Finch's request to clear brush, not to burn the barn. The trial court granted summary judgment to Orchard Mutual. The Court of Appeals held that the exclusion covers only acts of the insured and that Finch did not himself intend the loss; it reversed and remanded, reasoning that exclusions are read narrowly against the insurer.
solution: Citation: Finch v. Orchard Mutual (State Ct. App. 2020). Facts: Finch's barn burned; his policy covered fire but excluded intentional acts of the insured; his farmhand, acting at Finch's request to clear brush, set the fire; Finch did not intend to burn the barn. Procedural history: the insurer denied the claim; the trial court granted summary judgment to the insurer; Finch appealed. Issue: Under a policy excluding intentional acts of the insured, is a fire loss excluded when the insured's farmhand, not the insured, set the fire without intent to destroy the barn? Holding: No; the exclusion reaches only the insured's own intentional acts. Rule: exclusions are construed narrowly against the insurer. Reasoning: the exclusion's text refers to the insured, and ambiguity is resolved against the drafter. Disposition: reversed and remanded.
rubric: Facts are limited to those that matter, and the farmhand's lack of intent to burn the barn is included.
rubric: The issue pairs the exclusion's text with the key facts and is phrased as a question.
rubric: The holding is stated on these facts and is separate from the rule.
rubric: The disposition (reversed and remanded) and the standard of review or posture (summary judgment) are stated.
hint: Start with the posture (summary judgment for the insurer), then find the question the court answered.

@quiz mc
prompt: On appeal from a summary judgment ruling, a party argues the trial court misread the meaning of a contract term. What standard of review applies to the pure question of law?
choice: Clear error, because the trial court heard the facts.
choice: Abuse of discretion, because contract interpretation is committed to the trial judge's judgment.
choice*: De novo, because the appellate court decides questions of law without deference.
choice: Substantial evidence, because the court is reviewing an administrative record.
hint: Questions of law get fresh review.
solution: Pure questions of law, including summary-judgment rulings that turn on legal interpretation, are reviewed de novo. Clear error governs fact-finding, and abuse of discretion governs discretionary rulings.

@quiz ms
prompt: A court holds that Employer breached a promise to pay a bonus, writing: "The bonus was promised in writing. We need not decide whether an oral promise would suffice, though we doubt it would. The trial court therefore erred in dismissing." Which statements are accurate?
choice*: The statement that the bonus was promised in writing and the dismissal was error is part of the holding.
choice*: The remark about oral promises is dictum.
choice: The remark about oral promises is binding rule on later courts because the court wrote it in the opinion.
choice*: The disposition is most likely reversal, with a remand.
hint: Which statements were necessary to the result?
solution: The court's decision turned on the written promise, so that is the holding, and the dismissal was error, so reversal and remand follow. The comment about oral promises was not needed to decide the case and is dictum, which is not binding.
