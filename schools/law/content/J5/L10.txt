@article
There are millions of known species. **Classification** sorts them into groups based on shared features and evolutionary relationships, so scientists everywhere can communicate clearly. The science of naming and classifying organisms is called **taxonomy**.

### Levels of classification

From the largest group to the smallest, the eight levels are:

$$\text{Domain} \to \text{Kingdom} \to \text{Phylum} \to \text{Class} \to \text{Order} \to \text{Family} \to \text{Genus} \to \text{Species}$$

A common memory aid is "Do Keep Pond Clean Or Frogs Get Sick." Organisms in the same species can breed and produce fertile offspring. Each level down the list holds fewer organisms that are more alike.

### Scientific names

Each species has a two-part name, called **binomial nomenclature**. The first word is the **genus** (capitalized) and the second is the **species** (lowercase). Both are written in italics. Humans are *Homo sapiens*, and the domestic dog is *Canis familiaris* (often written *Canis lupus familiaris*). Species in the same genus are more closely related to each other than to species in other genera. For example, *Felis catus* (house cat) and *Felis silvestris* (wildcat) share a genus.

### Domains and kingdoms

All life is placed into three **domains**:

1. **Bacteria**: single-celled, no nucleus.
2. **Archaea**: single-celled, no nucleus, often living in extreme environments.
3. **Eukarya**: cells with a nucleus, including four kingdoms: **protists** (mostly single-celled, such as amoebas), **fungi** (mushrooms, yeasts), **plants** and **animals**.

### Dichotomous keys

A **dichotomous key** identifies an organism through a series of paired statements. At each pair, called a **couplet**, you choose the one that fits and follow its instruction until you reach a name.

A sample key:

1a. Has a backbone: go to 2
1b. No backbone: go to 4
2a. Has feathers: **bird**
2b. No feathers: go to 3
3a. Has fur: **mammal**
3b. Has scales and lays eggs on land: **reptile**
4a. Has a hard outer skeleton and six legs: **insect**
4b. Soft body with no legs: **worm**

A key for $n$ organisms needs $n - 1$ couplets, because each couplet splits one group into two.

### Building a key

To build a key, pick a feature that splits your group into two clear parts, such as "has wings" or "no wings," and keep splitting each part until every item is alone. Use features you can see, not opinions like "pretty."

@example
title: Using a key
problem: Use the sample key to identify a corn snake, which has a backbone, no feathers, no fur, and scales, and lays eggs on land.
step: Couplet 1: the snake has a backbone, so choose 1a and go to 2.
step: Couplet 2: no feathers, so choose 2b and go to 3.
step: Couplet 3: no fur, and scaly skin, so choose 3b: the key gives reptile.

@mistakes
- Writing a scientific name as "homo Sapiens"; the genus is capitalized and the species is lowercase.
- Choosing a key feature that is vague, like "big" or "pretty," instead of one that can be observed.
- Thinking species in the same kingdom must be closely related; kingdoms are very large groups.
