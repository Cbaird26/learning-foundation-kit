@article
**Rhetoric** is the study and practice of effective communication, especially persuasion. The classical Greek and Roman tradition shaped Western education and legal advocacy, and its vocabulary is still the one lawyers and debaters use. In his *Rhetoric*, Aristotle defined rhetoric as the ability to see the available means of persuasion in a given case.

### The three artistic proofs

Aristotle identified three modes of persuasion that a speaker creates.

**Ethos** is the appeal to the speaker's credibility. Aristotle located it in three qualities: practical wisdom (good sense), virtue (good character) and goodwill toward the audience. In modern terms, **competence, character and caring**. Ethos is built by preparation, accuracy, fairness to opposing views and honesty about limits. A judge who catches a lawyer misstating the record stops trusting everything else she says.

**Pathos** is the appeal to the audience's emotions. Emotions are not irrational noise; they are part of how people judge. Vivid stories, imagery and tone engage pathos. An ethical appeal ties emotion to a true and relevant fact. Emotion cut loose from the facts, such as fear stirred up to hide a weak case, is manipulation.

**Logos** is the appeal to reason: claims, evidence and inference. It includes deduction, induction and examples. Aristotle noted that speakers usually reason with the **enthymeme**, a syllogism in which a premise is unstated and supplied by the audience: "He broke the law, so he should be held accountable" assumes "people who break the law should be held accountable."

Strong speeches blend the three, in proportion to the audience and occasion. A skeptical, technical audience needs heavy logos and careful ethos; a eulogy leans on pathos.

### Genres of rhetoric

- **Deliberative** rhetoric concerns the **future** and what should be done: legislative debate and policy speeches.
- **Forensic** (judicial) rhetoric concerns the **past**: was an act done, and was it just? Courtroom argument is the classic example.
- **Epideictic** (ceremonial) rhetoric concerns the **present** and praises or blames: eulogies, toasts, award speeches.

### The five canons

Roman writers organized the art into five **canons**: **invention** (finding arguments), **arrangement** (organizing them), **style** (choosing language), **memory** (retaining the material) and **delivery** (voice and gesture). They map onto the parts of this course: research, organizing, language, notes and delivery.

### Kairos

**Kairos** is the sense of the opportune moment: what to say, and when, given the circumstances. A fair argument made at the wrong moment can fail; recognizing the audience's readiness is part of rhetorical skill.

### Using the framework

When you analyze a speech, ask: how does the speaker earn trust (ethos), what feelings are engaged and are they tied to facts (pathos), and what is the reasoning and is it valid (logos)?

@example
title: Labeling appeals in a passage
problem: A hypothetical lawyer tells a jury: "I have represented tenants in this county for twenty years, and I will not ask you to believe anything that the records do not show. The landlord's own logs show that the heat was off for nineteen days in January. Imagine your child sleeping in that apartment." Identify the appeals.
step: "Twenty years" and "I will not ask you to believe anything the records do not show" build ethos through experience and a promise of honesty.
step: "The landlord's own logs show the heat was off for nineteen days" is logos: a specific, verifiable fact from the opposing party's own records.
step: "Imagine your child sleeping in that apartment" is pathos: an emotional appeal using imagery.
step: The pathos is tied to the proven fact, which is ethical. Because it is a jury address about a past event, the genre is forensic.

@mistakes
- Treating pathos as manipulation by definition; the problem is emotion detached from true, relevant facts.
- Believing a speech should rely on only one appeal.
- Confusing the genres: forensic concerns the past, deliberative the future.
- Forgetting that ethos is earned during the speech and can be lost by one misstatement.

solution: An enthymeme is an argument in which a premise or conclusion is left unstated because the audience will supply it. Example: "The contract was signed under threat, so it should not be enforced." The unstated premise is "contracts signed under threat of harm should not be enforced." The speaker saves time and involves the audience, but the hidden premise must be true and acceptable, so a careful advocate checks it.
