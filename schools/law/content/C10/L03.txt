@article
A claim without support is only an opinion. **Supporting material** is the evidence you use to make a claim believable, clear or memorable. Strong speeches rest on research done before the outline: you cannot decide your main points until you know what the evidence actually says.

### Finding sources

Start broad with reference works to learn vocabulary, then move to **primary sources** (original documents, data, statutes, court opinions, interviews) and **secondary sources** (books, articles and reports that interpret primary sources). For law-related topics, a court opinion or statute is a primary source; a news story about it is secondary. Library databases, government statistical sites and peer-reviewed journals are generally more reliable than anonymous web pages.

### Evaluating a source

Ask four questions.

1. **Authority.** Who produced it, and what qualifies them?
2. **Accuracy.** Is it supported by verifiable evidence, and do other reliable sources agree?
3. **Currency.** Is it recent enough for the claim? Law and statistics change; a decision overruled last year is useless as current law.
4. **Purpose.** Why was it created: to inform, sell, or persuade? A source with a stake in the outcome may still be useful, but you must account for the bias.

### Kinds of supporting material

- **Examples** are specific instances (brief or extended) that make an idea concrete. A **hypothetical example** describes an imagined case and must be labeled as such.
- **Statistics** are numbers that summarize data. They show scale and trend. Use few, round them sensibly, and explain what they mean.
- **Testimony** is the words of others: **expert testimony** from people with relevant training, **lay testimony** from those with direct experience.
- **Analogies** compare something unfamiliar to something familiar.
- **Narratives** are short stories that carry emotional force, best paired with data so the audience sees the story is typical.

### Using statistics honestly

A percentage change is $\frac{\text{new} - \text{old}}{\text{old}} \times 100\%$. If a city's reported incidents rise from 120 to 150, the increase is $30/120 = 25\%$. Check what is being compared: a rise from 2 to 4 is a $100\%$ increase but only two events. Notice also the difference between the **mean** (the average) and the **median** (the middle value): one extreme value can pull the mean far from what is typical, so in skewed data such as incomes the median is usually more representative.

### Citing sources aloud

Listeners cannot see footnotes, so give **oral citations**: name the author or organization, the credential or type of source, and the date. For example: "According to a 2023 report from the state court administrator's office, filings in housing court rose by a quarter." Leave out full bibliographic detail, but have it ready on a written list. Oral citation protects your credibility and avoids **plagiarism**: presenting someone else's words or ideas as your own, which is dishonest in a speech just as in an essay.

### Organizing research

Keep a running record of each source and the exact fact or quote you used, with page or location. For quotations, use exact words and attribute them; for paraphrase, still credit the source.

@example
title: Testing a statistic before you use it
problem: A source says, "Average complaints per month at a clinic doubled." You find monthly counts of 4, 5, 4, 6 and 30 for five months. Is "average" a fair description of a typical month, and what is the mean?
step: Compute the mean: $(4 + 5 + 4 + 6 + 30)/5 = 49/5 = 9.8$.
step: Find the median by ordering the values: $4, 4, 5, 6, 30$. The middle value is $5$.
step: One extreme month (30) pulls the mean to $9.8$, nearly double the typical month. The median of $5$ describes a typical month better.
step: In a speech, you would say, "In a typical month the clinic receives about five complaints, though one month spiked to thirty," and cite the source aloud.

@mistakes
- Using the first search result without checking who produced it and why.
- Reading statistics without explaining what they mean or what they compare.
- Citing a source's name but not its date, so listeners cannot judge currency.
- Presenting a hypothetical example as though it were a real event.

@practice num
prompt: Reported thefts on a campus rose from 80 in one year to 100 in the next. By what percent did they rise?
answer: 25
check: (100 - 80) / 80 * 100
unit: percent
hint: Divide the increase by the old value, not the new one.
solution: The increase is $20$, and $20/80 = 0.25$, so $25\%$.

@practice ms
prompt: Which of the following are good tests of a source's credibility?
choice*: Does the author have relevant training or a verifiable track record?
choice*: Is the information recent enough for the claim being made?
choice: Does the source use confident language?
choice*: Does the source have a purpose, such as selling or persuading, that might bias it?
choice: Does the source appear first in a web search?
hint: Confidence and search rank do not measure reliability.
solution: Authority, currency and purpose are standard tests. Confident tone and search ranking say nothing about accuracy.

@practice num
prompt: Five households report monthly rents of 900, 950, 1000, 1050 and 4000 dollars. What is the median rent?
answer: 1000
check: float(np.median([900, 950, 1000, 1050, 4000]))
unit: dollars
hint: Order the values and take the middle one.
solution: The values are already in order, and the middle (third) value is $1000$. The mean would be pulled up to $1580$ by the single high rent.

@quiz mc
prompt: A speaker says "Crime is up 200% this year!" The count rose from one incident to three. What is the most important problem with presenting this statistic alone?
choice: The percentage should have been calculated from the new value.
choice*: A large percentage of a tiny base can mislead; she should also give the actual counts.
choice: Statistics should never be used in speeches.
choice: Percentages can only describe averages.
hint: Look at the absolute numbers underneath the percent.
solution: The 200 percent increase is correct arithmetic but misleading without the base. Give the counts so listeners can judge scale.

@quiz num
prompt: Reported cases in a county fell from 250 in one year to 200 the next. By what percent did they decrease?
answer: 20
check: (250 - 200) / 250 * 100
unit: percent
hint: Use the old value in the denominator.
solution: The decrease is $50$, and $50/250 = 0.2$, so $20\%$.
