@article
Almost everyone who speaks to a group feels nervous. Surveys of students regularly rank public speaking among their most common fears, and experienced speakers still report a surge of adrenaline before they begin. For a future lawyer this matters: oral argument, client meetings, depositions and trial all ask you to think clearly while being watched. The goal of this course is not to remove nervousness but to **manage** it so that it does not control your performance.

### Where the anxiety comes from

Communication researchers use the term **communication apprehension** for fear or anxiety about speaking. It has two layers.

- **Physical arousal.** Your body treats an audience as a possible threat: heart rate rises, hands shake, mouth dries, breathing becomes shallow. This is the ordinary stress response.
- **Mental interpretation.** Thoughts such as "everyone will notice my mistakes" or "I will forget everything" turn arousal into dread.

Two facts help. First, the **spotlight effect**: audiences notice far less of your nervousness than you think. A trembling hand that feels enormous to you is usually invisible from the third row. Second, audiences generally **want you to succeed**; a boring or failed talk is uncomfortable for them too.

### Techniques that work

**1. Prepare thoroughly.** Most anxiety is anticipation of the unknown. Know your material, your opening and your closing. A memorized first thirty seconds gets you through the most nervous moment.

**2. Rehearse aloud, on your feet, and on time.** Silent reading-through is not rehearsal. Speaking aloud tests your word choices, your pacing and your breathing. Run the full speech several times with a timer and, when possible, in front of a listener or a recording device.

**3. Reframe arousal.** The same racing heart accompanies excitement. Telling yourself "I am excited and energized" has been associated with better performance than trying to "calm down." This is **cognitive reappraisal**, a form of **cognitive restructuring**: replace catastrophic predictions with realistic ones ("I may stumble once, and I can recover").

**4. Visualize success.** **Visualization** means mentally rehearsing a confident performance, including the room, the first sentence and a smooth finish. It supports, but never replaces, real practice.

**5. Control your body.** Slow, deep breaths before you begin, a relaxed stance and a pause before your first word all lower the physical symptoms. Gradual exposure, speaking in low-stakes settings first, is the principle of **systematic desensitization**.

### Planning your preparation time

Preparation is a budget. If you will give a three-minute talk, a single full run-through takes three minutes, but each run also needs a short review of what went wrong. A sensible plan spreads sessions over several days rather than cramming the night before, because sleep helps memory.

### Skills for the lawyer

An advocate adds one more technique: **know your first and last sentences cold**. Judges and juries remember beginnings and endings. If your opening is secure, nervousness has less room to grow.

@example
title: Building a rehearsal schedule
problem: You must give a 4-minute talk in 3 days. You plan 5 full aloud run-throughs, each followed by 2 minutes of notes on what to fix. How many minutes of rehearsal time do you need, and how should you spread it?
step: Each session is one run-through plus review: $4 + 2 = 6$ minutes.
step: Five sessions take $5 \times 6 = 30$ minutes of rehearsal time in total.
step: Spread the sessions across the three days rather than all at once: for example two on day one, two on day two and one on the morning of the talk, so that each day's fixes can be tested the next.
step: Add a separate five minutes to memorize the opening and closing sentences. Preparation time is then $30 + 5 = 35$ minutes plus research and outlining.

@mistakes
- Rehearsing silently or only in your head, so timing and wording problems appear for the first time in front of the audience.
- Trying to eliminate nervousness completely instead of channeling it into energy.
- Cramming all practice into one session the night before.
- Memorizing the whole speech word for word, which makes one forgotten word feel like a disaster.
