@article
Words in a speech are heard once, at the speaker's pace. That fact shapes style. Good spoken language is **clear**, **concrete**, **vivid** and **appropriate** to the audience and occasion. It is not simply written language read aloud.

### Oral style versus written style

Spoken style uses shorter sentences, simpler words, more repetition and more direct address ("you", "we") than writing. Listeners cannot go back, so you repeat key ideas and use signposts. Contractions ("we're", "can't") sound natural. Long, nested sentences with several clauses overload the listener's memory; break them up.

### Clarity and concreteness

Prefer **concrete** words ("the tenant lost her home") to **abstract** ones ("adverse housing outcomes"). Define unavoidable jargon the first time it appears. A lawyer addressing a jury says "contract" and "promise," not "instrumentality of exchange." Use **active voice** ("the board approved the rule") unless you have a reason to hide the actor.

### Vividness: figures of speech

A **simile** compares using "like" or "as" ("the statute is like a locked door"). A **metaphor** says one thing is another ("the courtroom was a stage"). **Imagery** appeals to the senses; **examples** and **narratives** make abstractions visible. Avoid **mixed metaphors** ("we'll burn that bridge when we cross it") and clichés that deaden attention.

### Rhythm and repetition

Repetition is a feature of speech, not a defect. Common devices:

- **Anaphora**: repeating the same opening words in successive clauses ("We will listen. We will learn. We will act.").
- **Parallelism**: giving related ideas the same grammatical form, which makes them easy to follow.
- **Antithesis**: placing contrasting ideas in balanced structure ("Ask not what the rule allows, but what it requires.").
- **Tricolon** or the **rule of three**: a list of three items, which sounds complete.
- **Alliteration**: repeating initial consonant sounds, used lightly.
- **Rhetorical question**: a question asked for effect, not for an answer.

Use devices in moderation. Overloaded ornament sounds insincere, and in a legal setting plain precision usually persuades better than flourish.

### Inclusive and respectful language

Choose words that include rather than exclude. Use gender-neutral terms ("chair," "firefighter") where gender is irrelevant, and describe people as they describe themselves. Avoid slang or idioms that some listeners will not know.

### Script length and rate

If you work from a manuscript or want to estimate length, use your speaking rate. Conversational public speaking commonly runs at about 120 to 160 words per minute; plan at your own measured rate. At 130 words per minute, a 3-minute speech is about $130 \times 3 = 390$ words. Remember that pauses, laughter and visual aids consume time, so an unrehearsed script should be a little short.

@example
title: Making a sentence speakable
problem: Rewrite for the ear: "The implementation of the new policy necessitates that all affected individuals be notified in writing." Then estimate how long a 5-minute speech is at 140 words per minute.
step: Replace abstractions with concrete words and the passive with an active verb: "We have to tell everyone affected, in writing, before the policy starts."
step: Break it into two short sentences and add a signpost: "Here is what happens next. Everyone affected gets a letter before the policy starts."
step: Estimate the length of the whole speech: at 140 words per minute, $140 \times 5 = 700$ words.
step: Keep your script near that figure, trimmed slightly, then time a rehearsal aloud and adjust.

@mistakes
- Writing a speech as an essay and reading it aloud, with long sentences and no repetition.
- Piling on devices until the speech sounds like a performance rather than a conversation.
- Using jargon or abbreviations the audience has not been given.
- Mixing metaphors or leaning on clichés.
