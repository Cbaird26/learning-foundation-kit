@article
**Debate** is structured argument between opposing positions before an audience or judge. Whether or not you debate formally, the skills transfer directly to oral advocacy: you must build your own case, find the weak point in the other side's case and answer it fairly.

### Key concepts

The side that proposes change or makes a claim carries the **burden of proof**: the obligation to give reasons and evidence. The standard varies: in a debate it is persuading the judge; in a civil case it is usually a preponderance of the evidence; in a criminal case, proof beyond a reasonable doubt. A related idea, the **presumption**, favors the status quo until the burden is met.

**Clash** is direct engagement with the other side's arguments. A debate in which each side only reads its own speeches never clashes and is rarely persuasive.

### Stasis: finding the real disagreement

Classical rhetoric offered **stasis theory** as a way to find the point of disagreement. The questions move in order.

1. **Fact**: did it happen? (Is there a problem?)
2. **Definition**: what should we call it? (What is the category?)
3. **Quality**: how serious or good or bad is it?
4. **Policy** (or procedure): what should be done, and who decides?

If the two sides disagree about facts, arguing about policy is premature. Lawyers use the same ladder: dispute the facts, then the legal category, then the consequence.

### Four-step refutation

Clear refutation uses four steps.

1. **Signpost**: tell the listener which argument you answer ("On their second point").
2. **State** the opposing claim fairly.
3. **Respond**: explain why it is wrong, weak or irrelevant, and give evidence.
4. **Impact**: explain why that matters to the outcome.

A useful variant is "they say, but, because, therefore." Attack the argument, never the person.

Ways to respond: point out missing evidence; show the evidence is outdated or from a biased source; show that the conclusion does not follow from the evidence; offer a counterexample; or show that, even if the claim were true, it does not decide the question (an **"even if"** response; this differs from a **turn**, which argues that the opponent's point actually supports your side).

### Common fallacies

A **fallacy** is an error in reasoning that can be persuasive despite being flawed.

- **Ad hominem**: attacking the person instead of the argument.
- **Straw man**: misrepresenting a position to make it easy to defeat.
- **False dilemma**: presenting only two options when more exist.
- **Slippery slope**: claiming, without evidence, that one step will inevitably lead to a catastrophe.
- **Hasty generalization**: drawing a broad conclusion from too few cases.
- ***Post hoc***: assuming that because B followed A, A caused B.
- **Begging the question**: assuming what you are trying to prove.
- **Red herring**: introducing an irrelevant point to distract.
- **Appeal to authority**: relying on an authority outside their field or without evidence.

Name the fallacy briefly and then explain, in plain words, why the reasoning fails.

### Notes during a debate

Debaters keep a **flow**, a column of notes tracking each argument and its answers, so that nothing is dropped. A dropped argument is usually treated as conceded.

@example
title: Refuting an argument in four steps
problem: Your opponent says in a hypothetical policy debate: "Since the city installed new streetlights, burglaries rose. Therefore the lights cause burglaries." Refute it.
step: Signpost: "On my opponent's lighting argument."
step: State fairly: "She says burglaries rose after the lights went in, so the lights caused it."
step: Respond: this is the *post hoc* fallacy. Timing alone does not show cause. Burglaries across the whole region may have risen, and the speaker offered no comparison with neighborhoods that had no new lights.
step: Impact: "Without evidence of cause, this gives no reason to remove the lights, so the proposal stands."

@mistakes
- Misrepresenting the opponent's argument, which is a straw man and damages your ethos.
- Attacking the person rather than the reasoning.
- Naming a fallacy but not explaining why the reasoning fails.
- Failing to explain the impact of a successful refutation.

@practice self
prompt: A hypothetical opponent argues, "Because the school added a homework app, grades rose. So the app works, and any school that does not buy it is hurting students." Write a four-step refutation (signpost, state, respond, impact) and name two fallacies it contains.
hint: Look for a cause claimed from timing alone, and a leap from one school to every school.
solution: Signpost: On my opponent's homework-app argument. State: She says grades rose after the app and so every school must buy it. Respond: This is post hoc, because timing does not show cause; teachers, tests or the cohort may have changed, and one school is a hasty generalization to all schools. She gave no comparison group. Impact: Without proof that the app caused the rise, there is no reason to require all schools to buy it, and the plan fails.
rubric: Includes all four steps in order.
rubric: States the opponent's claim fairly.
rubric: Names post hoc and hasty generalization (or equally valid fallacies) and explains each.
rubric: Ends with the impact on the decision.
