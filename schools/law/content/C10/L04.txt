@article
Listeners cannot reread. If they lose the thread, it is gone. For that reason speeches need a more visible structure than essays: a clear opening, a small number of main points, repeated signposts, and an unmistakable ending. A common classroom rule is "tell them what you will tell them, tell them, then tell them what you told them." It is repetitive on paper and useful in the air.

### The introduction

A good introduction does five jobs in under a minute.

1. **Gain attention** with a story, a surprising fact, a question or a quotation.
2. **Establish relevance** by showing why this audience should care.
3. **Establish credibility** by saying briefly why you can speak on this.
4. **State the thesis**, one sentence giving the central idea or claim.
5. **Preview the main points** so listeners can follow.

### The body: main points

For a short speech, **three main points** is a good limit; listeners rarely retain more. Each main point is a complete sentence expressing one idea, supported by evidence. Choose an **organizational pattern** that fits your purpose.

| Pattern | Use when |
|---|---|
| **Chronological** | Events or steps unfold over time |
| **Spatial** | Parts are arranged by location |
| **Topical** | The subject divides into natural categories |
| **Cause and effect** | You explain why something happens or its consequences |
| **Problem and solution** | You show a problem, then propose a remedy |

### Transitions and signposts

**Transitions** link ideas: "That covers the cost; now let's look at the benefit." **Signposts** tell listeners where they are: "My second point is." **Internal previews and summaries** recap a section. These devices feel excessive when written, yet are essential in speech.

### The conclusion

A conclusion should (1) **signal the end** ("In closing"), (2) **restate the thesis and main points**, and (3) **end memorably**, perhaps by returning to the opening story. Never introduce new evidence, and never trail off with "so, um, that's it."

### Outlining

A **full-sentence outline** is the working plan; a **speaking outline** of key words is what you carry. Conventions: Roman numerals for main parts (I, II, III), capital letters for subpoints (A, B), and numerals for details (1, 2). **Subordination** means each lower level supports the level above it, and every level with a subpoint 1 must have a subpoint 2 (a topic cannot be divided into only one part). Each main point should be one idea.

### Timing

Budget time as a percentage of the speech. A common allocation is about 10 to 15 percent for the introduction, 75 to 80 percent for the body and 5 to 10 percent for the conclusion. In a 3-minute (180-second) speech, an introduction of 15 percent is $0.15 \times 180 = 27$ seconds.

@example
title: Budgeting a 5-minute speech
problem: A 5-minute speech has a 12 percent introduction, a 6 percent conclusion and three equal main points. How many seconds does each main point get?
step: The speech lasts $5 \times 60 = 300$ seconds.
step: The introduction takes $0.12 \times 300 = 36$ seconds and the conclusion $0.06 \times 300 = 18$ seconds.
step: The body has $300 - 36 - 18 = 246$ seconds.
step: Each of three equal main points gets $246/3 = 82$ seconds. Leave a few seconds inside that for transitions.

@mistakes
- Having four or five main points in a short speech, so listeners remember none.
- Writing a thesis that is only a topic ("Tenant law") rather than a claim.
- Introducing new evidence in the conclusion.
- Using an outline with an "A" and no "B" under a heading.
