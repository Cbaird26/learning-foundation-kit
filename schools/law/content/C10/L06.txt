@article
Delivery is how the message reaches the audience. Research and common experience agree on the main point: effective delivery looks and sounds like **enhanced conversation**. It is natural, energetic and directed at the listeners, and it never draws attention to itself.

### Four methods of delivery

- **Manuscript**: reading from a full text. Precise, but often stiff; used where exact words matter (a formal statement).
- **Memorized**: delivering from memory. Looks polished, but a lapse can derail the speech and it often sounds recited.
- **Impromptu**: speaking with little or no preparation. Common in meetings and in questions from a judge.
- **Extemporaneous**: carefully prepared and rehearsed, delivered from a brief outline in words chosen in the moment. This is the usual best choice, and the one advocates use.

### Voice

Five features of voice matter.

1. **Volume**: loud enough for the farthest listener; vary it for emphasis.
2. **Rate**: words per minute. A typical range is 120 to 160; speaking faster or slower than the topic needs loses listeners. Vary the rate for emphasis.
3. **Pitch**: highness or lowness of voice. Avoid a flat **monotone**; let pitch rise and fall naturally.
4. **Pauses**: silent beats before key points and after questions. Pausing feels long to the speaker and sounds confident to the audience.
5. **Articulation and pronunciation**: speaking each sound distinctly and saying words correctly.

**Vocal fillers** ("um", "like", "you know") are a common problem. Replace them with a silent pause, which is more authoritative.

### Body

**Eye contact**: look at individuals for a thought or a sentence at a time, scanning the whole room. **Posture and movement**: stand balanced and move with purpose, for example to signal a transition, not pace nervously. **Gestures**: use natural, open gestures that match the words; avoid repetitive fidgeting. **Facial expression** should match the mood of the message. **Appearance** should suit the occasion; for formal legal settings, conservative professional dress is the norm.

### Notes

Notes are a safety net, not a script. Use a **key-word outline** on a few cards or one page, in large type. Write out your opening and your closing sentences if that helps. Glance down, then look up and speak. Never hide behind the paper.

### Measuring and critiquing

When you record a speech, count the words and the minutes to find your **speaking rate**: $\text{words} \div \text{minutes}$. A critique should use criteria: rate, volume, pitch variety, fillers, eye contact, gestures and use of notes. Name one strength and one or two specific changes.

### Technology

If you use slides or a microphone, practice with them. Visual aids should support the speech, not replace it; a slide crowded with text invites the audience to read instead of listen.

@example
title: Measuring your speaking rate from a recording
problem: You record a speech of 468 words that runs 3 minutes 0 seconds with 24 seconds of pauses included. What is your rate, and is it in the usual range?
step: Use the total running time, since pauses are part of delivery: 3 minutes.
step: Rate $= 468 \div 3 = 156$ words per minute.
step: The usual range is about 120 to 160, so 156 is at the fast end of normal.
step: Critique: strength, good energy; change, add two deliberate pauses before key claims and slow slightly in the middle section, aiming for about 140.

@mistakes
- Reading the speech word for word from a manuscript with the head down.
- Filling every silence with "um" instead of pausing.
- Using repetitive, distracting movement such as pacing, swaying or playing with a pen.
- Putting a full script in your notes so you cannot make eye contact.
