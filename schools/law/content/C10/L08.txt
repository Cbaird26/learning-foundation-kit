@article
A persuasive speech tries to change or reinforce beliefs, attitudes or actions. Structure matters even more than in an informative speech, because you are asking the audience to move.

### Propositions

A persuasive speech defends a **proposition**, a claim. There are three types.

- **Proposition of fact**: whether something is or is not true ("The new ordinance reduced noise complaints").
- **Proposition of value**: whether something is good, just or wrong ("Mandatory arbitration clauses are unfair to consumers").
- **Proposition of policy**: what should be done ("The county should fund a public defender office"). Policy speeches usually require showing a need, a workable plan and a net benefit.

### Monroe's motivated sequence

**Monroe's motivated sequence** is a five-step pattern designed to move listeners from attention to action. It is especially useful for policy speeches and short persuasive talks.

1. **Attention**: gain interest and introduce the topic.
2. **Need**: show a real problem that affects the audience, with evidence of its seriousness and its roots.
3. **Satisfaction**: present your solution and show how it meets the need, including answers to likely objections.
4. **Visualization**: help the audience picture the future with the solution (positive) or without it (negative), or both.
5. **Action**: tell listeners exactly what to do, specifically and soon.

Typical timing puts more weight on need and satisfaction than on attention and visualization. If the audience already agrees there is a problem, shorten the need step.

### Other patterns

**Problem-solution** (a simpler cousin of Monroe's), **problem-cause-solution**, **comparative advantage** (showing that your plan beats the alternatives, useful with an audience that already agrees on the problem), and **refutation** (organizing around the opposition's best objections, useful for a hostile audience).

### Toulmin's model

Stephen Toulmin offered a way to see the parts of any argument.

- **Claim**: the conclusion you want accepted.
- **Grounds** (data): the evidence offered.
- **Warrant**: the general principle linking grounds to claim; often unstated, as in an enthymeme.
- **Backing**: support for the warrant.
- **Qualifier**: the strength of the claim ("probably", "in most cases").
- **Rebuttal**: conditions under which the claim would not hold.

Using it, you can test your own speech. Weak speeches have a claim and grounds but no accepted warrant, or ignore the rebuttal. The model also resembles legal reasoning: the claim is the conclusion, the grounds the facts, the warrant the rule.

### Persuading ethically

Adapt to the audience's attitude. Start with common ground, offer evidence in proportion, acknowledge the other side's strongest point, and make your call to action realistic and specific.

@example
title: Time allocation in a motivated-sequence speech
problem: A 6-minute (360-second) persuasive speech uses these shares: attention 10%, need 25%, satisfaction 30%, visualization 15%, action 20%. How many seconds does each step get?
step: Check that the shares total $10 + 25 + 30 + 15 + 20 = 100\%$.
step: Attention: $0.10 \times 360 = 36$ seconds. Need: $0.25 \times 360 = 90$ seconds.
step: Satisfaction: $0.30 \times 360 = 108$ seconds. Visualization: $0.15 \times 360 = 54$ seconds.
step: Action: $0.20 \times 360 = 72$ seconds. The five steps total $36 + 90 + 108 + 54 + 72 = 360$ seconds. Use the sheet when rehearsing.

@mistakes
- Spending most of the speech on the problem and leaving thirty seconds for the solution and call to action.
- Asking for a vague action ("think about this") instead of a specific, achievable one.
- Treating the warrant as obvious when the audience may not share it.
- Offering a solution without evidence that it works.

@practice self
prompt: Write a one-paragraph outline of a persuasive speech using Monroe's motivated sequence on this topic: "Colleges should offer a short course on understanding contracts." Label all five steps with one or two sentences each, ending with a specific action.
hint: Decide the specific action first, then work backward through the steps.
solution: Attention: Imagine signing a gym membership you cannot cancel. Need: Students sign leases, loans and phone plans without training, and consumer complaints show many are surprised by fees and renewals. Satisfaction: A one-credit contracts course teaching how to read terms, spot automatic renewal and ask questions would address this, and a similar workshop is already low-cost to run. Visualization: Picture graduating able to read any lease and keep your money; without it, picture another surprise charge. Action: Sign the petition at the door today and email the dean's office before Friday.
rubric: All five steps are present, in order, and correctly labeled.
rubric: Need is supported by evidence or a plausible example, not just assertion.
rubric: Satisfaction explains how the solution meets the need.
rubric: Action is specific and achievable.
