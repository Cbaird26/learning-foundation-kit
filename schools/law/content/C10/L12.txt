@article
Persuasion changes minds; that makes it powerful, and so it carries responsibility. Rhetoric has been criticized since antiquity as the art of making the weaker case seem the stronger. The answer is not to avoid persuasion but to practice it ethically.

### Persuasion and manipulation

**Ethical persuasion** respects the audience's ability to reason: it uses accurate evidence, sound reasoning and emotions that are connected to true and relevant facts, and it discloses the speaker's purpose. **Manipulation** bypasses reason: it relies on deception, hidden motives, fabricated evidence or emotional pressure unconnected to the facts. A useful test is publicity: would the speaker be comfortable if the audience knew exactly how and why they were being persuaded?

### Speaker duties

- **Truthfulness**: do not assert what you know to be false; do not omit facts that would change the audience's conclusion in a way that deceives.
- **Accuracy of evidence**: quote and cite faithfully; do not cut a quotation so that it says the opposite. Do not invent statistics.
- **Attribution**: credit sources; plagiarism is deceit.
- **Fairness to opponents**: represent opposing views accurately, avoiding the straw man.
- **Respect**: avoid abusive language, stereotypes and personal attacks.

### Special duties of the lawyer

A lawyer is an advocate and an **officer of the court**. The ABA Model Rules of Professional Conduct, adopted in some form by nearly every state, set the minimum.

- **Rule 3.3 (Candor toward the tribunal)**: a lawyer may not knowingly make a false statement of fact or law to a court, may not offer evidence known to be false, and must disclose controlling legal authority directly adverse to the client's position that opposing counsel has not disclosed. The advocate may then distinguish or argue against it.
- **Rule 3.4 (Fairness to opposing party and counsel)**: no falsifying evidence, and at trial no alluding to matters not supported by admissible evidence and no stating personal opinion on the justness of a cause or the credibility of a witness. (Rule 3.4(e) covers allusions and personal knowledge.)
- **Rule 3.5 (Impartiality and decorum of the tribunal)**: no improper influence on judges and jurors, and no disruption of the proceeding.
- **Rule 4.1 (Truthfulness in statements to others)**: no knowingly false statements of material fact or law to a third person.
- **Rule 8.4(c)**: it is professional misconduct to engage in conduct involving dishonesty, fraud, deceit or misrepresentation.

These rules show that advocacy is not winning at any cost. A lawyer may argue forcefully for a client, may choose which arguments to make and may decline to volunteer harmful facts the rules do not require disclosing, but may not lie to the court.

### Ethical choices in practice

An advocate who is candid about a weakness often persuades more, not less, because credibility is the advocate's main asset. When a judge asks about adverse authority, an honest answer, followed by the best distinction, is both required and effective.

### The deliverable: argue and answer

This week's exercise is the block deliverable: prepare and give a 5-minute argument for one side of a supplied problem, then answer questions. Build a roadmap, make two points with headings, concede what is true, request relief and prepare three questions.

@example
title: Handling adverse authority honestly
problem: In a hypothetical appeal, you represent a landlord. You know of a controlling decision from your own court (call it the *Hypothetical Tenant Case*) that holds a repair waiver unenforceable, and the other side has not cited it. A judge asks whether any controlling case addresses repair waivers. What do you do?
step: Identify the duty: under Model Rule 3.3(a)(2) the lawyer must disclose controlling authority directly adverse to the client's position that opposing counsel has not disclosed.
step: Answer directly: "Yes, Your Honor. There is the Hypothetical Tenant Case, which held such a waiver unenforceable."
step: Then advocate: distinguish it truthfully: "In that case the tenant had no other remedy, while here the lease preserved a repair-and-deduct right."
step: Result: you meet the duty and keep your credibility, and you still make the best argument for the client.

@mistakes
- Treating persuasion as justifying any means, including misquotation or invented facts.
- Thinking the duty of candor only forbids lies and does not require disclosure of directly adverse controlling authority.
- Giving a personal opinion on the justness of the cause or a witness's credibility in a trial argument.
- Assuming emotional appeals are always manipulative, rather than judging whether the emotion is tied to true facts.

@practice self
prompt: Hypothetical problem: A city fined Ben for operating a small food stand at a park under an ordinance that bans "commercial activity in the park without a permit." Ben had applied for a permit and was told, without reasons, that none were being issued. The court of appeals reviews de novo. Prepare a 5-minute oral argument for Ben as appellant (outline and key sentences), including a roadmap, two point headings, one concession, relief requested and one anticipated question with an answer.
hint: Think what the ordinance allows and whether a refusal to issue any permit makes the permit rule fair.
solution: Opening: "May it please the Court. I represent Ben. Review is de novo. The question is whether the city may fine someone for lacking a permit it refuses to issue to anyone. It may not, for two reasons." Point I: A permit requirement presupposes a way to obtain a permit; here the city told Ben none were issued, so the ordinance cannot be applied to punish him. Point II: Even if the city may restrict commercial activity, a fine without any lawful path to comply denies fair notice. Concession: The city may require permits in general, and Ben does not contest that. Anticipated question: "Could Ben have sold without a permit and challenged the denial?" Answer: "That is a possible route, Your Honor, but the question here is whether the penalty can be imposed on someone who tried to comply." Relief: "We ask the Court to reverse the judgment and vacate the fine."
rubric: Has a greeting, standard of review and roadmap of two reasons.
rubric: Point headings are complete claims tied to the facts supplied.
rubric: Concedes a true point and does not misstate the facts or the law.
rubric: Answers the anticipated question directly and ends with the relief requested.
