@article
For many speakers the question period is more frightening than the speech, yet it is where trust is won or lost. In law, questions are the center of the activity: a judge's questions, a witness's cross-examination and a client's worries are all questions you must answer on the spot. The skill is partly **anticipation** and partly a method.

### A five-step method

1. **Listen** to the whole question without planning your reply. Note the key words.
2. **Pause.** A two-second silence looks thoughtful and gives you time to think.
3. **Restate or clarify** if the question was long, unclear or hostile; this also lets the whole room hear it. A restatement should be neutral, not slanted to suit you.
4. **Answer directly**, giving the answer first and the reason second ("Yes, because ..."). If the true answer is "no" or "I don't know," say so.
5. **Check and close**: briefly ask "Does that answer your question?" or connect back to your main point, then move on.

Direct your answer to the whole audience, not only to the questioner, but begin with eye contact on the person who asked.

### Types of difficult questions

- **Loaded questions** assume something you reject ("When did you stop cutting corners?"). Do not accept the premise; say, "The question assumes that I did, and I did not, because ..."
- **Hostile questions** are aggressive in tone. Stay calm, thank the questioner if you can, and respond to the substance. Do not match the tone.
- **Multi-part questions** ask several things at once. Answer in order, or say, "There are two questions; let me take the first."
- **Hypotheticals** ask what would happen if the facts changed. Answer the principle, saying where your answer would change.
- **Off-topic or rambling questions**: connect them to your subject politely or offer to talk afterwards.

### Honesty

Never invent an answer. "I don't know, but I will find out and send it to you" preserves credibility; a fabricated statistic destroys it. Do not **bridge** away from a question by pretending to answer and then switching topics; audiences, and judges especially, notice. It is acceptable to acknowledge a fair criticism ("That is a real limitation") and then state why your position still holds.

### Preparing for hostile questions

Before any speech, list the five hardest questions you could be asked and write a short answer to each, in the order: concede what is true, answer the question, then restate your point. Rehearse them aloud. This preparation lowers anxiety, because few questions will be a complete surprise.

### Managing the time

Set a limit and signal it ("We have time for three more questions"). If a Q&A has 10 minutes and each question with its answer takes about 2.5 minutes, you can take $600 \div 150 = 4$ questions.

@example
title: Answering a loaded question
problem: After a talk urging a city council to fund a public defender office, a member asks: "Why do you want to waste taxpayers' money on guilty people?" Construct an answer.
step: Pause for two seconds, then state the problem neutrally: "The question assumes that those who need a public defender are guilty, and that the money is wasted."
step: Correct the premise: "In our system, people charged with a crime are presumed innocent until proven guilty, and the right to counsel in serious cases applies to everyone who cannot afford a lawyer."
step: Answer the core concern with evidence: "Wrongful convictions and repeated trials are expensive, and adequate defense reduces them. That is the use of money I am asking you to support."
step: Close and connect: "I understand the concern about cost, and that is why I have presented the costs openly."

@mistakes
- Answering a different question than the one asked.
- Becoming defensive or sarcastic with a hostile questioner.
- Inventing an answer rather than admitting you do not know.
- Accepting the premise of a loaded question by answering it as asked.

solution: Q1 Isn't this too expensive? A: There is a cost, and I do not dismiss it. A one-credit course reuses existing faculty, and it costs far less than the fees students pay by mistake. Q2 Don't students already know this? A: Some do, but surveys of the sort I cited suggest most do not, and a short course reaches everyone. Q3 Can't students look it up online? A: They can, but they do not know what to look for; teaching gives them the questions. Q4 Why not a workshop? A: A workshop is a start, and I would welcome it, but a course gives practice and feedback. Q5 Is this not the lawyer's job? A: Lawyers help after a problem; this helps students avoid one.
rubric: Provides five realistic hostile or skeptical questions.

rubric: Answers are direct and honest rather than evasive.
