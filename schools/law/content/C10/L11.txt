@article
**Oral argument** is a lawyer's live presentation to an appellate court, followed by questions from the judges. It is not a speech to a crowd: it is a conversation with a few expert listeners who have already read the briefs. Everything you have learned so far applies, but the audience changes the goal. The judges want help answering a question of law, and they use questions to test the limits of your position.

### The setting

The party who lost below and appeals is the **appellant** (in the Supreme Court, the **petitioner**); the other side is the **appellee** (or **respondent**). The appellant argues first and may **reserve rebuttal time**, a short final turn used only to answer the other side's points. The appellee has no rebuttal. Time limits are strict and vary by court: federal courts of appeals often allow about 10 to 20 minutes per side, and the Supreme Court has traditionally allowed 30 minutes per side. Check the court's rules and the clerk's notice.

Counsel begins by greeting the court ("May it please the Court") and giving their name and the party they represent. Address judges as "Your Honor" or "Judge [Name]" and never interrupt a judge: if a judge begins to speak, stop.

### Standards of review

Because the appeals court reviews a lower court's decision, your argument must fit the **standard of review**. Questions of law are reviewed **de novo**, with no deference to the lower court. Findings of fact by a judge after a trial are reviewed for **clear error**. Discretionary decisions, such as evidence rulings and many case-management orders, are reviewed for **abuse of discretion**. The appeal of a summary judgment is reviewed de novo. A good advocate states the standard early and frames the case to win under it.

### The shape of an argument

1. **Opening and roadmap** (30 to 45 seconds): the question presented, your answer in a sentence and the two or three reasons you will give.
2. **Argument by point headings**: your strongest argument first, each reason stated as a short claim, supported by the record and the law. Cite the record and key authority briefly.
3. **Conclusion**: one sentence stating the **relief** you request ("For these reasons, we ask the Court to reverse").

### Answering the bench

A question from a judge is not an interruption; it shows you what concerns the court, and it is your chance to persuade. Answer directly first (**"Yes, Your Honor, and the reason is ..."** or **"No, Your Honor"**), then explain. Do not say "I'll get to that" without a good reason. If you do not know, say so, and offer to file a letter.

**Concessions** are tools. Conceding a point that is clearly true or irrelevant to the outcome builds credibility and frees time for the points that matter. Never concede the decisive point.

### Honesty and candor

Cite the record accurately, and state the law accurately, including controlling authority against you. After *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo* (2024), which overruled *Chevron*, a federal court decides the best reading of a statute itself, and an agency's view receives only *Skidmore* respect, so an argument that asks a federal court to defer to an agency's "reasonable" reading under *Chevron* misstates current law.

### Managing time

Plan your argument to fill roughly two-thirds of your time, because questions will take the rest. If you have 15 minutes and reserve 3 for rebuttal, you have 12 minutes in opening argument; if questions consume a third of that, about 8 minutes remain for prepared material. Watch the clock, and when the light signals, conclude your sentence and sit down.

@example
title: A roadmap and a direct answer
problem: In a hypothetical case, Ana, a tenant, appeals a summary judgment for her landlord. The issue is whether the lease clause waiving her right to repairs is enforceable as a matter of law. Draft an opening and an answer to the judge's question, "Counsel, would your rule let a tenant refuse to pay any rent?"
step: Open: "May it please the Court. I am counsel for Ana, the appellant. This Court reviews summary judgment de novo. The question is whether a lease can waive the landlord's duty to keep the home habitable. It cannot, for two reasons: the duty is a matter of public policy, and the clause here would leave tenants without any remedy. I would like to reserve three minutes."
step: Direct answer: "No, Your Honor. Our rule is narrow. A tenant must still give notice and may withhold only as the law allows, and the clause here is not saved by that limit."
step: Explain: "The question is about the decisive limit. A rule that preserves a tenant's remedy for serious defects, but leaves rent due otherwise, is what we ask the Court to adopt."
step: Close: "For these reasons, we ask the Court to reverse the judgment and remand."

@mistakes
- Reading a prepared script rather than conversing with the court.
- Saying "I'll get to that" or evading a judge's question.
- Spending the entire time on the weakest arguments or a long recitation of facts.
- Stating the law inaccurately or ignoring controlling authority that cuts against you.
