@article
A speech is not complete when you have good ideas; it is complete when a particular audience can follow, accept and remember them. **Audience analysis** is the process of learning about your listeners and using what you learn to make decisions about topic, purpose, language, examples and delivery. Lawyers do this constantly: an argument to a skeptical appellate panel is built differently from a client explanation or a jury address.

### Three kinds of information

**Demographic factors** describe who the audience is: age, education, occupation, gender, cultural and religious background, group memberships. They are easy to observe but risky to over-use. Treating people as stereotypes of a category is both inaccurate and disrespectful, so use demographics to form hypotheses and then test them.

**Psychological factors** describe what the audience thinks and feels: their **attitudes** (evaluations of the topic), **beliefs** (what they hold true) and **values** (what they care about most). These predict how people will respond to your message better than age or occupation do.

**Situational factors** describe the setting: audience size, physical space, time of day, the occasion, the speaking time you have, and whether the audience chose to be there (a **voluntary** audience) or must attend (a **captive** audience).

### Audience attitude toward your topic

Classify the audience's starting point, because it sets your realistic goal.

| Audience attitude | Typical goal |
|---|---|
| **Favorable** | Reinforce belief and call for action |
| **Neutral or uninformed** | Educate first, then give a reason to care |
| **Apathetic** | Show relevance to their own lives |
| **Hostile** | Aim for modest movement: open-mindedness, not conversion |

A common error is to design a speech to convert a hostile audience fully. Persuasion is usually incremental; shifting a hostile listener a little is a win.

### Gathering information

You can learn about an audience by **asking** (surveys, a short questionnaire, a conversation with the organizer), **observing** (the setting, past events) and **inferring** carefully from demographics. A small survey of the audience is a quick tool. If 28 of 40 respondents say they favor a proposal, then $28/40 = 70\%$ favor it, and you plan for a mostly favorable group while still addressing the other 30%.

### Using the analysis

Turn each finding into a choice. A technical audience may need fewer definitions; a mixed one needs plain language and a memorable example. A late-afternoon audience needs a faster pace and more energy. A small room allows questions; a large hall needs larger gestures. If listeners are skeptical, begin with common ground and use evidence they already trust.

### The target audience

When the audience is mixed, choose a **target audience**: the subgroup you most need to reach (for example, the undecided members of a city council), while making sure the rest are not alienated.

### Ethics note

Adapting to an audience means changing emphasis and style, never changing the truth. Do not tell different groups contradictory facts.

@example
title: Analyzing an audience for a 3-minute talk
problem: You will give a 3-minute talk urging first-year students to attend a campus legal-aid clinic workshop. A short survey of 50 students shows 15 have heard of the clinic, 10 are already planning to attend, and the rest are undecided. What is a sensible analysis and goal?
step: Compute the share who have heard of the clinic: $15/50 = 30\%$. So most are uninformed, and the talk needs one sentence explaining what the clinic does.
step: The share planning to attend is $10/50 = 20\%$. The remaining $40$ students are the undecided target audience, that is $80\%$.
step: Situational factors: a short talk, a voluntary audience, probably during a break. Keep it concrete and brief, with a clear time and place.
step: Goal: move undecided listeners to attend the workshop, using a short example of a common legal problem students face, and end with one specific action.

@mistakes
- Treating demographic categories as stereotypes instead of hypotheses.
- Writing a speech for yourself rather than for the audience.
- Aiming to convert a hostile audience completely in one speech.
- Ignoring situational factors such as time of day, room size and speaking time.

choice: The audience is over 60.
choice: The audience is retired.
choice*: The audience values financial security.
choice: The audience meets in a large auditorium at 7 p.m.
hint: Psychological factors are attitudes, beliefs and values.
solution: Values are psychological. Age and retirement are demographic; the room and time are situational.

@practice num
prompt: In a quick poll before your talk, 36 of 48 listeners say they oppose your proposal. What percent oppose it?
answer: 75
check: 36 / 48 * 100
unit: percent
hint: Divide the number who oppose by the total, then multiply by 100.
solution: $36/48 = 0.75$, which is $75\%$.

@practice self
prompt: Write an audience analysis for a 3-minute talk urging classmates in a pre-law course to join a debate club. Include at least two demographic, two psychological and two situational observations (invent plausible ones and mark which are assumptions), classify their likely attitude toward the topic, and state your goal and two speech choices that follow.
hint: Sort each observation under demographic, psychological or situational before you decide on a goal.
solution: Demographic: mostly 18 to 22, mixed majors, most have never debated (assumption). Psychological: many value career preparation and fear being judged in public; they are probably neutral or apathetic toward debate (assumption from a survey I would run). Situational: 3 minutes at the start of class; voluntary but short on time; 30 students in a classroom. Attitude: neutral to apathetic. Goal: move the undecided to attend one meeting. Choices: open with the benefit they care about, such as stronger LSAT reasoning and interview poise, and offer a no-pressure first meeting as one specific action.
rubric: Separates demographic, psychological and situational information correctly.
rubric: Classifies the audience attitude and states a realistic goal for it.
rubric: Turns at least two findings into specific speech choices.
rubric: Marks assumptions as assumptions and avoids stereotyping.

@quiz num
prompt: A survey of 80 attendees finds that 52 favor your proposal and 12 are undecided. How many percent of the audience are the other responses (neither favor nor undecided), assuming everyone answered?
answer: 20
check: (80 - 52 - 12) / 80 * 100
unit: percent
hint: First find how many are neither in favor nor undecided.
solution: $80 - 52 - 12 = 16$ people, and $16/80 = 20\%$.
