title: Moot argument on a short case file
overview: Using the hypothetical case file supplied below, prepare and record (or deliver to a listener) a 10-minute argument for one side, with a written outline and a list of anticipated questions and answers. The parties, record, court and two of the authorities are invented for training and are labeled hypothetical; two authorities are real Supreme Court cases summarized in your own words.
goal: Plan and deliver a structured 10-minute oral argument with a roadmap, point headings and a request for specific relief.
goal: Anticipate and answer hard questions from the bench honestly and directly.
goal: Apply audience analysis, organization, delivery and the ethics of candor to a legal problem.
step: Read the case file and choose a side (Ruiz as appellant or the City as appellee). Write the question presented in one sentence and your answer in one sentence.
step: Analyze your audience: a three-judge panel that has read the briefs. Write four observations (what they know, what they will test, their time limit, what relief you need) and one decision each observation drives.
step: Draft a full-sentence outline: opening and roadmap, two or three point headings with record citations (use "R." and the paragraph number in the file), one strategic concession, and a conclusion stating the relief. Budget the time in minutes, including about 2 minutes reserved for rebuttal if you are the appellant, and leave room for questions.
step: Write a list of at least eight anticipated questions from the bench, including at least three hostile or loaded ones, and a two- or three-sentence answer to each that gives the direct answer first. Include one question about adverse authority and answer it candidly.
step: Rehearse aloud at least three times with a timer, standing, using a key-word outline instead of a script. Record the final run or deliver it to a listener who asks you at least three of your questions plus one that you did not anticipate.
step: Critique your recording using the delivery criteria (rate, pauses, fillers, eye contact, notes) and write down two specific changes. Re-record the opening if time permits.
deliverable: A written outline (one to two pages) and a list of at least eight anticipated questions with answers.
deliverable: A recording or the name of a listener and a short note of their feedback, plus your self-critique with a measured speaking rate and two specific changes.
rubric: The opening states the standard of review, the question presented, a one-sentence answer and a roadmap, and the conclusion states specific relief.
rubric: Arguments are tied to the record and authorities supplied, with accurate statements of the file and the law, and no invented facts or authorities presented as real.
rubric: Anticipated answers are direct, concede what is true and return to the main point; one answer deals honestly with adverse authority.
rubric: Delivery is conversational and within the time limit, and the critique uses measured criteria and proposes specific changes.
tutor: Which of my point headings is weakest, and how could I restate it as a complete claim tied to the record?
tutor: Play a skeptical judge and ask me five questions about my argument, then tell me which answers dodged the question.
tutor: What is the strongest argument for the other side, and how should I concede or answer it?
material: **Case file (entirely hypothetical): *Ruiz v. City of Marlow*.** The case, the parties, the ordinance and the court are invented for this exercise. Treat the paragraph numbers below as record citations ("R. 3" means paragraph 3).

R. 1. The City of Marlow (hypothetical) enacted Ordinance 14-07, which provides: "No person shall use any device that amplifies sound in Linden Park, except at an event sponsored by the City." The ordinance has no hours limit and no decibel limit.

R. 2. Linden Park is the City's central public park, 12 acres, with a bandstand, a playground and a central lawn. The nearest homes are about 150 yards from the central lawn. Other public sidewalks and a plaza lie outside the park, and amplified sound is permitted there between 8 a.m. and 9 p.m.

R. 3. Ana Ruiz, a volunteer organizer for a tenants' association, planned a rally of about 60 people on the central lawn on a Saturday afternoon. She planned to use a battery-powered handheld megaphone for about 30 minutes. She did not ask for permission, because the ordinance provides no permit process for non-City events.

R. 4. A park ranger told Ruiz to stop, and the rally continued without the megaphone. Ruiz said afterwards that the group at the back could not hear the speakers. The City issued a citation and a 150-dollar fine.

R. 5. The City's records show 11 complaints about amplified sound in Linden Park over the previous twelve months. Nine concerned City-sponsored concerts and two concerned a private birthday party. The City has no record of any complaint about a political rally.

R. 6. The City's Parks Director testified that the ordinance exists to keep the park peaceful for quiet recreation and to protect nearby residents. He agreed that City-sponsored concerts use powerful amplification, that the City has not tried a volume limit, and that the City never considered a permit system.

R. 7. Ruiz sued in federal court, claiming the ordinance violates the First Amendment, on its face and as applied to her. The district court granted summary judgment to the City, holding the ordinance a valid content-neutral regulation. Ruiz appeals. Review of summary judgment is de novo. Assume the parties agree that the park is a traditional public forum.

**Authorities.**

1. *Ward v. Rock Against Racism* (1989), a real Supreme Court case: a content-neutral time, place and manner regulation of speech in a public forum is valid if it is justified without reference to the content of the speech, is narrowly tailored to serve a significant government interest, and leaves open ample alternative channels for communication. Narrow tailoring does not require the least restrictive means, but the regulation may not burden substantially more speech than necessary to further the government's legitimate interests.

2. *McCullen v. Coakley* (2014), a real Supreme Court case: when a state restricts speech in a public forum, it must show that it seriously considered less intrusive alternatives to the restriction. The Court held that a Massachusetts buffer zone around abortion clinics was not narrowly tailored because the state had not shown that other tools had been tried or considered.

3. *Eastvale v. Brandt* (a hypothetical case from the hypothetical circuit): the court upheld a town's ban on amplified sound in a small residential square that had a nearby alternative plaza, noting the ban applied equally to all speakers and the town had earlier tried a volume cap that failed. This case is invented for training.

4. *Lowell Park Alliance v. Dunmore* (a hypothetical case from the same circuit): the court struck down a city's total ban on amplified sound in a large park because the city exempted its own events and had not tried any lesser measure. This case is invented for training.

Your argument may cite only the record above and the four authorities. Do not invent other cases, facts or quotations. Note that the circuit's two hypothetical cases pull in different directions, so a candid advocate will discuss both.

@review
This review follows the weeks of the course.

**Week 1, speaking anxiety.** Nervousness is normal arousal; you can reframe it as energy. Prepare, rehearse aloud and on time, visualize, and memorize your opening. **Week 2, audience analysis.** Demographic, psychological and situational factors; classify attitude (favorable, neutral, uninformed, apathetic, hostile) and set a realistic goal; choose a target audience. **Week 3, research.** Primary and secondary sources; test authority, accuracy, currency and purpose; examples, statistics, testimony, analogies; check statistics (percent change, mean versus median); give oral citations. **Week 4, organization.** Introduction (attention, relevance, credibility, thesis, preview), three main points with transitions, conclusion; organizational patterns; outline notation and timing. **Week 5, language.** Oral style is short, concrete and repetitive; devices (anaphora, parallelism, antithesis, tricolon, metaphor); script length equals rate times minutes. **Week 6, delivery.** Extemporaneous delivery; volume, rate, pitch, pauses; eye contact, gesture; key-word notes; speaking rate equals words divided by minutes.

**Week 7, classical rhetoric.** Ethos, pathos, logos; enthymeme; deliberative, forensic, epideictic; five canons; kairos. **Week 8, persuasive structures.** Propositions of fact, value and policy; Monroe's motivated sequence (attention, need, satisfaction, visualization, action); Toulmin's claim, grounds, warrant, backing, qualifier, rebuttal. **Week 9, debate.** Burden of proof, clash, stasis; four-step refutation; fallacies. **Week 10, questions.** Listen, pause, restate, answer directly, close; loaded, hostile and multi-part questions; honesty about what you do not know. **Week 11, oral argument.** Appellant and appellee, rebuttal, standards of review (de novo, clear error, abuse of discretion), roadmap, answering the bench, concessions, time; after *Loper Bright* (2024), no *Chevron* deference. **Week 12, ethics.** Persuasion versus manipulation; Model Rules 3.3 (candor, adverse controlling authority), 3.4, 3.5, 4.1, 8.4(c).

**Self-check.** Can you state your thesis in one sentence? Can you name your audience's attitude and your goal? Can you time a speech from its word count? Can you refute an argument in four steps? Can you answer a loaded question without accepting its premise? Can you open an oral argument with the standard, the question and a roadmap?

**Common errors.** Reading a script; silent rehearsal; topics instead of theses; misleading statistics; naming a fallacy without explaining it; accepting the premise of a loaded question; evading a judge's question; ignoring adverse authority; using Chevron as current law.

Which description of this opening is most accurate?
choice: Weak, because counsel should begin with a long statement of the facts before any answer.
choice*: Strong, because it greets the court, states the standard of review, the question and a one-sentence answer, and previews the reasons.
choice: Improper, because counsel may not state the standard of review in argument.
choice: Improper, because counsel must reserve rebuttal time before saying anything else.
hint: Compare the opening with the five parts of an appellate opening taught in the course.
solution: A good opening greets the court, states the standard of review, gives the question presented, answers in a sentence and previews the reasons. Reserving rebuttal can be done at the start, but it is not a prerequisite, and stating the standard is proper.

Coakley (2014) (the government must show it seriously considered less intrusive alternatives); and the hypothetical Eastvale v. Brandt (the town had first tried a volume cap that failed). Draft a 90-second rebuttal for Ruiz (appellant) responding to this point by the City: "The ordinance is content-neutral, there are alternatives outside the park, and the ordinance does not need to be the least restrictive means." Use the four-step refutation, make one honest concession and cite the record by paragraph.
hint: Concede what Ward allows, then use the City's own testimony about alternatives it never tried.
solution: "Three points in rebuttal. First, the City says the ordinance is content-neutral and need not be least restrictive. I agree that Ward does not demand the least restrictive means. But Ward does bar burdening substantially more speech than necessary, and McCullen asks whether the City seriously considered less intrusive tools. The Parks Director admitted the City never tried a volume limit and never considered a permit system (R. 6). Second, the City points to the plaza and sidewalks. But the people at the back of Ms. Ruiz's group could not hear the speakers on the central lawn without the megaphone (R. 4), and the City exempts its own concerts, which caused nine of eleven complaints (R. 5). Third, the City relies on Eastvale, but there the town had tried a cap first; here the City tried nothing. For these reasons we ask the Court to reverse."

rubric: Cites the record paragraphs correctly (R. 4, R. 5, R. 6).
