@article
Every group has norms, and every group responds when they are broken. **Social control** is the set of mechanisms by which a society encourages conformity and discourages deviance.

### Formal and informal control

**Informal social control** is enforced by ordinary people through disapproval, gossip, praise, shaming or exclusion: a friend's frown, a family rule, a workplace norm. **Formal social control** is carried out by organizations with official authority, such as police, courts, regulators and licensing boards, through written rules and defined penalties. A **sanction** is a reward or punishment that enforces a norm. Positive sanctions reward conformity (a promotion, a thank-you); negative sanctions punish deviance (a fine, a reprimand, a prison term).

The two work together. Most people obey most laws mainly because of informal controls (conscience, relationships, reputation) rather than fear of formal punishment. Formal control tends to appear when informal control fails or when the stakes are high. Informal control can also be harsh or discriminatory, and formal control can be absent where it is most needed.

### What counts as deviance?

**Deviance** is behavior that violates a group's norms. It is relative: what is deviant varies by time, place and audience. Deviance is broader than crime. Some crimes are widely tolerated (minor traffic violations), and some deviance is lawful (extreme dress). Sociologists ask why norms are what they are and why some people are labeled deviant.

### Theories of deviance

- **Strain theory:** deviance can arise when a society promotes goals (such as financial success) but offers unequal legitimate means to reach them, pushing some people toward innovation, withdrawal or rebellion.
- **Differential association:** deviance is learned in interaction. People learn techniques and attitudes toward law from the groups they spend time with; a person offends when exposure to attitudes favorable to violation outweighs exposure to unfavorable ones.
- **Labeling theory:** deviance is partly a result of how others respond. Being labeled a deviant can change how others treat a person and how the person sees himself or herself, which may encourage further deviance (secondary deviance). Critics point out that behavior comes first and that labeling cannot explain every pattern.
- **Control theory:** the question is why people conform. Strong bonds to family, school and work (attachment, commitment, involvement and belief) restrain deviance.

These theories are not mutually exclusive, and each has partial empirical support.

### Deterrence

**Deterrence** assumes people weigh costs and benefits. A simple model compares the gain from an act with the expected penalty, which is the probability of being sanctioned multiplied by the size of the sanction. Reviews of the research generally find that the *certainty* of being caught is more strongly associated with lower offending than the *severity* of the penalty, although the findings vary by offense and population and are hard to measure. People often do not know the penalties, and many offenses are impulsive or committed under the influence. Deterrence research is difficult because enforcement and crime affect each other, so correlations are easy to misread.

### The broken windows idea

A popular policy idea holds that visible disorder invites more serious crime, so quickly addressing minor offenses prevents bigger ones. Evidence on this is contested: some studies find that targeted attention to disorder is associated with less crime, while others find little effect or argue that the effects come from other features of policing, and critics raise concerns about fairness and community relations. Treat it as an open question rather than settled.

@example
title: Expected penalty and a rational offender
problem: In a hypothetical model, an offender gains \$1,000 from an act. If caught (probability 0.20) the fine is \$3,000. Another policy lowers the fine to \$1,500 but raises the chance of being caught to 0.50. Which policy has the larger expected penalty, and what does this suggest about certainty and severity?
step: Expected penalty equals probability times fine. Policy A: 0.20 multiplied by \$3,000 is \$600.
step: Policy B: 0.50 multiplied by \$1,500 is \$750.
step: Policy B has the larger expected penalty even though its fine is half as large. Under this simple model, with gain \$1,000, Policy A leaves a net gain of \$400 while Policy B leaves only \$250, so B deters more even though neither removes the gain entirely.
step: This is invented data meant only to show the logic. Real people may not compute probabilities this way, so the model is a starting point for research, not a description of everyone.

@mistakes
- Equating deviance with crime; many deviant acts are lawful and some crimes are widely tolerated.
- Assuming formal control is the main reason people obey the law, ignoring informal control.
- Claiming that research proves harsher penalties always deter more, when the evidence points more to certainty than to severity.
