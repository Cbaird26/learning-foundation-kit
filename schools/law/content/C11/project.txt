title: Research proposal on a legal-system question
overview: You will design a study, with invented data allowed, on a question such as lineup procedures or plain-language jury instructions. The proposal includes a literature summary, methods, expected results and ethical safeguards, in about 1,800 words.
goal: Turn a legal-system question into a testable hypothesis with a sound design.
goal: Summarize what research suggests while being cautious about its limits.
goal: Plan ethical safeguards and honest reporting of results.
step: Choose one of the two sample questions in the material below, or your own approved question, and state it as a testable hypothesis.
step: Write a literature summary of about 400 words using the background notes, your course readings and your own library search; do not invent studies or statistics, and label anything hypothetical.
step: Write the methods: participants, design (random assignment or another design), procedure, measures and analysis.
step: Write the expected results using the hypothetical pilot data, labeled as invented, and explain what each pattern would mean.
step: Discuss threats to validity (selection, confounding, measurement, generalizability) and limits.
step: Describe ethical safeguards: consent, confidentiality, debriefing, review board, and fair treatment of participants.
step: Revise to about 1,800 words and check that every claim of fact is supported or labeled.
material: **Option A background notes (lineup procedures).** Research in psychology suggests that double-blind administration, fair fillers and neutral instructions can reduce suggestion in lineups, and that early confidence is more informative than later confidence. Studies differ on how large effects are and on whether sequential or simultaneous presentation is better. Use your own sources for details.
material: **Option B background notes (plain-language jury instructions).** Studies of jury instructions suggest that comprehension of standard instructions is often limited and that revised plain-language versions can improve comprehension, with less clear effects on verdicts. Use your own sources for details.
material: **Hypothetical pilot data (invented).** In an invented pilot, 100 mock witnesses viewed culprit-absent lineups: 50 with a non-blind administrator and 50 with a double-blind administrator. False identifications: 20 of 50 in the non-blind group and 12 of 50 in the double-blind group. For Option B, an invented pilot gave 40 mock jurors a standard instruction and 40 a plain-language one; mean quiz scores out of 10 were 5.8 and 7.1.
deliverable: A proposal of about 1,800 words with sections for hypothesis, literature summary, methods, expected results, limits and ethics.
deliverable: A one-paragraph note listing which numbers are invented.
rubric: The hypothesis is testable and the design can answer it, with a suitable comparison group.
rubric: The literature summary is accurate, cautious and does not invent studies.
rubric: Measures and analysis match the hypothesis.
rubric: Threats to validity are identified honestly.
rubric: Ethical safeguards are specific and appropriate.
safety: Use only invented or properly anonymized data and do not recruit real participants without review board approval.
tutor: What would be a good comparison group for my hypothesis, and why?
tutor: What threats to validity does my design leave open?
tutor: How should I phrase my results so I do not overclaim?

### Key ideas by week

1. **Law as institution:** functions, perspectives (functionalism, conflict, interactionism), law in books versus law in action.
2. **Social control and deviance:** formal and informal control, theories of deviance, deterrence (certainty over severity, with caution).
3. **Memory:** reconstructive memory, estimator and system variables, lineup reforms, *Manson* and *Perry*.
4. **Interrogation:** voluntariness, *Miranda*, types and risk factors of false confession; three factors reducing eyewitness accuracy.
5. **Judgment and bias:** heuristics, hindsight, confirmation, base rates, debiasing.
6. **Juries:** size, unanimity (*Ramos*), *Batson*, conformity and polarization.
7. **Courts and profession:** dual system, workgroup, pleas, *Gideon*, regulation.
8. **Policing:** legitimacy, procedural justice, *Terry*, force, evidence on strategies.
9. **Access to justice:** repeat players, *Lassiter*, *Turner*, legal aid.
10. **Punishment:** purposes, Eighth Amendment cases, recidivism, evidence on counsel.
11. **Social change:** implementation gaps, backlash, causation.
12. **Evaluation:** designs, validity, ethics.

### Self-check

- Can you explain each design and its main weakness?
- Can you separate percentage points from percent change?
- Can you state the key legal rule and a caution for each research area?

### Common errors

- Treating correlation as causation and one study as settled.
- Overstating false-confession or implicit-bias research.
- Presenting contested policy questions as having one side.
- Forgetting that survey findings show association.

@exam num
prompt: Hypothetical lineups: 300 culprit-absent lineups were run with a non-blind administrator, and 90 produced an identification of someone. 300 were run double-blind, and 60 produced an identification. By how many percentage points did the false identification rate fall under double-blind administration? (Invented data.)
answer: 10
check: (90 / 300 - 60 / 300) * 100
unit: percentage points
hint: Find each group's rate.
hint: Subtract the second rate from the first.
solution: 90/300 is 30 percent, 60/300 is 20 percent, and the fall is 10 percentage points. Random assignment would be needed to attribute it to the procedure.

@exam mc
prompt: A study of 200 defendants finds that those who had lawyers received lighter sentences than those who did not. A commentator says lawyers cause lighter sentences. Which critique is strongest?
choice: Lawyers cannot affect sentences.
choice*: Defendants with lawyers may differ in case strength and resources, so the comparison may be confounded.
choice: Sentences cannot be measured.
choice: Only studies of 1,000 or more people are valid.
hint: Think about how people end up with lawyers.
solution: Selection and confounding can explain the difference without a causal effect of lawyers; a stronger design would use random or as-if random assignment.

@exam self
prompt: Write a 200-word plan to evaluate whether a new lineup procedure reduces false identifications in a police department. Include the design, the measure, one threat to validity and one ethical safeguard.
hint: Consider random assignment of lineups to procedure and a measure of identification outcomes.
solution: Randomly assign lineups conducted by participating officers to double-blind or standard procedure, recording all instructions and the witness's confidence. Because truth is often unknown in real cases, use outcomes such as filler identifications (known errors) and compare their rate between conditions, supplemented by a lab study with culprit-absent lineups. A threat is that officers might not follow assignments or that cases differ; check compliance and balance case features. Safeguards: protect witness and suspect confidentiality, obtain agency approval and ethics review, and ensure no one's legal rights are reduced.
rubric: Uses a comparison with random assignment or another credible design.
rubric: Names an outcome measure that can be observed.
rubric: Identifies a threat to validity.
rubric: Includes an ethical safeguard.
