@article
Most people first meet law as a list of rules. A sociologist or a social psychologist asks a different question: what is law *doing* in a society, and how do real people experience it? This course uses that second lens, and this first week sets it up.

### What is a social institution?

A **social institution** is a lasting set of norms, roles and organizations that a society uses to meet a basic need: family, education, religion, the economy, government. Law fits the definition. It has norms (rules and procedures), roles (judge, lawyer, juror, officer, litigant) and organizations (courts, agencies, police departments, bar associations). It also has a distinctive feature: it claims the authority to back its commands with organized force, and most people accept that claim most of the time.

### What law is said to do

Scholars usually list several functions. Law **resolves disputes** by offering a forum other than private revenge. It **controls behavior** by threatening sanctions. It **expresses and reinforces norms**, so a statute can signal what a community condemns. It **allocates power and resources**, deciding who may own property or hold office. And it can **channel social change**, sometimes leading it and sometimes following it. These functions can conflict: a rule that controls behavior well may resolve disputes poorly.

### Three sociological perspectives

- **Functionalism** asks how law helps society hold together. It treats law as a response to a need for order and shared expectations. A well-known version contrasts *repressive* law (punishment, typical of small, similar communities) with *restitutive* law (compensation and contracts, typical of complex societies with specialized roles).
- **Conflict theory** asks whose interests law serves. It stresses that groups with more wealth or power can shape which acts are crimes, how rules are enforced and who can afford a lawyer. Critics reply that this overstates how often law simply mirrors elite interests, since laws also protect weaker groups and have been won through reform movements.
- **Symbolic interactionism** studies the meanings people attach to law in everyday encounters: how a traffic stop, a courtroom or a form is understood by those involved. It focuses on small-scale interaction rather than whole-society structure.

No single perspective is "correct". Each highlights questions the others can miss.

### Law in the books and law in action

**Law in the books** is what statutes, rules and opinions say. **Law in action** is what officials and citizens actually do. The gap can be wide: a speed limit of 55 may be enforced only above 65; a right to counsel on paper may be hollow if no one explains it. Much of this course is about measuring that gap with evidence rather than assuming it.

### Kinds of law

**Criminal law** defines offenses against the public and is enforced by the state. **Civil law** governs disputes between private parties (contracts, torts, family matters). **Public law** covers constitutions and the powers and duties of government. Most legal activity that ordinary people see is not a dramatic criminal trial: in the United States the vast majority of cases in state courts are traffic, small-claims, family and other high-volume matters, and most cases that are filed end without a trial.

### Evidence, not slogans

Claims such as "the law deters crime" or "the law reflects our values" are empirical claims about a society. Throughout the course you will ask what evidence supports them, how strong that evidence is, and what it cannot show.

@example
title: Reading one practice through three perspectives
problem: A city passes an ordinance banning sleeping in public parks. Describe how each of the three perspectives would frame the ordinance.
step: Functionalism asks what need the ordinance serves: it might be framed as protecting shared use of parks and public order, a rule that coordinates expectations for everyone.
step: Conflict theory asks who bears the burden: people without housing are most affected and have the least power to shape the rule, so the ordinance may be read as reflecting the interests of better-off residents. A reply is that many residents of all incomes use and value the parks.
step: Interactionism asks about meaning in practice: how an officer's tone, a warning versus a citation, and the person's understanding of the encounter shape what the ordinance means on the ground.
step: Each framing yields a different empirical question (order outcomes, who is cited, how encounters unfold), so together they give a fuller picture than any one alone.

@mistakes
- Treating law in the books as a description of what actually happens, without checking enforcement and outcomes.
- Saying a perspective is "right" or "wrong" instead of asking what question it is best suited to answer.
- Assuming most legal disputes end in a trial, when most civil and criminal cases are resolved earlier.
