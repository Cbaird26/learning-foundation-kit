@article
Legal decisions are made by people. Jurors weigh testimony, judges set sentences, officers decide whom to stop, prosecutors decide whom to charge. Psychology offers a systematic account of how human judgment works and where it goes predictably astray.

### Two modes of thinking

A popular framework distinguishes **System 1** (fast, automatic, intuitive) from **System 2** (slow, deliberate, effortful). Intuitive thinking is usually efficient, but it relies on **heuristics**, mental shortcuts that can produce systematic errors called **biases**. A bias here is a predictable tendency, not necessarily a moral failing or an intention to discriminate.

### Common heuristics and biases

- **Availability:** judging how likely something is by how easily examples come to mind. A vivid news story about a rare crime can make it seem common.
- **Representativeness:** judging by resemblance to a stereotype while ignoring base rates, the underlying frequency of the category. This feeds **base-rate neglect**.
- **Anchoring:** estimates are pulled toward an initial number, even an arbitrary one. Experiments have found that a mentioned figure (a demand for damages, a sentencing recommendation) can influence judgments, although the size of the effect varies.
- **Hindsight bias:** after learning an outcome, people overestimate how predictable it was. This matters for negligence and foreseeability, because a jury knows the harm occurred.
- **Confirmation bias:** seeking and favoring information that fits a belief already held. Investigators who settle early on a suspect may interpret ambiguous facts as confirming guilt, sometimes called tunnel vision.
- **Outcome bias:** judging the quality of a decision by how it turned out.
- **Fundamental attribution error:** explaining others' behavior by their character while underweighting the situation.

### Group-based bias

**In-group bias** is favoring members of one's own group. **Implicit bias** refers to associations that can influence judgments without conscious endorsement, often measured with reaction-time tasks. This is a contested area. Researchers disagree about how well such tests predict behavior in real settings, and about how stable the scores are. At the same time, studies of decisions in policing, charging and sentencing find disparities across groups in many settings; researchers disagree about how much is explained by bias, by differences in circumstances and by other factors. A careful statement is: disparities exist, causes are debated, and several mechanisms may operate together.

### Base rates

Suppose a screening test is accurate but the condition it detects is rare. Most positives may then be false. The reasoning uses **Bayes' rule**: the probability that a positive result is real equals true positives divided by all positives (true plus false). Failing to account for base rates leads fact-finders to overvalue forensic or screening evidence, an error sometimes called the **prosecutor's fallacy** when a small chance of a random match is confused with a small chance of innocence.

### Reducing bias

No single fix removes bias, but some practices help. **Consider-the-opposite** instructions ask decision-makers to generate reasons a judgment could be wrong. **Blind review** and **sequential unmasking** shield forensic examiners from irrelevant case information. **Structured decision tools** (checklists, written reasons) reduce reliance on intuition. **Diverse decision-making groups** can surface other viewpoints. Evidence on training aimed at implicit bias is mixed: awareness alone does not reliably change behavior. Designing the decision process is often more effective than urging individuals to try harder.

@example
title: A base-rate problem in forensic screening
problem: In a hypothetical lab, 1,000 evidence items are screened. 50 are truly contaminated. The test flags 90 percent of contaminated items and wrongly flags 10 percent of clean items. If an item is flagged, what is the probability it is truly contaminated? (Invented data.)
step: True positives: 90 percent of the 50 contaminated items is 45.
step: Clean items: 1,000 minus 50 is 950. False positives: 10 percent of 950 is 95.
step: All flagged items: 45 plus 95 is 140.
step: Probability contaminated given a flag: 45 divided by 140, which is 9/28, about 32 percent. Even with an apparently accurate test, most flags are false because contamination is rare.

@mistakes
- Treating bias as a character flaw or proof of intent rather than a systematic tendency of human judgment.
- Reading a high test accuracy as meaning that most positives are true, ignoring the base rate.
- Overstating what implicit bias measures can show about an individual.
- Assuming awareness training alone eliminates bias.

@practice self
prompt: In 150 to 200 words, explain why hindsight bias is a problem when jurors decide whether a defendant's conduct was negligent, and suggest two ways a court or lawyer could reduce its effect.
hint: Think about what jurors know that the defendant did not know at the time.
solution: Negligence asks whether the defendant acted reasonably given what was known beforehand. Jurors know the harm occurred, and hindsight bias makes them overestimate how predictable it was, which can make the conduct look more careless. Ways to reduce this include instructing jurors to judge from the viewpoint of the time, focusing the presentation of evidence on what information was available then, asking jurors to list reasons the harm might not have occurred, and bifurcating the trial so some issues are decided without outcome details. Research suggests such measures help only partly.
