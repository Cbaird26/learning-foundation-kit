@article
Punishment is the most visible way the law imposes consequences. Sociology and psychology ask what it is for and what it does.

### The purposes of punishment

- **Retribution:** punishment is deserved; the penalty should be proportionate to blame.
- **Deterrence:** punishment discourages the offender (specific) and others (general). As Week 2 showed, certainty tends to matter more than severity in research.
- **Incapacitation:** offenders cannot offend in the community while confined; the benefit shrinks if offenders are replaced or age out of crime.
- **Rehabilitation:** programs reduce future offending by treating causes.
- **Restoration:** repairing harm through restitution or victim-offender processes.

Theories conflict in practice: a long sentence may serve retribution and incapacitation while doing little for rehabilitation.

### Eighth Amendment limits

The Eighth Amendment bars cruel and unusual punishments and excessive fines. Key cases: *Gregg v. Georgia* (1976) allowed capital punishment under guided procedures; *Atkins v. Virginia* (2002) bars executing people with intellectual disability; *Roper v. Simmons* (2005) bars the death penalty for crimes committed under 18; *Graham v. Florida* (2010) bars life without parole for juveniles in non-homicide cases; *Miller v. Alabama* (2012) bars mandatory life without parole for juveniles, and *Jones v. Mississippi* (2021) held that a sentencer need not make a separate finding of permanent incorrigibility. The Court has been deferential on prison length: *Harmelin v. Michigan* (1991) and *Ewing v. California* (2003) upheld very long sentences. *City of Grants Pass v. Johnson* (2024) held that enforcing generally applicable public-camping bans is not cruel and unusual punishment. *Timbs v. Indiana* (2019) applied the Excessive Fines Clause to the states.

### Corrections in the United States

The United States has a high incarceration rate compared with other wealthy countries. People disagree about why and what to do. Descriptive facts include racial disparities in imprisonment and large numbers held in local jails before trial; explanations are debated and include differences in offending, enforcement patterns and sentencing laws.

### What the evidence suggests

- **Incapacitation** prevents some crime, with diminishing returns as more lower-risk people are confined.
- **Prison and reoffending:** studies disagree; some find confinement increases later offending, others find little effect, and design matters.
- **Recidivism** depends on definition and follow-up. A majority of people released from state prison are rearrested within several years, but rearrest is not reconviction.
- **Rehabilitation:** evaluations of cognitive-behavioral and education programs find modest reductions in reoffending on average, with large variation in quality.

### Arguments

Supporters of longer sentences and stricter enforcement emphasize public safety, victims' interests and moral desert. Supporters of reducing incarceration emphasize costs, collateral consequences for families and communities, and limited marginal benefits. Evidence helps assess trade-offs but cannot decide the values at stake.

### Deliverable: evidence on access to counsel in civil cases

To summarize the evidence, state what the studies compare (represented and unrepresented litigants), what outcomes they measure (eviction, benefits, protective orders), what designs they use (randomized assignment of offers of help, quasi-experiments, observational comparisons), and what they find: effects are sometimes large and sometimes small, and depend on case type and the kind of help. Then name limits: selection bias in observational studies, differences between full representation and advice, and generalizability.

step: Restitution serves restoration by repairing the victim's loss.
step: Counseling aims at rehabilitation.

@mistakes
- Treating one purpose of punishment as the only legitimate one.
- Equating rearrest with reconviction or reimprisonment.
- Assuming harsher sentences always deter more.
- Stating that the Eighth Amendment forbids all long sentences, when the Court has upheld very long ones.
