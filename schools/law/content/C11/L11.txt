@article
Does law change society, or does society change law? Both, and the interaction is what this lesson studies.

### Law as cause and consequence

Law can **lead** change by prohibiting discrimination, setting standards or creating institutions; it can **follow** change by recognizing shifts in norms and technology. A sociological idea called **cultural lag** notes that law and institutions often adapt more slowly than technology and everyday practice. Social movements use **legal mobilization**: litigation, lobbying and public campaigns that frame demands as rights.

### Examples to study

- *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954) held that racial segregation in public schools violated equal protection. Scholars debate how much court rulings alone changed school integration: widespread change came after the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and federal enforcement, and some argue that political action mattered more than the ruling, while others credit the ruling with shifting norms and enabling movements.
- *Loving v. Virginia* (1967) struck down bans on interracial marriage; it came when public opinion was changing, and it is often used to illustrate courts acting with and after social change.
- *Obergefell v. Hodges* (2015) recognized a constitutional right to same-sex marriage. The majority reasoned from liberty and equality under the Fourteenth Amendment; dissenters argued the question should be decided through the democratic process. Scholars study its effects on attitudes and on opposition, and findings are mixed.
- State marijuana laws illustrate **federalism and legal conflict**: states changed their laws while federal law still prohibits the drug (*Gonzales v. Raich* (2005) upheld federal power to regulate homegrown marijuana).
- Tobacco and seat-belt laws illustrate how law, enforcement and shifting norms can reinforce one another, with compliance rising over time in many places.

### Why legal change does not simply "work"

- **Implementation gap:** a rule on paper may not be followed or enforced; compliance depends on resources, monitoring and acceptance.
- **Symbolic effects:** laws may change expressed norms or beliefs about what is acceptable even before behavior changes.
- **Backlash:** a visible legal change may mobilize opponents, though whether backlash cancels the change is debated and varies by case.
- **Unintended effects:** laws may produce responses that lawmakers did not foresee, such as shifting conduct to an unregulated area.
- **Diffusion:** policies spread among states as officials copy neighbors, influenced by interest groups and public opinion.

### Courts and public opinion

Studies suggest decisions of courts tend to track broad public opinion over time, partly because of how judges are appointed and partly because of social context; researchers disagree about how strong the relationship is and how to measure it. Courts can also affect opinion, though effects are typically modest.

### Thinking about causation

Claims such as "the ruling caused the change" require a **counterfactual**: what would have happened without the ruling? Because that cannot be observed, researchers compare places and times, use natural experiments and examine timing. Trends before the law and other events at the same time are common alternative explanations.

@example
title: Percentage points versus percent change
problem: In a hypothetical survey, support for a policy was 45 percent before a court decision and 58 percent after. By how many percentage points did support rise, and what can and cannot be concluded?
step: Percentage points: 58 minus 45 is 13 points.
step: The relative change is 13 divided by 45, about 29 percent; keep points and percent distinct.
step: The data show a change but not that the decision caused it; support might already have been rising.
step: A better design would compare to a similar group not exposed to the decision, or examine the trend before the decision.

@mistakes
- Assuming a legal change automatically changes behavior.
- Treating timing alone as proof of causation.
- Confusing percentage points with percent change.
- Presenting backlash or success as settled for contested rulings, when scholars disagree.

@practice self
prompt: Explain in 150 to 200 words how you would decide whether a court decision caused a change in public attitudes. Include one alternative explanation and one kind of evidence you would look for.
hint: State what you would compare and name something else that could explain the change.
solution: I would first define the attitude measure and compare surveys before and after the decision, checking whether attitudes were already trending. An alternative explanation is that other events or a broader trend caused the change. I would look for a comparison group, such as states not affected by the decision, and test whether change was larger where the decision applied. Even then, I would acknowledge limits: surveys differ in wording, and the decision might have both caused and reflected the change.
