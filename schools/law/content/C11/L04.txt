@article
Confessions are among the most persuasive kinds of evidence. Jurors tend to find them compelling, and a person who confesses is often not tested as hard by later evidence. That is why researchers worry about confessions that are false, which do occur: some DNA exonerations involve people who had confessed.

### Interrogation methods

Police interrogations differ in style. An **accusatory** style is built around confronting a suspect believed to be guilty, and may use isolation, repeated denial of the suspect's explanations and persuasion. An **information-gathering** style seeks the suspect's account through open questions and then tests it against evidence; some countries train officers this way. In the United States, police may lawfully use deception such as claiming to have evidence they lack (*Frazier v. Cupp* (1969)), although the tactic is controversial and some states or jurisdictions restrict it, especially for juveniles.

Two themes researchers study are **maximization** (exaggerating the strength of the evidence and the consequences of denial) and **minimization** (suggesting the offense is understandable or that confessing will lead to leniency). Minimization can implicitly communicate a benefit of confessing. Supporters of accusatory methods argue they elicit true confessions that solve serious crimes, and researchers cannot easily measure how often confessions are false because the truth is often unknown. Both points are part of an unresolved debate.

### Types of false confession

- **Voluntary:** a person confesses without pressure, for reasons such as seeking attention or protecting someone.
- **Compliant:** a person confesses to escape the interrogation or gain a perceived benefit while knowing they are innocent.
- **Internalized:** a vulnerable person comes to believe they may have committed the act, often after being told that evidence proves it.

### Risk factors

Research links false confessions to youth, intellectual disability, mental illness, sleep deprivation or intoxication, a long interrogation, false-evidence ploys and minimization, and also to an innocent person's belief that the truth will come out ("I have nothing to hide"), which may lead them to waive rights. Details in a confession that match the crime may come from the police themselves (**contamination**) rather than from the suspect's knowledge, which is why researchers recommend checking for details only the culprit would know and whether they were disclosed earlier.

### Law

Two constitutional sources govern. First, due process requires that a confession be **voluntary**; *Colorado v. Connelly* (1986) held that coercive police activity is a necessary predicate for finding a confession involuntary under the Due Process Clause. Second, *Miranda v. Arizona* (1966) requires warnings (right to remain silent, that statements can be used, right to counsel, and appointed counsel if indigent) before custodial interrogation, and *Dickerson v. United States* (2000) held that *Miranda* is a constitutional rule that Congress cannot overrule by statute. A waiver must be knowing, intelligent and voluntary. In *Berghuis v. Thompkins* (2010), the Court held that a suspect must invoke the right to silence unambiguously; *Vega v. Tekoh* (2022) held that a *Miranda* violation alone does not support a § 1983 damages claim. An involuntary confession admitted at trial can be harmless error under *Arizona v. Fulminante* (1991). A growing number of states and many departments require **electronic recording** of custodial interrogations, at least for serious offenses, so a judge or jury can see the whole process rather than only the final statement.

### How jurors respond

Mock-jury studies suggest people tend to believe confessions even when they were obtained through pressure, and that jurors often underestimate the pressures of interrogation. Expert testimony and recording are among the responses discussed, but evidence on how well they fix this is limited.

### Linking back: eyewitness accuracy

The block deliverable for weeks 3 and 4 asks you to explain three factors that reduce eyewitness accuracy. For example: a witness under high stress and a short, poorly lit view, cross-race identification, and postevent information or suggestive lineups all raise the risk of error, and each is a different mechanism (attention and encoding, face-recognition expertise, and memory updating or procedure).

title: Analyzing a hypothetical interrogation
problem: After eleven hours, a tired 16-year-old is told, falsely, that a polygraph shows he failed. The detective says the court will be lenient with someone who shows remorse. The teen signs a statement. List the risk factors and the legal question.
step: Risk factors: youth, fatigue and the long duration, a false-evidence ploy (the invented polygraph result) and minimization (suggested leniency).
step: Legal questions: Was the statement voluntary under due process, which looks at the totality of circumstances including age and the police conduct? If it was custodial, were *Miranda* warnings given and was the waiver knowing, intelligent and voluntary?
step: Evidence questions: Was the interrogation recorded, and does the statement contain details that only the culprit could know, and were those details possibly supplied by the police?
step: Caution: these facts show risk, not proof that the statement is false.

@mistakes
- Saying police may never lie to suspects; deception about evidence is generally permitted in the United States, though some places limit it.
- Assuming a confession is involuntary whenever the suspect felt pressure, even though *Connelly* requires coercive police activity.
- Treating false confessions as common or as impossible; research shows they occur but cannot give a precise base rate.
