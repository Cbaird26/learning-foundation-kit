@article
Law promises equal treatment, but using law takes money, time, knowledge and confidence. Inequality in these resources means that people experience the legal system very differently.

### Inequality and the legal system

Sociologists describe a pattern: **repeat players** (insurers, landlords, creditors, the government) have experience, lawyers and the ability to shape rules over time, while **one-shotters** (an individual tenant or debtor) face the system occasionally and often without help. Researchers studying "who wins" in this way suggest that repeat players tend to have advantages, though the size of those advantages depends on the setting.

### The justice gap

Surveys of low-income households, including studies commissioned by the federal legal aid funder, have found that many have experienced civil legal problems involving housing, family, debt, health, or benefits, and that most of those problems received little or no legal help. Findings depend on how problems are defined and what households report, and the figures vary by study, but the general conclusion that civil legal needs go largely unmet is widely accepted. Reasons include cost, the sense that a problem is not a "legal" problem, lack of nearby services and limited legal aid funding.

### The legal rules on counsel in civil cases

There is no general constitutional right to appointed counsel in civil cases.

- *Lassiter v. Department of Social Services* (1981): the Court presumed that appointed counsel is required only where the person may lose physical liberty, and held that for termination of parental rights due process requires a case-by-case determination of whether counsel is needed.
- *Turner v. Rogers* (2011): a person facing jail for civil contempt for not paying child support does not automatically have a right to appointed counsel, but the state must provide substitute procedural safeguards, such as notice that ability to pay is critical and a fair opportunity to show inability to pay.
- *Gideon* (1963) covers criminal felony cases and *Argersinger* (1972) extends to cases with actual imprisonment, but neither applies to ordinary civil matters.

States and localities can provide more. Several cities and a few states have enacted **right-to-counsel** programs for tenants facing eviction, and many courts provide representation in some child-welfare or guardianship matters.

### Legal aid

The **Legal Services Corporation (LSC)**, created by Congress in 1974, funds local legal aid programs for low-income people in civil matters, supplemented by state funds, bar foundations, IOLTA interest and private donations. LSC funding carries restrictions on the kinds of cases and clients that grantees may take. Programs usually must turn away many eligible applicants for lack of capacity.

### Approaches to expanding access

- **Right to counsel in high-stakes civil matters** (such as eviction): supporters argue it protects basic needs and helps courts function; opponents cite cost and question whether representation always changes outcomes.
- **Limited-scope representation ("unbundling")**: a lawyer handles only part of a matter.
- **Self-help resources and court navigators:** plain-language forms, online document assembly, help centers.
- **Regulatory reform:** allowing licensed paraprofessionals or non-lawyer ownership to lower costs; a few states such as Utah and Arizona have experimented, and evidence is still emerging on quality and access.
- **Pro bono** services by lawyers, which the Model Rules encourage but do not require.

### What the evidence suggests

Studies on whether lawyers change civil outcomes are mixed and context-specific: some randomized or quasi-experimental studies of representation in housing or benefits cases find that represented litigants do substantially better, while others find small or no differences, depending on the type of case, the kind of help (full representation versus advice) and how outcomes are measured. Selection effects make non-randomized comparisons unreliable, because people who obtain lawyers may differ from those who do not.

problem: A hypothetical legal aid office has 20 lawyers, each able to close 150 cases a year. It receives 5,000 requests from eligible people. What percentage of the requests can it serve, and what does this say about the justice gap?
step: Capacity: 20 times 150 is 3,000 cases.
step: Share served: 3,000 divided by 5,000 is 0.6, or 60 percent.
step: The remaining 40 percent are turned away or served with less help; this is only one office with invented data.
step: Many people with legal problems never ask for help, so requests understate need.

@mistakes
- Saying there is a constitutional right to appointed counsel in all civil cases.
- Treating one study of representation as the final word, when results vary with case type and the kind of help.
- Assuming turned-away applicants are the full measure of unmet need.
