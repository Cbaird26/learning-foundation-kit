@article
Reforms are proposed constantly: new lineup rules, bail changes, plain-language instructions, right-to-counsel laws. Evidence-based evaluation asks: did the reform work, for whom, and at what cost?

### The causal question

The central difficulty is the **counterfactual**: what would have happened without the reform? Because we cannot observe both, we compare groups or periods that differ as little as possible except for the reform.

### Designs

- **Randomized controlled trial (RCT):** units (people, courtrooms, precincts) are randomly assigned to the reform or a comparison. Randomization makes groups similar on average, including on unmeasured traits. Limits: cost, ethical and legal constraints, and limited generalizability.
- **Natural experiment:** a rule or lottery assigns treatment in a way that is as-if random, such as random assignment of cases to judges.
- **Difference-in-differences:** compare change over time in a treated group with change in a comparison group. The estimate is (treated after minus treated before) minus (comparison after minus comparison before). It relies on **parallel trends**: without the reform, both groups would have changed similarly.
- **Interrupted time series:** examine a long series of measurements before and after a reform for a break in trend. It is vulnerable to other events at the same time.
- **Regression discontinuity:** compare units just on either side of a cutoff.
- **Observational comparison:** compare those who did and did not experience the reform; vulnerable to **selection** and **confounding**.

### Threats and good practice

- **Confounding:** a third factor affects both reform and outcome.
- **Selection bias:** the groups differ before the reform.
- **Measurement validity and reliability:** does the measure capture the concept (rearrest is not the same as offending), and is it consistent?
- **Statistical vs practical significance:** a result can be statistically significant but small; report **effect sizes** and uncertainty (such as confidence intervals).
- **Sample size:** with a proportion $\hat p$ from $n$ people, an approximate 95 percent margin of error is $1.96\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}$.
- **External validity:** will results hold elsewhere?
- **Pre-registration** of hypotheses reduces selective reporting.
- **Unintended consequences** and costs should be measured alongside intended outcomes.

### Ethics

Research with people follows principles such as respect for persons (informed consent, extra protection for vulnerable groups), beneficence (minimize harm and maximize benefit) and justice (fair distribution of burdens and benefits). Institutional review boards review studies. In legal settings, randomizing access to a service such as counsel raises questions; one approach is to randomize when resources are limited and demand exceeds supply, and to ensure that no one is denied a legal right. Protect confidentiality of records, and be cautious with data that could identify defendants or witnesses.

### The deliverable: design a study

A good design states a question, hypothesis, population, reform, comparison, outcome measures, analysis plan, threats and safeguards. For example: "Do double-blind lineups reduce false identifications?" Randomly assign lab or field lineups to double-blind or non-blind administration, measure false identification rates in culprit-absent conditions, compare proportions, and pre-register the analysis.

title: Difference-in-differences with invented data
problem: County A adopts a pretrial reform. Failure-to-appear rates per 1,000 hearings: County A fell from 40 to 30; comparison County B fell from 38 to 35. What is the difference-in-differences estimate? (Invented data.)
step: Change in County A: 30 minus 40 is negative 10.
step: Change in County B: 35 minus 38 is negative 3.
step: Estimate: negative 10 minus negative 3, which is negative 7 per 1,000.
step: This attributes a 7-per-1,000 reduction to the reform if parallel trends hold; check pre-reform trends and other changes.

@mistakes
- Using a before-after comparison with no comparison group and concluding the reform caused the change.
- Treating statistical significance as proof of an important effect.
- Ignoring ethical review and consent when studying people.
- Measuring only intended outcomes and missing side effects.
