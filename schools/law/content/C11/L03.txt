Few kinds of evidence move a jury like a witness who points at the defendant and says, "That's the person." Yet decades of research show that confident eyewitnesses are sometimes wrong, and in many DNA-based exonerations a mistaken identification played a part. This lesson explains why.

### How memory works

Memory involves three stages. **Encoding** is taking in information, **storage** is retaining it, and **retrieval** is recalling it. Memory is not a video recording. It is **reconstructive**: we rebuild an event each time we recall it, filling gaps with expectations and with information acquired later. This makes memory feel vivid even when it is partly wrong.

### Estimator and system variables

Researchers separate two kinds of factors. **Estimator variables** are conditions of the event that the justice system cannot control but can estimate the effects of. **System variables** are choices the justice system makes, such as how a lineup is run, which can be improved.

Estimator variables that research has linked to lower accuracy include:

- **Stress and high arousal** during the event.
- **Weapon focus:** attention drawn to a weapon may reduce memory for the face, although the size of the effect varies across studies.
- **Cross-race effect:** people tend to identify faces of their own race more accurately than faces of other races.
- **Poor viewing conditions and short exposure**, such as bad lighting, distance or a brief glimpse.
- **Time delay:** memory fades, and the risk of error rises with a longer interval.

System variables include **suggestive procedures**: a lineup in which the suspect stands out, an officer who knows who the suspect is and unintentionally signals it, repeated showings of the same suspect, and confirming feedback ("Good, that's our guy"). **Postevent information** (the misinformation effect) can also alter memory, for example when a witness hears others' accounts or sees media coverage.

### Confidence

Jurors often treat a witness's certainty as a sign of accuracy. Research suggests the relationship is more complicated. A witness's confidence stated at the *first* identification, under fair procedures, is more informative than confidence stated later. Confidence can grow after feedback or repeated questioning even when accuracy does not. This is why recording confidence immediately matters.

### Recommended lineup reforms

Many researchers and professional bodies recommend:

- **Double-blind administration**, so the officer running the lineup does not know who the suspect is.
- **Fair fillers** who match the witness's description of the culprit, so the suspect does not stand out.
- **Pre-lineup instructions** that the culprit may or may not be present and that the investigation will continue either way.
- **Recording the witness's confidence statement** in the witness's own words immediately.
- Avoiding repeated identification procedures with the same suspect.

### Law

Under the due process framework of *Neil v. Biggers* (1972) and *Manson v. Brathwaite* (1977), a court first asks whether the police procedure was unnecessarily suggestive; if it was, the court considers whether the identification is still reliable under the totality of the circumstances, including the witness's opportunity to view, degree of attention, accuracy of the prior description, level of certainty and the time between crime and identification. In *Perry v. New Hampshire* (2012), the Supreme Court held that this due process review applies only when the suggestive circumstances were arranged by law enforcement. Some states go further. For example, the New Jersey Supreme Court in *State v. Henderson* (2011) revised its framework in light of scientific research. Courts may allow expert testimony on memory, and many state and federal jurisdictions also give cautionary jury instructions; practices vary.

### What studies show, and do not show

Laboratory studies can manipulate conditions, but participants may not feel the stakes real witnesses feel. Field data show real outcomes but cannot always reveal the truth in each case. Treat the findings as strong evidence about *risk factors*, not a rule that any particular identification is wrong.

@example
title: Spotting suggestiveness in a lineup
problem: A witness described the culprit as a tall man with a beard and a red jacket. The only man in the lineup wearing a red jacket was the suspect, and the officer running the lineup knew who the suspect was and said, "Take your time, I think you'll recognize him." The witness picked the suspect and said, "I'm pretty sure." Identify the problems.
step: The fillers do not match the description in one key feature: only the suspect has the red jacket, so the suspect stands out.
step: The officer knew the suspect and gave a comment that may signal the expected choice. A double-blind administrator would not have known whom to signal.
step: The instruction omitted a warning that the culprit might not be in the lineup, which can push the witness to choose the closest match.
step: Legally, a court applying *Manson v. Brathwaite* would ask whether the procedure was unnecessarily suggestive and, if so, whether the identification is nonetheless reliable.

@mistakes
- Assuming a confident witness is an accurate witness.
- Treating memory as a recording rather than a reconstruction.
- Confusing estimator variables (event conditions) with system variables (procedures the system controls).
- Thinking suggestive procedures automatically make an identification inadmissible, when courts also weigh reliability.
