@article
The jury is one of the most studied institutions in law. It puts ordinary people in charge of deciding facts, and it also tests how groups think.

### Law: who sits and how they decide

The Sixth Amendment guarantees an impartial jury in serious criminal cases, and the Seventh preserves jury trial in many federal civil cases. Key rules:

- **Size:** a jury of twelve is not constitutionally required. *Williams v. Florida* (1970) allowed six, and *Ballew v. Georgia* (1978) held that five is too few. In federal civil cases, six is permitted (*Colgrove v. Battin* (1973)).
- **Unanimity:** *Ramos v. Louisiana* (2020) held that the Sixth Amendment requires unanimous verdicts for serious criminal offenses in state as well as federal court, overruling *Apodaca v. Oregon* (1972). Many states allow non-unanimous verdicts in civil cases.
- **Fair cross-section:** juries must be drawn from a pool representing a fair cross-section of the community (*Taylor v. Louisiana* (1975)). A jury that ends up unrepresentative is not itself unconstitutional.
- **Selection:** in **voir dire**, lawyers question prospective jurors. **Challenges for cause** remove jurors who cannot be impartial, and **peremptory challenges** remove a limited number without stating a reason, but not on race (*Batson v. Kentucky* (1986)) or sex (*J.E.B. v. Alabama* (1994)). *Flowers v. Mississippi* (2019) illustrates how courts review claims that strikes were race-based.
- **Deliberation secrecy:** statements during deliberations usually cannot be used to attack a verdict, but *Peña-Rodriguez v. Colorado* (2017) created an exception where a juror's statements show racial animus was a significant motivating factor in the vote to convict.

### How juries decide

Research on real and mock juries yields some patterns, though conclusions depend on method:

- **Majority influence:** the initial majority position usually prevails in the verdict, though minorities sometimes change outcomes.
- **Conformity:** people go along with others for informational reasons (they assume others know better) and normative reasons (they want acceptance). Strong dissent from even one ally can reduce conformity.
- **Group polarization:** group discussion can move a group toward a more extreme version of the view it began with.
- **Leniency bias:** some studies find that deliberation moves groups toward leniency when the case is close, though results vary.
- **Comprehension:** jurors often have difficulty understanding legal instructions, and revising instructions into plain language tends to improve comprehension. Effects on verdicts are less clear.
- **Evidence processing:** jurors may rely on stories (narratives) rather than item-by-item analysis, and on vivid evidence.

### Strengths and criticisms

Supporters argue that juries bring community judgment, check government power and legitimate verdicts. Critics argue that jurors lack expertise and are affected by bias and by emotional appeals. Real juries can be hard to study because deliberations are private. Researchers use mock juries, post-trial interviews and archival data, each with limits: mock jurors may take the task less seriously, and interviewees may remember selectively. **Jury nullification** (acquitting despite finding the facts satisfy the law) occurs because verdicts are not reviewable, but judges generally do not instruct jurors that they may nullify, and courts disagree on the topic.

### Using this lesson on a sample deliberation

To identify biases in a deliberation, read it for specific cues: statements justified by a stereotype (in-group or out-group bias), hindsight, anchoring on the first number mentioned, confirmation of an early view, pressure from the majority, and group polarization.

@example
title: Spotting biases in a sample deliberation
problem: In a hypothetical civil case, jurors discuss a wage claim. Juror 1: "The plaintiff asked for \$500,000, so the real value must be near \$400,000." Juror 2: "I knew from the first day the company was careless; everything I've heard fits." Juror 3: "I don't see that, but everyone else agrees, so I'll go along." Identify the processes.
step: Juror 1 uses the plaintiff's number as the starting point, a sign of anchoring.
step: Juror 2 treats all evidence as fitting a belief formed early, which is confirmation bias.
step: Juror 3 has doubts but goes along with the majority, which is conformity, driven by normative influence.
step: A foreperson could ask each juror to restate the instruction on damages, invite the quiet juror's reasons, and keep the discussion on the evidence.

@mistakes
- Believing the Constitution requires twelve jurors; the requirement is for an impartial jury, and size has a floor of six.
- Saying peremptory challenges may be used for any reason; they cannot be used because of race or sex.
- Assuming the majority always wins; the initial majority usually does, but not always.
- Treating mock-jury findings as describing every real jury.
