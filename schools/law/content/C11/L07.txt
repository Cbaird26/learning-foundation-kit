Courts look, in popular culture, like places of dramatic trials. Sociologically, they are organizations that process large numbers of cases under pressure of time and resources, staffed by professionals who work together repeatedly.

### The court systems

The United States has a **dual court system**: a federal system and fifty state systems. State courts hear the great majority of cases, including most criminal, family, contract and tort cases, while federal courts hear cases arising under federal law, disputes between citizens of different states above a statutory amount, and certain other matters. Many state courts are **courts of limited jurisdiction** that handle traffic, small claims, misdemeanors and similar high-volume matters, often quickly, and often with litigants who have no lawyer.

### The courtroom workgroup

Researchers describe judges, prosecutors, defense attorneys and clerks as a **courtroom workgroup**: a group of repeat players with shared goals of moving cases efficiently, maintaining relationships and avoiding uncertainty. This helps explain why cases are routinely resolved by negotiation. It also raises questions about whether speed and cooperation can come at the expense of individual attention to each case. Defenders of the system respond that negotiation reflects shared judgments about likely outcomes and spares everyone the cost and risk of trial.

### Plea bargaining

In the United States, well over 90 percent of criminal convictions result from guilty pleas, and in the federal system the share is about the same. In **plea bargaining**, the defendant pleads guilty in exchange for a concession, such as reduced charges, a recommended sentence or dropped counts. The Supreme Court has accepted the practice as constitutional (*Santobello v. New York* (1971) held that a prosecutor must keep a plea promise; *Bordenkircher v. Hayes* (1978) upheld a prosecutor's threat to pursue more serious charges if the defendant refused a plea). The Court has also said that the Sixth Amendment right to effective assistance of counsel applies to plea negotiations (*Missouri v. Frye* (2012) and *Lafler v. Cooper* (2012)) and that counsel must advise a noncitizen defendant of deportation risks (*Padilla v. Kentucky* (2010)).

Arguments about pleas differ. Supporters say that pleas conserve resources, give defendants certainty and let prosecutors set priorities. Critics argue that the **trial penalty**, the gap between the plea offer and the sentence after conviction at trial, can pressure even innocent defendants to plead, and that heavy caseloads reduce careful review of evidence. Empirical researchers continue to study how often this happens; it is hard to measure because the truth in a plea case is rarely tested.

### Counsel for the accused

*Gideon v. Wainwright* (1963) guarantees appointed counsel for indigent defendants charged with felonies, and *Argersinger v. Hamlin* (1972) extends the right to any case in which imprisonment is actually imposed. Public defenders often carry very high caseloads, and professional groups have debated caseload standards.

### The legal profession

Lawyers are regulated largely by state supreme courts, which decide admission (typically a law degree, a bar exam and a character and fitness review) and discipline, usually through rules patterned on the ABA Model Rules of Professional Conduct. The profession's **self-regulation** is justified by its specialized knowledge; critics argue it can protect the profession's market and limit affordable services. **Unauthorized practice of law** rules bar non-lawyers from giving legal advice, which defenders say protects clients from incompetence and critics say limits access to services. Some jurisdictions have experimented with allowing licensed paraprofessionals or relaxing rules on non-lawyer ownership, and the results are still being studied.

### Judges

State judges are chosen by election, appointment or a mixed system; federal judges are appointed by the President with Senate confirmation and hold office during good behavior. Supporters of elections cite accountability; supporters of appointment cite independence. Research on the effects of selection methods is mixed.

@example
title: Reading a plea-rate statistic
problem: A hypothetical county reports 1,000 felony convictions in a year, of which 940 resulted from guilty pleas. What percentage resulted from trial, and what can and cannot be concluded?
step: Trial convictions: 1,000 minus 940 is 60.
step: Percentage from trial: 60 divided by 1,000 is 6 percent, so 94 percent came from pleas.
step: A high plea rate shows how cases are resolved, not whether pleas are fair, whether defendants are guilty, or whether charges would have been proved at trial.
step: To assess fairness, a researcher would need other evidence, such as sentencing differences between plea and trial or case outcomes after review.

@mistakes
- Assuming most criminal cases are decided by juries; most convictions come from pleas.
- Treating the plea rate as proof that pleas are coerced, or as proof they are fair.
- Confusing the right to appointed counsel in felony cases (*Gideon*) with a general civil right to counsel.
