@article
Police are the most visible part of the legal system. How they use their authority shapes whether people see the law as legitimate, and whether they cooperate with it.

### Legitimacy and compliance

**Legitimacy** is the belief that an authority has the right to rule and should be obeyed. People can follow the law because they fear punishment (**deterrence-based compliance**) or because they feel an obligation to obey and trust authorities (**legitimacy-based compliance**). Studies in several countries find that people who see legal authorities as legitimate report greater willingness to comply and cooperate, for example by calling the police or reporting crime. Most of this research is survey-based, so it shows associations; researchers continue to test whether legitimacy causes compliance or whether the two influence each other.

### Procedural justice

**Procedural justice** is the perceived fairness of the *process* by which authorities make decisions and treat people. Four elements are commonly identified: **voice** (a chance to tell one's side), **neutrality** (decisions based on facts and consistent rules), **respect** (courteous, dignified treatment) and **trustworthiness** (a sense that the authority is sincere and cares about the person's needs). A central finding is that people's judgments of fairness depend on the process as well as the outcome: someone who gets a ticket but feels heard and respected may regard the encounter as more legitimate than someone treated dismissively.

This explains why procedural justice matters for compliance: if people see authorities as fair, they are more likely to obey voluntarily, which is cheaper and more durable than enforcement by threat. Evidence on training officers in procedural justice is promising but mixed, and some evaluations find modest effects on attitudes and fewer clear effects on outcomes such as crime.

### Legal rules for stops and force

- *Terry v. Ohio* (1968): police may briefly stop a person on reasonable suspicion of criminal activity and frisk for weapons if they reasonably believe the person is armed and dangerous.
- *Whren v. United States* (1996): a traffic stop supported by probable cause of a violation is reasonable regardless of the officer's subjective motive, so claims of selective enforcement are brought under equal protection rather than the Fourth Amendment.
- *Tennessee v. Garner* (1985) and *Graham v. Connor* (1989): force is judged by objective reasonableness; deadly force against a fleeing suspect requires probable cause that the suspect poses a serious threat. *Barnes v. Felix* (2025) held that courts must consider the totality of the circumstances, not only the moment of threat.
- *Case v. Montana* (2026): police may enter a home without a warrant when they have an objectively reasonable basis to believe someone inside needs emergency aid. *Chatrie v. United States* (2026) held that obtaining location data through a geofence warrant is a Fourth Amendment search.

### Strategies and what the evidence suggests

Reasonable people disagree about policing policy. Several arguments are commonly made:

- **Hot-spots policing** concentrates patrols on small high-crime places. Reviews generally find it reduces crime in those places without simply moving it elsewhere, though effects are modest and vary. Critics raise concerns about repeated contact with residents.
- **Proactive stops** are defended as a way to prevent crime and recover weapons, and criticized for disparities and for harming trust; research on net effects is contested.
- **Body-worn cameras** have been studied in randomized and non-randomized evaluations that find mixed or small average effects on use of force and complaints; they may help in resolving disputes about what occurred.
- **Community policing** emphasizes partnerships; evidence suggests improvement in attitudes more than in crime.

Proponents of more enforcement stress public safety and victims; proponents of reform stress fairness, trust and costs of contact. The evidence informs the trade-offs but does not settle them.

@example
title: Explaining procedural justice
problem: Two drivers receive identical tickets. Driver A was told why she was stopped, allowed to speak and treated politely. Driver B was cut off and addressed rudely. Explain, using procedural justice, why their later willingness to comply might differ.
step: Driver A experienced voice (she spoke), respect (politeness) and some trustworthiness (an explanation).
step: Driver B experienced none of these and may judge the authority illegitimate despite the same outcome.
step: The outcome is the same, so a difference in later compliance would point to the process, though a single comparison cannot prove causation.
step: To test this, researchers would compare many encounters, ideally with random assignment to different officer communication styles.

@mistakes
- Treating procedural justice as only about winning or losing; it is about how the process is experienced.
- Concluding that survey correlations prove legitimacy causes compliance.
- Presenting a contested policing strategy as settled either way.
- Stating *Terry* as requiring probable cause; it requires reasonable suspicion.
