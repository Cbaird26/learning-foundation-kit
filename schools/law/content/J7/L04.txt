@article
Mountains look permanent, but they are slowly being worn down while new mountains rise elsewhere. Three processes do the wearing and moving.

- **Weathering** breaks rock into smaller pieces or changes it chemically, **in place**.
- **Erosion** moves the pieces from one place to another.
- **Deposition** drops them in a new place when the moving agent slows down.

### Mechanical weathering

This breaks rock apart without changing what it is made of. In **frost wedging**, water seeps into cracks and freezes. Ice takes up about 9% more volume than liquid water, so it pushes the crack wider. Plant roots pry rock apart. **Abrasion** is the scraping of rock by wind-blown sand or river gravel. Pressure release can make outer layers peel off in sheets, called **exfoliation**.

### Chemical weathering

This changes the minerals themselves. Rainwater absorbs carbon dioxide and becomes weak carbonic acid, which slowly dissolves limestone and makes caves. Oxygen reacts with iron-bearing minerals and forms rust-colored **oxides**. Warm, wet climates speed up chemical weathering.

### Surface area matters

Weathering happens on the surface of rock. Breaking a rock into smaller pieces **increases the total surface area** exposed, so weathering speeds up. A 2 cm cube has a surface area of $6 \times 2^2 = 24$ cm$^2$, but eight 1 cm cubes cut from it have a total of $8 \times 6 = 48$ cm$^2$.

### Erosion and deposition

The agents of erosion are **running water**, **wind**, **glaciers (ice)** and **gravity**. When a river enters a lake or the ocean it slows and drops sediment, building a **delta**. Wind builds **dunes**, glaciers leave piles called **moraines**, and a stream leaving a mountain forms an **alluvial fan**. Fast water can carry large pieces. As it slows, it drops the biggest, heaviest pieces first.

### Soil

Weathered rock mixed with decayed plant and animal matter is **soil**. Soil forms in layers called horizons: topsoil (A) rich in organic matter, subsoil (B), partly weathered rock (C) and solid bedrock below.

### Back to the rock cycle

Weathering, erosion and deposition make the sediment that, once buried, is compacted and cemented into sedimentary rock. They link the rock cycle's igneous and metamorphic rocks to new sedimentary ones.

@example
title: Surface area after cutting a cube
problem: A cube of rock is 3 cm on each side. It is cut into 27 smaller cubes, each 1 cm on each side. By what factor does the total surface area increase?
step: Surface area of the large cube: a cube has 6 faces, each $3 \times 3 = 9$ cm$^2$, so $6 \times 9 = 54$ cm$^2$.
step: Surface area of one small cube: $6 \times 1 = 6$ cm$^2$. For 27 cubes: $27 \times 6 = 162$ cm$^2$.
step: Compare: $\frac{162}{54} = 3$.
step: The total surface area triples, so weathering can act three times as quickly on the pieces.

@mistakes
- Using "erosion" and "weathering" as the same word. Weathering breaks rock down in place; erosion moves it.
- Thinking chemical weathering only happens in wet places. Water helps a lot, but oxygen and acids also act in dry regions, just more slowly.
- Believing smaller pieces have less surface area. The pieces together have much more.
- Thinking deposition happens when water speeds up. It happens when the agent slows down and loses energy.
