@article
A **thesis** is the central claim of an essay, stated in a sentence or two, that a reasonable reader could dispute and that the rest of the essay supports. It answers the question "So what are you arguing?" Law students learn that every brief, memo and motion is built around a clear point; this week you practice the same discipline on ordinary essays.

### Topic, fact, thesis

- A **topic** is a subject: *juvenile curfews*.
- A **fact** is settled and not worth arguing: *Many cities have juvenile curfews.*
- A **thesis** takes a position that needs support: *City curfews for minors should be repealed because they burden lawful activity without measurably reducing crime.*

An **arguable** thesis is one where informed people could disagree and where evidence and reasoning can move a reader. "Curfews are bad" is not yet a thesis; it is a mood.

### Qualities of a strong thesis

1. **Arguable.** It takes a side.
2. **Specific.** It names the policy, group, text or period, and the grounds for the position.
3. **Scoped.** It claims what the essay can prove in its length. "Social media ruined society" cannot be proven in 1,800 words; "Platforms should be required to label automated accounts" can be argued.
4. **Focused on one controlling idea.** Two unrelated claims split the essay.
5. **Previewing reasons (often).** A "because" clause tells the reader the plan: *X should happen because of A, B and C.*

A useful test is to ask the **opposite question**: could a thoughtful person argue the opposite? If not, the thesis is a fact. A second test: after reading your thesis, can the reader ask "why?" or "how?" Those questions are the essay's body.

### From thesis to outline

An **outline** turns the thesis into a plan. A **topic sentence outline** lists the thesis followed by one full sentence for each body paragraph, each stating a claim that supports the thesis. Reading only the topic sentences should reproduce the argument in miniature.

A common structure for an argumentative essay:

1. **Introduction:** context, the question at issue, the thesis.
2. **Background** (if needed): what the reader must know.
3. **Reasons:** one section per main reason, strongest or most foundational first or last (usually not buried in the middle).
4. **Counterargument and rebuttal** (Week 7).
5. **Conclusion:** what follows from the argument; not a copy of the introduction.

Other patterns include **problem-solution** (define the problem, propose and defend a remedy), **cause-effect**, and the **Rogerian** pattern (state the opposing view fairly, find common ground, then argue). Choose the pattern that fits your claim, not the other way round.

### Checking the outline

- Does every topic sentence support the thesis directly? Delete or revise any that merely describe.
- Is the order logical: each reason building on the last, or ranked by strength?
- Is the evidence you plan to use listed under each topic sentence?
- Are there gaps: a claim that needs a premise you have not planned to prove?

Expect the thesis to change as you outline and draft. A working thesis is a hypothesis; you revise it as you learn what the evidence supports.

@example
title: Strengthening a weak thesis and outlining
problem: A student's first thesis is "Standardized tests are controversial." Revise it into an arguable thesis about a university's use of standardized admissions tests, and write three topic sentences.
step: Diagnose: "controversial" is a fact about the topic, not a position. A reader cannot argue with it, and it names no policy or reason.
step: Pick a side and a scope: the university's admissions decision. Add the grounds: "Universities should make standardized tests optional in admissions because scores add little beyond grades, penalize applicants who cannot afford preparation, and can be replaced by better measures."
step: Turn each reason into a topic sentence. (1) Grades already predict first-year performance, and test scores add little to that prediction. (2) Test preparation costs create an advantage unrelated to ability. (3) Portfolios and course rigor can supply the information a test is meant to provide.
step: Check: each sentence supports the thesis, and a reader could ask for evidence for each. A counterargument paragraph (tests help compare applicants across schools) would be added next.

@mistakes
- Announcing a topic ("This essay is about school uniforms") instead of making a claim.
- Writing a thesis so broad that no paragraph could support it, or so narrow that it leaves nothing to argue.
- Writing topic sentences that describe the paragraph ("This paragraph discusses cost") instead of making a claim.
- Treating the first thesis as final and bending the evidence to fit it.

@practice self
prompt: Choose a debated public question (for example, whether schools should start later). Write a one-sentence arguable thesis with a "because" clause, then three topic sentences for body paragraphs.
hint: Write the thesis first, then turn each reason into a claim.
solution: Thesis: High schools should start no earlier than 8:30 a.m. because later starts improve student sleep, raise attendance and cost less to adopt than critics claim. Topic sentences: (1) Adolescents' sleep cycles shift later, so early starts leave many chronically sleep-deprived. (2) Districts that delayed start times report higher attendance and fewer tardies. (3) Transportation changes, the main cost, can be managed with staggered routes.
rubric: The thesis takes a side, is specific and includes reasons.
rubric: Each topic sentence is a claim, not a description.
rubric: The three topic sentences together would prove the thesis.
