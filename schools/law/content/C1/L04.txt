@article
A **paragraph** develops one idea. In an argument, that idea is a claim that supports the thesis. Readers use paragraph breaks as signals: a new paragraph says "new step." If a paragraph holds two ideas, the reader loses the step; if one idea is spread over several paragraphs, the reader loses the thread.

### Unity: one controlling idea

A paragraph has **unity** when every sentence serves its **topic sentence**, the sentence (usually first) that states the paragraph's claim. A reliable structure is the **claim-evidence-analysis** pattern, sometimes taught as **PEEL** (point, evidence, explanation, link):

1. **Claim (topic sentence):** what this paragraph argues.
2. **Evidence:** a fact, statistic, example or quotation.
3. **Analysis:** why the evidence supports the claim. This is the part students most often omit. Evidence does not speak for itself.
4. **Link:** what the paragraph contributes to the thesis, or a bridge to the next paragraph.

A paragraph that is all evidence reads as a list. A paragraph that is all assertion reads as opinion. A strong one alternates evidence and analysis.

### Length and sentence order

There is no ideal length, but in academic prose a paragraph of one or two sentences is usually underdeveloped and a paragraph of more than about 250 words usually holds more than one idea. Begin a new paragraph when you start a new reason, move from claim to rebuttal, or shift time, place or speaker. In legal writing the habit carries on: each paragraph in a memo makes one point, announced at its start.

### Coherence: how sentences hold together

A paragraph has **coherence** when readers can follow how sentence connects to sentence. Three tools help:

- **Old-to-new order.** Begin a sentence with information the reader already knows (often from the end of the previous sentence) and end with the new information. "The city adopted a parking fee. *The fee* cut traffic by six percent. *That reduction* …" The repeated words are not redundancy; they are handrails.
- **Repetition of key terms and pronouns with clear referents.** Use "this" or "that" with a noun ("this reduction"), not alone, so the reader knows what it points to.
- **Transitions.** Words and phrases that tell the reader the logical relationship between ideas.

### Choosing the right transition

A transition is a claim about logic, so a wrong one misleads. Match the word to the relationship:

| Relationship | Transitions |
|---|---|
| Addition | also, moreover, in addition, furthermore |
| Contrast | however, but, in contrast, on the other hand |
| Concession | although, even though, admittedly, granted |
| Cause / result | therefore, as a result, consequently, thus |
| Example | for instance, for example, in particular |
| Sequence | first, next, then, finally |
| Conclusion | in short, in sum, as a result |

"However" announces that the next idea cuts against the last. Use it only when it does. "Therefore" announces that the next claim follows from the last; if it does not follow, the word is a bluff.

Transitions also work between paragraphs. The best ones refer back and look forward: "Cost is only one objection; a more serious objection concerns fairness."

### Revising a paragraph

Cover everything but the first sentences of your paragraphs. Do they, read together, give the argument? Then for each paragraph underline the sentence that does not belong; move it or cut it. Finally, read aloud for lurches, places where the connection between two sentences exists only in your head.

@example
title: Repairing a paragraph that lacks unity and analysis
problem: Revise this paragraph for a thesis arguing that a city should add protected bike lanes: "Bike lanes are common in Europe. The city spent \$2 million on road repairs last year. In one neighborhood, injuries to cyclists fell by a third after protected lanes were added. Many people like to cycle."
step: Choose the claim the paragraph will make: protected lanes improve cyclist safety. Everything must serve it.
step: Cut or move the sentences that do not serve the claim: the sentence about Europe and the one about road repairs belong elsewhere or nowhere, and "many people like to cycle" is a different point.
step: Keep the evidence (injuries fell by a third) and add analysis that explains why it supports the claim: physical separation keeps cars and bicycles apart.
step: Add a topic sentence at the start and a link at the end: "Protected bike lanes make cycling safer. In one neighborhood, injuries to cyclists fell by a third after protected lanes were added. Because a barrier separates riders from traffic, collisions between cars and bicycles become much less likely. This safety gain is the first reason the city should extend the network."

@mistakes
- Starting a paragraph with evidence and never saying what it proves.
- Using "however" or "therefore" when the logical relationship is different, or sprinkling transitions mechanically.
- Packing two different claims into one paragraph, or splitting one claim into three short ones.
- Using "this" with no noun, so the reader cannot tell what it refers to.

@practice self
prompt: Write a paragraph of 100 to 150 words that argues one claim, with a topic sentence, one piece of evidence (you may invent a plausible, clearly hypothetical statistic), analysis and a closing link. Underline the transitions you use.
hint: Start from the claim, then add evidence, analysis and a link.
solution: Model: Public libraries should abolish late fines. Fines do not recover much money; in one hypothetical district, fine revenue covered under one percent of the budget while staff spent hours collecting it. Because the cost of enforcement approaches the revenue, the fines accomplish little except to deter the families who most need borrowing. Staff time spent on collecting and recording small payments could instead go to reading programs and story hours that bring families in. Moreover, when the same district stopped fining children, borrowing by children rose. These facts show that fines undermine the library's mission. For that reason, abolishing them serves the purpose the library exists for.
rubric: The first sentence states one clear claim and every other sentence serves it.
rubric: There is analysis explaining how the evidence supports the claim, not only evidence.
rubric: Transitions match the logical relationships they signal.

choice*: It contains a sentence about a different claim from the topic sentence.
