@article
Grammar is often taught as a list of rules to avoid embarrassment. This week treats it differently: grammar is a tool for **precision**. A misplaced comma or modifier can change who gets money under a will or what conduct a rule prohibits. Lawyers learn this the hard way, and you can learn it now.

### Sentence boundaries

A **sentence** needs a subject and a verb and must express a complete thought.

- A **fragment** lacks one or is a dependent clause standing alone: "Because the data were incomplete." Attach it: "Because the data were incomplete, the study was withdrawn."
- A **comma splice** joins two independent clauses with only a comma: "The test was long, it was fair." A **fused sentence** (run-on) joins them with nothing. Fix with a period, a semicolon, or a comma plus a coordinating conjunction (*for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so*): "The test was long, but it was fair."
- Use a **semicolon** between two closely related independent clauses, or between items in a series that already contain commas. Words such as *however* and *therefore* are not conjunctions: "The test was long; however, it was fair."
- Use a **colon** after an independent clause to introduce a list, explanation or quotation: "She had one goal: to finish."

### Commas that change meaning

- A **restrictive** (essential) clause identifies which thing and takes no commas: "Students who cheat are disciplined." (only the ones who cheat). A **nonrestrictive** clause adds extra information and is set off: "The students, who cheat, are disciplined" (all the students cheat). In American usage, restrictive clauses often use *that* and nonrestrictive clauses *which*.
- The **serial (Oxford) comma** before *and* in a list prevents ambiguity. "I leave my estate to my sisters, Ana and Beth" could name two sisters or give to the sisters plus two others. Whether you use the serial comma, be consistent and check for ambiguity.
- Use a comma after an introductory element and around interrupters.

### Apostrophes

Use an apostrophe for **possession** (the court's ruling, the judges' opinions) and for contractions (it's = it is). The possessive **its** has no apostrophe. Never use an apostrophe to make a plural: *the 1990s*, not the 1990's.

### Agreement

- **Subject-verb agreement:** the verb agrees with the true subject, not the nearest noun: "The list of reasons *is* long." Subjects joined by *and* are plural; with *or* or *nor*, the verb agrees with the nearer subject. *Data* is treated as plural in many academic fields.
- **Pronoun agreement:** a pronoun matches its antecedent in number, and the antecedent must be clear. "When the manager spoke to the clerk, she was upset" does not say who. Singular *they* is widely accepted for one person.

### Modifiers and parallelism

A **misplaced modifier** is put where it seems to describe the wrong word: "She only asked for water" versus "She asked for only water." A **dangling modifier** has no word to modify: "Walking to class, the rain began." Repair by naming the actor: "Walking to class, Ben felt the rain begin."

**Parallelism** puts items in a series in the same grammatical form: "The policy aims to reduce costs, to improve service and to increase access," not "…and increasing access." Parallel structure makes lists easier to read and makes comparisons fair.

### Proofreading for precision

Read the draft backward sentence by sentence to catch errors you would otherwise skim past. Look for the errors you personally make most. Do not rely on a grammar checker alone: it misses meaning-changing mistakes (a correctly spelled wrong word, a misplaced "only").

@example
title: Fixing a splice and a dangling modifier
problem: Fix: "Reviewing the documents, the error was obvious, the clerk had copied the wrong date."
step: Identify the dangling modifier: "Reviewing the documents" has no person doing the reviewing; "the error" cannot review. Supply the actor: "Reviewing the documents, we saw that the error was obvious."
step: Identify the comma splice: "the error was obvious" and "the clerk had copied the wrong date" are independent clauses separated by only a comma.
step: Choose a repair that shows the relationship: the second clause explains the first, so a colon works: "Reviewing the documents, we saw the error: the clerk had copied the wrong date."
step: Check meaning and agreement: the sentence now names an actor, uses a colon after an independent clause, and says exactly one thing.

@mistakes
- Using a comma between two independent clauses without a conjunction.
- Writing "its" with an apostrophe as a possessive or "it's" as a possessive.
- Putting commas around an essential clause, or omitting commas around extra information, and so changing the meaning.
- Letting a modifier float: "only," "almost" and "nearly" belong immediately before the word they limit.

@practice mc
prompt: Which sentence is correctly punctuated?
choice: The brief was filed late, the court denied the motion.
choice*: The brief was filed late; the court denied the motion.
choice: The brief was filed late the court denied the motion.
choice: The brief was filed, late; the court, denied the motion.
hint: Two independent clauses need a semicolon, a period, or a comma plus a conjunction.
solution: The semicolon joins two independent clauses correctly. The version with only a comma is a comma splice and the version with no punctuation is fused.

@practice mc
prompt: Which sentence uses "its" and "it's" correctly?
choice: The committee issued it's report; its due on Monday.
choice: The committee issued its report; its due on Monday.
choice*: The committee issued its report; it's due on Monday.
choice: The committee issued it's report; it's due on Monday.
hint: "It's" means "it is"; "its" shows possession.
solution: "Its report" is possessive and "it's due" means "it is due."

@practice ms
prompt: Select every sentence that contains a dangling or misplaced modifier.
choice*: Running for the bus, my backpack fell open.
choice: After the meeting ended, Ana walked home.
choice*: The clerk handed the form to the client with the errors.
choice: Because the data were incomplete, the study was withdrawn.
choice*: Covered in dust, we found the old file.
hint: Ask who or what the modifier actually describes.
solution: In the backpack and form sentences the modifier seems to describe the wrong thing (the backpack running, the client having errors). "Covered in dust, we found..." makes the people dusty. The others are clear.

@practice mc
prompt: A park rule reads: "No vehicles or bicycles with motors." The park intends to ban every vehicle that has a motor, and to allow bicycles without motors. Which rewrite says this unambiguously?
choice: No vehicles or bicycles with motors.
choice: No vehicles with motors or bicycles.
choice*: No motor vehicles, including motorized bicycles.
choice: No bicycles or vehicles.
hint: The original does not make clear whether "with motors" modifies only bicycles or both nouns.
solution: The correct rewrite states that every motor vehicle is banned, motorized bicycles included, and says nothing against ordinary bicycles. The original repeats the ambiguity, "No vehicles with motors or bicycles" bans all bicycles, and "No bicycles or vehicles" bans all vehicles and bicycles.

@practice self
prompt: Rewrite each sentence to fix the problem named. (a) Comma splice: "The report was brief, it omitted the data." (b) Dangling modifier: "After reading the contract, the clause seemed unfair." (c) Faulty parallelism: "The plan will cut costs, improve access and increasing speed."
hint: For (b) decide who is doing the reading; for (c) match the verb forms.
solution: (a) The report was brief; it omitted the data. (Or: The report was brief, and it omitted the data.) (b) After reading the contract, Ana thought the clause unfair. (c) The plan will cut costs, improve access and increase speed.
rubric: Fixes (a) with a semicolon, period or conjunction.
rubric: Fixes (b) by supplying a person who reads.
rubric: Fixes (c) by making all three verbs the same form.

choice*: The group of students is late.
choice: The group of student's is late.
choice: The group of students, is late.
hint: Find the true subject.
