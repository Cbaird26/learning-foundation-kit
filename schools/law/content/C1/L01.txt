@article
An **argument**, in the sense used in academic writing and in law, is a set of statements in which some statements, the **premises**, are offered as reasons to accept another statement, the **conclusion**. A fight is not an argument; a list of facts is not an argument; a strongly worded opinion with no reasons is not an argument. What makes something an argument is the attempt to support a claim with reasons.

### Find the conclusion first

Begin every close reading by asking: *what is the author trying to get me to believe or do?* The answer is the conclusion (also called the **claim** or, in a longer essay, the **thesis**). Premises can be shuffled around, but you can always test a candidate conclusion by asking whether the other statements are offered in support of it.

Authors often signal structure with **indicator words**. Conclusion indicators include *therefore, thus, hence, so, it follows that, consequently*. Premise indicators include *because, since, for, given that, as, after all*. Indicators help, but they are not a guarantee: "since" can also mean "from a time on," and many arguments have no indicators at all. Always check the sense.

### Explanation versus argument

Compare two sentences. "The bridge closed because the cables corroded" explains something both speakers already accept. "The bridge should stay closed because the cables corroded" argues for something. In an explanation the *because* clause accounts for a fact; in an argument it supports a claim that a reader might doubt.

### Unstated assumptions

Nearly every real argument leaves something unsaid. An **assumption** (an unstated premise) is a claim the argument needs but does not state. Suppose someone writes: "The city's new parking fee reduced downtown traffic, so the fee was a good policy." The missing premise is something like "Reducing downtown traffic is worth whatever else the fee costs." Finding the assumption is the quickest way to find where an argument is vulnerable. A useful test is the **negation test**: if the negation of a candidate assumption would destroy the argument, the candidate is an assumption the argument depends on.

This is also the core skill behind the Logical Reasoning sections of the LSAT, of which there are two scored sections today, and behind the single scored Reading Comprehension section: both ask you to locate the point, trace the support and notice the gap. Academic writing uses the same skill in a slower form.

### A method for close reading

1. **Skim, then read.** Note the title, the first and last paragraphs and any headings to predict the claim.
2. **Read with a pen.** Underline the conclusion once; number the premises; circle indicator words; bracket any evidence (statistics, examples, quotations).
3. **Restate.** Write the argument as a short chain: Premise 1, Premise 2, therefore Conclusion.
4. **Probe.** For each link ask: is this stated or assumed? Is the evidence relevant, sufficient and from a trustworthy source? What would a skeptic say?
5. **Separate summary from evaluation.** Understand what the author said before you judge it. Agreeing or disagreeing too early makes you read what you expect rather than what is on the page.

Reading closely also means noticing **tone and word choice**. Calling a policy "a handout" versus "an investment" carries a conclusion inside a noun. Flag such loaded words, then ask what the argument would look like with neutral wording.

@example
title: Reconstructing a short argument
problem: Identify the conclusion, the premises and one unstated assumption: "Our county library should open on Sundays. Most residents work on weekdays, and the library's Saturday hours are already its busiest. Since extra Sunday staffing would cost little compared with the benefit, the board ought to approve it."
step: Ask what the author wants: the library should open on Sundays. The last sentence repeats it as what the board "ought to approve," so that is the conclusion.
step: List the support. P1: Most residents work on weekdays. P2: Saturday is already the library's busiest day. P3: Sunday staffing would cost little compared with the benefit. The word "since" marks P3 as a premise.
step: Look for the gap. The premises show demand on a non-working day, but they never say that Sunday demand would resemble Saturday demand.
step: State the assumption: residents who cannot visit on weekdays and who crowd the library on Saturday would also use it on Sundays in large numbers. If Sunday visits would be rare, P2 does not support the conclusion.

@mistakes
- Choosing the first sentence as the conclusion automatically; sometimes the claim comes last, or is only implied.
- Treating every "because" as a premise marker in an argument, when it may introduce an explanation of an accepted fact.
- Calling a premise an "assumption" when it is stated; an assumption is what the author leaves unsaid.
- Judging the argument before restating it, so that you critique a weaker version than the one on the page.
