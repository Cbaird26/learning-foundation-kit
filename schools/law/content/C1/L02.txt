@article
A **summary** is a shortened account of a text's main ideas, in your own words, that represents the original fairly. A **paraphrase** restates a specific passage in your own words at about the same length. Both are acts of understanding: you cannot compress or rephrase what you have not grasped. In law school you will do this constantly when briefing cases, and on the LSAT Reading Comprehension section you are rewarded for knowing a passage's main point and structure.

### The summary is a map, not a miniature

A good summary does not shrink every paragraph by the same proportion. It keeps the **skeleton**: the author's conclusion, the main reasons, and the kind of evidence offered. It drops illustrations, repetition, asides and most numbers. If a 1,000-word argument must become 150 words, you keep roughly 15 percent of the length, so you can afford only a handful of sentences:

1. One sentence naming the author's claim (the thesis).
2. Two or three sentences for the main reasons, in the author's order.
3. One sentence for the most important qualification, objection answered, or the practical recommendation.

Begin with a **signal phrase** that attributes the ideas: "The author argues that…," "According to the report…." Use reporting verbs that fit the move the author makes: *argues, claims, concedes, proposes, objects*. A verb such as "proves" is a claim of your own about the strength of the argument; use it only if the author really does prove something.

### Fairness and distortion

A summary **distorts** the original when it:

- **Omits** a limit the author stated ("in most cases" becomes "always").
- **Overstates** or **understates** the author's confidence.
- **Reverses emphasis**, giving a minor point the weight of the main one.
- **Slants** with evaluative words ("the author wrongly insists").
- **Adds** your own view or information that the text does not contain.

The test of fairness: could the author read your summary and say, "Yes, that is what I argued"?

### Paraphrase

A paraphrase is not a synonym swap. Changing a few words but keeping the original sentence pattern is **patchwriting**, and it can count as plagiarism (more on this in Week 6). To paraphrase well:

1. Read the passage until you could explain it aloud without looking.
2. Cover the original and write from your understanding.
3. Compare: have you kept the meaning, changed the structure, and kept any technical term that has no good substitute?
4. Cite the source. A paraphrase still needs attribution because the idea is not yours.

Original: "Because the survey sampled only people who answered landline telephones, its findings say little about younger households." A sound paraphrase: "The poll reached only people with landlines, so it tells us little about the opinions of younger families." A weak one: "Since the survey sampled merely people who answered landline phones, its findings reveal little about younger households." The second keeps the original's skeleton and swaps words.

### A drafting routine

Summarize in three passes. First, mark the conclusion and the premises (Week 1). Second, write a one-sentence version. Third, expand to the required length by adding reasons in order of importance, then trim every word that does not carry the argument. Count your words, because a length limit is part of the task.

@example
title: Summarizing in about 35 words
problem: Summarize in one or two sentences: "Cities that remove minimum-parking requirements are often accused of causing street congestion. But the evidence from the three cities studied shows that developers still build parking where buyers want it; they simply build less of it where it is unwanted. Housing costs also fell in two of the three cities. The author concedes that the sample is small, yet concludes that the requirements are better left to the market."
step: Find the claim: parking requirements are better left to the market (the last sentence). The first sentence is the view the author is answering.
step: Choose the main reasons: developers still build wanted parking, and housing costs fell in two of three cities. Drop the example-level detail.
step: Keep the qualification: the author admits the sample is small. Leaving it out would overstate the author's confidence.
step: Draft with a signal phrase: "The author argues that minimum-parking requirements are better left to the market, because developers still build wanted parking and housing costs fell in two of three studied cities, while conceding the sample is small." (about 35 words)

@mistakes
- Copying phrases or the sentence pattern of the original and changing only a few words (patchwriting).
- Letting your opinion into the summary through words such as "unfortunately" or "the author naively claims."
- Summarizing the first half of the text in detail and running out of room for the conclusion.
- Dropping the author's qualifications, so a cautious claim sounds absolute.

@practice mc
prompt: The original says: "Most of the surveyed tenants reported mild improvement after repairs, though a minority saw none." Which summary is the fairest?
choice: Tenants reported that repairs helped.
choice*: Most surveyed tenants noticed some improvement after repairs, but a minority did not.
choice: Repairs improved life for every tenant surveyed.
choice: The survey proved that repairs are worthless to a minority of tenants.
hint: Check each choice for omitted limits and added claims.
solution: The correct choice keeps "most," "mild" in the sense of some improvement, and the minority exception. The others drop the qualification, overstate it with "every," or add "proved" and "worthless."

@practice ms
prompt: Select every defect that makes a summary UNFAIR to its source.
choice*: Turning "often" into "always"
choice*: Adding "which is clearly foolish" about the author's proposal
choice: Leaving out a vivid anecdote the author uses only as an illustration
choice*: Spending most of the summary on a minor example and leaving out the conclusion
choice: Using "argues" instead of "says"
hint: A summary may drop detail, but it may not change the claim, the confidence or the emphasis.
solution: Changing the strength of a claim, adding your evaluation and misplacing emphasis distort the source. Cutting an illustration and choosing a reporting verb are normal.

@practice self
prompt: Paraphrase this sentence in your own words, changing structure as well as vocabulary, and add a signal phrase: "Although the new filing system saved the office considerable time, staff morale suffered because the change was imposed without consultation."
hint: Keep both halves of the sentence and rebuild its structure.
solution: One acceptable version: The report observes that staff were unhappy with the new filing system, even though it sped up the work, because management never asked them about it beforehand.
rubric: Keeps both halves of the meaning: time saved and morale harmed because of the lack of consultation.
rubric: Changes the sentence structure, not just individual words.
rubric: Attributes the idea with a signal phrase and adds no opinion.

@quiz mc
prompt: Which is the best paraphrase of this sentence? "Since the pilot program enrolled only volunteers, its apparent success may reflect the volunteers' motivation rather than the program itself."
choice: Because the pilot enrolled just volunteers, its evident success could reflect volunteers' motivation instead of the program itself.
choice*: Only people who chose to join took part in the pilot, so its good results might be due to how motivated they were, not to the program.
choice: The pilot was a success because volunteers are motivated.
choice: The pilot program had volunteers, and it worked.
hint: Look for the choice that changes structure and keeps the cautious "may."
solution: The correct choice rebuilds the sentence and keeps the hedge. The choice that keeps "Because the pilot enrolled just volunteers" only swaps words, and the others change the meaning.
