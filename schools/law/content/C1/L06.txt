@article
When you use someone else's words or ideas, you owe the reader two things: a clear line between your voice and the source's, and a way to find the source. **Citation** does both. **Plagiarism** is presenting another person's words, ideas or data as your own, whether you copy exactly, paraphrase too closely, or simply omit credit. It can be intentional or careless; either way it is an integrity violation, and universities and bar authorities treat it seriously.

### Quote, paraphrase or summarize?

- **Quote** when the exact wording matters: it is memorable or technically precise, the author is an authority you are analyzing, or you are about to disagree with the phrasing itself. Keep quotations short and purposeful.
- **Paraphrase** to present a specific point in your voice and fit it to your sentence (Week 2).
- **Summarize** to report a whole argument or section briefly.

A paper that is mostly quotation shows that the writer has not done the thinking. A useful rule is that quoted material should be a small share of your text, with your own analysis taking the larger share.

### The quotation sandwich

Do not drop a quotation into a paragraph by itself. Frame it:

1. **Lead in** with a signal phrase naming the source and its role: "As the report's authors observe, …"
2. **Quote** accurately, in quotation marks (or as a block quotation if long).
3. **Explain** what the quotation shows and why it matters to your claim.

Vary the reporting verb (*notes, argues, concedes, finds, objects*) and make sure it fits what the source does. Use brackets for words you add or change: "the [study] found." Use an ellipsis (. . .) for words you omit, never so as to change the meaning. Use **block quotation** (indented, no quotation marks) for long quotations: in APA, 40 words or more; in MLA, more than four lines of prose.

### Citation styles

Different fields use different styles. All pair an **in-text citation** with a full entry at the end.

- **MLA** (humanities): author and page in parentheses, no comma: (Rivera 42). Full entries go on a "Works Cited" page.
- **APA** (social sciences): author, year and, for quotations, page: (Rivera, 2019, p. 42). Entries go on a "References" page.
- **Chicago** (history and many other fields): notes-bibliography uses footnotes or endnotes; an author-date variant resembles APA.

When the author's name is in your signal phrase, give only the rest: According to Rivera (2019), … (APA) or Rivera argues … (42) (MLA). Use one style consistently, and follow your instructor's choice. The names and works here are invented examples.

**Legal writing** uses *The Bluebook* (and, in many courts, local rules or the *ALWD Guide*) for citing cases, statutes and articles; those forms differ from MLA and APA. You will meet them in law school. For now the principle carries over: attribute precisely and let the reader find the source.

### What needs a citation

Cite direct quotations, paraphrases and summaries, statistics and data, specific facts that are not common knowledge, and other people's ideas, images and arguments. You need not cite **common knowledge** (facts widely known and easily found in many sources, such as that water boils at a certain temperature at sea level), but when in doubt, cite.

### Avoiding plagiarism

- Take notes in a way that separates quotation, paraphrase and your own thoughts.
- Record full source details at the time you read.
- Paraphrase by understanding, not by rewriting phrase by phrase (patchwriting).
- Put quotation marks around every borrowed phrase, even a short distinctive one.
- Citing a source does not excuse copying its wording without quotation marks.

@example
title: Integrating a source in three forms
problem: A source (invented for practice), Rivera (2019, p. 42), says: "Cities that guaranteed counsel at first appearance saw missed hearings fall by almost a third." Show a quotation, a paraphrase and a summary in APA style, each in your own sentence about guaranteed counsel.
step: Quotation with signal phrase: Rivera (2019) reports that "cities that guaranteed counsel at first appearance saw missed hearings fall by almost a third" (p. 42). The wording is exact and in quotation marks; the page appears because it is a quotation.
step: Paraphrase with changed structure: When cities provided a lawyer at the first appearance, defendants skipped far fewer hearings, a drop of nearly one third (Rivera, 2019). Meaning is preserved; wording and structure are new; still cited.
step: Summary of the article's main point: Rivera (2019) argues that early access to counsel improves court attendance. It is shorter and may omit the page.
step: Add your analysis after any of these: "This suggests that lack of counsel, not indifference, explains many missed hearings." The source supplies evidence; your claim connects it to your thesis.

@mistakes
- Dropping in a quotation with no lead-in and no explanation.
- Paraphrasing so closely that the sentence structure and key phrases are the source's, with no quotation marks.
- Citing the source but omitting quotation marks around exact words.
- Mixing citation styles in the same paper, or listing sources in the text with no matching entry at the end.
