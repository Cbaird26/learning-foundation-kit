@article
A **fallacy** is a pattern of reasoning in which the premises do not properly support the conclusion, even though the argument may seem persuasive. A fallacy is a flaw in the *connection* between premises and conclusion, not necessarily in the facts. An argument with a false premise is unsound; an argument whose premises are true but irrelevant is fallacious. Learning the names of fallacies gives you precise vocabulary for the gaps you noticed in Week 1, and it is a staple of LSAT Logical Reasoning questions that ask what is wrong with an argument.

Naming a fallacy is not the end of an analysis. Explain in a sentence *why* this reasoning fails here, and remember that a conclusion can be true even when it is supported by a fallacy.

### Fallacies of relevance

- **Ad hominem** attacks the person making a claim instead of the claim. "She is a lobbyist, so her statistics are worthless." (A source's interests are a reason to check the evidence, not a refutation of it.) A related fallacy is the **genetic fallacy**, judging a claim by its origin.
- **Straw man** distorts a position into a weaker one and attacks that. "Critics of the budget want to abolish all schools."
- **Red herring** introduces an irrelevant topic to divert attention.
- **Appeal to emotion** replaces reasons with feelings: fear, pity or pride. Emotion is legitimate when it fits the facts; the fallacy is using it instead of reasons.
- **Appeal to popularity** (*ad populum*): "Most people believe it, so it is true."
- **Appeal to authority**, when the authority has no expertise on the point, is unnamed, or the experts disagree. Appeal to a genuine, relevant expert is a reasonable form of evidence.

### Fallacies of causation and generalization

- **Post hoc ergo propter hoc** ("after this, therefore because of this"): assuming that because B followed A, A caused B.
- **Correlation as causation:** treating two trends moving together as proof that one causes the other (Week 5).
- **Hasty generalization:** drawing a broad conclusion from too few or unrepresentative cases.
- **Slippery slope:** claiming that one step will inevitably lead to a chain of extreme results without showing the links. A slippery-slope argument is reasonable only when each step is supported with evidence.

### Fallacies of presumption and structure

- **False dilemma** (false dichotomy): presenting two options as the only ones when others exist. "Either we cut the program or we go bankrupt."
- **Begging the question** (circular reasoning): the conclusion is assumed in the premise. "The law is just because it is fair." (In casual usage "begs the question" often means "raises the question"; in logic it means circularity.)
- **Equivocation:** using a key word in two senses within one argument.
- **Composition and division:** assuming that what is true of the parts is true of the whole (or reverse).
- **Loaded question:** a question that presupposes something unproven: "Have you stopped wasting money?"

### Using fallacy names well

1. **Be charitable.** Check that the argument really has the flaw; a reasonable argument from an expert is not a fallacy just because it mentions an authority.
2. **Look for the missing link.** Ask what the argument would need to be a good one. A slippery slope needs evidence of each step; a causal claim needs ruling out other causes.
3. **Do not write "that is a fallacy" and stop.** Say which fallacy and why it fails in this case.
4. **Check your own drafts.** Fallacies sneak in when you are most certain.

@example
title: Naming and explaining a fallacy
problem: "Since the town added bike lanes in June, car theft has risen. The lanes obviously cause car theft, so they should be removed." Identify the fallacy and explain why it fails.
step: Spot the structure: two events occurred in sequence (lanes added, thefts rose) and the argument concludes that the first caused the second.
step: Name it: post hoc ergo propter hoc, closely related to treating correlation as causation.
step: Explain the gap: the argument does not rule out other causes, such as seasonal patterns, changes in policing, or one local theft ring, and it gives no mechanism by which lanes would produce theft.
step: State what would repair it: compare theft in similar towns without new lanes, check earlier years for the same pattern, and find evidence of a mechanism before concluding anything.

@mistakes
- Labeling any argument you dislike as a fallacy, or naming a fallacy without saying why it fails.
- Calling every appeal to an expert an "appeal to authority"; a relevant, qualified expert is good evidence.
- Assuming that a fallacious argument must have a false conclusion; the conclusion may be true but unsupported.
- Confusing false premises with fallacies; a fallacy is a flaw of connection.

choice: Straw man

@practice ms
prompt: Select every argument that commits a causal fallacy.
choice*: "I wore my lucky socks and we won, so the socks made us win."
choice: "The new medicine was tested against a placebo in a randomized trial and patients improved more, so it likely works."
choice*: "Rooster crowing precedes sunrise, so the rooster makes the sun rise."
choice*: "Cities with more firefighters have more fires, so firefighters cause fires."
choice: "The reservoir ran dry after a drought, so the drought likely caused the shortage."
hint: A causal fallacy infers causation from sequence or association alone.
solution: Lucky socks, the rooster and the firefighters examples infer cause from sequence or correlation. The randomized trial and the drought example give a plausible, supported causal link.

@practice mc
prompt: "This policy is unjust because it treats people unfairly." What is the main flaw?
choice: It attacks a person.
choice: It assumes a slippery slope.
choice*: It begs the question: "unfairly" restates "unjust" without giving a reason.
choice: It relies on a hasty generalization.
hint: Does the premise say anything beyond the conclusion?
solution: The premise repeats the conclusion in different words, so the argument is circular.

@practice self
prompt: Explain, in three or four sentences, why this argument is a slippery slope and what evidence would make it reasonable: "If we let students retake one exam, soon they will demand to retake every assignment, and then standards will collapse."
hint: Identify the chain of steps and ask which have support.
solution: The argument claims that one small policy will inevitably cause a chain of increasingly extreme results, but it supplies no evidence for any link. It assumes that allowing one retake means retakes will be demanded and granted everywhere. It would be reasonable only if the writer showed, for example, that schools with a retake policy did expand retakes this way and saw standards fall. Without that, the argument rests on fear rather than support.
rubric: Names the slippery slope and identifies the chain of claimed steps.
rubric: Explains that the steps are asserted without support.
rubric: Says what kind of evidence would make the argument reasonable.

@quiz mc
prompt: "Every member of our team is an excellent player, so our team must be an excellent team." Which fallacy is this?
choice: Ad hominem
choice*: Composition: assuming that what is true of each part is true of the whole
choice: False dilemma
choice: Red herring
hint: The argument moves from members to the group.
solution: Excellent individuals can still form a poorly coordinated team, so inferring a property of the whole from its parts is the fallacy of composition.

@quiz ms
prompt: Select every statement that is true about fallacies.
choice*: A fallacy is a flaw in how premises connect to a conclusion.
choice: An argument that uses a fallacy must have a false conclusion.
choice*: Citing a qualified expert on a point within her field is not automatically a fallacy.
choice*: A good analysis names the fallacy and explains why it fails in the context.
choice: Any argument that appeals to emotion is a fallacy.
hint: Consider what a fallacy is and what it is not.
solution: Fallacies are flaws in support; the conclusion may still be true. Relevant expert evidence is legitimate, and emotion becomes fallacious only when it replaces reasons.
