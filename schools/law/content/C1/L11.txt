@article
The same facts can need very different writing depending on who will read them and why. The **rhetorical situation** has four elements: the **writer**, the **audience**, the **purpose** and the **context** (genre, medium, deadline, and the conversation the text joins). Before you write, answer: *Who is reading? What do they already know? What do they need to decide or do? What will they do with this text?* Lawyers write for judges, clients, colleagues and the public, often about the same matter, and they must change the writing, not the facts.

### Know your reader

- **Specialist reader.** Shares your vocabulary and background. Can handle precise technical terms and wants evidence, method and nuance.
- **General (educated, nonexpert) reader.** Intelligent but unfamiliar with jargon. Needs terms defined, steps spelled out and a reason to care.
- **Decision-maker under time pressure.** A judge, manager or client who needs the answer first, the reasons in order of importance, and a clear recommendation.

**Bottom line up front** is standard in professional writing: state the conclusion or request in the opening, then support it.

### Translating technical content

To rewrite for a general reader:

1. **Identify the core message** the reader needs, in one sentence.
2. **Define or replace jargon.** Define a term only if the reader needs it later; otherwise use a plain equivalent.
3. **Use concrete examples or analogies**, and check that the analogy does not mislead.
4. **Shorten sentences** and use active verbs; place the familiar before the new.
5. **Keep the accuracy.** Simplify, but do not distort: hedges that matter ("probably," "in this sample") must survive.
6. **Order by the reader's questions:** what is it, why does it matter, what should I do?

Plain language is not "dumbing down"; it is respect for the reader's time. A clear explanation shows that the writer understands the subject.

### Genre and register

**Register** is the level of formality. A text message to a friend, an email to a professor and a letter to a client use different registers. **Genre** conventions guide structure. In law, for instance:

- A **client letter** is plain, avoids jargon, explains options and next steps, and states uncertainty honestly.
- An **internal memorandum** to a supervising attorney is objective, states the question, a brief answer, the facts, the analysis and a conclusion, and weighs both sides.
- A **brief** to a court is persuasive but candid, organized around point headings that state claims, and bound by rules about format and length.

You will learn these genres in law school; here the lesson is that each has a predictable shape, and readers rely on it.

### Measuring readability

A **readability formula** such as the Flesch-Kincaid grade level estimates difficulty from sentence length and word length:

$$\text{Grade} = 0.39\left(\frac{\text{words}}{\text{sentences}}\right) + 11.8\left(\frac{\text{syllables}}{\text{words}}\right) - 15.59.$$

Shorter sentences and shorter words lower the score. Formulas are rough guides: they ignore organization, definitions and content, so a text can score well and still be confusing.

### Tone and inclusion

Use respectful, neutral wording about people. Avoid unnecessary assumptions about the reader's identity. Do not talk down, and avoid slang in formal documents.

@example
title: Rewriting a technical paragraph for a general reader
problem: Rewrite for a general reader: "The 95 percent confidence interval for the mean reduction in systolic pressure was 4 to 9 mmHg, indicating that the intervention's effect is statistically distinguishable from zero but of modest clinical magnitude." Assume the reader needs to decide whether the program is worth joining.
step: Identify the core message: the program probably lowers blood pressure a little, and the effect is real but small.
step: Replace jargon: "systolic pressure" becomes "the top number in a blood pressure reading"; "confidence interval" becomes "a range we can be fairly confident includes the true average effect."
step: Keep the accuracy and hedges: the range is 4 to 9 units, which is a modest change; say "on average" and "in this study."
step: Draft: "In this study, the average drop in the top number of a blood pressure reading was probably somewhere between 4 and 9 points. That is a real but modest drop, so it may help, but it is not a dramatic change by itself." The reader now knows what to expect.

@mistakes
- Writing for yourself or for an expert when the reader is a nonexpert.
- Simplifying until the claim is no longer accurate, or dropping the hedges that matter.
- Burying the answer at the end of a document for a busy reader.
- Using the same tone for every audience.

@practice num
prompt: A passage has 100 words, 5 sentences and 150 syllables. Using the Flesch-Kincaid grade-level formula $0.39(\text{words}/\text{sentences}) + 11.8(\text{syllables}/\text{words}) - 15.59$, find the grade level to two decimal places.
answer: 9.91
check: 0.39 * (100 / 5) + 11.8 * (150 / 100) - 15.59
tol: 0.01
hint: Words per sentence is $100 / 5$; syllables per word is $150 / 100$.
hint: $0.39 \times 20 + 11.8 \times 1.5 - 15.59$.
solution: $0.39 \times 20 = 7.8$ and $11.8 \times 1.5 = 17.7$, so the grade is $7.8 + 17.7 - 15.59 = 9.91$.

@practice self
prompt: Rewrite this sentence for a general reader (about 40 words), keeping it accurate: "The study's findings suggest that, controlling for income, children in households with consistent bedtime routines exhibited modestly higher reading scores, although causal inference is limited by the observational design."
hint: What must survive? A small difference, a comparison group, and a caution about cause.
solution: Model: In this study, children who had regular bedtimes tended to score a little higher on reading tests than children with similar family incomes who did not. But because the researchers only observed families, we cannot be sure the bedtimes caused the better scores.
rubric: Keeps the main finding and its small size.
rubric: Keeps the caution that the study cannot prove cause.
rubric: Replaces jargon with plain words and uses short sentences.
