@article
Good writers are mostly good revisers. A first draft is a way of finding out what you think; the essay readers see is the result of rethinking it. Revision is not a sign of failure but the main stage of writing. The order of the work matters: fixing commas in a paragraph you will delete wastes time.

### Three levels of work

1. **Revising** (global): the argument, evidence, organization and audience. Ask: Is the thesis clear and arguable? Does every section support it? Is the evidence enough? Is the counterargument fair and answered? Are the paragraphs in the best order? Revising can mean cutting, adding, moving and rewriting whole paragraphs.
2. **Editing** (sentence level): clarity, concision, word choice, transitions, sentence variety, and the grammar and punctuation of Week 10.
3. **Proofreading** (surface): typos, spelling, formatting, citation details, page numbers. Do it last, on a clean copy.

### The reverse outline

A **reverse outline** is made *after* drafting. For each paragraph write one line: what does it *say* (its claim) and what does it *do* (introduce, give evidence, answer an objection)? Then read the lines in order.

- If two paragraphs make the same point, merge them.
- If a paragraph has no clear claim, find its point or cut it.
- If a paragraph does not support the thesis, cut it or revise the thesis.
- If the order is illogical, rearrange.
- If a needed step is missing, add a paragraph.

Compare the reverse outline with your original outline: where the draft differs, decide which version is better.

### Distance and feedback

Put the draft away for a day if you can; you will read it more like a reader. **Read aloud** to hear awkward sentences. Ask a **peer** or tutor to read and tell you in their own words what your thesis is and what they found weak: if their version of your thesis differs from yours, the draft is unclear.

When you **give** feedback, be specific and kind: say what works, then name one or two major problems and suggest a fix. Avoid "good" or "confusing" without examples. When you **receive** feedback, listen first, ask questions, and decide what to change; you are not bound to accept every suggestion, but a reader's confusion is always data.

### A revision checklist

- **Thesis:** arguable, specific and consistent with the conclusion.
- **Structure:** topic sentences that read as an argument; logical order.
- **Evidence:** relevant, sufficient and cited; quotations framed and explained.
- **Counterargument:** fair and answered.
- **Style:** active verbs, no padding, parallel structure.
- **Mechanics:** consistent citation style; no fragments or splices; spelling checked by reading, not only by software.

Keep a personal **error log** of the mistakes you repeat; most writers have a few habitual errors, and a short list is easy to check for.

### Writing under time pressure

In a timed exam (for example, a 120-minute final with a long response), budget the time before you begin: a short planning stage (read the prompt twice, decide the thesis, sketch topic sentences), drafting, and a final review for the thesis, the topic sentences and obvious errors. Skipping planning usually costs more time than it saves. Leave minutes for a last read, because even a short review catches omissions and slips. Law-school exams and the LSAT's writing task reward the same discipline: a clear position, organized support and visible reasoning.

@example
title: Using a reverse outline
problem: A draft has four body paragraphs with these one-line summaries: (1) Cites a study showing sleep loss harms grades. (2) Describes how long the school day is. (3) Explains a later start time's costs and answers them. (4) Cites a second study that sleep loss harms grades. The thesis is that the school should start later. What should the writer do?
step: Note what each paragraph says and does. Paragraphs 1 and 4 make the same point, supported by different studies.
step: Merge 1 and 4 into one paragraph with a strong topic sentence and both studies as evidence, with analysis of why the evidence supports the thesis.
step: Examine paragraph 2: it describes the schedule but does not argue for the thesis. Cut it or turn it into a claim, for example that the current schedule begins before most teenagers' bodies are ready.
step: Keep paragraph 3, the counterargument and rebuttal, and place it after the reasons. The revised order: reason (sleep and grades), reason (schedule fit), counterargument and rebuttal, conclusion.

@mistakes
- Proofreading before revising: polishing sentences in paragraphs that will be cut.
- Revising only by changing words, not the argument or structure.
- Rejecting all feedback, or accepting every suggestion without thinking about it.
- Leaving no time to revise on a timed task.
