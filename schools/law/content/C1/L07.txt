@article
A thesis that has never met an objection is untested. A **counterargument** is a reasonable position or objection against your thesis; a **rebuttal** is your response to it. Including both makes your essay more credible, because it shows that you understand the issue well enough to see where it is contested. Lawyers know this well: a brief that ignores the other side's best argument loses credibility with the court, and an advocate must prepare for what the opponent will say.

### Why address opposing views

- It earns trust. Readers who hold the opposing view can see you took it seriously.
- It protects you. A weakness you name and answer cannot be used against you as a surprise.
- It sharpens the thesis. Often, an objection shows that your claim needs a qualification, and a qualified claim is easier to defend.

### Be fair: the steel man

Avoid the **straw man** (see Week 8): distorting the opposing view into a weaker version that is easy to knock over. Instead, present the **strongest** version, sometimes called a *steel man*. Test: would a thoughtful supporter of the other side read your paragraph and say, "Yes, that is our best point"? Use neutral wording and, where possible, a real source for the view.

### Rebuttal moves

1. **Refute.** Show that the objection is mistaken: its evidence is weak or misread, or its reasoning has a gap. This is the strongest response when it is available.
2. **Concede and qualify.** Grant that the objection is right as far as it goes, then show that it does not defeat the thesis, or narrow your thesis so that it fits. Conceding what is true makes the rest of your case more believable.
3. **Reframe (outweigh).** Accept the objection but argue that other considerations matter more. Cost, risk and principle are often weighed against each other.
4. **Show a limit.** Show that the objection applies only in special cases that your thesis does not cover.

### Structure of the passage

A good counterargument passage has four parts:

1. **Signal** that you are moving to the other side: "Critics of this policy reasonably point out that …"
2. **State** the objection fairly and, if you can, with its best support.
3. **Turn**: a clear pivot such as "Yet," "Even so," "Admittedly, … but."
4. **Answer** the objection and link back to the thesis.

Position varies: before the conclusion is common; at the start works when the essay is mainly an answer to a rival view; within a body paragraph works for objections to a single reason.

### The Toulmin vocabulary

The Toulmin model of everyday argument names six parts: the **claim**, the **grounds** (evidence), the **warrant** (the assumption that links grounds to claim), the **backing** for the warrant, the **qualifier** ("probably," "in most cases") and the **rebuttal** (conditions under which the claim would fail). Using this vocabulary, a counterargument often attacks your warrant or points to a condition your qualifier should cover, and a strong essay anticipates it.

### A caution on tone

Fairness is not hesitation. State your thesis with confidence, and state the opposing view with respect. Words like "ridiculous" or "obviously" signal that you could not answer the objection on the merits.

@example
title: A counterargument paragraph with a rebuttal
problem: Thesis: High schools should start no earlier than 8:30 a.m. Write a fair counterargument and rebuttal paragraph about the cost of bus rescheduling.
step: Choose the strongest opposing point, not the weakest: rescheduling buses is expensive and may force changes to elementary start times and after-school activities.
step: Signal and state fairly: "Some districts argue, with reason, that later high school start times require new bus routes, which can raise costs and push elementary schools to earlier hours."
step: Turn and answer with a concession and evidence: "This concern is real, and it explains why some districts have hesitated. Yet districts that staggered existing routes instead of adding buses report little or no added cost, and the cost of chronic sleep deprivation, such as lower attendance, falls on families and schools."
step: Link to the thesis: "The cost objection therefore shows how to implement the change, not a reason to avoid it." The paragraph ends in support of the thesis.

@mistakes
- Choosing a weak or silly objection so that the rebuttal is easy (a straw man).
- Mentioning the objection and then not answering it.
- Conceding so much that the essay no longer supports its own thesis.
- Using dismissive wording ("only a fool would think …") instead of reasons.

@practice self
prompt: Thesis: "Universities should require students to take a course in writing." Write a counterargument and rebuttal paragraph (100 to 150 words) in which the objection is that the requirement adds cost and delays graduation.
solution: Model: Some argue, reasonably, that a required writing course adds tuition cost and may delay graduation for students already close to their credit limits. These concerns are real for students with heavy requirements. However, the course can be designed to count toward existing general-education credits, which removes the delay for most students, and clear writing is useful in nearly every career. The cost of one course is small beside the lifelong advantage of writing well. A single course taken early also helps students in every later class that requires papers. The objection therefore points to how the requirement should be designed, not to dropping it.
rubric: States the objection fairly, in its strongest form, with a signal phrase.
rubric: Contains a clear turn and an answer that uses a reason or evidence.
rubric: Ends by linking back to the thesis without mockery or overstatement.
hint: Name the cost and delay fairly first, then say which move you will use (refute, concede or outweigh).
