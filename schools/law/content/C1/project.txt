@project
title: Argumentative research essay
overview: Choose a debated public question, research both sides, and write an 1,800-word argumentative essay with a clear thesis, evidence from at least three sources, a fair counterargument with rebuttal, and correct citations. You will plan, draft, cut, revise and edit it using the skills of Weeks 1 to 12.
goal: Build and defend an arguable thesis with organized, well-evidenced reasoning.
goal: Integrate at least three credible sources with signal phrases, accurate quotation, paraphrase and a consistent citation style, with no plagiarism.
goal: State the strongest opposing view fairly and answer it.
goal: Revise for clarity and concision and proofread to a professional standard.
step: Choose a question that is debated by informed people and narrow enough for 1,800 words (for example, whether a city should adopt a specific policy). Write it as a question, then draft a working thesis with a "because" clause.
step: Research both sides. Find at least three credible sources, including at least one that disagrees with your thesis. Record full citation details and write a three-sentence fair summary of each source (Week 2). Evaluate each source for authority, accuracy, currency and purpose (Week 5).
step: Make a topic sentence outline: thesis, three to four reasons stated as claims, a counterargument and rebuttal section, and a conclusion. Note under each topic sentence the evidence you will use (Weeks 3 and 4).
step: Write a full draft of about 2,200 words without worrying about length. Frame every quotation with a signal phrase and analysis, and cite in one style (APA or MLA) as you write (Week 6).
step: Write the counterargument paragraph in the strongest fair form of the opposing view and choose a rebuttal move. Check the whole draft for fallacies (Weeks 7 and 8).
step: Cut the draft to about 1,800 words by removing whole units first and then padding (Week 9). Make a reverse outline and fix any repeated, missing or off-thesis paragraphs (Week 12).
step: Edit for grammar, punctuation and style, then proofread a clean copy aloud and against your error log (Week 10). Prepare the references or works-cited list and check that every in-text citation has an entry.
step: Give the essay to a reader who knows nothing about the topic and ask them to state your thesis and name your main reason; revise any part that was unclear (Week 11).
deliverable: The final essay of about 1,800 words with a references or works-cited list.
deliverable: Your topic sentence outline and your reverse outline, showing how the structure changed.
deliverable: A one-paragraph reflection naming the biggest revision you made and why.
rubric: Thesis is arguable, specific and supported throughout, and the conclusion follows from the argument.
rubric: At least three credible sources are integrated with signal phrases, accurate quotation and paraphrase, and consistent citations with no plagiarism.
rubric: The counterargument is fair and strongest-form, and the rebuttal answers it with reasons or evidence; no fallacies in the essay's own reasoning.
rubric: Paragraphs are unified, topic sentences read as an argument, and transitions match the logic.
rubric: Prose is clear and concise, grammar and punctuation are correct, and the essay meets the length target.
material: **Planning template.** Question: ____ ? Working thesis: ____ because ____. Reason 1 (claim, evidence, source): ____. Reason 2: ____. Reason 3: ____. Strongest objection: ____. Rebuttal move (refute, concede and qualify, reframe): ____. Conclusion: what follows if the thesis is right: ____.
material: **Citation patterns (invented example source).** APA in-text for a quotation: (Rivera, 2019, p. 42). APA in-text for a paraphrase: (Rivera, 2019). MLA in-text for a quotation: (Rivera 42). Whichever style you choose, use it for every source and match each in-text citation to one entry in the list at the end.
tutor: I think my thesis is a topic, not a claim. Can you ask me questions that would help me make it arguable and specific?
tutor: Here is my counterargument paragraph. Is it the strongest fair version of the opposing view, and does my rebuttal actually answer it?
tutor: Here are my topic sentences. Can you read only those and tell me what argument you think my essay makes?

@review
### Week by week

- **Week 1, reading arguments:** find the conclusion, number the premises, notice indicator words, name the unstated assumption.
- **Week 2, summary and paraphrase:** keep claim, main reasons and qualifications; use your own words and structure; add no opinion.
- **Week 3, thesis and structure:** an arguable, specific, scoped thesis; a topic sentence outline in which every sentence supports it.
- **Week 4, paragraphs and transitions:** claim, evidence, analysis, link; transitions that signal the real logic.
- **Week 5, evidence and sources:** relevant, sufficient, representative evidence; primary versus secondary sources; authority, accuracy, currency, purpose; correlation is not causation.
- **Week 6, quotation and citation:** the quotation sandwich; patchwriting is plagiarism; APA, MLA, Chicago; legal writing uses its own citation manual.
- **Week 7, counterargument:** state the opposing view in its strongest fair form, then refute, concede and qualify, or reframe.
- **Week 8, fallacies:** ad hominem, straw man, false dilemma, slippery slope, begging the question, post hoc, hasty generalization; name the fallacy and explain why it fails.
- **Week 9, style:** characters as subjects, actions as verbs; cut padding but keep necessary hedges; a third off means $W - W/3$ words.
- **Week 10, grammar and punctuation:** fragments, splices, restrictive clauses, agreement, modifiers, parallelism, and ambiguity that changes meaning.
- **Week 11, audience:** analyze the rhetorical situation; bottom line first; translate technical content accurately.
- **Week 12, revision:** revise, then edit, then proofread; reverse outline; feedback; time budgeting.

### Self-check

- Can I state my thesis, my three best reasons and the strongest objection in one minute without notes?
- Can I summarize any 1,000-word argument in 150 words without distortion?
- Is every quotation framed, explained and cited, and every paraphrase truly rephrased?
- Can I name a fallacy and say exactly why it fails?
- Did I revise before I edited?

### Most common errors

1. A thesis that announces a topic or states a fact.
2. Evidence with no analysis.
3. Patchwriting and missing quotation marks.
4. A straw-man counterargument.
5. Cutting qualifications instead of padding.
6. Proofreading before revising.
7. Naming a fallacy without explaining it.

@exam mc
prompt: A student writes a paragraph for the thesis "Cities should abolish late fines at public libraries." The paragraph reads: "Fines are widely used. Everyone knows libraries are wonderful. In one hypothetical district, abolishing fines raised returns by 12 percent. The mayor, who dislikes libraries, opposes the change, so his view should be ignored." Which revision would most improve the paragraph's reasoning?
choice: Add a sentence on how many books the library owns.
choice*: Cut the unrelated sentences and the attack on the mayor, add a topic sentence stating the claim, and add analysis explaining why the returns data support the claim.
choice: Replace "hypothetical district" with "a district" and add no analysis.
choice: Add a transition such as "therefore" before the first sentence.
hint: Look for unity, analysis and a fallacy.
solution: The paragraph lacks a topic sentence and analysis, includes filler, and dismisses the mayor ad hominem. The correct choice repairs all three. Hiding that the district is hypothetical would be misleading, and adding a transition does not fix the structure.

@exam num
prompt: A 750-word draft is cut by a third. Quotations may then make up at most 10 percent of the shortened essay. What is the maximum number of words of quotation?
answer: 50
check: (750 - 750 / 3) * 0.10
unit: words
hint: First find the shortened length: remove one third of 750.
hint: The shortened essay has 500 words.
solution: A third of 750 is 250, so the essay has $750 - 250 = 500$ words. Ten percent of 500 is 50 words.

@exam self
prompt: Read this invented passage and do three things. (a) Summarize it in about 40 words, fairly. (b) State its conclusion and one unstated assumption. (c) Name one fallacy-risk in the argument and say how a careful writer would fix it. Passage: "The town should end its free summer swim program. Attendance dropped by a third over two years, and the lifeguard budget rose by a fifth. Some residents say the program helps low-income families, but those families can use the public beach. The program is clearly a failure."
hint: Keep the author's hedges and the objection the author answers; then ask what must be true for the conclusion to follow.
solution: (a) The author argues the town should end its free summer swim program because attendance fell by a third while lifeguard costs rose by a fifth, and dismisses the view that it helps low-income families by pointing to the public beach. (b) Conclusion: the town should end the program. Assumption: falling attendance and rising cost alone show that the program is not worth keeping, and the public beach is an adequate substitute for low-income families (that it is as accessible and safe as the pool). (c) The last sentence overstates ("clearly a failure") and the argument weighs only cost and attendance; a careful writer would qualify the claim, supply evidence on whether the beach is a real substitute, and answer the objection fairly rather than dismiss it.
rubric: The summary reports the conclusion, the two reasons and the answered objection, in about 40 words and without added opinion.
rubric: Correctly identifies the conclusion and an assumption that is not stated.
rubric: Identifies a real weakness (overstatement, dismissed objection or incomplete weighing) and proposes a specific fix.
