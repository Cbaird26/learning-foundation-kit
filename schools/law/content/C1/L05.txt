@article
An argument is only as strong as the evidence beneath it. **Evidence** is any information offered to show that a claim is true or reasonable. Learning to choose and judge evidence is the foundation of research writing, and it is the daily work of lawyers, who are expected to know what supports a position and how much.

### Kinds of evidence

- **Statistics and data:** numbers from surveys, experiments or records. Strong for questions of scale; vulnerable to bad sampling and misleading presentation.
- **Examples and cases:** specific instances. Vivid, but a single case may be unrepresentative.
- **Expert testimony:** the judgment of someone with relevant training. Strong when the expert speaks within their field and is not simply an interested party.
- **Documents and records:** laws, reports, letters, minutes. Often the best evidence of what was said or done.
- **Analogy:** an argument that cases alike in relevant ways should be treated alike. (Lawyers use this constantly in reasoning from precedent.)

### Sufficient, relevant, representative

Three tests apply to any piece of evidence:

1. **Relevant:** does it bear on this claim, and not just on the topic?
2. **Sufficient:** is there enough of it? One study or one story rarely settles a broad claim.
3. **Representative:** does it reflect the group or situation the claim covers? A poll of gym members does not describe the whole city.

### Types of sources

A **primary source** is original material: a statute, a court opinion, a dataset, a diary, an interview you conducted. A **secondary source** interprets or analyzes primary material: a journal article about a statute, a book about the period. A **tertiary source** summarizes secondary sources: an encyclopedia or textbook. Which counts as which depends on your question. A newspaper article is secondary for a historical event but primary if your essay analyzes how newspapers covered the event.

**Scholarly sources** are written by experts for experts, usually reviewed by other experts (**peer review**) before publication, and cite their own sources. **Popular sources** (magazines, many news sites) are written for general readers, are reviewed by editors rather than specialists, and may cite sparingly. Both can be useful; peer review is a strong signal but not a guarantee, and a news report can be the right source for a recent event.

### Evaluating a source: a checklist

Ask:

- **Authority:** who wrote it, and what qualifies them? What institution stands behind it?
- **Accuracy:** are claims backed by data and citations? Can you verify them elsewhere? (Look for **lateral reading**: leave the page and see what other reliable sources say about the source and the claim.)
- **Currency:** when was it published, and does the question depend on recent information?
- **Purpose and bias:** why does this exist: to inform, to sell, to persuade? A source with a stake in the outcome is not useless, but its claims need independent confirmation.
- **Fit:** does it actually address your question?

Be careful with **statistical** claims. Ask about the sample (size and selection), what was measured, and whether a **correlation** is being presented as **causation**. Two things moving together do not show that one caused the other; a third factor, or the reverse direction, may explain it.

### Building a source set

For a research essay, gather sources that cover both sides of the question so you can represent them fairly. Keep a record of each source's full details as you go; reconstructing citations later invites mistakes and, sometimes, plagiarism. Prefer the original source over a report of it: if a news story cites a study, read the study.

@example
title: Judging evidence for a claim
problem: A student claims "Daily recess improves elementary students' classroom behavior." Evidence offered: (a) a peer-reviewed study of 12 districts that compared behavior reports before and after adding recess; (b) a blog post by a recess-equipment company saying that "teachers love recess"; (c) the student's own memory of liking recess. Evaluate each.
step: Test relevance. (a) bears directly on classroom behavior; (b) concerns teacher opinion and is only loosely related; (c) concerns enjoyment, not behavior.
step: Test authority and purpose. (a) is peer reviewed and has no obvious stake in the result. (b) comes from a company that sells equipment, so it has an interest in the conclusion.
step: Test sufficiency and representativeness. (a) covers 12 districts, a reasonable base, though the student should still check whether the districts were typical. (c) is one person's recollection.
step: Conclude: use (a) as the main evidence; treat (b) cautiously, if at all, and flag the seller's interest; drop (c) or use it only as an illustration, not proof.

@mistakes
- Treating any source found online or in a database as automatically reliable, or treating a peer-reviewed article as unquestionable.
- Using an interested source as if it were neutral without noting its stake.
- Presenting a correlation as proof of causation.
- Relying on one example or one study for a broad claim.

choice: The study must be wrong because the claim is absurd.
choice*: The two may both rise with a third factor, such as hot weather, so correlation does not show causation.
choice: Ice cream should be banned near water.
choice: The result shows only that drownings are common.
hint: Ask whether something else could explain both trends.
solution: Warm weather increases both swimming and ice cream sales, which explains the correlation without causation. A better response identifies the confounding factor rather than rejecting the data.

@practice self
prompt: A claim says that a new job-training program "works." Write five questions you would ask about a study that is offered as evidence, covering sample, measurement, comparison, causation and source.
hint: Think sample, measurement, comparison, causation and source.
solution: (1) How many participants were studied, and how were they chosen? (2) What exactly counts as "works": jobs found, wages, or retention? (3) Was there a comparison group of similar people who did not take the program? (4) Could something else, such as a rising economy or participants' prior motivation, explain the results? (5) Who funded and published the study, and was it reviewed by independent experts?
rubric: Asks about the size and selection of the sample.
rubric: Asks how success was measured and whether there was a comparison group.
rubric: Raises causation or alternative explanations.
rubric: Raises the source's authority, funding or review.

@quiz mc
prompt: Which source would be best for evidence about the current state of a scientific question that changed rapidly in the last two years?
choice: A general encyclopedia article last revised eight years ago.
choice*: A recent peer-reviewed review article by researchers in the field.
choice: An advertisement from a company selling a related product.
choice: A friend's social media post summarizing a news headline.
hint: Weigh currency, authority and purpose.
solution: A recent review by field experts combines currency and authority. The encyclopedia is outdated, the ad has a stake, and the post is unverified secondhand information.
