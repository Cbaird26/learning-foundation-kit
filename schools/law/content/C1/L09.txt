@article
Clear writing is not simple thinking; it is complicated thinking made easy to follow. **Clarity** means the reader can tell who did what. **Concision** means every word earns its place. Concision is not brevity at any cost: a short sentence that omits a necessary qualification is worse than a longer accurate one. Courts, clients and graders all read under time pressure, so prose that gets to the point is a professional skill.

### Start with characters and actions

Readers understand a sentence fastest when its **subject** names the main character and its **verb** names the main action. Compare:

- *Weak:* "The implementation of the new policy by the committee resulted in a reduction of complaints."
- *Better:* "The committee's new policy reduced complaints."

The weak sentence hides the action ("implemented," "reduced") inside nouns (**nominalizations**: *implementation, reduction*) and buries the character ("the committee") in a prepositional phrase. To revise, ask: *Who is doing what?* Make that the subject and verb.

### The usual sources of wordiness

1. **Empty openers and filler:** *it is important to note that, there is/there are, the fact that, in order to, it should be noted.* Delete or replace: "in order to" becomes "to"; "due to the fact that" becomes "because"; "at this point in time" becomes "now."
2. **Redundancy:** *past history, end result, completely eliminate, each and every.* Keep one.
3. **Nominalizations:** turn *make a decision* into *decide*, *conduct an investigation of* into *investigate*.
4. **Needless passives.** The **passive voice** ("The ball was thrown by Ana") is not an error. It is right when the actor is unknown or unimportant, or when the receiver is the topic ("The defendant was acquitted"). It is wordy when it hides an actor the reader needs: "Mistakes were made" says nothing about who made them.
5. **Stacked modifiers and vague words:** *very, really, quite, kind of, a lot, things, aspects.* Replace with a precise word or delete.
6. **Wordy sentence frames:** "There are many students who struggle" becomes "Many students struggle."

### Precision and qualification

Cutting is not all. Prefer **concrete** words ("rose 6 percent") to abstractions ("a significant increase"). Keep **hedges** the evidence demands: "most," "likely," "in these cases." Do not delete a qualification to save a word. Define technical terms the first time you use them.

### Cutting a draft by a third

Cutting deliberately beats trimming here and there. Work in passes:

1. **Cut whole units first:** a repeated point, an aside, an example that adds nothing to the thesis.
2. **Merge sentences** that make the same point.
3. **Prune phrases** with the list above.
4. **Reread** to check meaning: did every claim, qualification and piece of evidence that matters survive?
5. **Count words** and compare with the target. To cut a draft of $W$ words by a third, the target is $W - \frac{W}{3} = \frac{2W}{3}$.

Read the revision aloud. If you stumble, the reader will.

### Variety and rhythm

Mix sentence lengths. A very short sentence after two long ones carries emphasis. Put the most important idea at the end of the sentence, where the reader's attention peaks (the *stress position*). Avoid long strings of sentences that all begin the same way.

@example
title: Trimming a wordy sentence
problem: Revise: "It is important to note that there are a number of students who are unable to attend the meeting due to the fact that they have classes at that time." (30 words)
step: Delete the filler opener "It is important to note that" and the frame "there are a number of students who are."
step: Find the real subject and action: students cannot attend. Replace "are unable to attend" with "cannot attend."
step: Replace "due to the fact that" with "because" and "at that time" with "then": "Some students cannot attend the meeting because they have classes then."
step: Count: the revision has 11 words, saving 19. The meaning is intact, and "some" keeps the original's "a number of" qualification.

@mistakes
- Cutting qualifications and evidence, not padding, to hit a word count.
- Replacing every passive mechanically, even when the actor is unimportant or unknown.
- Swapping long words for fancy ones; plain words are usually clearer.
- Reading only for length, not for what the sentence means now that words are gone.
