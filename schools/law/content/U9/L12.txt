@article
A secured party's rights under Article 9 are state law; the **Bankruptcy Code** (Title 11 of the U.S. Code) is federal law that can override them. A secured creditor is generally in the best position in bankruptcy, because it is paid from its collateral before unsecured creditors, but bankruptcy adds **delay**, **limits** on enforcement, and **avoidance risks**. Perfection is the creditor's main protection.

### The automatic stay (§ 362)
The filing of a bankruptcy petition **automatically stays** acts to **repossess** property of the estate, to enforce liens, to collect prepetition debts, and to create or perfect liens (§ 362(a)). A repossession or sale after the petition without relief from the stay is a **stay violation**; acts in violation are void, and an individual debtor injured by a **willful** violation may recover damages (§ 362(k)). The creditor may ask for **relief from the stay** (§ 362(d)) for **cause**, including **lack of adequate protection** of its interest, or, as to property, if the debtor has **no equity** in it and it is **not necessary to an effective reorganization**. **Adequate protection** (§ 361) may take the form of periodic payments, additional or replacement liens, or other relief to offset a decline in the collateral's value.

### The trustee's avoiding powers
- **Strong-arm power (§ 544(a)(1)).** On the petition date the trustee has the rights of a **hypothetical lien creditor**. Under UCC § 9-317(a)(2), a lien creditor beats a security interest that is **unperfected** at that moment, so the trustee can **avoid an unperfected security interest**, and the avoided lien is preserved for the estate (§ 551). The creditor is left with an **unsecured** claim.
- **Preferences (§ 547).** The trustee may avoid a transfer of the debtor's property **to or for the benefit of a creditor**, **on account of an antecedent debt**, **made while the debtor was insolvent** (presumed during the 90 days before the petition), **within 90 days** before the petition (**one year** for **insiders**), that lets the creditor **receive more than it would in Chapter 7**. A security interest is a transfer. **Late perfection** matters: a transfer is deemed made when it takes effect between the parties **if perfected within 30 days**; otherwise, when it is **perfected** (§ 547(e)(2)). So a security interest perfected more than 30 days after attachment may be a preference if perfection occurred within the 90 days. Defenses include contemporaneous exchange (§ 547(c)(1)), ordinary-course payments (§ 547(c)(2)), a **purchase-money security interest** perfected within **30 days** after the debtor receives the collateral (§ 547(c)(3)), and the **improvement-in-position test** for floating liens on inventory and receivables (§ 547(c)(5)).

### Valuation: § 506(a)
A creditor has a **secured claim** only **to the extent of the value of its collateral**; the rest is an **unsecured claim**. So a creditor owed \$50,000 secured by collateral worth \$35,000 has a \$35,000 secured claim and a \$15,000 unsecured claim. An **oversecured** creditor may also recover **postpetition interest, fees and costs** (§ 506(b)). In a Chapter 13 case where the debtor keeps the collateral, value is **replacement value**, not foreclosure value (*Associates Commercial Corp. v. Rash* (1997)).

### After-acquired property: § 552
A prepetition **after-acquired property clause** does not reach property the debtor acquires **after the petition** (§ 552(a)), but the lien continues in **proceeds, products, offspring and profits** of prepetition collateral, to the extent provided by the security agreement and nonbankruptcy law, subject to the court's consideration of the equities (§ 552(b)).

### Cash collateral: § 363
A trustee or debtor in possession **may not use cash collateral** (cash and equivalents in which the estate and another entity have an interest, such as proceeds of a secured creditor's inventory) unless **each interested entity consents** or the **court authorizes** the use after notice and a hearing (§ 363(c)(2)), and the court must **condition** the use to provide **adequate protection** (§ 363(e)). Property may also be sold free and clear of liens (§ 363(f)) with the lien attaching to proceeds.

### Plans and the secured creditor
- **Chapter 7**: the debtor may **surrender** the collateral, **redeem** it by paying its value in a lump sum (§ 722), or **reaffirm** the debt (§ 524(c)).
- **Chapter 13**: a plan may **bifurcate** (cram down) an undersecured claim to the collateral's value if the creditor keeps its lien and receives payments with a **present value** at least equal to the allowed secured claim (§ 1325(a)(5)(B)). The interest rate is determined by a formula approach, a prime-plus rate (*Till v. SCS Credit Corp.* (2004), plurality).
- **The 910-day rule** (§ 1325(a), hanging paragraph): § 506 does **not** split the claim of a creditor holding a **purchase-money security interest in a motor vehicle acquired for the debtor's personal use within 910 days** before the petition, or in **other personal property acquired within one year**, so the whole debt is treated as secured and cannot be crammed down.
- **Chapter 11**: a plan can be confirmed over a secured class's rejection if it is fair and equitable, which includes keeping liens and receiving payments with present value equal to the collateral's value, or selling with liens attaching to proceeds, or giving the **indubitable equivalent** (§ 1129(b)(2)(A)).

### Applying it
For a secured creditor facing bankruptcy: (1) Was the security interest **attached and perfected** on the petition date? (2) If perfected late, is there a **preference**? (3) What is the **secured claim** under § 506(a)? (4) Is there a **stay** problem for repossession? (5) Can the debtor use **cash collateral**? Always separate **pre-bankruptcy enforcement** (Article 9 Part 6) from **in-bankruptcy** rules.
@example
title: Late perfection and the trustee
problem: On January 1, Lender lends \$100,000 to Debtor Co. under a signed security agreement covering its equipment. Lender does not file until March 15. Debtor Co. is insolvent throughout, and files a Chapter 7 petition on April 20. Can the trustee avoid Lender's security interest?
step: Strong-arm power: on April 20 Lender's interest was perfected (it filed March 15), so § 544(a)(1) alone does not defeat it; the trustee's hypothetical lien creditor arose after perfection.
step: Preference: the security interest secured a debt already owing (the January 1 loan) by the time it was perfected, so for preference purposes the transfer is on account of an antecedent debt, and Debtor Co. was insolvent.
step: Timing: perfection occurred 73 days after attachment (in a non-leap year), more than 30 days, so under § 547(e)(2) the transfer is deemed made on March 15, when perfected. That is 36 days before the petition, within the 90-day period.
step: Conclusion: the trustee can likely avoid the lien as a preference, because the creditor would receive more than in Chapter 7 (it would be paid from the collateral instead of sharing as an unsecured creditor). Lender becomes an unsecured creditor and the lien is preserved for the estate.
@mistakes
- Repossessing property after the debtor files a petition without first obtaining relief from the stay.
- Treating an undersecured creditor as fully secured. The secured claim is limited to the collateral's value (§ 506(a)).
- Overlooking that a security interest perfected more than 30 days after attachment is deemed transferred at perfection, which may fall in the preference period.
- Confusing the strong-arm power (unperfected on the petition date) with the preference power (late perfection within the look-back period).

@practice self
prompt: Debtor Co. owes Lender \$90,000 under a loan secured by its machinery (equipment) and defaults. Lender repossesses the machinery peaceably, incurs \$3,000 in reasonable expenses, and mails notice of a private sale 12 days before it sells the machinery to Lender's wholly owned affiliate for \$40,000. Lender claims a \$53,000 deficiency. Debtor Co. proves that a commercially reasonable disposition to an unrelated buyer would have brought \$65,000. Assume no bankruptcy case has been filed. Analyze the repossession and Lender's deficiency claim.
hint: Test the repossession, the notice, the affiliate sale and then compute the deficiency under § 9-626 or § 9-615(f).
solution: Issue: whether Lender repossessed and disposed of the machinery properly, and what deficiency it can recover. Rule: after default a secured party may repossess without judicial process if there is no breach of the peace (§ 9-609); in a non-consumer transaction, notice sent after default 10 days or more before disposition is reasonable as a matter of law (§ 9-612(b)); a private disposition to the secured party itself is allowed only for collateral sold on a recognized market (§ 9-610(c)); if the buyer is related to the secured party and the price is significantly below the range of prices of a disposition to an unrelated buyer, the deficiency is computed as if proceeds were the amount a complying sale would have brought (§ 9-615(f)); in a non-consumer case a noncomplying secured party's deficiency is limited under § 9-626. Application: repossession was peaceable and the 12-day notice satisfies the safe harbor, but the sale to Lender's affiliate at \$40,000, far below the \$65,000 a complying sale would have brought, is subject to § 9-615(f) (and fails the commercial reasonableness requirement of § 9-610(b) if no market exception applies). The deficiency is therefore computed as obligation plus expenses minus \$65,000: \$90,000 plus \$3,000 minus \$65,000, or \$28,000. Conclusion: Lender's deficiency claim is limited to \$28,000, not \$53,000.
rubric: Concludes the repossession and notice were proper (peaceable; 12 days in a non-consumer case).
rubric: Recognizes the affiliate sale at a significantly low price and applies § 9-615(f) or § 9-626.
rubric: Computes the deficiency as \$28,000 using the \$65,000 figure.
