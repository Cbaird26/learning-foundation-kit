@article
Article 2 remedies aim to put the aggrieved party in as good a position as performance would have (§ 1-305), but not a better one. Each side has a menu of remedies, and the key skill is picking the right measure and computing it.

### Anticipatory repudiation and assurance
If one party **repudiates** before performance, and the loss will substantially impair the value of the contract, the other party may **await performance for a commercially reasonable time** or resort to any remedy (§ 2-610), and may suspend its own performance. The repudiating party can **retract** until the other has cancelled, materially changed position or indicated that it considers the repudiation final (§ 2-611). When reasonable grounds for insecurity arise, a party may **demand adequate assurance** in writing, and may suspend performance until it is received; failure to provide assurance within a reasonable time (not exceeding 30 days) is a repudiation (§ 2-609).

### Buyer's remedies
When the seller fails to deliver or repudiates, or the buyer rightfully rejects or revokes (§ 2-711), the buyer may cancel and recover the price paid, and also:
1. **Cover (§ 2-712).** The buyer may, **in good faith and without unreasonable delay**, buy substitute goods. Damages are
$$\text{cover price} - \text{contract price} + \text{incidental damages} + \text{consequential damages} - \text{expenses saved}.$$
Failure to cover does not bar other remedies.
2. **Market damages (§ 2-713).** Without cover, damages are market price **at the time the buyer learned of the breach** (at the place for tender), minus the contract price, plus incidental and consequential damages, less expenses saved.
3. **Specific performance or replevin (§ 2-716)** where goods are unique or in other proper circumstances (such as inability to cover).

**Incidental damages (§ 2-715(1))**: expenses reasonably incurred in inspection, receipt, transportation and care and custody of rejected goods, and commercially reasonable charges for effecting cover. **Consequential damages (§ 2-715(2))**: losses resulting from general or particular requirements and needs of which the seller had **reason to know** at contracting and that **could not reasonably be prevented by cover**, and injury to person or property proximately resulting from breach of warranty. The rule parallels *Hadley v. Baxendale* (1854).

**Accepted goods (§ 2-714).** The buyer's damages for breach in regard to accepted goods are the difference at the time and place of acceptance between the **value of the goods accepted** and the **value they would have had if as warranted**, plus incidental and consequential damages. The buyer may deduct damages from the price still due after notifying the seller (§ 2-717).

### Seller's remedies (§ 2-703)
If the buyer wrongfully rejects or repudiates, or fails to pay, the seller may withhold delivery, **stop delivery** (§ 2-705), **resell** (§ 2-706), recover **damages for nonacceptance** (§ 2-708), recover the **price** in limited cases (§ 2-709), or cancel.
1. **Resale (§ 2-706).** The seller may resell in good faith and in a commercially reasonable manner, and recover contract price minus resale price, plus incidental damages, less expenses saved. A **private sale** requires reasonable notice to the buyer; a **public sale** requires notice of time and place. A seller who resells in good faith need not account to the buyer for any profit.
2. **Market damages (§ 2-708(1)).** Without resale, contract price minus **market price at the time and place of tender**, plus incidental damages, less expenses saved.
3. **Lost profits (§ 2-708(2)).** If the market formula is inadequate to put the seller in as good a position as performance, damages are the **profit (including reasonable overhead)** the seller would have made, plus incidental damages, less credits. This protects the **lost-volume seller**, whose supply is unlimited, so that the resale to another would have been a second sale in any event.
4. **Action for the price (§ 2-709).** Only for (a) goods **accepted**; (b) **conforming goods lost or damaged after risk of loss passed** to the buyer; or (c) **goods identified to the contract** if the seller is unable after reasonable effort to resell them at a reasonable price. Otherwise the seller's remedy is damages.

**Seller and insolvent buyer (§ 2-702).** A seller discovering that the buyer received goods on credit while insolvent may **reclaim** them on demand within **ten days** after receipt (no time limit if the buyer misrepresented solvency in writing within three months before delivery), subject to the rights of a **buyer in ordinary course or other good faith purchaser**.

### Liquidated damages (§ 2-718)
Parties may fix damages by agreement, but only in an amount **reasonable in light of anticipated or actual harm**, difficulty of proof and inconvenience of other remedies; an unreasonably large amount is a void **penalty**.

### Applying it
Identify who is the aggrieved party and the breach; choose among cover, market, resale, lost profits and the price; compute from the stated figures; add incidental and (if foreseeable and not avoidable by cover) consequential damages; subtract expenses saved.
@example
title: Cover damages
problem: Seller agrees to deliver 2,000 units to Buyer at \$25 per unit, a contract price of \$50,000. Seller repudiates. Buyer in good faith and without delay buys 2,000 substitute units from another supplier at \$29 per unit, paying \$1,500 in commercially reasonable broker and transport charges to arrange cover. Buyer had paid nothing in advance. What are Buyer's damages?
step: Identify the remedy. Seller repudiated and Buyer covered in good faith and without unreasonable delay, so § 2-712 applies. Cover is not required, but when made properly it is the measure that fits.
step: Compute the cover differential: cover price is 2,000 times \$29, or \$58,000; contract price is \$50,000; so the difference is \$8,000.
step: Add incidental damages under § 2-715(1): commercially reasonable charges for effecting cover are \$1,500. There are no consequential damages and no expenses saved.
step: Total: \$8,000 plus \$1,500, or \$9,500. If Buyer had paid a deposit, Buyer could also recover it under § 2-711(1).
@mistakes
- Measuring market damages at the time of trial. Under § 2-713 the measure is at the time the buyer learned of the breach.
- Awarding a seller the full price for goods that the buyer wrongfully refused and that the seller can resell. The action for the price is limited by § 2-709.
- Forgetting to subtract expenses saved (for the seller) or to add incidental damages.
- Using resale damages without notice of a private sale, or without commercial reasonableness.
