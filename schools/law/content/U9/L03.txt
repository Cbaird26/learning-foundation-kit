@article
A **warranty** is a seller's promise or assurance about the goods, and breach of warranty is the most common claim after a sale. Article 2 recognizes four sources: warranty of title, express warranty, and two implied warranties. Warranty claims sit in contract (with UCC remedies), alongside tort claims such as strict products liability.

### Warranty of title and against infringement (§ 2-312)
The seller warrants that the title conveyed is good, the transfer rightful, and the goods are delivered free from any security interest or lien of which the buyer lacks knowledge. A **merchant** regularly dealing in goods of the kind also warrants that the goods are delivered free of any rightful claim of infringement. These warranties can be excluded only by specific language or circumstances (for example, a sheriff's sale).

### Express warranties (§ 2-313)
An express warranty arises from:
- an **affirmation of fact or promise** relating to the goods that becomes **part of the basis of the bargain**;
- a **description** of the goods that becomes part of the basis of the bargain; or
- a **sample or model** that becomes part of the basis of the bargain.
No formal words ("warrant," "guarantee") or intent to warrant is required. A mere statement of the **seller's opinion or commendation** ("the best sofa you will ever own") is **puffery** and creates no warranty. Statements in a brochure, an advertisement or on a label can qualify. Courts differ on how much reliance is needed; the Code's "basis of the bargain" language is read by many courts to presume reliance once the statement is made, which the seller can rebut.

### Implied warranty of merchantability (§ 2-314)
Unless excluded, a warranty that the goods are merchantable is **implied in a contract for their sale if the seller is a merchant with respect to goods of that kind**. Goods are merchantable if, among other things, they would pass without objection in the trade under the contract description, are fit for the **ordinary purposes** for which such goods are used, are adequately packaged and labeled, and conform to any promises on the label. Serving food or drink for consumption on the premises is a sale.

### Implied warranty of fitness for a particular purpose (§ 2-315)
Where the seller **has reason to know** any **particular purpose** for which the goods are required, and that the buyer is **relying on the seller's skill or judgment** to select suitable goods, there is an implied warranty that the goods are fit for that purpose. **The seller need not be a merchant.** A particular purpose is narrower than the ordinary purpose (a buyer who needs boots for mountaineering in snow, not just walking).

### Disclaimers (§ 2-316)
- **Express warranties.** Words of disclaimer and words of warranty are construed as consistent where possible, but a disclaimer **inconsistent** with an express warranty is inoperative to that extent (and the parol evidence rule limits what can be proved).
- **Merchantability.** To exclude it, the language must **mention merchantability**, and if in writing it must be **conspicuous**. (A conspicuous writing is required to be noticeable to a reasonable person.)
- **Fitness.** The exclusion must be **in writing and conspicuous**. A sentence such as "There are no warranties which extend beyond the description on the face hereof" suffices for fitness.
- **"As is," "with all faults"** or other language that calls the buyer's attention to the exclusion and makes plain there is no implied warranty excludes all implied warranties.
- **Examination.** If the buyer has examined the goods as fully as desired, or refused to examine them after the seller demanded it, there is no implied warranty for defects that examination ought to have revealed.
- Course of dealing, course of performance or usage of trade can also exclude implied warranties.

The federal **Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act** (15 U.S.C. § 2308) adds that a supplier who gives a written warranty on a consumer product may not disclaim implied warranties (a limited warranty may limit their duration).

### Who may sue (§ 2-318)
The Code offers three alternatives on third-party beneficiaries (the family and household members and guests of the buyer, or any person who may reasonably be expected to use the goods and is injured). States adopted different alternatives, so state the minority and majority positions only when the facts require it.

### Limiting remedies and time
- **Remedy limitations (§ 2-719).** Parties may limit remedies to repair or replacement or exclude consequential damages, but if an **exclusive remedy fails of its essential purpose** (the seller cannot repair), the buyer may pursue the Code's other remedies. An exclusion of consequential damages is valid unless **unconscionable**; limiting consequential damages for **personal injury in the case of consumer goods** is **prima facie unconscionable**; for commercial loss it is not. Courts split on whether a failed limited remedy automatically voids a consequential damages exclusion; most treat them independently.
- **Notice (§ 2-607(3)(a)).** After tender and acceptance, the buyer must **notify the seller of breach within a reasonable time after discovering it** or be barred from any remedy.
- **Limitations (§ 2-725).** An action for breach must be brought within **four years after the cause of action accrued**; the parties may reduce the period to **not less than one year** but may not extend it. A cause of action for breach of warranty accrues when **tender** is made, regardless of the buyer's knowledge, unless the warranty explicitly extends to future performance.

### Applying it
Take each warranty in turn: identify the statement or the seller's status, test the element, check for an effective disclaimer, then check notice and timing.
@example
title: A paint recommendation and a fine-print disclaimer
problem: Homeowner tells Hardware Store's clerk that she needs paint to coat an outdoor deck in a region with harsh freezing winters and asks for a recommendation. The clerk selects Brand X. The sales receipt contains, in small gray print at the bottom, "Seller makes no warranties." The paint peels within two months. Does Homeowner have an implied warranty claim?
step: Identify the warranties. Hardware Store is a merchant in paint, so merchantability (§ 2-314) is implied. Homeowner also told the clerk her particular purpose and relied on the clerk's judgment, so fitness for a particular purpose (§ 2-315) is implied. The paint may be fit for the ordinary purpose of painting (a closer call), so fitness is the stronger claim.
step: Test the fitness elements: seller had reason to know the particular purpose (a freezing-climate deck), and the buyer relied on the seller's selection. Both are met.
step: Test the disclaimer. To exclude merchantability the writing must mention merchantability and be conspicuous. To exclude fitness, it must be in writing and conspicuous. Small gray print is not conspicuous, and "no warranties" does not mention merchantability.
step: Conclusion: the disclaimer is ineffective, and Homeowner can recover for breach of the implied warranty of fitness (and probably merchantability). She should notify the store of the breach within a reasonable time and sue within four years of tender.
@mistakes
- Treating puffery ("top quality") as an express warranty, or treating any statement of fact as a warranty without asking whether it was part of the basis of the bargain.
- Requiring the seller to be a merchant for the implied warranty of fitness. Only merchantability requires a merchant.
- Using a disclaimer of merchantability that does not mention the word, or that is not conspicuous.
- Forgetting § 2-607(3)(a) notice. A buyer who does not give timely notice of breach is barred from any remedy.

Rule: an affirmation of fact or description that is part of the basis of the bargain creates an express warranty (§ 2-313); "as is" in conspicuous language excludes all implied warranties (§ 2-316(3)(a)), but a disclaimer inconsistent with an express warranty is inoperative to that extent (§ 2-316(1)).
