@article
Businesses buy and sell on preprinted forms: a purchase order from the buyer, an acknowledgment from the seller, each with its own fine print. The forms rarely match. At common law the **mirror image rule** would treat a response with different terms as a counteroffer, and the **last shot** (the last form sent before performance) would win. Section 2-207 rejects that result.

### Section 2-207(1): formation
A **definite and seasonable expression of acceptance**, or a written confirmation, operates as an acceptance **even though it states terms additional to or different from the offer**, unless acceptance is **expressly made conditional on assent to the additional or different terms**. Courts read "expressly conditional" narrowly: the response must clearly show that the offeree is unwilling to proceed without the offeror's assent to the new terms (*Dorton v. Collins & Aikman* (6th Cir. 1972)). Language such as "subject to the terms on the reverse" is not enough.

### Section 2-207(2): additional terms
If a contract is formed under (1), **additional** terms (terms not addressed in the offer) are **proposals for addition**. Between merchants they become part of the contract **unless**:
(a) the offer expressly limits acceptance to its own terms;
(b) the new terms **materially alter** it (they would cause **surprise or hardship**; official comments give a warranty disclaimer as the standard example, and a clause for interest on overdue invoices or a reasonable complaint period as non-material examples); or
(c) the offeror has already objected, or objects within a reasonable time after notice.
If either party is **not a merchant**, the additional terms remain mere proposals and do not become part unless the other side agrees.

### Different terms
When the forms **conflict** (for example, the offer says 90 days to complain and the acceptance says 10), the Code does not say. Most courts apply the **knockout rule**: the conflicting terms cancel each other, and the UCC gap-filler supplies the term. A minority treat different terms like additional terms under (2); another minority let the offer's terms control.

### Section 2-207(3): contract by conduct
If the writings do not form a contract (the response was expressly conditional) but the parties **act as though there is one** by shipping and accepting goods, a contract exists whose terms are those on which the writings **agree**, plus the Code's **supplementary terms** (gap-fillers, such as the implied warranty of merchantability). Conflicting terms drop out. The seller's expressly conditional terms do not come in merely because the buyer accepts the goods.

Some courts have departed from § 2-207 for terms in a package opened after payment: *ProCD v. Zeidenberg* (7th Cir. 1996) treated the vendor as master of the offer, while *Step-Saver Data Systems v. Wyse Technology* (3d Cir. 1991) applied § 2-207 and refused to enforce box-top terms.

### Parol evidence (§ 2-202)
A writing that the parties intended as the **final expression** of the terms it contains cannot be **contradicted** by evidence of a prior agreement or a contemporaneous oral agreement. But it may be **explained or supplemented** by (a) course of performance, course of dealing or usage of trade, and (b) evidence of **consistent additional terms**, unless the court finds the writing was intended as a **complete and exclusive** statement of the terms. A **merger clause** is strong evidence of completeness. Evidence of fraud, mistake, duress or illegality is never excluded by the rule.

### Hierarchy of sources (§ 1-303(e))
1. **Express terms.**
2. **Course of performance**, the parties' own conduct under this contract.
3. **Course of dealing**, conduct in earlier transactions between these parties.
4. **Usage of trade**, practice regularly observed in the trade.
Express terms win over all; each later item yields to the earlier ones.

### Modification (§ 2-209)
A modification of an Article 2 contract needs **no consideration** but must satisfy **good faith**. A signed agreement that excludes modification except by signed writing is enforceable (but, unless both parties are merchants, such a clause on a form supplied by a merchant must be separately signed by the other party), and the statute of frauds applies if the modified contract is within it.

### Method
For a battle of the forms: identify merchants, find the offer, decide whether the response is a definite acceptance or expressly conditional, classify each extra term as additional or different, then apply (2) or the knockout rule, or apply (3).
@example
title: A conditional acknowledgment and shipment
problem: Buyer, a merchant, sends a purchase order for 1,000 gears at \$5 each with delivery by May 1. The order is silent on warranties. Seller, a merchant, returns an acknowledgment confirming quantity, price and delivery date but adding a conspicuous disclaimer of the implied warranty of merchantability and stating, "Seller's acceptance is expressly conditional on Buyer's assent to the terms stated here." Buyer says nothing. Seller ships on April 25, and Buyer accepts and pays. The gears are defective. Does the disclaimer bind Buyer?
step: Identify the offer and the response. The purchase order is the offer. Seller's acknowledgment states different and additional terms, so ask whether it is an acceptance under § 2-207(1).
step: The acknowledgment is expressly conditional on Buyer's assent, which is the clear unwillingness to proceed that § 2-207(1) requires. It is therefore a counteroffer, not an acceptance, and the writings alone form no contract.
step: Buyer's silence is not assent, and accepting the gears is not assent to the disclaimer. But the parties performed, so § 2-207(3) recognizes a contract by conduct.
step: Under § 2-207(3) the terms are those on which the writings agree (goods, quantity, price, delivery date) plus UCC gap-fillers. The disclaimer appears only in Seller's writing, so it is out, and the implied warranty of merchantability (§ 2-314) is supplied by the Code.
step: Conclusion: the disclaimer does not bind Buyer, and Buyer can sue on the implied warranty of merchantability.
@mistakes
- Letting the last form win. Under § 2-207 the last shot does not control; the terms of the offer plus agreed terms and gap-fillers do.
- Treating "subject to our terms" as expressly conditional. Courts require clear language that the offeree will not proceed without assent.
- Applying the merchant rule of § 2-207(2) when one party is a consumer, or applying it to different terms (where most courts knock out the conflict).
- Using the parol evidence rule to exclude evidence of course of dealing or usage of trade. It supplements and explains, but cannot contradict express terms.
