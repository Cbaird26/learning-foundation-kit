@article
Article 2 of the Uniform Commercial Code (UCC) is state law, adopted in every state except Louisiana (which adopted only parts). It governs the **sale of goods**. Everything else (services, land, most software disputes, ordinary loans) is governed by the common law or other statutes. Where Article 2 is silent, the common law fills the gaps (§ 1-103), so you still use offer, acceptance, consideration, mistake and the rest.

### Scope: "transactions in goods"
Section 2-102 applies Article 2 to "transactions in goods." Under § 2-105, **goods** are all things movable at the time of identification to the contract, including the unborn young of animals, growing crops, and things attached to realty that are to be severed (§ 2-107 sets the rules for minerals, structures and crops). Money, investment securities and "things in action" (such as contract rights) are not goods. Leases of goods are in Article 2A, not Article 2.

**Mixed contracts.** When a contract combines goods and services, most courts ask which is the **predominant purpose** of the contract: is it a sale of goods with incidental service, or a service with incidental goods? (*Bonebrake v. Cox* (8th Cir. 1974) is the standard illustration.) A contract to buy and install a furnace usually looks like goods; a contract to build a house is usually a service. Some courts apply Article 2 only to the goods portion of a divisible contract. Software varies by court and by whether the deal looks like a sale of a product or a license of a service.

### Who is a merchant?
Under § 2-104 a **merchant** is a person who deals in goods of the kind, or who by occupation holds out as having knowledge or skill peculiar to the practices or goods involved, or who employs an agent or broker who does. Many Article 2 rules apply to everyone, but some apply only to merchants (firm offers, the merchant's confirmation rule, the implied warranty of merchantability, the risk of loss rules, and the battle-of-the-forms additional-terms rule). Always decide first: **is each party a merchant, and for which rule?**

### Formation
Section 2-204 relaxes the common law: a contract may be made in any manner sufficient to show agreement, even if the moment of making is uncertain, and it does not fail for indefiniteness if the parties intended a contract and there is a reasonably certain basis for a remedy. Open price, delivery and payment terms are filled by gap-fillers (§§ 2-305 to 2-310).

- **Firm offer (§ 2-205).** A merchant's signed written offer that gives assurance it will be held open is irrevocable for the stated time, or a reasonable time if none is stated, with no consideration needed, but never longer than three months. (A form supplied by the offeree must be separately signed.)
- **Acceptance (§ 2-206).** Unless the offer unambiguously requires a particular manner, an offer to buy goods for prompt shipment may be accepted by a prompt promise to ship or by prompt shipment. Shipping nonconforming goods is both an acceptance and a breach, unless the seller seasonably notifies the buyer that the shipment is only an accommodation.

### The statute of frauds (§ 2-201)
A contract for the sale of goods for **\$500 or more** is not enforceable unless there is a writing sufficient to indicate that a contract has been made, **signed by the party against whom enforcement is sought** (or the agent), and stating a **quantity**. Price, time and place of delivery need not appear, and an omitted or incorrect term is fine, but enforcement is limited to the quantity stated. Exceptions:
1. **Merchant's confirmation (§ 2-201(2)):** between merchants, a written confirmation sent within a reasonable time that satisfies the section against the sender also satisfies it against the recipient, unless the recipient gives written notice of objection within **ten days** after receiving it. This removes the defense only; the sender must still prove an agreement was actually made.
2. **Specially manufactured goods (§ 2-201(3)(a)):** not suitable for sale to others in the ordinary course of the seller's business, and the seller has made a substantial beginning of manufacture or commitments before notice of repudiation.
3. **Admission (§ 2-201(3)(b)):** the party admits in pleading, testimony or otherwise in court that a contract was made (enforceable only up to the quantity admitted).
4. **Part performance (§ 2-201(3)(c)):** goods received and accepted, or payment made and accepted, but only for the goods paid for or accepted.

(Some states have raised the dollar threshold; the Code's figure is \$500.)

### Applying it
Use IRAC: (1) Is it a sale of goods, and who is a merchant? (2) Was there an offer and acceptance under §§ 2-204 to 2-206? (3) If \$500 or more, is there a signed writing with a quantity, or an exception? (4) Conclude with the rule that decides the case.
@example
title: Oral deal, later confirmation
problem: Retailer and Supplier are both merchants. They agree by telephone that Supplier will sell Retailer 400 jackets for \$8,000. The next day Retailer mails Supplier a signed memo stating "Confirming 400 jackets at \$25 each." Supplier receives it on June 2 and says nothing. On June 20 Supplier refuses to deliver and pleads the statute of frauds. Is Supplier's defense good?
step: Governing law and parties. Jackets are goods, so Article 2 applies. Both parties are merchants (they deal in the goods), so the merchant's confirmation rule is available. The price is \$8,000, over \$500, so § 2-201 applies.
step: Is there a writing signed by Supplier? No. Supplier signed nothing, so § 2-201(1) is not met directly against Supplier.
step: Merchant's confirmation: the memo is signed by Retailer, states a quantity, was sent within a reasonable time, and Supplier had reason to know its contents. Supplier gave no written objection within ten days of receipt (June 2 to June 12).
step: Conclusion: the writing satisfies § 2-201 against Supplier, so the statute of frauds is not a defense. Retailer must still prove that the oral agreement was actually made. The misstated unit price does not matter because price need not appear.
@mistakes
- Treating Article 2 as applying only to merchants. Article 2 governs any sale of goods, and only particular rules require merchant status.
- Thinking the merchant's confirmation rule proves the contract. It only removes the statute of frauds defense.
- Requiring the writing to state the price or all material terms. Only a quantity (and an indication of a contract and a signature) is required.
- Forgetting that a firm offer lasts at most three months even if the offeror promised longer.
