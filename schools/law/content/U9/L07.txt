@article
A **secured transaction** gives a creditor a property interest in the debtor's personal property so that, if the debtor does not pay, the creditor can take and sell that property. Article 9 of the UCC governs these transactions. Three vocabulary words anchor everything: the **debtor** (the person with an interest in the collateral, usually the borrower), the **secured party** (the lender or seller holding the interest), and the **collateral** (the property that secures payment). A **security interest** is an interest in personal property or fixtures that secures payment or performance of an obligation (§ 1-201(b)(35)).

### Scope (§ 9-109)
Article 9 applies to **any transaction, regardless of its form, that creates a security interest in personal property or fixtures by contract**, including a pledge, chattel mortgage, conditional sale, trust receipt, or a retention or reservation of title for security (see also § 2-401, which limits a seller's retention of title to a reservation of a security interest). It also applies to **sales of accounts, chattel paper, payment intangibles and promissory notes**, to **consignments** (a delivery of goods worth \$1,000 or more to a merchant who deals in goods of that kind under a name other than the consignor's, as defined in § 9-102(a)(20)), and to agricultural liens. A **true lease** is not a security interest; whether a lease is disguised security depends on § 1-203. A lease is security if the lessee's obligation for the term cannot be terminated and, in addition, the term equals or exceeds the goods' remaining economic life, the lessee is bound to renew for that life or to become owner, or the lessee has an option to renew or to become owner for no or nominal additional consideration.

Article 9 **does not apply**, among other things, to **real property interests** and landlords' liens; to a lien given by statute or other rule of law for services or materials (other than an agricultural lien); to wage assignments; to transfers of an interest in an insurance policy (with exceptions); to the creation of a security interest in a **tort claim** (other than a commercial tort claim); and to most **deposit accounts in consumer transactions** (§ 9-109(d)). Federal law preempts it in some fields (for example, aircraft and certain intellectual property recording systems).

### Classifying collateral
Most Article 9 rules turn on **type of collateral**, so classify before you analyze. For **goods** the test is the **debtor's use**, not the nature of the item:
- **Consumer goods** are used or bought primarily for **personal, family or household** purposes (§ 9-102(a)(23)).
- **Equipment** is goods other than inventory, farm products or consumer goods (§ 9-102(a)(33)); it is used in a business.
- **Farm products** are crops or livestock of a debtor engaged in farming (§ 9-102(a)(34)).
- **Inventory** is goods held for sale or lease, or to be furnished under a service contract, or raw materials, work in process or materials used or consumed in a business (§ 9-102(a)(48)).

The same television is **inventory** for a retailer, **equipment** in a hotel and **consumer goods** in a family's den. Other types are **accounts**, **chattel paper**, **instruments**, **deposit accounts**, **investment property**, **documents**, **general intangibles**, **letter-of-credit rights**, **commercial tort claims**, and **fixtures**.

### Attachment: the three requirements (§ 9-203(b))
A security interest **attaches** (becomes enforceable against the debtor) when **all** of the following are true:
1. **Value** has been given (§ 1-204): any consideration that would support a simple contract, a binding commitment to extend credit, or the satisfaction of a preexisting claim.
2. The debtor has **rights in the collateral** or the power to transfer rights in it to a secured party. The debtor need not have full ownership (a buyer under a contract who has possession and identified goods has rights), but a thief has none, and a debtor who has not yet acquired the goods has no rights yet.
3. One of the following **evidentiary** requirements is met: (a) the debtor has **authenticated a security agreement** (signed it or adopted it with a symbol or process) that **describes the collateral**; or (b) the secured party has **possession** of the collateral (or **control** of deposit accounts, investment property, electronic chattel paper, letter-of-credit rights or similar collateral) **under the debtor's agreement**.

Attachment gives the secured party rights against the **debtor** and the collateral (§ 9-201). It does not protect the secured party against other creditors; that requires **perfection** (next lesson). Attachment occurs when the last requirement is met, so the order in which the elements occur does not matter.

### Describing collateral (§ 9-108)
A description is sufficient if it **reasonably identifies** the collateral, by specific listing, category, a type defined in the UCC (for example, "inventory" or "equipment"), quantity, a formula, or any objectively determinable method. A description is **not** sufficient if it merely says "all the debtor's assets" or "all the debtor's personal property" (§ 9-108(c)). (A **financing statement** may use "all assets," § 9-504(2).) In a consumer transaction, a description only by a UCC type is insufficient for consumer goods.

### After-acquired property and future advances (§ 9-204)
A security agreement may cover **after-acquired collateral**, and the security interest attaches when the debtor acquires rights in it (a "floating lien" on inventory and receivables). But a security interest does **not** attach under an after-acquired clause to **consumer goods** acquired more than **10 days** after the secured party gives value (§ 9-204(b)(1)). The agreement can also secure **future advances** (§ 9-204(c)).

### Proceeds (§ 9-315, § 9-203(f))
Attachment to collateral automatically gives an attached interest in its **identifiable proceeds** (what the debtor receives on sale or disposition), unless the agreement says otherwise.

### Applying it
Identify the transaction, the parties' roles, the type of collateral and how it is used; then run the three elements (value, rights, security agreement or possession); then note any special rule (after-acquired, consumer goods, proceeds).
@example
title: Attachment to inventory acquired later
problem: On January 1, Debtor Corp., a merchant of furniture, signs a security agreement granting Bank a security interest in "all inventory now owned or hereafter acquired" to secure a \$100,000 revolving line. Bank funds \$40,000 on January 5. On February 1 Debtor Corp. buys new sofas for resale. Does Bank's interest attach to the sofas?
step: Classify the collateral. The sofas are held for sale by a furniture dealer, so they are inventory (§ 9-102(a)(48)).
step: Value. Bank gave value when it funded \$40,000 on January 5 (and a binding commitment would also be value).
step: Security agreement. Debtor Corp. authenticated an agreement describing the collateral by a UCC type ("inventory"), which satisfies § 9-108, and the after-acquired clause is valid under § 9-204(a).
step: Rights in the collateral. The sofas became Debtor Corp.'s on February 1, so on that date it acquired rights in them and the last element was met.
step: Conclusion: Bank's security interest attached to the sofas on February 1. It is enforceable against Debtor Corp., but unless Bank has also perfected (for example, by filing), it is unprotected against other creditors.
@mistakes
- Treating attachment and perfection as the same. Attachment makes the interest enforceable against the debtor; perfection (filing, possession, control) protects it against third parties.
- Treating "all assets" as a sufficient description in the security agreement. It works only in the financing statement.
- Classifying goods by what they are rather than by how the debtor uses them.
- Forgetting the 10-day limit on after-acquired consumer goods.
