@article
Article 9 does not define **default**; the **security agreement** does (usually non-payment, but also breach of covenants such as insuring the collateral). After default, Part 6 gives the secured party a strong set of remedies, balanced by mandatory protections for the debtor that cannot be waived in advance (§ 9-602).

### Rights after default (§ 9-601)
The secured party may **reduce its claim to judgment**, **foreclose** on the collateral, or otherwise enforce the security interest by any available judicial procedure. These rights are **cumulative**. For accounts and similar collateral, the secured party may **notify account debtors to pay it directly** (§ 9-607).

### Repossession (§ 9-609)
After default, the secured party may **take possession** of the collateral:
- **without judicial process, if it proceeds without breach of the peace** (self-help); or
- **by action** (replevin or similar judicial process).
It may also **render equipment unusable** and dispose of collateral on the debtor's premises (§ 9-609(a)(2), (c)). "Breach of the peace" is not defined; courts look at whether the repossessor used or threatened violence, entered a **closed or locked** garage or home, **persisted over the debtor's contemporaneous objection**, or involved police in an unlawful way. Taking a car from an open driveway or street while the debtor is absent is the classic **lawful** self-help. A secured party that breaches the peace has repossessed wrongfully and is liable under § 9-625.

### Disposition (§§ 9-610 to 9-614)
- **Commercial reasonableness (§ 9-610(b)).** The secured party may sell, lease or license the collateral, publicly or privately, but **every aspect** of the disposition (method, manner, time, place and terms) must be **commercially reasonable**. It may buy at a **public** disposition; at a **private** disposition only if the collateral is of a kind customarily sold on a recognized market or covered by standard price quotations (§ 9-610(c)).
- **What counts (§ 9-627).** A price **alone** is not enough to make a disposition unreasonable: the fact that a better price could have been obtained by a different method or at a different time does not prove a lack of commercial reasonableness. A disposition is commercially reasonable if made in the usual manner on a recognized market, at the price current in that market, or in conformity with reasonable commercial practices among dealers in the type of property.
- **Notice (§ 9-611).** The secured party must send a **reasonable authenticated notification of disposition** to the debtor, any secondary obligor and other secured parties who have filed or have control, unless the collateral is **perishable**, **threatens to decline speedily in value**, or is **customarily sold on a recognized market**. In a **non-consumer** transaction, notice sent after default and **at least 10 days before** the earliest disposition is **reasonable as a matter of law** (§ 9-612(b)). In a **consumer-goods** transaction the notice must contain the statutory content of § 9-614 (the § 9-613 information, including the debtor's right to an accounting and the time after which a private disposition will occur, plus a description of any liability for a deficiency and a telephone number for learning the amount needed to redeem).
- **Application of proceeds (§ 9-615(a)).** Proceeds go in order to (1) **reasonable expenses** of retaking, holding, preparing and selling (and attorney's fees if the agreement provides and law allows); (2) the **secured obligation**; (3) **subordinate security interests** and liens whose holders send an authenticated demand before distribution; and (4) the **debtor** (the surplus). The debtor remains liable for any **deficiency** (§ 9-615(d)).

$$\text{deficiency} = \text{obligation owed} + \text{expenses of enforcement} - \text{disposition proceeds (before expenses)}.$$

- **Insider sales (§ 9-615(f)).** If the buyer is the secured party, a related person or a secondary obligor, and the price is **significantly below** the range of prices a disposition to an unrelated buyer would have brought, the deficiency is computed as if the proceeds were the amount a complying sale would have brought.

### Acceptance of collateral: "strict foreclosure" (§ 9-620)
After default, a secured party may **propose to keep the collateral** in **full** or (non-consumer only) **partial** satisfaction. It needs the **debtor's consent** after default (in an authenticated record) or, in full satisfaction in a non-consumer case, no objection within **20 days** of the proposal; and no other interested party may object. In a **consumer-goods transaction** the secured party **may not** take the collateral in **partial** satisfaction, and if the consumer has paid **at least 60%** of the cash price (for a purchase-money security interest) or of the principal amount (otherwise), the secured party **must dispose** of the collateral within **90 days** of taking possession unless the debtor waives in an authenticated agreement made after default (§ 9-620(e), (f); § 9-624).

### Redemption (§ 9-623)
Until the secured party has collected, disposed of, contracted to dispose of or accepted the collateral, the **debtor, any secondary obligor or another secured party** may **redeem** by tendering **all** obligations secured plus reasonable expenses (not just the missed installments, unless the agreement says so, and an **acceleration** clause makes the entire balance due).

### Remedies for noncompliance (§§ 9-625, 9-626)
- A debtor or other injured person may obtain **injunctive relief** and recover damages for any **loss caused** by a failure to comply (§ 9-625(a), (b)).
- In a **consumer-goods transaction**, a consumer obligor may also recover statutory damages of **not less than the credit service charge plus 10% of the principal amount** of the obligation (or the time-price differential plus 10% of the cash price) (§ 9-625(c)(2)).
- **Non-consumer rule (§ 9-626):** the **rebuttable presumption** rule applies. If the secured party fails to prove compliance, its **deficiency is limited** to the amount by which the secured obligation plus expenses exceeds the **greater** of the actual proceeds and the amount a **complying** disposition would have produced; the secured party has the burden to prove compliance when the issue is raised.
- **Consumer transactions:** Article 9 leaves the effect on a deficiency to the courts, which are split between an **absolute bar** to a deficiency and the rebuttable presumption rule; check your jurisdiction.

### Applying it
Sequence: Was there a **default**? Was **repossession** peaceful? Was the **notice** timely and complete? Was the **disposition** commercially reasonable? Compute the **deficiency or surplus**, and then ask what remedies follow from any breach of Part 6.
@example
title: A deficiency after a repossession
problem: Debtor Co. owes Lender \$18,000 on a loan secured by its delivery van (equipment). Debtor Co. defaults. Lender takes the van from Debtor Co.'s open lot at night without incident, incurs \$1,200 in towing and storage and preparation costs, and sends Debtor Co. written notice of a public sale 14 days ahead. The van is sold at a commercially reasonable public sale for \$11,500. What may Lender recover from Debtor Co.?
step: Repossession. Taking the van from an open lot without confrontation is self-help without breach of the peace (§ 9-609(b)(2)). It is lawful.
step: Disposition. The transaction is non-consumer, and notice was sent after default more than 10 days before the sale, which is reasonable as a matter of law (§ 9-612(b)). The sale was a public sale conducted in a commercially reasonable manner.
step: Compute the deficiency under § 9-615: \$18,000 owed plus \$1,200 of expenses is \$19,200; subtract the sale proceeds of \$11,500.
step: Lender may recover a deficiency of \$7,700 from Debtor Co. (an unsecured claim). There is no surplus.
@mistakes
- Approving repossession whenever the debtor is in default. Self-help is allowed only without breach of the peace.
- Treating a low price alone as proof that the sale was not commercially reasonable.
- Forgetting that notice must be sent to the debtor and other secured parties and that, for consumer goods, the notice must contain the statutory content.
- Applying the 10-day notice safe harbor to consumer-goods transactions.

@practice self
prompt: Consumer buys a used car for personal use under a PMSI financed by Lender, and defaults after paying 35% of the cash price. Lender repossesses it peaceably, mails Consumer a note saying only "We will sell your car at a private sale on or after May 20," and sells it on May 25 to a dealer owned by Lender's brother for \$6,000, well below the \$9,000 that a sale to an unrelated dealer would have brought. Lender claims a deficiency of \$14,000. Analyze Consumer's rights.
hint: Check the notice content, the insider sale and the remedies for noncompliance in a consumer transaction.
solution: Issue: whether Lender complied with Part 6 and what follows for the deficiency. Rule: in a consumer-goods transaction the notification of disposition must contain the content required by § 9-614 (the debtor's right to an accounting, the time after which a private disposition will occur, a description of any deficiency liability and a telephone number for the amount needed to redeem, among other things); a private sale to a related person at a price significantly below the range of prices brought by an unrelated disposition is calculated under § 9-615(f); a consumer may recover damages under § 9-625(b) and statutory damages of not less than the credit service charge plus 10% of the principal amount of the obligation under § 9-625(c)(2); the effect of noncompliance on the deficiency in a consumer transaction is left to the courts (absolute bar or rebuttable presumption). Application: the notice does not state Consumer's rights to an accounting and to redeem, so it is noncompliant. The sale to Lender's brother's dealership at \$6,000 is a related-party sale at a price significantly below the \$9,000 range, so under § 9-615(f) the deficiency would be computed as if proceeds were \$9,000, not \$6,000. Consumer can claim statutory damages and actual loss under § 9-625. Conclusion: Lender violated Part 6; in a state following the absolute bar rule it may lose its deficiency; in a rebuttable presumption state its deficiency is reduced (computed with at least \$9,000 proceeds); and Consumer may recover statutory damages.
rubric: Finds the notice defective for lacking the § 9-614 content.
rubric: Applies § 9-615(f) to the related-party sale.
rubric: States the § 9-625(c)(2) statutory damages and the split on the effect on the deficiency in consumer cases.
