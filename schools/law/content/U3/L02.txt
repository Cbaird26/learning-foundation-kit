@article
Every duty in this course depends on a relationship, so the first question in an ethics problem is whether one exists, and the last is whether it has ended.

### Formation

No written contract and no fee is required. Under the Restatement (Third) § 14 approach, a client–lawyer relationship forms when a person manifests intent to have a lawyer provide legal services and the lawyer **manifests consent** (expressly or by conduct), or the lawyer fails to manifest lack of consent and **knows or reasonably should know** the person is relying on the lawyer. A casual setting does not prevent formation: advice given at a party, in an email or online chat can create a relationship if the listener reasonably relies. Lawyers avoid accidental clients by saying clearly and in writing when they decline, ideally also naming deadlines the person should watch. A court-appointed lawyer has a client from appointment, and Rule 6.2 says a lawyer shall not seek to avoid an appointment by a tribunal except for good cause (for example, a violation of the Rules, an unreasonable financial burden, or a client or cause so repugnant that it impairs the relationship).

### Prospective clients: Rule 1.18

A person who consults a lawyer about possibly forming a relationship is a **prospective client**, even if no relationship results. The lawyer must keep information learned in the consultation confidential, as for a former client (Rule 1.9). The lawyer may not later represent a client with materially adverse interests in the same or a substantially related matter if she received information that could be **significantly harmful** to the prospective client, unless both give informed consent, or she took reasonable measures to avoid exposure to more information than necessary, she is timely screened with no share of the fee, and written notice goes promptly to the prospective client. In that last case the firm's other lawyers may proceed.

### Who decides: Rule 1.2

The **client** decides the objectives of the representation. The client also decides, in a criminal case, the plea, whether to waive a jury and whether to testify, and in any case whether to settle. The lawyer must consult about the means and may make tactical and technical decisions without consulting unless the client has expressed a view. Under Rule 1.2(c) a lawyer may **limit the scope** of the representation if the limitation is reasonable under the circumstances and the client gives informed consent. Under Rule 1.2(d) a lawyer may not counsel or assist conduct she knows is criminal or fraudulent but may discuss the legal consequences of a proposed course of conduct and may help a client test the law's validity or meaning in good faith.

### Ending the relationship: Rule 1.16

**Mandatory withdrawal.** A lawyer must withdraw (or decline) if (1) the representation will result in violation of the Rules or other law, (2) the lawyer's physical or mental condition materially impairs the ability to represent the client, or (3) the lawyer is **discharged**. A client may discharge a lawyer at any time, with or without cause.

**Permissive withdrawal** (Rule 1.16(b)): when it can be done without material adverse effect on the client's interests, or when the client persists in a course of action the lawyer reasonably believes is criminal or fraudulent; the client used the lawyer's services to perpetrate a crime or fraud; the client insists on action the lawyer finds repugnant or with which she has a fundamental disagreement; the client substantially fails to fulfill an obligation (such as paying fees) after reasonable warning; the representation will cause an unreasonable financial burden or has been rendered unreasonably difficult; or other good cause exists.

**Tribunal permission.** If a tribunal's rules require permission, the lawyer must continue until the tribunal grants it, even if the lawyer has good cause (Rule 1.16(c)).

**Duties on termination** (Rule 1.16(d)): give reasonable notice, allow time to employ other counsel, surrender papers and property to which the client is entitled, and refund any unearned advance fee. The lawyer may retain papers only to the extent other law permits, and many states forbid doing so where it would prejudice the client. A lawyer discharged from a contingent-fee matter usually recovers the reasonable value of services (quantum meruit), not the contract fee.

@example
title: Did a relationship form at a party?
problem: At a party, Dana describes her landlord dispute to Eli, a lawyer. Eli says, "Sure, I'll take care of it, don't worry about any deadlines," and gives Dana his card. Dana waits. Eli does nothing, and her claim is later time-barred. Was a client–lawyer relationship formed?
step: Ask whether Dana manifested intent to obtain legal services. She described the dispute to a lawyer and accepted his offer to handle it.
step: Ask whether Eli manifested consent. "I'll take care of it" is express consent, and "don't worry about deadlines" invites reliance.
step: Reliance by Dana was reasonable, and Eli knew of it. A party setting, the absence of a writing and the absence of a fee do not prevent formation.
step: Conclusion: a relationship formed, so Eli owed duties of competence, diligence (Rule 1.3) and communication (Rule 1.4) and is exposed to discipline and malpractice liability.

@mistakes
- Requiring a fee or a written retainer before finding a relationship.
- Forgetting that prospective clients are owed confidentiality and that their information can disqualify the lawyer later.
- Treating a lawyer's good cause for withdrawal as enough when the court has not granted leave to withdraw.
- Letting a lawyer keep a client's file as leverage for unpaid fees even when the client would be prejudiced.
