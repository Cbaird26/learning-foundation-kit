@article
The three duties in this lesson are the ones clients complain about most. A lawyer who is competent but slow, or diligent but silent, still violates the Rules.

### Competence: Rule 1.1

A lawyer must provide competent representation, which requires the **legal knowledge, skill, thoroughness and preparation** reasonably necessary for the representation. Competence is judged against the matter, not against a lawyer's specialty. A lawyer may take a matter outside her experience if she can reach the necessary competence by **reasonable study** or by **associating with a competent lawyer**, and without unreasonable delay or expense to the client. In an emergency a lawyer may give the limited help that is reasonably necessary. Competence cannot be waived by a client, but a lawyer may limit the scope of the work under Rule 1.2(c) with informed consent, so long as the limitation is reasonable.

Comment 8 adds that keeping abreast of changes in law and practice includes the benefits and risks of relevant **technology**. ABA Formal Opinion 512 (2024) applies this to generative AI tools: a lawyer must understand the capabilities and limits of the tool, independently verify its output before relying on it (fabricated citations are a recurring danger), protect client information, communicate with the client when the tool's use bears on the representation, and charge reasonable fees for the time actually spent. Entering confidential client information into a tool that may retain or disclose it implicates Rule 1.6.

### Diligence: Rule 1.3

A lawyer must act with **reasonable diligence and promptness**. Procrastination, an overloaded docket, neglected files and missed deadlines all violate it. A lawyer who is overextended must decline new matters or withdraw. A sole practitioner is expected to have a plan for her clients if she dies or becomes disabled. Diligence does not require the lawyer to press every available point or to be offensive; it requires zealous but reasonable advocacy within the law.

### Communication: Rule 1.4

The lawyer must:

1. promptly inform the client of any decision or circumstance requiring informed consent;
2. reasonably consult about the means of achieving the client's objectives;
3. keep the client reasonably informed about the status of the matter;
4. promptly comply with reasonable requests for information; and
5. consult about relevant limitations on her conduct when the client expects assistance the Rules do not allow.

The lawyer must explain a matter to the extent reasonably necessary to permit the client to make **informed decisions**. Because the client decides whether to settle (Rule 1.2(a)), every **settlement offer** must be promptly communicated, unless the client has previously authorized rejection; a lawyer may not decline an offer on the client's behalf because she thinks it is low. In rare cases, such as information that would harm a client with a serious mental condition, a lawyer may delay transmitting information, but never to serve the lawyer's own convenience.

### Diminished capacity: Rule 1.14

A lawyer must, **as far as reasonably possible, maintain a normal client–lawyer relationship** with a client whose capacity to make adequately considered decisions is diminished by minority, mental impairment or another reason. If the lawyer reasonably believes the client has diminished capacity, is at risk of **substantial physical, financial or other harm** unless action is taken, and cannot adequately act in her own interest, the lawyer may take reasonably necessary protective action. This can include consulting family members or professionals who can help, or seeking a guardian or conservator. Under Rule 1.14(c) the lawyer is impliedly authorized under Rule 1.6(a) to reveal information about the client, only to the extent reasonably necessary to protect her interests. Capacity is not all-or-nothing; a minor or an older client may be able to decide some things and not others.

### Applying the rules

Ask: what did the client need to know or do? What would a prudent lawyer have done by that point? Which rule is most precisely violated? If a lawyer ignored a matter for months, Rule 1.3 is the best fit; if she did the work but never told the client, Rule 1.4.

@example
title: A rejected offer the client never heard about
problem: A defendant offers \$50,000 to settle Ruth's personal-injury claim. Her lawyer thinks the claim is worth more and, without telling Ruth, replies that the offer is rejected. Ruth learns of the offer weeks later from a letter. Which rules were violated?
step: Identify who decides settlement. Under Rule 1.2(a), the client decides whether to settle.
step: A decision reserved to the client requires the lawyer to communicate the offer promptly, under Rule 1.4(a)(1) (a circumstance requiring informed consent) and Rule 1.4(b).
step: The lawyer had no prior authority from Ruth to reject offers, so rejecting it herself usurped Ruth's decision (Rule 1.2(a)).
step: Conclusion: the lawyer violated Rules 1.2(a) and 1.4; she should have promptly conveyed the offer, given her honest evaluation and let Ruth decide.

@mistakes
- Treating a client's lack of loss as the test; the Rules are violated by the conduct, whether or not the client is harmed.
- Thinking competence requires prior specialty experience; it can be acquired by study or association.
- Applying Rule 1.14 to decisions the client can still make; the lawyer's default is a normal relationship.
- Citing an AI tool's output without verifying it; the lawyer remains responsible.

@practice self
prompt: A lawyer uses a generative AI tool to draft a motion. She pastes in her client's confidential settlement terms, then files the motion without checking it. The court finds that two cited cases do not exist. Identify the Model Rules implicated and explain what she should have done.
solution: Rule 1.1 (competence): she must understand the tool's risks and verify its output (Formal Opinion 512). Rule 3.3(a)(1) and Rule 3.1: filing citations to nonexistent authority is a false statement of law to the tribunal, and an unverified filing is not a proper basis in law; she must also correct the filing (Rule 3.3(a)(1)). Rule 1.6(c): she must make reasonable efforts to prevent unauthorized disclosure, and pasting confidential terms into a tool without confirming its confidentiality protections may violate this. Rule 1.4: she may have to inform the client of the error. She should have read every cited authority, learned the tool's data-retention terms, avoided entering confidential information without client consent or adequate safeguards, and corrected the record at once when the problem surfaced.

rubric: Identifies Rule 3.3(a)(1) (candor to the tribunal) for the false authorities.
rubric: Identifies Rule 1.6(c) for the confidentiality risk.
