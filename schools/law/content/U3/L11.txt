@article
Lawyers may tell the public about their services, but clients are vulnerable, so the Rules prohibit misleading statements and pressure. The same part of the Rules protects the profession's independence by limiting who may practice law and share in fees.

### Communications: Rule 7.1

A lawyer shall not make a **false or misleading communication** about the lawyer or the lawyer's services. A communication is misleading if it contains a material misrepresentation of fact or law, or omits a fact necessary to keep the statement as a whole from being materially misleading. Examples: a statement likely to create an **unjustified expectation** about results (such as a guarantee), an unsubstantiated comparison with other lawyers, a misleading firm name, and past case results presented so that they suggest the same result in a new matter. Truthful statements may still mislead.

### Advertising: Rule 7.2

A lawyer may communicate information about the lawyer's services through any media (Rule 7.2(a)). The lawyer shall **not give anything of value to a person for recommending the lawyer's services**, except she may: pay the reasonable costs of advertisements; pay the usual charges of a **legal service plan or a not-for-profit or qualified lawyer referral service**; pay for a law practice purchased under Rule 1.17; refer clients to another lawyer or nonlawyer professional under a **reciprocal referral agreement** that is not exclusive, with the client informed of its existence and nature; or give a **nominal gift** as an expression of appreciation that is neither intended nor reasonably expected to be given in return for a recommendation. Every communication must include the **name and contact information** of at least one lawyer or law firm responsible for its content.

### Solicitation: Rule 7.3

A lawyer shall not, by **in-person, live telephone or real-time electronic contact**, solicit professional employment when a significant motive is the lawyer's pecuniary gain, unless the person contacted is another lawyer or has a family, close personal or prior business or professional relationship with the lawyer. Even where permitted, a lawyer shall not solicit when the target has made known that he does not want to be solicited, or when the solicitation involves **coercion, duress or harassment**. Written, recorded and other non-live communications, such as letters and email sent to targets such as people facing foreclosure, are **advertising**, not live solicitation, and are allowed if truthful and not coercive. The Model Rules no longer require an "Advertising Material" label (the 2018 amendments removed it), but some states still require labels such as "Advertising Material" or impose waiting periods.

**Constitutional limits.** Lawyer advertising is protected commercial speech (*Bates v. State Bar of Arizona* (1977)); a state may require appropriate disclosures (*Zauderer v. Office of Disciplinary Counsel* (1985)); targeted direct-mail letters cannot be banned (*Shapero v. Kentucky Bar Ass'n* (1988)); a state may ban in-person solicitation by a lawyer for pecuniary gain (*Ohralik v. Ohio State Bar Ass'n* (1978)); and a state may impose a reasonable waiting period for mailings to accident victims (*Florida Bar v. Went For It, Inc.* (1995)).

### Specialties: Rule 7.2(c)

Since the 2018 amendments, which deleted Rules 7.4 and 7.5, specialty communications are governed by Rule 7.2(c). A lawyer may communicate that she practices in particular fields of law, but may not state or imply she is a **certified specialist** unless the certifying organization has been approved by an appropriate state authority or accredited by the ABA and its name is clearly identified in the communication.

### Fee sharing and nonlawyer ownership: Rule 5.4

A lawyer shall not **share legal fees with a nonlawyer**, with exceptions: payments to a deceased lawyer's estate; payments for a purchased practice; **compensation and retirement plans for nonlawyer employees that are based on profit sharing**; and sharing court-awarded fees with a nonprofit that employed or recommended the lawyer. A lawyer shall not form a partnership with a nonlawyer if any activity consists of the practice of law, shall not permit a person who recommends, employs or pays the lawyer to direct her professional judgment, and shall not practice in a professional corporation or association in which a nonlawyer owns an interest or serves as director or officer with the right to direct the lawyer's judgment. A few jurisdictions allow nonlawyer ownership.

### Unauthorized practice: Rule 5.5

A lawyer shall not practice law in a jurisdiction in violation of that jurisdiction's regulation of the profession, or assist another in doing so. A lawyer not admitted in a jurisdiction shall not (b) **establish an office or other systematic and continuous presence** there for the practice of law, or **hold out** as admitted there, except as permitted. Rule 5.5(c) allows a lawyer admitted in another U.S. jurisdiction to provide services on a **temporary basis** if the services: (1) are undertaken in association with a lawyer admitted locally who actively participates; (2) are in or reasonably related to a pending or potential **proceeding** before a tribunal where the lawyer is, or reasonably expects to be, authorized to appear (typically by pro hac vice admission); (3) are in or reasonably related to a pending or potential **arbitration, mediation or ADR** proceeding and arise out of or relate to the lawyer's home practice; or (4) arise out of or are reasonably related to her practice in her home jurisdiction. Rule 5.5(d) lets a lawyer admitted elsewhere provide services to her **employer or its affiliates** (in-house) or services authorized by federal or other law. Rule 5.5(a) also bars a lawyer from assisting a nonlawyer in the unauthorized practice of law.

### Restrictions on practice: Rule 5.6

A lawyer shall not participate in an employment, partnership or other agreement that **restricts a lawyer's right to practice after termination** of the relationship, except an agreement about retirement benefits, or in a settlement that restricts a lawyer's right to practice. A covenant not to compete in a law firm agreement is therefore improper.

@example
title: Live solicitation or advertising?
problem: Lawyer Hal, whose significant motive is to gain clients, walks into a hospital room and hands his card to an accident victim, a stranger, saying he can recover compensation for her. Separately, he mails letters to homeowners listed in public foreclosure notices. Which is permitted?
step: The hospital visit is in-person solicitation by a lawyer with a pecuniary motive to a person with no relationship to him, so Rule 7.3(a) bars it; the Constitution permits the ban (*Ohralik*).
step: The mailed letters are written communications, which are advertising rather than live contact.
step: Truthful targeted letters are protected (*Shapero*) and allowed under Rule 7.2, subject to Rule 7.1 and any state labeling rules.
step: Conclusion: the hospital visit is prohibited; the letters are permitted if truthful and not coercive.

@mistakes
- Treating targeted letters as prohibited solicitation; only live person-to-person or real-time contact is barred.
- Allowing a lawyer to pay a nonlawyer a percentage of the fee for referrals.
- Forgetting that a lawyer who moves to a new state and sets up an office there needs admission or an exception.
- Assuming a lawyer's truthful statement cannot mislead.

@practice self
prompt: Lawyer Kai is admitted only in State A. A client asks her to (1) negotiate a deal in State B for the client's State A business, in a transaction arising out of Kai's State A practice; (2) appear in a State B court trial for a day; and (3) open a State B office and take State B clients. Analyze each under Rule 5.5.
solution: (1) Permitted as temporary practice under Rule 5.5(c)(4): the services arise out of or are reasonably related to her practice in State A, are temporary, and she does not establish an office or hold out as admitted in State B. (2) Permitted only if she is, or reasonably expects to be, authorized to appear, typically by pro hac vice admission, or if she associates with a lawyer admitted in State B who actively participates, under Rule 5.5(c)(1) or (c)(2). (3) Prohibited by Rule 5.5(b): opening an office is a systematic and continuous presence for the practice of law, and taking State B clients would be practicing law in State B without admission; she must become admitted first.
rubric: Applies Rule 5.5(c)(4) to the first request and notes it is temporary and related to her home practice.
rubric: Applies Rule 5.5(c)(1) or (c)(2) to the court appearance.
rubric: Applies Rule 5.5(b) to the office and holding out.
hint: Distinguish temporary services from an office or systematic and continuous presence.
hint: Court appearances usually depend on pro hac vice admission or local association.
