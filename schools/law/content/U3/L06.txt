@article
Confidentiality (Rule 1.6) is a professional duty that applies everywhere. The **attorney–client privilege** is an evidentiary rule: it lets a client (and the lawyer on the client's behalf) refuse to disclose certain communications when someone seeks them through subpoena, discovery or testimony. The MPRE tests the privilege as controlling law, so you need both.

### Elements

A communication is privileged if it is: (1) between the **client** (or a prospective client) and the **lawyer**, or their agents; (2) made **in confidence**, meaning made in circumstances reasonably indicating the client did not intend disclosure beyond those who are necessary; and (3) for the purpose of **obtaining or giving legal advice or assistance**. The privilege belongs to the **client**, who may waive it. It protects the **communication**, not the underlying **facts**. A client asked "did you run the light?" must answer, even if she told her lawyer the answer. The identity of a client and the fee arrangement are generally not privileged.

**Third parties.** The presence of an outsider generally destroys confidentiality. Agents necessary to the communication, such as a lawyer's paralegal, an interpreter, or an accountant hired to help the lawyer understand the client's finances (*United States v. Kovel* (1961)), do not. **Duration.** The privilege survives the client's death (*Swidler & Berlin v. United States* (1998), even in a criminal case).

### Corporate clients

*Upjohn Co. v. United States* (1981) rejected the narrow "control group" test in federal court. Communications between employees and corporate counsel are privileged when made at the direction of corporate superiors to secure legal advice for the company, concern matters within the employee's duties, and the employees know they are speaking to counsel for the company for legal purposes and that the information is confidential. Counsel interviewing employees should give an **Upjohn warning**: the lawyer represents the company, not the employee; the privilege belongs to the company, which may waive it. (Some states still use narrower tests.)

### Exceptions

- **Crime–fraud**: no privilege when the client sought the lawyer's services to commit or further a crime or fraud, even if the lawyer did not know.
- **Client–lawyer dispute**: communications relevant to a dispute between lawyer and client, such as a fee suit or malpractice claim.
- **Joint clients**: as between the clients, in a later dispute between them (not against third parties).
- **Attested document**: communications relevant to a document the lawyer attested, such as a will.
- **Testamentary**: communications relevant to a dispute between parties who claim through the same deceased client.

### Waiver

The client waives by voluntary disclosure to a third party, by failing to assert it, or by putting the advice at issue (for example, claiming reliance on counsel's advice as a defense). Under **FRE 502**, a disclosure in a federal proceeding or to a federal office does not waive the privilege if it was inadvertent, the holder took **reasonable steps to prevent** disclosure and **promptly took reasonable steps to rectify** the error (502(b)). A federal court may also enter an order, such as a clawback agreement, that disclosure in the litigation does not waive in any other proceeding (502(d)). For intentional disclosures, subject-matter waiver applies only when fairness requires it (502(a)). Amendments effective December 1, 2025 to FRCP 16(b) and 26(f) require the parties to address, in their discovery planning, how they will describe privileged material that is withheld.

### Work product

*Hickman v. Taylor* (1947) protected a lawyer's files and mental impressions prepared for litigation from discovery. **FRCP 26(b)(3)** now protects documents and tangible things prepared **in anticipation of litigation or for trial** by or for a party or its representative, including a lawyer or consultant. Ordinary work product is discoverable on a showing of **substantial need** and inability to obtain the substantial equivalent without **undue hardship**. **Opinion work product**, meaning mental impressions, conclusions, opinions and legal theories of counsel, must be protected, and is almost never discoverable. Work product protects materials prepared for litigation whether or not a client communication is involved, and it is waived only by disclosure that substantially increases the chance an adversary will obtain it.

### Privilege versus confidentiality

| | Confidentiality (Rule 1.6) | Privilege |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Ethical duty | Evidentiary rule |
| Scope | All information relating to the representation, any source | Confidential client–lawyer communications for legal advice |
| Applies | Always, including outside court | When disclosure is compelled in a proceeding |
| Facts | Covered | Not protected |

@example
title: Facts versus communications
problem: At a deposition after a crash, the plaintiff's lawyer asks the defendant, "Did you look at your phone before the collision?" The defendant's lawyer objects on privilege because the defendant told her lawyer the answer. Should the defendant answer?
step: Identify what is sought: a fact within the defendant's own knowledge, not the content of the conversation with the lawyer.
step: The privilege protects confidential communications, not the facts the communication discussed.
step: The question does not ask what the defendant told her lawyer, so the objection fails.
step: Conclusion: the defendant must answer. The plaintiff's lawyer may not ask what she told her lawyer about the phone, but she may ask about the underlying facts.

@mistakes
- Claiming privilege for facts or for documents that were not privileged before they were given to a lawyer.
- Forgetting that a third party's presence destroys confidentiality unless the person is a necessary agent.
- Assuming the privilege ends at the client's death.
- Treating work product as belonging only to the lawyer; it protects materials prepared by or for a party in anticipation of litigation.
