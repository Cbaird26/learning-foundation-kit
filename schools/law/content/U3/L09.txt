@article
A lawyer is a zealous advocate and also an **officer of the court**. The Rules in Part 3 mark where advocacy ends: no frivolous claims, no lies to the tribunal, no tampering with evidence.

### Meritorious claims: Rule 3.1 and expediting: Rule 3.2

A lawyer may not bring or defend a proceeding, or assert or controvert an issue, unless there is a basis in law and fact that is **not frivolous**, which includes a good-faith argument for extending, modifying or reversing existing law. A criminal defense lawyer may defend so as to require the prosecution to prove every element. FRCP 11 is the procedural counterpart: a motion for sanctions must be served on the other party and may not be filed until **21 days** after service, giving the party time to withdraw or correct the challenged paper (the "safe harbor", Rule 11(c)(2)). Rule 3.2 requires reasonable efforts to expedite litigation consistent with the client's interests.

### Candor to the tribunal: Rule 3.3

A lawyer shall not **knowingly**:

1. make a false statement of fact or law to a tribunal, or fail to correct a false statement of material fact or law previously made to it by the lawyer;
2. fail to disclose to the tribunal legal authority in the **controlling jurisdiction** known to the lawyer to be **directly adverse** to the client's position and **not disclosed by opposing counsel**; or
3. offer evidence the lawyer knows to be false.

If the lawyer, the client or a witness called by the lawyer has offered **material evidence** and the lawyer comes to know of its falsity, the lawyer must take **reasonable remedial measures**, including, if necessary, **disclosure to the tribunal**. A lawyer who merely **reasonably believes** evidence is false may **refuse to offer it**, except the testimony of a **criminal defendant**. Rule 3.3(b) applies the same remedial duty when the lawyer knows a person intends to engage, is engaging or has engaged in criminal or fraudulent conduct related to the proceeding. Under Rule 3.3(c) these duties **continue to the conclusion of the proceeding** and apply **even if compliance requires disclosure of information protected by Rule 1.6**. In an **ex parte** proceeding the lawyer must disclose all material facts that will enable the tribunal to make an informed decision, whether or not adverse (Rule 3.3(d)). "Knowledge" means actual knowledge, which can be inferred from circumstances (Rule 1.0(f)); a mere suspicion is not knowledge. Rule 3.3 applies in ancillary proceedings such as depositions.

**Remedial sequence (the usual approach).** (1) Remonstrate with the client and urge correction; (2) if that fails, seek to withdraw if permitted (withdrawal alone is not enough where the false evidence has been offered); (3) take other reasonable measures, such as having the witness correct it; (4) if necessary, disclose to the tribunal as much as is needed. **Criminal defendants.** *Nix v. Whiteside* (1986) held that a lawyer's refusal to assist a client's planned perjury, by threatening to withdraw and disclose, did not violate the defendant's Sixth Amendment right to counsel, because no right to present perjury exists. Under the Model Rules a lawyer cannot let the testimony proceed uncorrected; some jurisdictions permit the defendant to testify in narrative form, but that is a minority approach.

### Fairness: Rule 3.4

A lawyer shall not unlawfully obstruct access to evidence or **alter, destroy or conceal** it, or counsel or assist another to do so; **falsify evidence** or counsel or assist a witness to testify falsely; offer an **inducement to a witness** prohibited by law (paying a witness's reasonable expenses and reasonable compensation for lost time, and a reasonable expert's fee not contingent on the content of the testimony, are allowed); knowingly disobey a tribunal's rule, except through an open refusal based on an assertion that no valid obligation exists; make a frivolous discovery request or fail to make reasonably diligent efforts to comply with proper discovery; at trial allude to matters not reasonably believed relevant or supported by admissible evidence, assert personal knowledge of facts in issue, or **state a personal opinion** on the justness of a cause, a witness's credibility, or an accused's guilt; or request a person other than a client to refrain from giving information to another party unless the person is a relative, employee or agent of the client and the lawyer reasonably believes the person's interests will not be harmed.

### Impartiality, publicity and the lawyer as witness

Rule 3.5 bars ex parte communication with a judge, juror or other official about a pending matter, and post-discharge juror contact that is prohibited by law or court order, against a juror's wishes or involves misrepresentation, coercion or harassment. **Rule 3.6** bars an extrajudicial statement the lawyer knows or reasonably should know will be publicly disseminated and has a **substantial likelihood of materially prejudicing** the proceeding (*Gentile v. State Bar of Nevada* (1991)); a lawyer may state the claim, public record information and the like, and may respond to prejudicial publicity not initiated by the lawyer or the client. **Rule 3.7**: a lawyer shall not act as **advocate at a trial** in which she is likely to be a **necessary witness**, unless the testimony concerns an uncontested issue, the nature and value of legal services, or disqualification would work substantial hardship on the client. Other lawyers in her firm may act as advocate unless Rule 1.7 or 1.9 precludes it. **Rule 3.8**: a prosecutor must refrain from prosecuting charges not supported by probable cause, and must disclose to the defense all evidence or information that tends to negate guilt or mitigate the offense.

@example
title: Adverse authority in the controlling jurisdiction
problem: Lawyer Ana represents a defendant in State court in State S. She knows a decision of State S's highest court is directly adverse to her argument. Opposing counsel did not cite it. A decision of a different State's court, also adverse, is not controlling. What must she disclose?
step: Rule 3.3(a)(2) requires disclosure of legal authority in the controlling jurisdiction known to be directly adverse and not disclosed by opposing counsel.
step: The State S highest court decision is controlling and directly adverse, and opposing counsel omitted it, so Ana must disclose it.
step: The other State's decision is not in the controlling jurisdiction, so Rule 3.3(a)(2) does not require disclosing it.
step: Conclusion: disclose the State S decision, and she remains free to distinguish it or argue that it should be modified.

@mistakes
- Disclosing only cases: the duty to disclose adverse authority also covers controlling statutes and rules, but only in the controlling jurisdiction.
- Believing Rule 1.6 prevents disclosure under Rule 3.3; Rule 3.3(c) overrides it.
- Confusing suspicion with knowledge: with mere suspicion the lawyer may refuse the evidence, but disclosure duties arise only on knowledge.
- Treating withdrawal as always sufficient after false evidence has been given.

@practice self
prompt: In a civil case, your client testifies falsely at a deposition about a material fact; you know it is false. The case is still pending and the client refuses to correct the testimony. Explain what the Model Rules require, step by step, and whether Rule 1.6 changes the answer.
solution: Rule 3.3 applies to a deposition as an ancillary proceeding. Because the client has offered material evidence the lawyer knows is false, Rule 3.3(a)(3) requires reasonable remedial measures. Steps: (1) remonstrate with the client, explain the consequences and urge correction; (2) if the client refuses, consider withdrawal, which may be permitted or required (Rule 1.16), though withdrawal alone does not cure false evidence already offered; (3) take other reasonable measures to correct the record; (4) if necessary, disclose to the tribunal the facts reasonably necessary to remedy the false testimony. Rule 3.3(c) makes these duties continue to the end of the proceeding and apply even if compliance requires revealing information otherwise protected by Rule 1.6, so Rule 1.6 does not prevent disclosure.
