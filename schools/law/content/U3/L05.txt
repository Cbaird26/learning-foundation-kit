@article
Confidentiality is the duty that lets a client tell the lawyer everything. It is far broader than the evidentiary attorney–client privilege you will meet next week.

### The rule

Rule 1.6(a): a lawyer shall not reveal **information relating to the representation of a client** unless (1) the client gives **informed consent**, (2) the disclosure is **impliedly authorized** to carry out the representation (for example, telling opposing counsel facts needed to negotiate a deal), or (3) a Rule 1.6(b) exception applies. The duty covers **all** information relating to the representation, whatever its source, whether or not the client asked for secrecy, whether or not it is embarrassing, and **even if it is in the public record**. It continues after the representation ends (Rule 1.9(c)), though a lawyer may use information that has become generally known in a way that does not disadvantage the former client. Rule 1.8(b) separately bars **using** information relating to a representation to the client's disadvantage without informed consent.

### Discretionary exceptions: Rule 1.6(b)

A lawyer **may** reveal information to the extent the lawyer reasonably believes necessary:

1. to **prevent reasonably certain death or substantial bodily harm**;
2. to prevent the client from committing a **crime or fraud reasonably certain to result in substantial injury to the financial interests or property of another**, in furtherance of which the client has used or is using the lawyer's services;
3. to **prevent, mitigate or rectify** substantial financial injury from such a crime or fraud, in furtherance of which the client used the lawyer's services;
4. to **secure legal advice** about the lawyer's compliance with the Rules;
5. to establish a **claim or defense** on behalf of the lawyer in a controversy with the client, to defend against a criminal charge or civil claim based on conduct involving the client, or to respond to allegations in any proceeding about the lawyer's representation;
6. to **comply with other law or a court order**; and
7. to **detect and resolve conflicts of interest** arising from a lawyer's change of employment or a change in the firm, if the revealed information would not compromise the privilege or otherwise prejudice the client.

Three points matter on the exam. First, the Model Rule is **permissive**; the lawyer is not required to disclose under Rule 1.6(b), although a few states require disclosure to prevent death, serious harm or certain frauds. Second, disclosure must be **no more than reasonably necessary**, to the people who can prevent or remedy the harm, and the lawyer should first try to persuade the client where practicable. In a self-defense disclosure the lawyer should seek a protective order or confidentiality agreement where possible. Third, a **completed crime or fraud** that the lawyer did not facilitate is not within the exceptions. An exception for harm to others requires that the client used the lawyer's services in furtherance of it.

Other rules can compel or permit disclosure despite Rule 1.6: Rule 3.3(c) (candor to the tribunal) and a court order compel it, and Rule 1.13(c) permits it in an organizational setting. Rule 8.3 does not require reporting of Rule 1.6 information.

### Safeguarding: Rule 1.6(c)

A lawyer must make **reasonable efforts to prevent inadvertent or unauthorized disclosure** of, or access to, information relating to the representation. Factors include the sensitivity of the information, the likelihood of disclosure without safeguards, the cost and difficulty of added safeguards, and how much safeguards would impair the lawyer's ability to represent clients. A disclosure is not itself a violation if reasonable efforts were made. Lawyers using cloud storage, email or AI tools must assess the risk.

Rule 4.4(b): a lawyer who receives a document or electronically stored information relating to the representation of the client and knows or reasonably should know it was **inadvertently sent** must promptly notify the sender. The Model Rule does not require return or destruction; some states and procedural rules add duties.

### Reviews and public statements

ABA Formal Opinion 496 (2021) concludes that a negative online review by a former client does not by itself trigger the self-defense exception; the exception requires a controversy such as a formal claim or charge. A lawyer who responds publicly should say little and reveal no confidential information.

### Why the rule exists

If clients feared disclosure, they would withhold facts, and lawyers could not give sound advice or keep clients within the law. The exceptions mark cases where preventing grave harm outweighs that interest.

@example
title: How much may a lawyer reveal in self-defense?
problem: A former client sues his lawyer for malpractice, alleging she failed to file a motion. The lawyer wants to answer the complaint. May she reveal confidential information in her answer?
step: Identify the exception: Rule 1.6(b)(5) lets a lawyer reveal information to the extent reasonably necessary to establish a defense to a civil claim concerning her representation.
step: Apply the limit: she may reveal only what is reasonably necessary to respond, such as why the motion was not filed, not unrelated confidences.
step: She should seek a protective order or a confidentiality agreement limiting use of the information where practicable, and disclose to as few people as possible.
step: Conclusion: she may respond with information reasonably necessary to her defense; broader disclosures remain violations.

@mistakes
- Treating confidentiality as the same as privilege, or limiting it to information the client told the lawyer in confidence.
- Reading the Rule 1.6(b) exceptions as mandatory under the Model Rules.
- Revealing more than is reasonably necessary, or to people who cannot help prevent or fix the harm.
- Applying the harm-to-others exceptions to a crime or fraud in which the lawyer's services were not used.
