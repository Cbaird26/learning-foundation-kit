@article
Fees are where the lawyer's interests and the client's interests most visibly diverge, so the Rules regulate the amount, the form of the agreement, how fees are shared and how money is held.

### Reasonableness: Rule 1.5(a)

A lawyer shall not make an agreement for, charge or collect an **unreasonable fee or unreasonable amount for expenses**. The factors are: (1) time and labor required, novelty and difficulty of the questions, and skill needed; (2) likelihood that the work will preclude other employment; (3) the fee customarily charged locally; (4) the amount involved and results obtained; (5) time limitations imposed; (6) the nature and length of the relationship; (7) the experience, reputation and ability of the lawyer; and (8) whether the fee is fixed or contingent. A lawyer cannot bill for the same hour twice or charge overhead as an expense not agreed to.

### Communicating the fee: Rule 1.5(b)

The scope of the representation and the basis or rate of the fee and expenses must be communicated to the client, **preferably in writing**, before or within a reasonable time after the representation begins, unless the lawyer will charge a regularly represented client on the same basis. Changes must also be communicated.

### Contingent fees: Rule 1.5(c) and (d)

A contingent fee must be **in a writing signed by the client** stating: the method by which the fee is determined, including the percentage on settlement, trial or appeal; litigation and other expenses to be deducted from the recovery; and whether those expenses are deducted before or after the fee is calculated. The agreement must also say whether the client is liable for expenses regardless of outcome. At the end of the matter the lawyer must give the client a **written statement** of the outcome and, if there is a recovery, the remittance to the client and how it was determined. Contingent fees are prohibited in a **domestic relations matter** if contingent on securing a divorce or on the amount of alimony, support or property settlement, and in **criminal defense**. A lawyer discharged from a contingent matter usually recovers only quantum meruit.

### Dividing fees: Rule 1.5(e)

Lawyers **not in the same firm** may divide a fee only if (1) the division is in proportion to the services each performed, or each lawyer assumes **joint responsibility** for the representation; (2) the client agrees to the arrangement, including the share each lawyer will receive, and the agreement is **confirmed in writing**; and (3) the total fee is reasonable. A pure referral fee is therefore allowed when the referring lawyer assumes joint responsibility. A lawyer may not share fees with a nonlawyer (Rule 5.4(a)), with narrow exceptions.

### Retainers and advance fees

A true retainer, paid only to secure a lawyer's availability, is earned on receipt. An **advance payment for future services** belongs to the client until earned and must be deposited in the client trust account, from which the lawyer withdraws as fees are earned. Calling a deposit "nonrefundable" does not make a fee earned before the work is done, and unearned amounts must be refunded when the representation ends. Many states allow a flat fee that is clearly earned on receipt if the agreement says so and the fee is reasonable.

### Safekeeping property: Rule 1.15

A lawyer holds client and third-party property **separate from the lawyer's own**. Funds go in a **client trust account** in the state where the lawyer's office is located (or elsewhere with client consent). The lawyer may deposit only her own funds reasonably needed to pay bank charges. Required duties: promptly notify the client on receiving funds in which the client has an interest; promptly deliver funds the client is entitled to receive; render a prompt full accounting on request; and keep complete records for **five years** after the representation ends. When the lawyer and client **dispute** their interests in funds, the lawyer must keep the **disputed portion** in trust until the dispute is resolved and promptly distribute the **undisputed portion**. Using client money for the lawyer's own purposes is **conversion** (usually disbarment); mixing the lawyer's own funds with client funds is **commingling**.

### Financial assistance: Rule 1.8(e)

A lawyer may not give a client financial assistance in connection with litigation, except that she may advance court costs and expenses with repayment contingent on the outcome, or pay them for an indigent client. Since 2020, Rule 1.8(e)(3) also lets a lawyer representing an indigent client pro bono (on her own or through a nonprofit legal services organization or a law school program) give modest gifts for food, rent, transportation, medicine and other basic living expenses, if she does not promise them before retention or as an inducement, does not seek or accept reimbursement, and does not advertise them.

@example
title: Net recovery under a contingent fee
problem: A client's case settles for \$240,000. The written agreement provides a one-third contingent fee computed on the recovery after litigation expenses are deducted. Expenses were \$30,000. How much does the client receive?
step: The agreement calculates the fee after expenses, so deduct expenses first: \$240,000 - \$30,000 = \$210,000.
step: Compute the fee on the net amount: one-third of \$210,000 is \$70,000.
step: Client's share: \$210,000 - \$70,000 = \$140,000.
step: Check: \$140,000 + \$70,000 + \$30,000 = \$240,000. The sentence in the agreement about expenses before or after the fee changes the answer, which is why Rule 1.5(c) requires it.

@mistakes
- Computing the fee on the gross recovery when the agreement computes it after expenses (or the reverse).
- Treating an unearned "nonrefundable" advance fee as the lawyer's property.
- Putting an entire disputed settlement into the lawyer's account, or distributing the disputed part to the lawyer.
- Splitting a fee with a lawyer outside the firm without the client's written agreement.
