@article
Professional responsibility is the law that governs lawyers: what a lawyer must do, may do and must never do. You studied tort and contract law as rules about private conduct; here the rules are about a licensed profession, and the penalty for breaking them is discipline, not only damages.

### Who regulates lawyers

In the United States the **highest court of each state** has inherent authority to regulate the practice of law and to admit, discipline and disbar lawyers. Day-to-day work is done by a state bar or a disciplinary agency that investigates complaints, brings charges and recommends sanctions, which the court imposes. Federal courts also set rules for lawyers who appear before them, and administrative agencies may regulate practice before the agency.

### Sources of authority

The **ABA Model Rules of Professional Conduct** (1983, amended many times) are a model. They bind no one until a state's highest court adopts them, and almost every state has adopted a version, often with changes. California has its own distinct structure. The Model Rules are therefore the baseline for the MPRE and for this course, but the lawyer's actual duty is the rule of the jurisdiction. Other sources:

- The **Restatement (Third) of the Law Governing Lawyers**, persuasive on privilege, malpractice, fees and many other points.
- **ABA formal ethics opinions**, persuasive interpretations (for example, Formal Opinion 512 (2024) on generative AI tools).
- Procedural and evidence rules (FRCP 11, FRE 502), statutes, and case law on disqualification, malpractice and privilege.

A violation of the rules is a ground for discipline. The Scope section says a violation should not itself create a cause of action or a presumption of breach of a legal duty, although a court may treat the rules as evidence of the standard of care.

### Misconduct: Rule 8.4

It is professional misconduct to: (a) violate the Rules or knowingly assist or induce another to do so, or do so through the acts of another; (b) commit a criminal act that reflects adversely on honesty, trustworthiness or fitness as a lawyer; (c) engage in conduct involving dishonesty, fraud, deceit or misrepresentation; (d) engage in conduct prejudicial to the administration of justice; (e) state or imply an ability to improperly influence a government agency or official; (f) knowingly assist a judge in violating judicial conduct rules; and (g) (added 2016) engage in conduct the lawyer knows or reasonably should know is harassment or discrimination on specified grounds in conduct related to the practice of law. Rule 8.4(c) is not limited to law practice: a lawyer who lies on a personal loan application can be disciplined.

### Supervision and reporting

Rule 5.1: partners and lawyers with managerial authority must make **reasonable efforts to ensure the firm has measures** giving reasonable assurance that all lawyers conform to the Rules. A supervising lawyer is responsible for another lawyer's violation if she orders or knowingly ratifies the conduct, or knows of it when its consequences can be avoided or mitigated and fails to take reasonable remedial action. Rule 5.2: a subordinate lawyer is bound by the Rules, but does not violate them by acting in accordance with a supervisor's **reasonable resolution of an arguable question** of professional duty. Rule 5.3 extends supervisory duties to nonlawyer assistants.

Rule 8.3(a): a lawyer who **knows** another lawyer has violated the Rules in a way that raises a substantial question about that lawyer's honesty, trustworthiness or fitness must inform the appropriate professional authority. The duty yields to Rule 1.6: information protected by confidentiality is not reported without client consent.

### Which rules apply: Rule 8.5

A lawyer admitted in a jurisdiction is subject to its disciplinary authority wherever the conduct occurs. For choice of law: for conduct **in connection with a proceeding before a tribunal**, the rules of the jurisdiction where the tribunal sits apply, unless the tribunal's rules provide otherwise; for other conduct, the rules of the jurisdiction where the conduct occurred, or, if its predominant effect is elsewhere, that jurisdiction's rules. A lawyer is not subject to discipline if the conduct conforms to the rules of a jurisdiction where she reasonably believes the predominant effect will occur.

### The MPRE

The Multistate Professional Responsibility Examination has 60 multiple-choice questions in two hours (50 scored, 10 unscored pretest questions), scaled 50 to 150. Each jurisdiction sets its own passing score (75 to 86), and nearly all require it. Questions test the Model Rules, the Model Code of Judicial Conduct, and controlling law on privilege, malpractice and disqualification. Read the call of the question: "subject to discipline" and "likely to be disqualified" are different questions.

@example
title: Which jurisdiction's rule governs conduct before a tribunal?
problem: Ana is admitted in State A, where a rule permits a type of witness payment. She appears in a federal district court sitting in State B, whose local rule forbids the payment. The court's rules say nothing about which jurisdiction's ethics rules apply. Which rule governs her conduct?
step: Identify the conduct: it is connected with a proceeding before a tribunal, so Rule 8.5(b)(1) applies.
step: Rule 8.5(b)(1) points to the rules of the jurisdiction where the tribunal sits, unless the tribunal's own rules provide otherwise.
step: The tribunal sits in State B and its rules do not displace this default, so State B's rule governs.
step: Conclusion: Ana may not make the payment. Her admission in State A gives State A disciplinary authority, but State B's rule decides what conduct is allowed here.

@mistakes
- Treating the Model Rules as binding law in every state; only the adopted state version binds.
- Reading Rule 8.3 as requiring a lawyer to report violations she merely suspects, or to report information protected by Rule 1.6.
- Limiting Rule 8.4(b) and (c) to conduct inside law practice.
- Assuming a subordinate lawyer is never responsible because a supervisor gave the order.
