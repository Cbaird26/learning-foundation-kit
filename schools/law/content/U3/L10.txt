@article
A lawyer's duties of loyalty to the client do not license wrongdoing toward everyone else. Part 4 of the Rules sets minimum standards of honesty and respect for those who are not clients.

### Truthfulness: Rule 4.1

In the course of representing a client, a lawyer shall not **knowingly**: (a) make a **false statement of material fact or law** to a third person; or (b) fail to disclose a material fact to a third person when disclosure is necessary to avoid assisting a **criminal or fraudulent act** by a client, unless disclosure is prohibited by Rule 1.6. Ordinary negotiation conventions are not false statements of material fact: **estimates of price or value**, and statements about a party's **intentions as to an acceptable settlement**, are generally understood not to be statements of material fact. A lawyer who says "my client will not take a dollar less than \$500,000" when the client has authorized \$400,000 has not violated Rule 4.1, though outright false statements of fact (such as misrepresenting a document's existence or inventing a competing offer) would. Silence is not misrepresentation, but partially true statements that are misleading may be, and Rule 8.4(c) reaches dishonesty generally.

### Represented persons: Rule 4.2

In representing a client, a lawyer shall not **communicate about the subject of the representation** with a person the lawyer **knows to be represented by another lawyer in the matter**, unless she has the **other lawyer's consent**, or is authorized to do so by **law or a court order**. The rule applies **even if the represented person initiates the contact or consents**; only the other lawyer's consent counts. Parties may communicate directly with each other, and a lawyer may advise a client about what to say. For an **organization**, the rule covers a constituent who supervises, directs or regularly consults with the organization's lawyer about the matter, who has authority to obligate the organization with respect to the matter, or whose act or omission may be imputed to the organization. A receptionist who merely witnessed an accident is not covered. The rule does not stop a lawyer who represents no one in the matter from giving a second opinion to a represented person who asks for one. A lawyer who is a party and represents herself, however, is treated as representing a client: ABA Formal Opinion 502 (2022) says she may not contact a represented opposing party directly about the matter.

### Unrepresented persons: Rule 4.3

A lawyer shall not state or imply that she is **disinterested**. If the person misunderstands her role, she must correct the misunderstanding. She may not give **legal advice** to an unrepresented person, other than the advice to **secure counsel**, if the lawyer knows or reasonably should know that the person's interests may conflict with the client's. She may explain her client's position and prepare a document for signing.

### Respect for others: Rule 4.4

(a) A lawyer shall not use means that have **no substantial purpose other than to embarrass, delay or burden** a third person, or methods of obtaining evidence that violate the person's legal rights. (b) A lawyer who receives a document or electronically stored information and knows or reasonably should know it was **inadvertently sent** shall **promptly notify the sender**. The Model Rule does not require return, destruction or non-use; many states and procedural rules add duties (compare FRCP 26(b)(5)(B)).

### Assisting wrongdoing and giving advice: Rules 1.2(d) and 2.1

A lawyer may not **counsel or assist** a client in conduct the lawyer knows is criminal or fraudulent, but may discuss the legal consequences and may help a client make a good-faith effort to determine the validity or meaning of a law. Rule 2.1 requires **independent professional judgment** and candid advice, and permits referring to moral, economic, social and political factors. Rule 6.1 states an **aspirational** goal of at least 50 hours a year of pro bono service; it is not enforceable by discipline.

### The organization as client: Rule 1.13

A lawyer retained by an organization represents the **organization** acting through its duly authorized constituents, not the officers, directors or employees. If a lawyer knows that an officer or employee is acting, intends to act or refuses to act in a way that is a **violation of a legal obligation to the organization or a violation of law that might be imputed to it, and is likely to result in substantial injury to the organization**, the lawyer must **proceed as is reasonably necessary in the best interest of the organization**, which usually means referring the matter to **higher authority**, up to the highest authority that can act. If the highest authority insists on or fails to address a clear violation of law that is **reasonably certain to cause substantial injury** to the organization, the lawyer **may** reveal information relating to the representation outside the organization, even if Rule 1.6 would not allow it, to the extent reasonably necessary to prevent the injury (Rule 1.13(c)). This reporting out does not apply when the lawyer is retained to investigate or to defend the organization or a constituent against a claim of violation (Rule 1.13(d)). When the organization's interests may be adverse to a constituent's, the lawyer must **explain the identity of the client** to the constituent (Rule 1.13(f)); this is the corporate Upjohn warning. Dual representation is subject to Rule 1.7.

@example
title: Negotiation puffing
problem: In settlement talks, Lawyer Max says, "My client will never settle for less than \$1 million," though his client has authorized him to accept \$750,000. Has Max violated Rule 4.1?
step: Rule 4.1(a) forbids a knowing false statement of material fact or law to a third person.
step: Is the statement one of material fact? The Comment says statements about a party's intentions as to an acceptable settlement are ordinarily not taken as statements of material fact.
step: Compare a false statement such as "my client has already rejected a \$750,000 offer from another source," which would be a statement of fact.
step: Conclusion: no violation of Rule 4.1; but he may not fabricate facts, such as a competing offer that does not exist.

@mistakes
- Treating all negotiating exaggeration as lying, or all of it as permitted; the line is a false statement of material fact.
- Believing Rule 4.2 allows contact when the represented person starts the conversation.
- Giving an unrepresented opponent advice on whether to sign, beyond advice to get counsel.
- Reporting out under Rule 1.13 before using the organization's internal channels.

@practice self
prompt: In a civil case, your client lied at her deposition about a material fact and still refuses to correct it, and the case continues. Resolve the problem under the Model Rules. Then explain how the problem differs if the lying client is a criminal defendant who will testify at trial.
solution: Civil client. Rule 3.3 applies to a deposition as an ancillary proceeding. The lawyer knows the testimony is false, so Rule 3.3(a)(3) requires reasonable remedial measures: remonstrate with the client, urging correction; consider withdrawal if permitted or required, though withdrawal alone does not cure false evidence already given; take other reasonable measures; if necessary, disclose to the tribunal the facts reasonably necessary. Rule 3.3(c) makes the duty continue to the end of the proceeding and override Rule 1.6. Criminal defendant. The defendant decides whether to testify (Rule 1.2(a)), and, unless the lawyer knows the testimony will be false, the lawyer may not refuse to call her if she insists; if the lawyer knows it will be false, she may not offer it, and must take reasonable remedial measures if it is given. Nix v. Whiteside (1986) held that refusing to assist perjury and threatening to withdraw and disclose did not violate the Sixth Amendment. If the lawyer only reasonably believes, but does not know, the testimony is false, she may not refuse to call a criminal defendant to testify. A minority of jurisdictions allow narrative testimony.
rubric: Applies Rule 3.3(a)(3) and (c) to the civil deposition, including the remedial sequence.
rubric: States the client's right to testify in a criminal case and the lawyer's duty not to offer known false testimony.
rubric: Cites Nix v. Whiteside and notes the knowledge/belief distinction.
hint: Start with whether Rule 3.3 reaches a deposition and what triggers a remedial duty.
hint: Whose decision is it whether a criminal defendant testifies?
