@article
A client who ends the relationship is still owed loyalty on the matters the lawyer handled and confidentiality for what the lawyer learned. Last week's conflicts were about people the lawyer represents now; this week's are about people she used to represent, and about the lawyers around her.

### Former clients: Rule 1.9

**Rule 1.9(a).** A lawyer who has formerly represented a client in a matter shall not thereafter represent another person in the **same or a substantially related matter** in which that person's interests are **materially adverse** to the former client's, unless the former client gives **informed consent, confirmed in writing**.

A matter is **substantially related** if it involves the same transaction or legal dispute, or if there is a **substantial risk that confidential factual information as would normally have been obtained in the prior representation would materially advance the new client's position**. General knowledge of the former client's policies, practices or litigation philosophy ("playbook" knowledge) is ordinarily not enough. Passage of time may make information stale. The lawyer cannot defeat the rule by saying she has forgotten, or will not use, the information: the test looks at what she would normally have learned.

**Rule 1.9(b).** A lawyer shall not knowingly represent a person in the same or a substantially related matter in which a firm with which the lawyer **formerly practiced** had represented an adverse client, if the lawyer acquired protected information material to the matter. **Rule 1.9(c).** A lawyer may not **use** information relating to the former representation to the former client's disadvantage (unless it has become generally known, or the Rules permit), and may not reveal it.

### Imputation: Rule 1.10

**Rule 1.10(a).** While lawyers are associated in a firm, none of them shall knowingly represent a client when any one of them practicing alone would be prohibited by Rule 1.7 or 1.9, unless:

- the prohibition is based on a **personal interest** of the prohibited lawyer and does not present a significant risk of materially limiting the representation by the remaining lawyers; or
- the prohibition is based on Rule 1.9(a) or (b) and arises from a **lawyer's association with a prior firm**, and (i) the disqualified lawyer is **timely screened** from any participation in the matter and **apportioned no part of the fee**, (ii) **written notice** is promptly given to any affected former client, and (iii) **certifications of compliance** are provided to the former client at reasonable intervals upon written request.

The ABA permits this **screening of laterals**; a number of states are stricter (for example, refusing screening for a lawyer who substantially participated in the earlier matter), and the MPRE tests the Model Rule. Screening does not cure a conflict the lawyer created at the **current** firm, such as one arising from the firm's own client.

**Rule 1.10(b).** After a lawyer leaves a firm, the firm may represent a person with interests materially adverse to a client of the departed lawyer only if the matter is not the same or substantially related to the earlier matter, **or** no lawyer remaining in the firm has protected information material to the matter. **Rule 1.10(c).** An affected client may waive the conflict under Rule 1.7. Rule 1.10(d) assigns former government lawyers to Rule 1.11.

### Government lawyers: Rule 1.11

A lawyer who has left government service shall not represent a private client in a matter in which she **participated personally and substantially** while in government, unless the **appropriate government agency gives informed consent, confirmed in writing**. The conflict is imputed to her new firm unless she is **timely screened**, apportioned no part of the fee, and **written notice is promptly given to the agency**. A lawyer with **confidential government information** about a person that could be used to that person's material disadvantage may not represent a private client adverse to the person; here too the firm may avoid disqualification by screening and notice.

### Judges, arbitrators, mediators and law clerks: Rule 1.12

A lawyer may not represent anyone in a matter in which she participated personally and substantially as a judge, a law clerk to a judge, an arbitrator, a mediator or other neutral, unless **all parties give informed consent, confirmed in writing**. The firm is vicariously disqualified unless the lawyer is screened, receives no fee share, and gives written notice to the parties. A judge or neutral may not negotiate for employment with a party or a party's lawyer in a matter she is handling personally and substantially (Rule 1.12(b)). A **law clerk** to a judge is treated more leniently: she may negotiate for employment with a party or counsel only after notifying the judge, and, once she joins, the firm may be protected by screening and notice.

### Applying the rules

For a lateral move, ask three questions in order. Is the new matter the same or substantially related to something the lawyer did or learned before? Are the new client's interests materially adverse? If so, does Rule 1.9 consent, or Rule 1.10 / 1.11 / 1.12 screening, cure it?

@example
title: Playbook knowledge versus related matter
problem: Lawyer Vera defended Zeta Corp. in a product-liability suit five years ago. She now represents a customer suing Zeta for breach of a supply contract unrelated to products or to the earlier suit. Is she barred by Rule 1.9(a)?
step: Identify the former client and the adversity. Zeta is a former client and is adverse in the new matter.
step: Ask whether the matters are the same or substantially related. They involve different transactions and legal issues.
step: Ask whether confidential factual information normally obtained in the earlier matter would materially advance the customer's position. General knowledge of Zeta's litigation style is not enough.
step: Conclusion: Rule 1.9(a) does not bar the representation, but Rule 1.9(c) still forbids using or revealing Zeta's confidential information.

@mistakes
- Assuming that a former client's consent can be oral without a confirming writing.
- Reading "substantially related" as requiring identical legal issues; a shared factual core or the risk of using confidences is enough.
- Applying screening to a conflict that arose from the lawyer's own work at the current firm or to a lateral conflict where the jurisdiction forbids screening.
- Treating the personal-interest exception in Rule 1.10(a)(1) as covering a lawyer's duty to a former client.

@practice self
prompt: Firm F has represented Cora in trademark filings for three years. Cora is no longer a client. F's new client, Dax, wants F to sue Cora for infringing Dax's mark, which Dax says is confusingly similar to the mark F registered for Cora. Analyze under Rule 1.9 whether F may represent Dax and whether informed consent from Cora would cure the conflict.
solution: Issue: whether F may represent Dax against former client Cora. Rule: Rule 1.9(a) bars representing another person in the same or a substantially related matter with materially adverse interests to a former client unless the former client gives informed consent, confirmed in writing; Rule 1.9(c) bars using or revealing the former client's information. Application: Dax's suit is directly adverse to Cora, and the infringement claim concerns the very mark F registered for her, so the matters are substantially related and F likely holds confidential information that would advance Dax's position. Conclusion: F is barred unless Cora gives informed consent, confirmed in writing, which requires F to explain the risks, including that F could not use her confidences and may be limited in advocacy. Consent can cure this conflict because it is not a nonconsentable conflict under Rule 1.7(b)(3) (it is a former, not current, client; no claim in the same litigation as a current client), but F must also reasonably believe it can represent Dax competently. In practice Cora is unlikely to consent and F should decline; the conflict is imputed to all F lawyers under Rule 1.10(a).

rubric: States the Rule 1.9(a) consent requirement (informed consent, confirmed in writing).
rubric: Addresses Rule 1.9(c) and imputation under Rule 1.10(a).
rubric: Reaches a clear conclusion on whether consent can cure the conflict.
hint: Start with the elements of Rule 1.9(a).
hint: Consider whether the consent must come from Dax, Cora or both, and what must be explained.
