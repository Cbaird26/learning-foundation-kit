### The Model Code of Judicial Conduct

The ABA Model Code of Judicial Conduct (2007) has four Canons: (1) a judge shall uphold the independence, integrity and impartiality of the judiciary and avoid impropriety and its appearance; (2) a judge shall perform the duties of office impartially, competently and diligently; (3) a judge shall conduct extrajudicial activities to minimize the risk of conflict with judicial obligations; (4) a judge or candidate shall not engage in political or campaign activity inconsistent with the independence, integrity or impartiality of the judiciary. The Code binds judges only where a state has adopted it.

**Disqualification: Rule 2.11.** A judge shall disqualify herself in any proceeding in which her **impartiality might reasonably be questioned**, including when the judge: has a **personal bias or prejudice** concerning a party or lawyer, or personal knowledge of disputed facts; served as a lawyer in the matter or was associated with a lawyer who did so during their association; knows that she, her spouse or domestic partner, or a person **within the third degree of relationship** to either of them, is a party, a lawyer in the proceeding, or has more than a **de minimis interest** that could be substantially affected; or has made a public statement, other than in a court proceeding, that commits or appears to commit her to a particular result. Relationship degrees: parents and children are first degree; siblings, grandparents and grandchildren second; aunts, uncles, nieces, nephews and great-grandparents third. **Waiver:** apart from bias or prejudice, a judge subject to disqualification may disclose the basis on the record and ask the parties and lawyers to consider outside the judge's presence whether to waive; if they all agree, the judge may participate. **Due process.** *Caperton v. A.T. Massey Coal Co.* (2009) held that due process requires recusal when there is a serious risk of actual bias, such as when a litigant's extraordinary campaign support helped place the judge on the case.

**Ex parte communications: Rule 2.9.** A judge shall not initiate, permit or consider communications made to her outside the presence of the parties about a pending matter, with exceptions: scheduling and administrative matters not affecting the merits (if no party gains an advantage and the others are promptly notified); obtaining the written advice of a **disinterested expert** on the law if the judge notifies the parties of the expert's identity and the subject and gives them a chance to respond; consulting court staff; and conferring with the parties' consent in an effort to settle.

**Political activity: Rule 4.1.** A judge or candidate shall not act as a leader or hold office in a political organization, make speeches for or **publicly endorse or oppose** a candidate for other public office, or **personally solicit campaign contributions** (a campaign committee may solicit). *Republican Party of Minnesota v. White* (2002) struck down a ban on judicial candidates announcing views on disputed issues, and *Williams-Yulee v. Florida Bar* (2015) upheld a ban on personal solicitation of campaign funds, applying strict scrutiny.

### Lawyer discipline

The **highest court of the state** has final authority; a disciplinary agency investigates and prosecutes. A lawyer is subject to the authority of any jurisdiction in which she is admitted (Rule 8.5(a)). Sanctions range from private admonition, public reprimand or censure, and probation to **suspension and disbarment**; courts consider the duty violated, the lawyer's mental state, the harm and aggravating and mitigating factors. Discipline in one state ordinarily leads to **reciprocal discipline** in others. A lawyer faces proceedings that are **quasi-criminal** and must receive notice of the charges and a fair hearing (*In re Ruffalo* (1968)). Bar applicants have duties of candor (Rule 8.1), and a state may not exclude a person on arbitrary grounds (*Schware v. Board of Bar Examiners* (1957)). Lawyers must also report certain misconduct (Rule 8.3), subject to Rule 1.6.

### Legal malpractice

A malpractice claim is a tort or contract claim for breach of the duty owed in a client–lawyer relationship. The elements are: (1) a **duty** (a relationship, or duty to a nonclient in limited cases); (2) **breach**, meaning failure to use the skill, care and diligence of a reasonably prudent lawyer in the circumstances; (3) **causation**; and (4) **damages**. In a litigation-related claim the client must prove **causation by showing that, but for the lawyer's negligence, she would have won** the underlying matter ("**case within a case**"). Expert testimony on the standard of care is usually required, except where the error is obvious (such as missing a statute of limitations). A violation of the Model Rules does not itself create malpractice liability but may be evidence. Many states allow suit by a **nonclient intended beneficiary** of a lawyer's services (for example, a beneficiary named in a will the lawyer drafted badly). Breach of fiduciary duty can also support **fee forfeiture**. Under **Rule 1.8(h)**, a lawyer may not make an agreement prospectively limiting malpractice liability unless the client is independently represented in making it.

@example
title: Case within a case
problem: A lawyer negligently lets the statute of limitations expire on a client's contract claim. The client sues for malpractice. At the malpractice trial, the jury finds the underlying contract claim would have failed on the merits. May the client recover?
step: Elements: duty and breach are established, because lawyer missed the limitation deadline.
step: Causation requires proof that but for the negligence the client would have prevailed in the underlying claim.
step: The jury found she would have lost anyway, so the lawyer's breach did not cause damage.
step: Conclusion: no recovery for malpractice damages; the lawyer may still be subject to discipline for the neglect (Rule 1.3), and the client may seek a fee refund.

@mistakes
- Concluding a judge must disqualify only for a financial interest; bias, relationship and prior involvement also count.
- Thinking the parties may waive a judge's actual bias.
- Treating a Rule violation as automatic malpractice liability.
- Forgetting that a litigation malpractice plaintiff must prove she would have won the underlying case.
