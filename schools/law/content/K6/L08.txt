@article
Too many short sentences can sound choppy. We can join them so they flow.

### Joining sentences

A **complete sentence** has a subject and a verb and tells a whole idea. You can join two with a comma and a **joining word** such as **and, but, or, so.**

- "I like apples. I like pears." becomes "I like apples, and I like pears."
- "It was raining. We went out." becomes "It was raining, but we went out."

Use **and** to add, **but** to show a difference, **so** to show a result, and **or** to show a choice.

### Commas

- **In a list:** "We bought eggs, milk and bread."
- **After an opening word or phrase:** "Yes, I can help." "After lunch, we played."
- **Before the joining word** when you join two complete sentences.

### End marks

A telling sentence ends with a period. A question ends with a question mark. A strong feeling ends with an exclamation mark.

### Apostrophes

An **apostrophe** (') has two jobs.

1. **Contractions:** the apostrophe replaces missing letters. *do not* becomes *don't*. *it is* becomes *it's*.
2. **Possessives:** the apostrophe shows who owns something. *the dog's bone* means the bone of the dog.

Careful: **its** with no apostrophe shows ownership ("The cat licked its paw"). **It's** means "it is."

### Check it

Read your writing aloud. Pause where you hear a break, and check that each sentence has an end mark and a capital letter at the start.

@example
title: Combine and punctuate
problem: Combine the sentences and add commas: "Sam wanted a snack. There was no fruit."
step: Both are complete sentences. They show a difference, so use "but."
step: Put a comma before the joining word: "Sam wanted a snack, but there was no fruit."
step: Check the end mark. It is a period, and the first word has a capital letter.

@mistakes
- Joining two sentences with only a comma and no joining word.
- Writing "its" when you mean "it is," or the other way around.
- Putting an apostrophe in a plural, such as "apple's" for more than one apple.
