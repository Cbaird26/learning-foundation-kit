@article
This lesson covers the rest of the Sixth Amendment trial rights: jury, speedy trial and confrontation, plus the guilty plea that resolves most cases.

### The jury right

The Sixth Amendment right to an impartial jury applies to the states (*Duncan v. Louisiana*, 1968) in all **serious** offenses. An offense carrying more than six months of imprisonment is serious (*Baldwin v. New York*, 1970); an offense with a maximum of six months or less is presumptively petty (*Blanton v. City of North Las Vegas*, 1989).

- **Size:** a jury of six is constitutional (*Williams v. Florida*, 1970); a jury of five is not (*Ballew v. Georgia*, 1978).
- **Unanimity:** a conviction by a state jury for a serious offense must be unanimous (*Ramos v. Louisiana*, 2020, overruling *Apodaca*); a six-person jury must be unanimous (*Burch v. Louisiana*, 1979). *Ramos* does not apply retroactively on collateral review (*Edwards v. Vannoy*, 2021).
- **Jury finding of facts that increase punishment:** any fact, other than a prior conviction, that increases the statutory maximum must be found by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt (*Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 2000); this applies to guideline schemes that bind judges (*Blakely v. Washington*, 2004; *United States v. Booker*, 2005) and to facts that raise a mandatory minimum (*Alleyne v. United States*, 2013). *Erlinger v. United States* (2024) held that whether prior offenses were committed on different occasions under the Armed Career Criminal Act must be found by a unanimous jury beyond a reasonable doubt, not by a judge.
- **Fair cross-section:** the jury pool must be drawn from a fair cross-section of the community; a defendant shows a violation by showing that a distinctive group is underrepresented because of systematic exclusion (*Taylor v. Louisiana*, 1975; *Duren v. Missouri*, 1979).
- ***Batson* challenges.** The prosecution may not use peremptory strikes because of race (*Batson v. Kentucky*, 1986) or sex (*J.E.B. v. Alabama ex rel. T.B.*, 1994). Three steps: (1) the defendant makes a prima facie showing of discrimination; (2) the prosecutor offers a race-neutral reason; (3) the court decides whether the defendant has proved purposeful discrimination. *Flowers v. Mississippi* (2019) looks at the history of strikes in the case.
- **Jury impeachment:** the rule against inquiring into deliberations yields when a juror's statement shows that racial bias was a significant motivating factor in a conviction (*Peña-Rodriguez v. Colorado*, 2017).

### Confrontation

The accused has the right to confront witnesses. **Testimonial** hearsay is inadmissible unless the declarant is unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity to cross-examine (*Crawford v. Washington*, 2004). A statement is testimonial if its primary purpose is to create a record for later prosecution; statements to meet an ongoing emergency are not (*Davis v. Washington*, 2006). Forensic reports require the analyst to testify (*Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts*, 2009; *Bullcoming v. New Mexico*, 2011). In *Smith v. Arizona* (2024), the Court held that when a testifying expert conveys an absent analyst's statements as the basis for the expert's opinion and the statements support that opinion only if true, they are offered for their truth, so the Confrontation Clause applies if they are testimonial.

### Speedy trial

The constitutional right is judged by the four factors of *Barker v. Wingo* (1972): **length of delay** (a threshold: delay long enough to be "presumptively prejudicial"), **reason for delay**, the **defendant's assertion** of the right, and **prejudice**. The remedy is dismissal with prejudice. *Doggett v. United States* (1992) found a violation after delay of over eight years caused by government negligence, even without proof of specific prejudice. Delay caused by defense counsel is attributed to the defendant (*Vermont v. Brillon*, 2009). The right does not apply after conviction to sentencing delay (*Betterman v. Montana*, 2016).

The federal **Speedy Trial Act**, 18 U.S.C. § 3161, requires an indictment within **30 days** of arrest, and trial within **70 days** of the filing of the indictment or the defendant's first appearance before a judicial officer, **whichever is later**, excluding periods of delay such as the time from filing a pretrial motion to its prompt disposition. The court may dismiss with or without prejudice.

### Plea bargaining

Guilty pleas are valid if **knowing, voluntary and intelligent** (*Boykin v. Alabama*, 1969; *Brady v. United States*, 1970). Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 11 requires a colloquy and a factual basis. Prosecutors may threaten to bring more severe charges they could properly bring if the defendant does not plead (*Bordenkircher v. Hayes*, 1978). A defendant may plead guilty while maintaining innocence (*North Carolina v. Alford*, 1970). A prosecutor must keep the promises that induced the plea (*Santobello v. New York*, 1971). Statements made in plea discussions are generally inadmissible against the defendant under Federal Rule of Evidence 410, though the protection may be waived (*United States v. Mezzanatto*, 1995). A guilty plea does not bar a claim that the statute is unconstitutional (*Class v. United States*, 2018). Ineffective assistance claims about pleas are discussed in Lesson 10.

### Due process: disclosure

The prosecution must disclose evidence favorable to the accused that is **material** to guilt or punishment, whether or not requested (*Brady v. Maryland*, 1963), including impeachment evidence (*Giglio v. United States*, 1972). Evidence is material if there is a reasonable probability that disclosure would have changed the result (*Kyles v. Whitley*, 1995). The prosecution may not knowingly use false testimony (*Napue v. Illinois*, 1959). The Court has held that the government need not disclose impeachment material before a plea (*United States v. Ruiz*, 2002).

### Application

For each trial-rights issue, identify the source (jury clause, confrontation clause, speedy trial, due process) and its test, and distinguish the constitutional rule from the statutory or state rule that may give more.

@example
title: Counting a Speedy Trial Act deadline
problem: Ana is indicted on June 1. She is arrested and makes her first appearance 4 days later. A pretrial motion is pending for 10 days, which are excluded from the clock. By how many days after the indictment must trial begin at the latest?
step: The 70-day period runs from the later of the indictment and the first appearance. The first appearance came 4 days after the indictment, so it is the later date.
step: Start at day 4 and add 70 days: day 74.
step: Add the 10 excluded days: day 84.
step: Trial must begin by 84 days after the indictment was filed (ignoring other exclusions).

@mistakes
- Using the Barker factors for the Speedy Trial Act, or the reverse: one is constitutional and balancing, the other statutory and numeric.
- Reading Apprendi to cover prior convictions or facts that a judge finds within an unchanged sentencing range.
- Forgetting that a six-person jury is allowed but must be unanimous.
- Treating a Batson challenge as a single question; it has three steps.

@practice self
prompt: State prosecutes Dee for robbing a store clerk at gunpoint. At trial the only disputed issue is whether Dee was the robber; the jury acquits. The State then charges Dee with robbing a customer during the same incident. The federal government separately charges her with a federal offense for the same robbery. Analyze (1) whether the second state prosecution is barred, and (2) whether the federal prosecution is barred.
solution: (1) The clerk and customer robberies have separate victims and are different offenses under Blockburger, so Blockburger does not bar the second prosecution. But collateral estoppel (Ashe v. Swenson) applies: the acquittal necessarily decided that Dee was not the robber, and identity is also the issue in the second prosecution. Because the only contested fact was identity, the State may not relitigate it, so the second prosecution is barred. (2) Under dual sovereignty (Gamble), the federal government may prosecute Dee for a federal offense arising from the same conduct, because the state and federal governments are separate sovereigns. Conclusion: the second state prosecution is barred, but the federal prosecution is not.
hint: Ask separately whether Blockburger and Ashe bar the second state case.
hint: Federal and state governments are separate sovereigns.
rubric: Concludes that Blockburger does not bar the second state charge but that Ashe collateral estoppel does.
rubric: Explains that the acquittal must have necessarily decided identity.
rubric: Applies dual sovereignty to permit the federal prosecution.
