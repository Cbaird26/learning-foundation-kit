@article
The Warrant Clause says that "no Warrants shall issue, but upon probable cause, supported by Oath or affirmation, and particularly describing the place to be searched, and the persons or things to be seized." The general rule is that a warrantless search is presumptively unreasonable unless it falls within an exception (Lessons 3 and 4). The warrant requirement is the preferred route because it interposes a neutral official between police and citizen.

### Probable cause

**Probable cause to search** means a fair probability, in light of the totality of the circumstances, that contraband or evidence of a crime will be found in a particular place (*Illinois v. Gates*, 1983). **Probable cause to arrest** means facts that would lead a reasonable officer to believe that the suspect has committed or is committing a crime. Probable cause is more than a hunch or reasonable suspicion but less than proof by a preponderance. It is judged objectively; the officer's subjective intent is irrelevant (*Whren v. United States*, 1996).

### Tips and informants

*Gates* abandoned the rigid two-prong *Aguilar-Spinelli* test, which required a separate showing of the informant's veracity and basis of knowledge. Those two factors are still relevant, but a strong showing on one may offset a weak showing on the other. Corroboration of even innocent details, especially predictions of future behavior, can show that the informant has inside information. An uncorroborated anonymous tip, by contrast, is ordinarily too thin. For reasonable suspicion, *Florida v. J.L.* (2000) held that an anonymous tip that a person is carrying a gun, without more indicating reliability, does not justify a stop and frisk.

Probable cause must be current. **Stale** information (long ago, about a transient crime) does not support a search for items easily moved or consumed.

### What makes a warrant valid

1. **Neutral and detached magistrate.** A prosecutor who is leading the investigation does not qualify (*Coolidge v. New Hampshire*, 1971). The magistrate need not be a lawyer (*Shadwick v. Tampa*, 1972).
2. **Probable cause**, shown by facts, not conclusions, in an affidavit.
3. **Oath or affirmation.**
4. **Particularity**: the place and the items must be described so the officer executing the warrant can identify them. A warrant that fails to describe the things to be seized is invalid even if the affidavit describes them (*Groh v. Ramirez*, 2004). Honest mistakes in identifying the place, such as a warrant for one apartment on a floor that turns out to have two, are judged by what the officers reasonably knew (*Maryland v. Garrison*, 1987).

**Anticipatory warrants** (valid if probable cause exists that evidence will be at the place once a triggering condition occurs) are allowed (*United States v. Grubbs*, 2006).

### Challenging the affidavit: Franks

Under *Franks v. Delaware* (1978), a defendant who makes a substantial preliminary showing that the affiant included a false statement **knowingly and intentionally, or with reckless disregard for the truth**, and that the statement was **necessary to the finding of probable cause**, is entitled to a hearing. If the defendant proves the falsehood by a preponderance and the affidavit's remaining content is insufficient, the warrant is void and the fruits are suppressed. Negligent mistakes do not qualify.

### Arrests, homes and prompt review

- **Public arrest:** an officer may arrest without a warrant on probable cause for a felony, or a misdemeanor committed in the officer's presence (*United States v. Watson*, 1976). A custodial arrest for a minor offense punishable only by a fine is also allowed (*Atwater v. Lago Vista*, 2001).
- **Entering a home to arrest:** an arrest warrant plus reason to believe the suspect is inside suffices to enter the suspect's own home (*Payton v. New York*, 1980, which forbids routine warrantless home arrests). To enter a **third party's** home to find the suspect, police need a search warrant, consent or exigency (*Steagald v. United States*, 1981).
- **Prompt judicial determination:** a person arrested without a warrant is entitled to a prompt neutral determination of probable cause (*Gerstein v. Pugh*, 1975). A determination within 48 hours is generally prompt; beyond 48 hours the government bears the burden of showing a bona fide emergency or other extraordinary circumstance (*County of Riverside v. McLaughlin*, 1991).
- **Occupants during a warrant search:** police may detain the occupants of the premises while a valid warrant for contraband is executed (*Michigan v. Summers*, 1981).

### Executing the warrant

Officers must ordinarily **knock and announce** their presence and wait a reasonable time (*Wilson v. Arkansas*, 1995). They may dispense with it on reasonable suspicion that announcing would be dangerous or futile (*Richards v. Wisconsin*, 1997). But a violation of the knock-and-announce rule does **not** lead to suppression (*Hudson v. Michigan*, 2006; see Lesson 5).

### Application

Ask in order: who is the affiant and what facts are alleged; do the facts, apart from conclusions, show a fair probability that the item will be at the place; is any statement false and material; does the warrant describe the place and items particularly; and was it executed properly. A defect in any step may lead to a motion to suppress, subject to the good-faith exception (Lesson 5).

@example
title: A corroborated tip
problem: An anonymous caller tells police that Ana will leave her house at noon in a gray van carrying cocaine to a motel. Officers see Ana leave at noon in a gray van and drive to the motel. They get a warrant to search the van. Is there probable cause?
step: Test: totality of the circumstances (Gates). The tip is anonymous, so veracity and basis of knowledge are unknown.
step: Corroboration: the caller predicted Ana's future movements, which only someone with inside knowledge would know. Corroborating innocent details can show reliability.
step: Weigh together: a strong corroborated prediction raises the tip above rumor, even though the cocaine itself was not seen.
step: Conclusion: there was a fair probability of contraband in the van, so the warrant is supported by probable cause.

@mistakes
- Requiring an informant to satisfy the two Aguilar-Spinelli prongs separately; Gates uses the totality of the circumstances.
- Suppressing evidence for a negligent mistake in the affidavit; Franks requires a deliberate or reckless falsehood that is necessary to probable cause.
- Treating an arrest warrant as authorizing entry into any home where the suspect might be; Steagald requires a search warrant for a third party's home.
