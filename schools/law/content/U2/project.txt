### Common errors

- Skipping the threshold question of whether there was a search.
- Treating an arrest warrant as authorizing entry to a third party's home.
- Applying Gant to the trunk, or Terry's frisk without suspicion that the person is armed.
- Treating the Miranda warnings as curing an illegal arrest.
- Treating an ambiguous reference to a lawyer as an invocation.
- Confusing the Sixth Amendment right (attaches at charge, offense-specific) with Miranda (custody, not offense-specific).
- Applying Blockburger to conduct rather than elements, or forgetting dual sovereignty.
- Saying Strickland prejudice requires proof of acquittal.

prompt: Officer Ro stops a car driven by Ana without any reasonable suspicion or traffic violation. Ben is a passenger. Ro orders both out and, without any basis to think Ben is armed, pats him down and finds a handgun in his jacket. Ben is taken to the station, where, an hour later, he is given Miranda warnings, waives them, and confesses to owning the gun. Nothing intervenes between the stop and the confession. Ben moves to suppress the gun and the confession. How should the court rule?
choice: Deny both, because as a passenger Ben has no standing to challenge the stop.
choice: Deny both, because under Strieff the police acted at most negligently.
choice: Suppress the gun but admit the confession, because Miranda warnings purge the taint of the earlier violation.
choice*: Suppress both, because a passenger is seized and may challenge the stop, the frisk lacked reasonable suspicion that Ben was armed, and the warnings alone do not attenuate the confession from the illegal seizure.
hint: Start with whether Ben was seized, then trace each item of evidence.
hint: Brendlin, Terry, then the Brown v. Illinois factors.
solution: Under Brendlin, a passenger is seized in a traffic stop and may challenge its legality. The stop lacked reasonable suspicion, and the frisk required a separate suspicion that Ben was armed. The gun is the fruit. The confession, an hour later with no intervening circumstance other than Miranda warnings, is not attenuated (Brown). Strieff does not apply because no valid pre-existing warrant was discovered.

@exam self
prompt: Police interrogate Ana in custody about a robbery for which she was indicted yesterday and appointed counsel. After warnings she says, "I'd like to talk to my lawyer first." The detective stops. Two days later, a different detective, who did not contact her lawyer, visits her in jail, gives warnings, and asks if she wants to talk. She says yes and confesses. At trial, the prosecution offers the confession. In a short memo, analyze (1) the Miranda and Edwards issue, (2) the Sixth Amendment issue, and (3) whether the confession could be used to impeach Ana if she testifies inconsistently and it is excluded from the case-in-chief.
solution: (1) Ana was in custody and interrogated. "I'd like to talk to my lawyer first" is an unambiguous invocation of counsel (contrast Davis). Under Edwards, police may not reinitiate interrogation until counsel is present unless the suspect initiates, and the 14-day Shatzer break did not occur because she remained in custody. Her later waiver following police-initiated contact is invalid, so the confession violates Miranda-Edwards. (2) The indictment attached the Sixth Amendment right. After Montejo, a defendant must invoke the right, and she did invoke counsel in the first session; a valid waiver of the Sixth Amendment right through Miranda warnings is possible under Patterson, but the Edwards rule makes the waiver ineffective. The confession is also the product of deliberate police-initiated contact after invocation. (3) A statement obtained in violation of Miranda or the Sixth Amendment may be used to impeach the defendant's inconsistent testimony if the statement was voluntary (Harris; Ventris), but not if it was involuntary under due process. Nothing in the facts suggests coercion, so impeachment is likely allowed.
hint: Treat the Fifth Amendment issue and the Sixth Amendment issue as separate questions.
hint: For impeachment, separate voluntariness from the Miranda or Massiah violation.
rubric: Identifies a clear invocation and applies Edwards, including the lack of a Shatzer break.
rubric: Addresses attachment of the Sixth Amendment right and the Patterson and Montejo waiver rules.
rubric: Explains that a voluntary but unwarned or Edwards-violating statement may impeach, citing Harris or Ventris.
