@article
A warrantless search or seizure is presumptively unreasonable unless it fits a recognized exception (*Katz v. United States*, 1967). The government bears the burden of showing that an exception applies. This lesson covers three exceptions that turn on circumstances rather than on a category of place.

### Exigent circumstances

Exigency excuses the warrant when the need for action is too urgent to wait. The recognized categories are:

- **Hot pursuit** of a fleeing felon (*Warden v. Hayden*, 1967). For a fleeing **misdemeanant**, there is no categorical rule: the officer may enter a home without a warrant only if the circumstances show a real exigency, judged case by case (*Lange v. California*, 2021).
- **Imminent destruction of evidence.** Police may enter when they reasonably believe evidence is about to be destroyed. If the police create the exigency, it still counts as long as their conduct before the entry did not violate, or threaten to violate, the Fourth Amendment (*Kentucky v. King*, 2011): knocking and announcing is lawful, so the occupants' scramble to destroy drugs justified entry.
- **Dissipation of blood alcohol.** Dissipation of alcohol is not a per se exigency for a warrantless blood draw in a drunk-driving case; the court looks at the totality of the circumstances (*Missouri v. McNeely*, 2013). When a driver is unconscious or stuporous, a blood draw without a warrant is generally permitted (*Mitchell v. Wisconsin*, 2019, plurality). A warrantless breath test is allowed as a search incident to a drunk-driving arrest; a warrantless blood test is not (*Birchfield v. North Dakota*, 2016).
- **Danger to life: the emergency-aid exception.** Officers may enter a home without a warrant when they have an objectively reasonable basis for believing that an occupant is seriously injured or imminently threatened with such injury (*Brigham City v. Stuart*, 2006, which rejected an inquiry into the officers' subjective motives). *Case v. Montana* (2026) confirmed that police may enter a home without a warrant when they have an objectively reasonable basis to believe someone inside needs emergency aid. Under *Brigham City*, they need not show probable cause of a crime. There is no free-standing "community caretaking" exception for the home (*Caniglia v. Strom*, 2021), so the entry must fit emergency aid or another exception, and its scope is limited to the emergency.

The policy is that the warrant requirement yields when delay would cost life, safety or evidence. The risk of abuse is why courts require an objective, fact-specific showing.

### Consent

Consent is a valid waiver of the warrant and probable-cause requirements if it is **voluntary** under the totality of the circumstances. Police need not tell the person of the right to refuse (*Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 1973). Submission to a claim of lawful authority is not consent: when an officer says he has a warrant, the occupant's acquiescence is not voluntary (*Bumper v. North Carolina*, 1968).

- **Scope** is measured by objective reasonableness: what a typical person would have understood from the exchange (*Florida v. Jimeno*, 1991). Consent to search a car for drugs covers a closed paper bag in it.
- **Third-party consent.** A person with **common authority** over the premises may consent to a search that binds a co-occupant (*United States v. Matlock*, 1974). Consent from someone whom the officers reasonably, but mistakenly, believed had authority is valid (*Illinois v. Rodriguez*, 1990, apparent authority).
- **Co-occupant objection.** A physically present co-occupant who objects defeats another occupant's consent (*Georgia v. Randolph*, 2006). The rule is narrow: if the objector is absent because police lawfully removed him, such as by a valid arrest, the remaining occupant's consent is effective (*Fernandez v. California*, 2014).

### Plain view

Police may seize an item without a warrant if (1) the officer is **lawfully in the place from which the item is seen**, (2) the item's incriminating character is **immediately apparent**, meaning that the officer has probable cause to believe it is contraband or evidence, and (3) the officer has a **lawful right of access** to the object itself (*Horton v. California*, 1990). Inadvertence is not required. The doctrine permits a seizure; it does not give a right to search.

If an officer must manipulate an object to see its incriminating nature, that act is a separate search that requires probable cause (*Arizona v. Hicks*, 1987, moving stereo equipment to read serial numbers). By the **plain feel** corollary, an officer conducting a lawful pat-down may seize an object whose contour makes its illegal nature immediately apparent, but not one she must squeeze and manipulate to identify (*Minnesota v. Dickerson*, 1993).

### Applying the exceptions

Ask: (1) Was there a search or seizure? (2) Was there a warrant? If not, (3) which exception does the government invoke and what are its elements? (4) Did the officers stay within the exception's scope? Many failures occur at step 4: for example, an emergency entry that turns into a general search for evidence.

@example
title: A welfare check that finds a gun
problem: A caller reports that her neighbor, Ana, fell in her kitchen and is not answering. Officers arrive, hear weak cries for help through the door, and enter without a warrant. They find Ana injured and, on the counter, a rifle that Ana, a convicted felon, is forbidden to possess. May the rifle be used against her?
step: Warrantless entry into a home: the government needs an exception. The relevant one is emergency aid.
step: Test: did the officers have an objectively reasonable basis to believe someone inside needed emergency aid? Yes: a report of a fall, no answer and weak cries.
step: Probable cause of a crime is not required (Brigham City), and the officers' own motives do not matter if the basis is objectively reasonable.
step: The rifle: officers were lawfully inside, its illegality as contraband is immediately apparent (Ana's status is known to the police), and they have lawful access, so plain view allows seizure. The motion to suppress should be denied.

@mistakes
- Requiring probable cause of a crime for emergency aid, or looking for the officers' subjective motive, when the test is an objectively reasonable basis to believe someone needs help.
- Treating the police-created exigency rule as barring all exigencies the police cause: it bars only those created by conduct violating or threatening to violate the Fourth Amendment.
- Saying consent requires a warning of the right to refuse, or that acquiescence to a claimed warrant is consent.
- Extending plain view to searches: moving an object to see what is underneath requires probable cause (Hicks).
