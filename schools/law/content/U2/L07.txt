@article
The Fifth Amendment provides that no person "shall be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself." The privilege protects against compelled **testimonial** communications; taking blood, fingerprints or a voice or handwriting sample is not testimonial (*Schmerber v. California*, 1966). A prosecutor may not comment on a defendant's refusal to testify at trial (*Griffin v. California*, 1965). *Miranda v. Arizona* (1966) created prophylactic procedures to protect the privilege during police interrogation, and *Dickerson v. United States* (2000) held that Miranda is a constitutional rule that Congress may not overrule.

### The rule and its triggers

Statements stemming from **custodial interrogation** may not be used in the prosecution's case-in-chief unless police first warned the suspect that: (1) he has the right to remain silent; (2) anything he says can be used against him; (3) he has the right to an attorney, and to have the attorney present during questioning; and (4) if he cannot afford an attorney, one will be appointed. Miranda applies only where **both** triggers are present.

**Custody** is an objective test: would a reasonable person in the suspect's position have felt that he was not at liberty to terminate the interrogation and leave, and that his freedom was restrained to the degree associated with formal arrest? The officer's undisclosed belief is irrelevant (*Stansbury v. California*, 1994). Examples:

- Ordinary **traffic stops** are not custody (*Berkemer v. McCarty*, 1984), and neither is a brief roadside stop for investigation, but a stop that escalates to the equivalent of arrest is.
- A prisoner questioned about a crime outside the prison is not automatically in custody (*Howes v. Fields*, 2012).
- A child's age, if known to the officer or objectively apparent, is relevant to the custody analysis (*J.D.B. v. North Carolina*, 2011).
- A voluntary station-house interview is not custodial merely because it is at a police station (*Oregon v. Mathiason*, 1977).

**Interrogation** is express questioning or its **functional equivalent**: words or actions the police should know are reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response (*Rhode Island v. Innis*, 1980). Police remarks that are normally attendant to arrest and custody are not interrogation, and a spontaneous statement is not covered. Allowing a suspect to speak with his wife while recording was not interrogation (*Arizona v. Mauro*, 1987).

### Exceptions

- **Public safety:** police may ask questions reasonably prompted by a concern for public safety, without warnings, and the answers are admissible (*New York v. Quarles*, 1984).
- **Booking questions** about identity and routine biographical data (*Pennsylvania v. Muniz*, 1990).
- **Undercover agents:** questioning by an undercover officer whom the suspect does not know is an officer is not coercive and requires no warnings (*Illinois v. Perkins*, 1990).

### Waiver

A suspect may waive the rights if the waiver is **knowing, intelligent and voluntary**; the government bears the burden by a preponderance. A waiver may be **implied** by the suspect's answering questions after warnings and understanding them (*Berghuis v. Thompkins*, 2010). Police need not tell a suspect that an attorney is trying to reach him (*Moran v. Burbine*, 1986). Coercion by the police is required to make a waiver involuntary (*Colorado v. Connelly*, 1986).

### Invocation

- **Silence:** the suspect must unambiguously invoke; remaining silent is not enough (*Berghuis*; see also *Salinas v. Texas*, 2013, plurality, noncustodial silence). If he invokes, questioning must cease and be "scrupulously honored"; police may return after a significant time with fresh warnings (*Michigan v. Mosley*, 1975).
- **Counsel:** the request must be unambiguous: "Maybe I should talk to a lawyer" does not require police to stop (*Davis v. United States*, 1994). Once counsel is clearly invoked, interrogation must stop and may not resume until counsel is present unless the suspect himself initiates further communication (*Edwards v. Arizona*, 1981; *Minnick v. Mississippi*, 1990). The protection ends 14 days after the suspect is released from custody and returns to his normal life (*Maryland v. Shatzer*, 2010).

### Remedies

A Miranda violation bars the statement from the prosecution's case-in-chief, but the statement may be used to impeach the defendant if it was voluntary (*Harris v. New York*, 1971). The prosecution may not use a defendant's post-warning silence to impeach (*Doyle v. Ohio*, 1976). In *Vega v. Tekoh* (2022), the Court held that a violation of Miranda alone does not support a damages claim under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, because Miranda warnings are a prophylactic rule, not the Fifth Amendment itself; the remedy is exclusion at trial.

### Application

Analyze in order: (1) Was the suspect in custody? (2) Was there interrogation or its functional equivalent? (3) Were adequate warnings given? (4) Did the suspect validly waive, or invoke silence or counsel? (5) Does an exception apply? (6) What can be used, in the case-in-chief and for impeachment?

@example
title: A station-house confession after an invocation
problem: Dee is arrested for robbery and brought to the police station. After warnings, she says, "I want a lawyer." Questioning stops, and she is returned to her cell. The next morning, a different detective gives her the warnings again and asks whether she will talk. She says "OK" and confesses. Is the confession admissible?
step: Custody and interrogation: Dee was in custody, and the detective's questioning was interrogation.
step: Invocation: "I want a lawyer" is an unambiguous request for counsel (contrast Davis's "maybe").
step: Edwards: once counsel is invoked, police may not reinitiate questioning until counsel is present, unless the suspect initiates contact or there has been a 14-day break in custody (Shatzer).
step: Dee remained in custody and did not initiate. Her later waiver does not cure the violation, so the confession is inadmissible in the case-in-chief (though possibly usable to impeach under Harris if voluntary).

@mistakes
- Treating every police questioning as requiring warnings: Miranda requires both custody and interrogation.
- Treating an ambiguous remark ("maybe I should talk to a lawyer") as an invocation, or treating silence as an invocation of silence.
- Allowing police to reinterrogate after invocation just because the suspect later "waives": under Edwards, a waiver after police-initiated contact is invalid.
- Saying that a Miranda violation produces a section 1983 damages claim; Vega v. Tekoh says no.
