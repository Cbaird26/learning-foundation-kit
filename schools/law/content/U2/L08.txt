@article
A confession can be challenged under three overlapping sources: the **Due Process Clause** (voluntariness), the **Fifth Amendment** (Miranda; Lesson 7) and the **Sixth Amendment** (counsel). A statement must survive all three.

### Due process voluntariness

A confession is inadmissible for any purpose, including impeachment, if it was involuntary under the totality of the circumstances: the court weighs the suspect's characteristics (age, intelligence, mental state, prior experience) and the details of the interrogation (length, repetition, deprivation of food or sleep, physical force, threats, promises) (*Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 1973, on the totality test; *Brown v. Mississippi*, 1936, for a confession obtained by torture). **Coercive police activity** is a necessary predicate: a confession is not involuntary under the Due Process Clause simply because the suspect was mentally ill or hearing voices (*Colorado v. Connelly*, 1986). Admission of an involuntary confession is subject to harmless-error review (*Arizona v. Fulminante*, 1991). Deception about the evidence alone is a factor, but not usually enough to make a confession involuntary.

### The Sixth Amendment right to counsel during questioning

The Sixth Amendment gives the accused the right to the assistance of counsel "in all criminal prosecutions." The right **attaches** at the initiation of adversary judicial proceedings, such as a formal charge, preliminary hearing, indictment, information or arraignment; in practice, the first appearance before a judicial officer where the defendant learns the charge and his liberty is subject to restriction (*Rothgery v. Gillespie County*, 2008). It does **not** attach at arrest or at the start of an investigation (*Kirby v. Illinois*, 1972).

After attachment, police may not **deliberately elicit** incriminating statements from the accused without counsel unless there is a valid waiver (*Massiah v. United States*, 1964). It applies even when the questioning is covert, by an informant working for the government (*Massiah*; *Maine v. Moulton*, 1985), but not to an informant who merely listens and does not engage the defendant (*Kuhlmann v. Wilson*, 1986).

- **Waiver:** a valid Miranda waiver given after the warnings is generally also a valid waiver of the Sixth Amendment right for police-initiated questioning (*Patterson v. Illinois*, 1988).
- **Montejo v. Louisiana** (2009) overruled *Michigan v. Jackson* (1986). A represented defendant is not given a special rule that bars all police-initiated contact; if the defendant wishes to invoke, he must do so (for example, under Miranda and Edwards); a defendant who waives after warnings may be questioned.
- **Offense-specific:** the right applies only to the charged offense, and not to uncharged offenses, even closely related ones, unless they are the same offense under *Blockburger* (*Texas v. Cobb*, 2001; Lesson 11). By contrast, invoking the Sixth Amendment right at an arraignment does not by itself invoke the separate Miranda-Edwards right (*McNeil v. Wisconsin*, 1991), while a suspect who invokes counsel in custodial interrogation is protected whatever offense the police ask about (*Arizona v. Roberson*, 1988).
- **Remedy:** statements elicited in violation of the right are inadmissible in the prosecution's case-in-chief; they may be used to impeach the defendant (*Kansas v. Ventris*, 2009).

### Two-step interrogation and physical fruits

- *Oregon v. Elstad* (1985): a voluntary but unwarned statement does not taint a later warned statement if the first violation was not coercive and no deliberate tactic was used.
- *Missouri v. Seibert* (2004): when police deliberately question first without warnings, obtain a confession, then warn and obtain a repetition, the second statement is inadmissible unless curative measures were taken. Justice Kennedy's concurrence, which most lower courts treat as controlling (the Supreme Court has not resolved the point), focuses on whether the two-step strategy was deliberate; the plurality used a broader effects-based test.
- *United States v. Patane* (2004): the physical fruits of an unwarned but voluntary statement (a gun found because the suspect described where it was) are admissible, because Miranda is prophylactic; the statement itself is excluded, but the fruit is not.

### Policy and application

Voluntariness protects the reliability and fairness of the criminal process; Miranda protects the privilege; the Sixth Amendment protects the adversary system once the state has committed to prosecute. Work through each:

1. Was the statement voluntary under due process (coercive police activity)?
2. Was the suspect in custody and interrogated? Were warnings given and was there a valid waiver or invocation?
3. Had formal charges or an appearance occurred, and did police deliberately elicit statements about the charged offense?
4. If a first statement was defective, is a later statement tainted (Elstad or Seibert), and what physical evidence can still be used (Patane)?

@example
title: An informant after indictment
problem: Ben is indicted for robbery and released on bail. Police ask a codefendant, Cara, who is cooperating, to wear a recording device and talk to Ben about the robbery. Cara asks Ben leading questions, and Ben admits his role. Is the recording admissible in the prosecution's case-in-chief?
step: Attachment: the indictment initiated adversary judicial proceedings, so the Sixth Amendment right to counsel attached.
step: Elicitation: Cara was a government agent and deliberately elicited the statements by asking questions about the charged offense.
step: Waiver: Ben did not know about the agent and did not waive the right.
step: Conclusion: Massiah bars the recording from the case-in-chief; it might still be used to impeach Ben if he testifies inconsistently (Ventris).

@mistakes
- Saying that the Sixth Amendment right attaches at arrest; it attaches at formal charge or initial appearance.
- Applying the Sixth Amendment to uncharged offenses: the right is offense-specific.
- Treating a mental illness or intoxication as making a confession involuntary without any police coercion.
- Suppressing the physical fruits of a voluntary statement taken without Miranda warnings.

@practice self
prompt: Police arrest Dee for robbery at 9:00 p.m. At the station an officer warns her and asks whether she will talk. Dee says, "I think I need a lawyer." The officer replies, "Do you want one or not?" Dee says, "No, I'll talk." She admits to the robbery. Before the questioning, in the patrol car, the arresting officer had asked her "Where did you put the gun?" and she answered, "In the river." Analyze whether the two statements can be used against her in the prosecution's case-in-chief.
solution: Patrol-car statement: Dee was in custody and the officer expressly questioned her without any warnings. Unless an exception applies, the statement is inadmissible in the case-in-chief. The public-safety exception (Quarles) could apply if the question was prompted by a concern about a gun posing a danger to the public, but the facts say nothing about an immediate danger, so the statement is likely suppressed in the case-in-chief (but usable for impeachment if voluntary: Harris). The gun itself, found because of a voluntary but unwarned statement, is admissible (Patane). Station statement: after warnings, "I think I need a lawyer" is likely an ambiguous reference (courts differ on close wordings), so under Davis the officer was not required to stop questioning (the officer's clarification was permitted). Dee then said she would talk, and her answer after warnings supports a knowing, intelligent and voluntary waiver. The first statement's lack of warnings does not taint the second under Elstad unless the officers deliberately used a two-step strategy (Seibert); nothing suggests this. The station statement is therefore likely admissible.
