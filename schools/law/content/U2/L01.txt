@article
The Fourth Amendment protects "the right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against unreasonable searches and seizures." Every Fourth Amendment problem has a threshold question: **did a government actor conduct a search or a seizure at all?** If not, the Amendment is not triggered and no warrant, probable cause or reasonableness is required.

### Who is acting

The Amendment restrains government agents. A private party's search is outside it, even if illegal or intrusive. Under the **private search doctrine** (*United States v. Jacobsen*, 1984), police may repeat a private search without a warrant, but may not exceed its scope. If a carrier opens a package and sees white powder, an officer may look at what the carrier saw; the officer's looking is not a new search.

### Two tests for a search

**1. The property-based (trespass) test.** A search occurs when the government physically intrudes on a constitutionally protected area (persons, houses, papers, effects) to obtain information. *United States v. Jones* (2012) held that attaching a GPS tracker to a car and monitoring it for weeks was a search. *Florida v. Jardines* (2013) held that bringing a drug-sniffing dog onto the front porch of a home is a search, because the porch is **curtilage** (the area immediately around the home) and the implied license to approach and knock does not cover that purpose.

**2. The reasonable-expectation-of-privacy test.** From *Katz v. United States* (1967) and Justice Harlan's concurrence: a search occurs when the government violates an expectation of privacy that (a) the person actually held and (b) society accepts as reasonable. Katz had such an expectation in a closed phone booth. This test supplements, rather than replaces, the property test.

### Applying the tests: what usually is and is not a search

| Police action | Search? | Case |
|---|---|---|
| Observing what is knowingly exposed to the public | No | *Katz* (what a person "knowingly exposes to the public" is unprotected) |
| Searching an **open field** (unoccupied land outside the curtilage) | No | *Oliver v. United States* (1984) |
| Inspecting garbage left at the curb | No | *California v. Greenwood* (1988) |
| Fixed-wing flyover at 1,000 feet; helicopter at 400 feet, in lawful airspace | No | *California v. Ciraolo* (1986); *Florida v. Riley* (1989) |
| Using a thermal imager on a home from the street | **Yes** | *Kyllo v. United States* (2001): sense-enhancing technology not in general public use, used to learn details of the home's interior |
| A dog sniff of luggage in a public place, or of a car during a lawful stop | No | *United States v. Place* (1983); *Illinois v. Caballes* (2005) (a sniff reveals only contraband) |
| Dog sniff at the front door of a house | **Yes** | *Jardines* |
| Following a car on public roads with a beeper | No | *United States v. Knotts* (1983) |

### The third-party doctrine and its limit

Information voluntarily conveyed to a third party carries no reasonable expectation of privacy: bank records (*United States v. Miller*, 1976) and the numbers dialed on a phone (*Smith v. Maryland*, 1979). The same logic covers a "false friend": a person who confides in an informant assumes the risk that the confidant is working for the police (*United States v. White*, 1971; *Hoffa v. United States*, 1966).

*Carpenter v. United States* (2018) refused to extend the doctrine mechanically to **historical cell-site location information (CSLI)**. Seven days of such data gives an intimate, retrospective record of a person's movements, so acquiring it is a search requiring a warrant; the Court called the decision narrow and left conventional business records and real-time tracking questions aside. Following that line, *Chatrie v. United States* (2026) held that obtaining a person's location data from Google with a geofence warrant is a Fourth Amendment search; whether a particular warrant was valid is a separate question (Lesson 2).

### Seizures, briefly

A **seizure of a person** occurs when an officer, by physical force or show of authority, restrains liberty so that a reasonable person would not feel free to leave (*United States v. Mendenhall*, 1980). A seizure by force occurs when an officer applies force with the intent to restrain, even if the person escapes (*Torres v. Madrid*, 2021); a seizure by show of authority requires submission (*California v. Hodari D.*, 1991). A seizure of property is meaningful interference with possessory interests.

### Policy and application

The tests guard against both unchecked surveillance and the narrowing of privacy by technology; *Kyllo* and *Carpenter* each protect what the Framers' world would have required a physical intrusion to learn. In an exam answer, use IRAC: ask first whether the actor is governmental; then whether the officer physically intruded on a house, paper, person or effect (or its curtilage) to gather information; then whether the target had a reasonable expectation of privacy (not exposed to the public, not given to a third party, not obtainable only through technology that exposes the interior of a home). Only after concluding that there was a search do you ask whether it was reasonable.

@example
title: A thermal scan and a package
problem: An officer parks on a public street and aims a handheld thermal imager at Ana's house. It shows unusually bright heat patterns in one room. Separately, a shipping clerk opens a damaged box addressed to Ben, sees bags of pills, and calls an officer, who looks into the box without a warrant. Which actions are searches?
step: The thermal scan: the officer is a government actor, standing in a public place, but he is using technology that is not in general public use to learn about the interior of a home.
step: Under Kyllo, that is a search even though there was no physical entry. It needs a warrant unless an exception applies.
step: The box: the clerk is a private party, so the clerk's opening is not governed by the Fourth Amendment.
step: The officer's look is lawful under Jacobsen because it replicates what the clerk already exposed. Had the officer tested or opened sealed bags the clerk had not opened, he would have exceeded the private search and likely conducted a search.

@mistakes
- Treating a search as a seizure of a person (or the reverse): a search invades privacy or property to gather information; a seizure restrains a person or takes possession of property.
- Saying "no search because the police were on public property." Kyllo (thermal imaging from the street) and Jones (a tracker on a car in public) are both searches.
- Applying the third-party doctrine to everything: Carpenter limits it for long-term location records.

solution: Issue: whether acquiring a customer's bank records from the bank is a search.
