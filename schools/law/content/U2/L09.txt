@article
Mistaken eyewitness identification is a leading cause of wrongful convictions. The Constitution regulates identifications through two separate tools: the **Sixth Amendment** (does the defendant have a right to counsel?) and the **Due Process Clause** (was the procedure so suggestive that the identification is unreliable?). Treat them as separate questions.

### Fifth Amendment and physical characteristics

Compelling a suspect to stand in a lineup, speak words for voice identification, or provide a handwriting sample does not violate the privilege against self-incrimination, because these are not testimonial (*United States v. Wade*, 1967; *Schmerber v. California*, 1966). Nor does a lineup, standing alone, violate the Fourth Amendment if the suspect is lawfully in custody.

### The Sixth Amendment: counsel at the lineup

*United States v. Wade* (1967) held that a **post-indictment lineup** is a critical stage at which the accused has the right to counsel, because the lineup is subject to suggestion and the defendant cannot reconstruct the event at trial. *Gilbert v. California* (1967) applied the same rule to testimony about the lineup. The remedy has two parts:

- The **out-of-court identification** is excluded as a per se matter if counsel was absent and the right was not waived.
- An **in-court identification** by that witness is admissible only if the prosecution shows, by **clear and convincing evidence**, that it has an **independent source** in the witness's observation of the crime.

The right is limited in two ways:

- It applies only after **adversary judicial proceedings have begun** (indictment, arraignment or preliminary hearing). A show-up or lineup before charging, even after arrest, does not trigger the right (*Kirby v. Illinois*, 1972). A lineup at or after a preliminary hearing is covered (*Moore v. Illinois*, 1977).
- It applies only when the **defendant is present** at a confrontation. A **photo display** shown to a witness without the defendant present is not a critical stage (*United States v. Ash*, 1973), even after indictment.

### Due process: suggestive procedures

Any identification, whether pre- or post-charge, may be challenged under the Due Process Clause if the police used an **unnecessarily suggestive** procedure that creates a very substantial likelihood of irreparable misidentification (*Stovall v. Denno*, 1967, show-up; *Simmons v. United States*, 1968, photographs). The courts use a two-step test:

1. **Was the procedure unnecessarily suggestive?** Single-suspect show-ups, a lineup in which the suspect alone matches the description, or a photo array in which the suspect's picture stands out are examples. A show-up justified by an emergency (for example, a dying victim who may not survive) may be necessary and therefore not improper (*Stovall*).
2. **If so, is the identification nevertheless reliable under the totality of the circumstances?** *Neil v. Biggers* (1972) and *Manson v. Brathwaite* (1977) list the factors: the witness's **opportunity to view** the perpetrator at the time of the crime; the witness's **degree of attention**; the **accuracy of the prior description**; the witness's **level of certainty** at the confrontation; and the **time between** the crime and the confrontation. These factors are weighed against the corrupting effect of the suggestive procedure. Reliability, not deterrence of police, is the core concern; the identification is admitted if reliable.

If the procedure is not suggestive, the due process inquiry ends. And if suggestiveness was not arranged by the police at all, there is no due process screening at all: *Perry v. New Hampshire* (2012) held that the Due Process Clause does not require a preliminary judicial inquiry into the reliability of an eyewitness identification when the suggestive circumstances were not arranged by law enforcement. The jury evaluates such identifications with the help of cross-examination, jury instructions and expert testimony.

### Policy

The two rules have different purposes: *Wade* protects the fairness of the trial process by providing an advocate who can observe and later cross-examine; the due process test protects against unreliable evidence. Many states have adopted additional rules on lineup administration (blind administration, fillers, recorded confidence statements), and their courts may exclude more than the Constitution requires.

### Application

(1) Has the Sixth Amendment right attached, and was the defendant physically present at the confrontation? If so, counsel was needed; if not, do not apply Wade. (2) Regardless of counsel, was the police-arranged procedure unnecessarily suggestive? (3) If so, apply the Biggers factors. (4) Decide the in-court identification separately: is there an independent source?

@example
title: A one-on-one show-up
problem: Ten minutes after a convenience-store robbery, an officer finds Ben, who matches the clerk's description, two blocks away. The officer drives the clerk to Ben, who is standing in handcuffs next to a patrol car. The clerk says, "That's him." The clerk had seen the robber's face under bright lights for about two minutes and had given a detailed and accurate description. Ben has not been charged. Is the identification admissible?
step: Sixth Amendment: no charge has been filed, so no adversary proceedings have begun (Kirby); Wade does not apply.
step: Due process, step one: a one-man show-up with a handcuffed suspect is suggestive. A quick show-up close in time to the crime can be justified, but suggestiveness exists.
step: Step two, reliability (Biggers): good opportunity to view, close attention, an accurate description, immediate certainty and only minutes between the crime and the confrontation.
step: Conclusion: the identification is reliable under the totality of the circumstances, so it is admissible; the jury will weigh it.

@mistakes
- Requiring counsel at every identification: Wade applies only to post-charge confrontations at which the defendant is present.
- Skipping the reliability step: a suggestive procedure does not automatically exclude the identification.
- Suppressing the in-court identification automatically after a Wade violation; it is admitted if the government proves an independent source by clear and convincing evidence.
- Applying a due process inquiry to a suggestive circumstance that the police did not arrange (Perry).
