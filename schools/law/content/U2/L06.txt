@article
Even a clear Fourth Amendment violation does not lead to suppression for every defendant or every piece of evidence. Two questions remain: **whose rights were violated**, and **how far the taint travels**.

### Standing: Fourth Amendment rights are personal

A defendant may seek suppression only if **his own** Fourth Amendment rights were violated (*Rakas v. Illinois*, 1978). Courts call this "standing," though the Court treats it as part of the substantive question: did the defendant have a **legitimate expectation of privacy in the place searched** (or a property interest in the thing seized or searched)? There is no "automatic standing" for a person charged with possessing the seized item (*United States v. Salvucci*, 1980), and a person's interest in the item seized does not by itself create standing in the place searched (*Rawlings v. Kentucky*, 1980).

Applications:

| Person | Result | Case |
|---|---|---|
| Passenger in a car, with no property or possessory interest in the car or the area searched | No standing to challenge the search of the car | *Rakas* |
| Overnight guest in a home | Has a legitimate expectation of privacy | *Minnesota v. Olson*, 1990 |
| Visitor in a home for a short commercial purpose (bagging drugs for a few hours) | No | *Minnesota v. Carter*, 1998 |
| Driver of a rental car who is not on the rental agreement but has the renter's permission | The absence from the agreement does not by itself defeat the expectation of privacy | *Byrd v. United States*, 2018 |
| Owner of the home or car searched | Yes | — |

A **passenger** in a car stopped by police is seized, so he may challenge the legality of the **stop** and seek the fruits of an unlawful stop (*Brendlin v. California*, 2007), even if he cannot challenge the later search of the car.

### The fruit of the poisonous tree

Evidence derived from an unlawful search, seizure or arrest is also suppressed unless the taint is cut (*Wong Sun v. United States*, 1963). The question is "whether, granting establishment of the primary illegality, the evidence ... has been come at by exploitation of that illegality or instead by means sufficiently distinguishable to be purged of the primary taint." Fruits include:

- **Physical evidence** found as a result of an illegal search.
- **Statements** obtained by exploiting an illegal arrest, even if the person received *Miranda* warnings; the warnings alone do not purge the taint (*Brown v. Illinois*, 1975; *Dunaway v. New York*, 1979). *Wong Sun* itself admitted a statement the defendant made days later, after release on his own recognizance, as attenuated.
- **Observations** made after an illegal entry.

### Limits on the fruit doctrine

- **Independent source** (*Murray v. United States*, 1988; *Segura v. United States*, 1984): evidence later obtained from a genuinely independent lawful source is admissible. If officers illegally enter, then obtain a warrant on information that did not come from the entry and that the magistrate's decision did not depend on, the evidence seized under the warrant is admissible.
- **Inevitable discovery** (*Nix v. Williams*, 1984): admissible if the government proves by a preponderance that lawful means would inevitably have found it.
- **Attenuation** (*Brown* factors: time, intervening circumstances, purpose and flagrancy).
- **Live-witness testimony** is less readily suppressed than physical evidence, because a witness's free will is an intervening factor (*United States v. Ceccolini*, 1978).
- **Identity of the defendant:** an illegal arrest does not bar prosecution or the in-court identification of the defendant (*United States v. Crews*, 1980).
- **Payton violations:** if police have probable cause but arrest a suspect in his home without a warrant, a statement made later at the station after being warned is admissible, because the station custody is lawful (*New York v. Harris*, 1990). Statements made in the home are suppressed.
- **Miranda:** the fruit doctrine applies differently to Miranda violations (Lesson 8), because physical fruits of an unwarned but voluntary statement are admissible (*United States v. Patane*, 2004).

### Policy and application

The fruit doctrine prevents the police from profiting from a violation, but attenuation, independent source and inevitable discovery balance that goal against the cost of suppression. Standing keeps the remedy for the person whose rights were actually invaded.

For an analysis of an arrest, work in order: (1) Was the arrest or search unlawful? (2) Whose rights were violated: which defendant may complain? (3) List each item of evidence and ask whether it is a direct or derivative product. (4) For each, test independent source, inevitable discovery and attenuation. (5) State the result item by item.

@example
title: A statement after an unlawful arrest
problem: Officers arrest Ana on the street without probable cause. Within an hour, at the station, they give her Miranda warnings. She waives her rights and confesses. No other event occurs in between. Is the confession admissible?
step: Violation: an arrest without probable cause violates the Fourth Amendment.
step: Fruit: the confession came from custody that began with the illegal arrest, so it is derivative evidence.
step: Attenuation (Brown): temporal proximity is short, no intervening circumstance exists (the Miranda warnings alone are not enough), and the arrest was an investigatory seizure without probable cause.
step: Conclusion: the confession is suppressed as the fruit of the illegal arrest; the Fifth Amendment is satisfied, but the Fourth Amendment taint remains.

@mistakes
- Thinking that Miranda warnings automatically cure the taint of an illegal arrest; they do not.
- Allowing any defendant to challenge any illegal search; a defendant may challenge only a search that violated his own expectation of privacy or property rights.
- Mixing independent source with inevitable discovery: the first requires an actual, untainted path; the second asks whether the evidence would have been found anyway.
- Suppressing the defendant's identity or body because of an illegal arrest.

@practice self
prompt: Officers arrest Ben on the street with no probable cause and search him incident to the arrest, finding heroin. At the station they give him Miranda warnings. An hour later he confesses and names his supplier, Cal. The police find Cal and he agrees to testify for the state about the sales. Ben and Cal are charged together. Decide what evidence is suppressed as to Ben, and whether Cal can challenge the arrest. Explain.
solution: The arrest was without probable cause and violated Ben's Fourth Amendment rights. The heroin is the direct product of the unlawful arrest and is suppressed as to Ben. The confession came an hour after the arrest, the only intervening event was the Miranda warnings, and the arrest was an investigatory seizure without probable cause. Under Brown v. Illinois it is not attenuated and is suppressed. Cal's testimony is a live-witness product of the confession; under Ceccolini, the witness's free will is a strong intervening factor and the testimony is likely admissible against Ben. Cal cannot challenge the arrest of Ben because Fourth Amendment rights are personal (Rakas), and he had no expectation of privacy in Ben's person.
hint: Take each item in turn: the heroin, the confession, Cal's testimony.
hint: Ask whose rights were violated before asking what is suppressed.
rubric: Suppresses the heroin and the confession and explains the Brown factors.
rubric: Treats Cal's testimony as a live-witness fruit under Ceccolini and concludes it is likely admissible.
rubric: States that Cal lacks standing to challenge Ben's arrest.

Using information from a separate informant, who had told them about the goods weeks before the entry, they obtain a warrant that does not mention what they saw in the entry. They would have sought the warrant regardless, and the magistrate's decision was not influenced by the entry. They seize the goods. Gil moves to suppress. How should the court rule?
choice: Grant, because the goods were first seen during an unlawful entry.
choice: Grant, because the police must prove that the goods would inevitably have been found.
choice*: Deny, because the warrant was based on an independent source that was untainted by the illegal entry.
choice: Deny, because police may always seize goods that they have seen once.
hint: Compare the actual path to the goods.
hint: Murray v. United States addressed the second look through a warrant.

@quiz mc
prompt: Officers stop a car without reasonable suspicion. The driver, Hal, owns the car; Ira is a passenger. During the stop the officers find a handgun under Hal's seat and arrest both. Ira, who was seized in the stop, moves to suppress the gun and argues that the stop was unlawful. What is the best statement of Ira's position?
choice: Ira may not challenge the stop, because only the car's owner may challenge a stop.
choice: Ira may challenge the stop and the gun is automatically suppressed as to him.
choice*: Ira may challenge the lawfulness of the stop, because a passenger is seized too, but he must show that the gun is the fruit of the unlawful stop, and attenuation may still apply.
choice: Ira may not challenge the stop, because Fourth Amendment standing requires an ownership interest in the car.
hint: Recall Brendlin v. California.
hint: Distinguish the seizure of the passenger from the search of the car.
solution: Under Brendlin, a passenger is seized by a traffic stop and may challenge its legality, and the fruits of the stop may be suppressed as to him subject to the usual limits such as attenuation. Ownership is not required to challenge a seizure of one's own person.
