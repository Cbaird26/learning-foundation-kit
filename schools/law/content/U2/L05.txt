@article
The Fourth Amendment does not say what happens when police violate it. The **exclusionary rule** is a judge-made remedy: evidence obtained by violating the Amendment is generally inadmissible in the prosecution's case-in-chief. *Weeks v. United States* (1914) applied it in federal court, and *Mapp v. Ohio* (1961) applied it to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment.

### Rationale and limits

The modern Court justifies the rule as a **deterrent** to police misconduct, not as a personal right of the defendant or a way to repair the injury. Exclusion is a "last resort": it applies only when its deterrence benefits outweigh its heavy social costs of letting the guilty go free. That reasoning produced these limits:

- **Proceedings other than the criminal trial.** The rule does not apply in grand jury proceedings (*United States v. Calandra*, 1974), federal civil deportation proceedings (*INS v. Lopez-Mendoza*, 1984) or parole revocation hearings (*Pennsylvania Board of Probation and Parole v. Scott*, 1998).
- **Impeachment.** Illegally obtained evidence may be used to impeach the defendant's own testimony (*Walder v. United States*, 1954; *Harris v. New York*, 1971, for a statement taken in violation of *Miranda*), but not to impeach other defense witnesses (*James v. Illinois*, 1990).
- **Knock-and-announce violations** do not require exclusion (*Hudson v. Michigan*, 2006).

### The good-faith exception

Evidence is not excluded when officers act in **objectively reasonable reliance** on a warrant later held invalid (*United States v. Leon*, 1984). Because the magistrate, not the officer, made the error, exclusion would not deter the police. Good faith **does not apply** if:

1. the affiant misled the magistrate with information he knew was false or recklessly disregarded (*Franks*);
2. the magistrate wholly abandoned the judicial role;
3. the affidavit was so lacking in probable cause ("bare bones") that belief in its existence was unreasonable; or
4. the warrant was so facially deficient (for example, failing to describe the place or things) that officers could not reasonably presume it valid.

The Court extended the same reasoning to reliance on a statute later held unconstitutional (*Illinois v. Krull*, 1987), on court records that were erroneous because of a court employee's error (*Arizona v. Evans*, 1995), and on **binding appellate precedent** later overruled (*Davis v. United States*, 2011). In *Herring v. United States* (2009), the Court declined to suppress evidence found after an arrest based on a database error caused by isolated police negligence: exclusion requires conduct that is deliberate, reckless or grossly negligent, or recurring or systemic negligence.

### Three ways the taint is cut

These doctrines (developed further in Lesson 6) show that evidence tied to a violation may still come in:

- **Independent source.** Evidence is admissible if obtained through a genuinely independent lawful source, such as a later warrant that was not prompted by what the illegal entry revealed (*Murray v. United States*, 1988; *Segura v. United States*, 1984).
- **Inevitable discovery.** Evidence is admissible if the government proves, by a preponderance, that it would inevitably have been found by lawful means (*Nix v. Williams*, 1984).
- **Attenuation.** The causal chain from the violation may be so weakened that the taint dissipates. *Brown v. Illinois* (1975) lists the factors: **temporal proximity** between the violation and the evidence, **intervening circumstances**, and the **purpose and flagrancy** of the misconduct. In *Utah v. Strieff* (2016), an officer's unlawful stop was followed by discovery of a valid, pre-existing arrest warrant. The Court held that the warrant was an intervening circumstance, and since the stop was at most negligent, the drugs found in the search incident to the warrant arrest were admissible.

### Policy and application

The deterrence rationale explains why the exclusion rules track police culpability: good-faith reliance on someone else's error is not deterred; a deliberate violation is. A defendant also faces a **standing** requirement (Lesson 6).

For an exam, work in this order: (1) Was there a Fourth Amendment violation? (2) Does the defendant have standing? (3) Is the evidence the direct or derivative product of the violation? (4) Does a limit apply: a proceeding outside the trial, good faith, independent source, inevitable discovery, attenuation, or impeachment? (5) Conclude on admissibility in the case-in-chief and for impeachment separately.

@example
title: Reliance on binding precedent
problem: At the time of Eli's arrest, binding circuit precedent allowed officers to search the passenger compartment of a car incident to the arrest of a recent occupant even if the occupant was handcuffed far away. Officers follow that precedent and find a gun. After the search, the Supreme Court decides Arizona v. Gant, which prohibits such searches. Is the gun excluded?
step: Violation: under Gant, which applies to cases pending on direct review, the search was unconstitutional.
step: Remedy: exclusion is a last resort, available only when it would deter police misconduct.
step: Davis v. United States: officers who conduct a search in objectively reasonable reliance on binding appellate precedent are not culpable, so excluding the evidence would deter nothing.
step: Conclusion: the gun is admissible, even though the search violated the Fourth Amendment as later interpreted.

@mistakes
- Treating the exclusionary rule as a constitutional right of the defendant, rather than a deterrent remedy.
- Applying good faith when the affidavit is bare bones or the officer lied or acted recklessly in preparing it.
- Confusing independent source (a different actual lawful path) with inevitable discovery (a hypothetical lawful path that would have led to the evidence).
- Forgetting that excluded evidence can still be used for impeachment of the defendant's own testimony.
