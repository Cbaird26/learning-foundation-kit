@article
The Sixth Amendment guarantees an accused the "Assistance of Counsel for his defence." Three questions arise: **when** counsel is provided, **whether** the counsel provided was constitutionally adequate, and **whether** the defendant may proceed without counsel.

### The right to appointed counsel

- *Powell v. Alabama* (1932) required counsel in a capital case; *Gideon v. Wainwright* (1963) held that states must provide counsel to indigent defendants charged with **felonies**, overruling *Betts v. Brady*.
- *Argersinger v. Hamlin* (1972) extended the right to any case in which **actual imprisonment** is imposed, and *Scott v. Illinois* (1979) held that counsel is not required if the sentence is only a fine. *Alabama v. Shelton* (2002) held that a **suspended** jail sentence that may be imposed on probation violation requires counsel.
- The right applies at all **critical stages** after attachment: arraignment, preliminary hearing, plea negotiations, sentencing and the like.
- On appeal, an indigent has a right to counsel on the **first appeal as of right** (*Douglas v. California*, 1963) but not for discretionary review (*Ross v. Moffitt*, 1974).
- **Counsel of choice:** a defendant who can pay has the right to choose his lawyer; wrongful denial is structural error requiring no showing of prejudice (*United States v. Gonzalez-Lopez*, 2006). A court may refuse a conflicted lawyer even if the defendant waives the conflict (*Wheat v. United States*, 1988).

### Self-representation

A defendant has a Sixth Amendment right to represent himself if he **knowingly and intelligently** waives counsel (*Faretta v. California*, 1975). A court may require counsel for a defendant who is competent to stand trial but suffers from severe mental illness that prevents him from conducting trial proceedings (*Indiana v. Edwards*, 2008). A lawyer may not concede the client's guilt over the client's express objection; the decision whether to maintain innocence belongs to the client (*McCoy v. Louisiana*, 2018).

### Effective assistance: Strickland

The right to counsel is the right to **effective** assistance. *Strickland v. Washington* (1984) set a two-part test, and the defendant must prove both:

1. **Deficient performance.** Counsel's representation fell below an **objective standard of reasonableness** under prevailing professional norms. Judicial scrutiny is highly deferential, with a **strong presumption** that counsel's conduct falls within the range of reasonable professional assistance; courts avoid hindsight. **Strategic choices** made after thorough investigation are virtually unchallengeable, and choices made after less than complete investigation are reasonable to the extent that reasonable judgment supports the limits on investigation (*Wiggins v. Smith*, 2003, failure to investigate mitigating evidence was deficient).
2. **Prejudice.** A **reasonable probability** that, but for counsel's errors, the result of the proceeding would have been different. A reasonable probability is a probability sufficient to undermine confidence in the outcome; it is **less** than a preponderance. The defendant need not show that counsel's errors more likely than not changed the outcome.

On federal habeas review of a state conviction, the standard is applied through the deference required by the federal statute (28 U.S.C. § 2254(d)), so the review is "doubly deferential" (*Harrington v. Richter*, 2011).

### Plea bargaining and appeals

Strickland applies to guilty pleas (*Hill v. Lockhart*, 1985). For prejudice, the defendant must show a reasonable probability that, but for counsel's errors, he would not have pleaded guilty and would have insisted on going to trial (*Lee v. United States*, 2017, applied this even where the chance of acquittal at trial was slim, because deportation was the decisive concern). Counsel must advise a noncitizen client of clear deportation consequences of a plea (*Padilla v. Kentucky*, 2010). Counsel must communicate formal plea offers and can be ineffective in advising on rejected offers (*Missouri v. Frye*, 2012; *Lafler v. Cooper*, 2012). When counsel fails to file a requested appeal, prejudice is presumed (*Roe v. Flores-Ortega*, 2000; *Garza v. Idaho*, 2019, even when the defendant waived appeal in the plea agreement).

### Presumed prejudice and conflicts

Prejudice is presumed when (1) counsel is entirely absent at a critical stage, (2) counsel entirely fails to subject the prosecution's case to meaningful adversarial testing, or (3) circumstances make it unlikely that any lawyer could provide effective assistance (*United States v. Cronic*, 1984). For **conflicts of interest** that were not objected to at trial, the defendant must show an **actual conflict** that adversely affected counsel's performance, with no separate showing of prejudice (*Cuyler v. Sullivan*, 1980). Where the court compelled joint representation over a timely objection, reversal is automatic (*Holloway v. Arkansas*, 1978).

### Application

When analyzing a Strickland claim, first isolate each error. For each, ask whether it was a reasonable strategic choice, an investigation failure or an oversight. Then ask whether, but for that error, there was a reasonable probability of a different result. Consider the errors cumulatively for prejudice. Because both prongs are required, a court may begin with prejudice if that is easier.

@example
title: The alibi witnesses nobody called
problem: Ana is convicted of robbery. Her lawyer never contacted two people who would have testified that she was at work at the time. He said he had no time to interview them. Ana files for relief, claiming ineffective assistance. The state's case rested on one eyewitness who saw the robber for a few seconds. Does she satisfy Strickland?
step: Deficient performance: the failure to investigate was not a strategic choice made after investigation; it was an omission. A reasonable lawyer in a case that turns on identity would interview alibi witnesses.
step: Prejudice: the evidence consisted of a brief identification, and two alibi witnesses would have given the jury a reason to doubt it. This probably creates a reasonable probability of a different result, enough to undermine confidence.
step: Standard: Ana does not need to prove an acquittal was more likely than not, only a reasonable probability.
step: Conclusion: she likely satisfies both prongs and is entitled to relief.

@mistakes
- Requiring the defendant to prove that she would have been acquitted: Strickland needs only a reasonable probability of a different outcome.
- Second-guessing strategy with hindsight; informed tactical choices are presumed reasonable.
- Forgetting the prejudice prong, or applying it to conflicts or absence of counsel, where prejudice is presumed or measured differently.
- Requiring counsel for every misdemeanor; the right depends on actual imprisonment, including a suspended sentence.

@practice self
prompt: Dee is charged with arson. Her lawyer (1) did not request a fire-science expert, although the state's case rested on a disputed theory that the fire was deliberately set; (2) did not move to exclude a confession even though Dee was never given Miranda warnings during custodial interrogation; and (3) told Dee that she had no chance at trial and failed to relay a plea offer of 2 years, after which she was convicted at trial and sentenced to 10 years. Apply Strickland to each error.
solution: Error 1 (no expert): in a case that depends on the contested science of arson, a reasonable lawyer would consult an expert; failure to investigate without strategic reason is likely deficient (Wiggins). Prejudice depends on whether a competent expert would likely have undermined the arson theory; if so, there is a reasonable probability of a different outcome. Error 2 (no motion to suppress): failing to file a meritorious suppression motion can be deficient if the motion would have succeeded and no strategic reason exists; unwarned custodial statements are inadmissible in the case-in-chief, so prejudice depends on how important the confession was and whether other evidence would have convicted her. Error 3 (plea offer): counsel must communicate formal offers (Frye). Deficient. Prejudice requires a reasonable probability that Dee would have accepted the offer, that the prosecution would not have withdrawn it, and that the court would have accepted it, and that the resulting sentence would have been less severe (Frye; Lafler). Here the 2-year offer versus a 10-year sentence supports prejudice. Overall, the strongest claim is the unrelayed plea offer.
hint: Isolate each error, then apply deficiency and prejudice separately.
hint: For the plea offer, recall what Frye requires for prejudice.

rubric: Treats investigation, suppression and plea communication with the correct authority (Wiggins, Frye or Lafler).
