@article
Last lesson's exceptions depend on the circumstances. These depend on **categories of place or event**: the car, the arrest, and the brief street stop.

### The automobile exception

If officers have **probable cause** to believe that a vehicle contains contraband or evidence, they may search it without a warrant (*Carroll v. United States*, 1925). The rationales are the vehicle's mobility and the reduced expectation of privacy in a car. The scope of the search is defined by what the probable cause covers: every part of the vehicle and every container in it that could hold the object sought (*United States v. Ross*, 1982; *California v. Acevedo*, 1991, closed containers). Police may search a passenger's belongings that could hold the object (*Wyoming v. Houghton*, 1999). The exception covers motor homes in use on the road (*California v. Carney*, 1985). It does **not** authorize officers to enter the curtilage of a home to search a car parked there (*Collins v. Virginia*, 2018).

### Inventory searches

When police lawfully impound a vehicle, they may inventory its contents without probable cause or a warrant if they follow **standardized criteria** and the purpose is administrative: protecting property, guarding against claims, and protecting officers (*South Dakota v. Opperman*, 1976; *Colorado v. Bertine*, 1987). Unfettered discretion to look for evidence defeats the exception (*Florida v. Wells*, 1990).

### Searches incident to arrest

A **lawful custodial arrest** allows a search of the person and of the area within the arrestee's immediate control (*Chimel v. California*, 1969). The search of the person is automatic and needs no additional justification (*United States v. Robinson*, 1973). Several limits apply:

- **Vehicles:** after the arrest of a recent occupant, police may search the passenger compartment only if (1) the arrestee is unsecured and within reaching distance of the compartment, or (2) it is reasonable to believe that evidence of the **offense of arrest** is in the vehicle (*Arizona v. Gant*, 2009). The trunk is not included.
- **Cell phones:** the data on a phone may not be searched incident to arrest; police generally need a warrant (*Riley v. California*, 2014). The phone itself may be inspected for physical threats.
- **In the home:** a **protective sweep** is allowed, incident to an in-home arrest, of spaces adjoining the place of arrest from which an attack could come, without suspicion; a broader sweep requires reasonable suspicion that a dangerous person is present (*Maryland v. Buie*, 1990).
- **Breath and blood tests** for drunk-driving arrests are discussed in Lesson 3.

### Stop and frisk

*Terry v. Ohio* (1968) created two separate lower-level intrusions:

1. A **stop** (investigative detention) needs **reasonable suspicion**, specific and articulable facts that criminal activity is afoot. It must be brief and last no longer than needed to carry out its purpose. A detention that becomes too long or is moved to the station may become an arrest requiring probable cause (*Dunaway v. New York*, 1979).
2. A **frisk** (a pat-down of outer clothing) needs a **separate** reasonable suspicion that the person is **armed and dangerous**. Its only purpose is to find weapons.

**Traffic stops.** A traffic violation, or reasonable suspicion of one, justifies a stop; the officer's subjective motive is irrelevant (*Whren v. United States*, 1996). A reasonable mistake of law can support reasonable suspicion (*Heien v. North Carolina*, 2014). Police may order the driver and passengers out of the car (*Pennsylvania v. Mimms*, 1977; *Maryland v. Wilson*, 1997) and may frisk a passenger they reasonably suspect is armed (*Arizona v. Johnson*, 2009). With reasonable suspicion that an occupant is dangerous, they may search the passenger compartment for weapons (*Michigan v. Long*, 1983). A stop may not be prolonged beyond the time reasonably needed to handle the traffic matter in order to conduct a dog sniff, absent independent reasonable suspicion (*Rodriguez v. United States*, 2015); the sniff itself is not a search (*Illinois v. Caballes*, 2005).

Other points: flight in a high-crime area can contribute to reasonable suspicion (*Illinois v. Wardlow*, 2000); an anonymous 911 call reporting dangerous driving can supply reasonable suspicion if it carries indications of reliability (*Navarette v. California*, 2014); and checkpoints for sobriety are allowed (*Michigan Dept. of State Police v. Sitz*, 1990), while those whose primary purpose is general crime control are not (*Indianapolis v. Edmond*, 2000).

### Force in a seizure

Excessive-force claims arise under the Fourth Amendment's reasonableness requirement (*Graham v. Connor*, 1989). In *Barnes v. Felix* (2025), the Court rejected the "moment-of-threat" rule, which looked only at the instant before force was used. Courts consider the **totality of the circumstances**, including events leading up to the moment of the shooting, though the question remains whether the force was objectively reasonable.

### Application

For any vehicle problem, test each exception in turn: stop, automobile, search incident to arrest, inventory and consent. Each has different conditions, and a search can fail one and satisfy another.

@example
title: A traffic stop that becomes a search
problem: An officer stops Ana for speeding and smells marijuana from her car. Ana is arrested for driving with a suspended license, handcuffed and placed in the patrol car. The officer searches the passenger compartment and opens a backpack on the back seat, finding cocaine. Is the search justified?
step: Search incident to arrest (Gant): Ana is secured and not within reach; the offense of arrest (a suspended license) does not give reason to believe evidence is in the car. This exception fails.
step: Automobile exception: the odor of marijuana can supply probable cause that the car contains contraband.
step: Scope: probable cause that contraband is in the car allows a search of every part and container that could hold it, including a backpack (Ross; Acevedo).
step: Conclusion: the search is valid under the automobile exception, even though it would not be valid as a search incident to arrest. (Some states, by law, have limited odor-based searches where marijuana is legal; check local law.)

@mistakes
- Using Gant to justify a search of the trunk, or of the passenger compartment when the arrestee is secured and the offense of arrest has no evidence to find.
- Frisking whenever there is a stop: a frisk needs a separate reasonable suspicion that the person is armed and dangerous.
- Treating pretext as a defense: under Whren, the officer's motive is irrelevant if there is probable cause (or reasonable suspicion) of a violation.
- Applying the automobile exception without probable cause: mobility is the rationale, but probable cause is still required.

hint: Take each justification in turn and list its requirements.
hint: Ask which facts satisfy or fail each requirement.
solution: (a) Search incident to arrest: under Gant, the passenger compartment may be searched only if the arrestee is unsecured and within reach, or if it is reasonable to believe evidence of the offense of arrest is in the car. Ana is handcuffed in the patrol car and a suspended-license arrest has no evidence to find in the car, so this exception fails.
