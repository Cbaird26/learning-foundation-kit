@article
The Fifth Amendment says that no person shall "be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb." The clause applies to the states (*Benton v. Maryland*, 1969). It gives three protections: against a **second prosecution after acquittal**, against a **second prosecution after conviction**, and against **multiple punishments for the same offense**. Every problem asks three questions: did jeopardy attach, is it the same offense, and did the first proceeding end in a way that bars the second?

### When jeopardy attaches

In a jury trial, jeopardy attaches when the **jury is empaneled and sworn** (*Crist v. Bretz*, 1978). In a bench trial, it attaches when the first witness is sworn. After a guilty plea, it attaches when the court accepts the plea. Before attachment, the prosecution may dismiss and refile.

### The same offense

- ***Blockburger* test.** Two statutory offenses are different if each requires proof of an element the other does not (*Blockburger v. United States*, 1932). If so, successive prosecutions are allowed, even for the same act. If one offense is a **lesser included offense** of the other (all of its elements are contained in the greater), they are the same offense, and conviction of one bars prosecution for the other (*Brown v. Ohio*, 1977). *United States v. Dixon* (1993) overruled *Grady v. Corbin*, which had used a same-conduct test.
- **Conspiracy and the completed offense** are generally separate offenses under *Blockburger*, because conspiracy requires an agreement and the completed crime requires an act.
- **Multiple punishments in a single trial** are allowed if the legislature clearly intended cumulative punishments, even for offenses that fail the *Blockburger* test (*Missouri v. Hunter*, 1983).
- **Dual sovereignty.** Different sovereigns may each prosecute for the same conduct, because each is enforcing its own law: a state and the federal government (*Gamble v. United States*, 2019, reaffirming the doctrine), or two states. A city and its state are the same sovereign (*Waller v. Florida*, 1970), and Puerto Rico and the federal government are the same sovereign (*Puerto Rico v. Sanchez Valle*, 2016). Many states provide statutory protection against successive prosecutions after a federal prosecution.

### How the first proceeding ended

- **Acquittal.** An acquittal is final and bars retrial even if erroneous, and the government may not appeal it (*Evans v. Michigan*, 2013, an acquittal on an incorrect legal ground; *United States v. Martin Linen Supply*, 1977, a judge-directed acquittal).
- **Conviction reversed on appeal.** If the defendant successfully appeals a conviction for **trial error** (for example, improper admission of evidence), retrial is allowed (*United States v. Ball*, 1896). But reversal because the evidence was **legally insufficient** to support the conviction bars retrial (*Burks v. United States*, 1978). Reversal because the verdict was against the weight of the evidence does not (*Tibbs v. Florida*, 1982). If a jury convicts on a lesser offense, silently refusing to convict on the greater, that is an implied acquittal of the greater offense, which bars retrial on it (*Green v. United States*, 1957).
- **Mistrial.** If the **defendant** asks for or consents to a mistrial, retrial is allowed unless the prosecution **intentionally goaded** the defendant into requesting it (*Oregon v. Kennedy*, 1982). If the **court** declares a mistrial over the defendant's objection, retrial is allowed only if there was **manifest necessity**, as with a genuinely deadlocked jury (*United States v. Perez*, 1824). A hung jury does not bar retrial (*Richardson v. United States*, 1984).
- **Dismissal for reasons unrelated to guilt** (such as a defense-requested dismissal on a technical ground) generally does not bar retrial (*United States v. Scott*, 1978).

### Collateral estoppel

Double jeopardy incorporates issue preclusion: when an issue of ultimate fact has been determined by a valid acquittal, it cannot be relitigated in a later prosecution of the same defendant (*Ashe v. Swenson*, 1970). The acquittal must have necessarily decided the issue (for example, identity as the perpetrator), and the later trial must require proof of it. Because an acquittal shows only reasonable doubt, it does not bar the government from offering evidence of the acquitted conduct, for a permissible purpose and under a lower standard of proof, at a later trial for a different offense (*Dowling v. United States*, 1990, evidence of an earlier acquitted robbery).

### Civil and criminal penalties

The clause bars only multiple **criminal** punishments. A civil sanction is punishment only if there is clear proof that it is so punitive in purpose or effect as to negate the legislature's civil intent (*Hudson v. United States*, 1997).

### Policy and application

The clause prevents the state from using its resources to wear down an individual through repeated trials and protects the finality of acquittals. Work in order: (1) Did jeopardy attach in the first proceeding? (2) Is the second charge the same offense under Blockburger, or does Ashe apply? (3) Is the second prosecution by the same sovereign? (4) How did the first proceeding end?

@example
title: Theft after joyriding
problem: State law defines "unauthorized use of a vehicle" as taking a vehicle without the owner's consent. It defines "auto theft" as taking a vehicle without the owner's consent with intent to deprive the owner permanently. Ana pleads guilty to unauthorized use of a car. The state then charges her with auto theft for taking the same car. Is the second prosecution barred?
step: Attachment: Ana was convicted after her plea was accepted, so jeopardy attached.
step: Blockburger: unauthorized use requires only taking without consent. Auto theft requires that and intent to deprive permanently. Every element of unauthorized use is contained in auto theft.
step: Unauthorized use is a lesser included offense of auto theft, so they are the same offense (Brown v. Ohio).
step: Same sovereign: the same state. Conclusion: the second prosecution is barred.

@mistakes
- Using a same-conduct test: Blockburger compares statutory elements, and Dixon overruled Grady.
- Applying double jeopardy before jeopardy has attached (for example, a dismissal before the jury is sworn).
- Forgetting dual sovereignty: a federal prosecution after a state prosecution is generally allowed, and vice versa.
- Barring retrial after any reversal on appeal: only reversal for insufficiency of evidence does.
