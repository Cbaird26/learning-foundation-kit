@article
The stock market crash of 1929 and the Great Depression that followed led to unemployment and bank failures on a vast scale. Franklin Roosevelt took office in 1933 and the **New Deal** programmes that followed raised a constitutional question: how far could Congress regulate the national economy?

### The Court resists

Early New Deal laws met resistance. In *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States* (1935), a unanimous Court struck down the National Industrial Recovery Act, holding that it unconstitutionally delegated legislative power to the President without adequate standards (the **nondelegation doctrine**) and exceeded the commerce power as applied to a local poultry business. In *United States v. Butler* (1936), the Court struck down the Agricultural Adjustment Act's processing tax as beyond Congress's power.

### The turning point

In 1937, President Roosevelt proposed adding up to six justices to the Supreme Court (a bench of up to fifteen); the plan failed in Congress. But the Court's direction changed. In *West Coast Hotel Co. v. Parrish* (1937), the Court upheld a state minimum-wage law and overruled *Adkins v. Children's Hospital* (1923), moving away from *Lochner*-style review of economic regulation (Week 10). In *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.* (1937), it upheld the National Labor Relations Act under the Commerce Clause, and in 1937 it also upheld the Social Security Act. In *Wickard v. Filburn* (1942), the Court held that Congress may regulate even home-grown wheat not sold in commerce, because the aggregate effect of many such activities could substantially affect interstate commerce. From 1937 on, courts reviewed ordinary economic regulation deferentially (rational-basis review).

### The administrative state

Congress delegated broad tasks to **administrative agencies**: some within executive departments, others "independent" commissions such as the Federal Trade Commission (1914), the Securities and Exchange Commission (1934) and the National Labor Relations Board (1935). Agencies combine rulemaking, enforcement and adjudication. The **Administrative Procedure Act** (1946) set the basic rules: how agencies make rules, conduct hearings and how courts review them.

### Judicial control: three recent changes

For decades, *Chevron U.S.A. v. NRDC* (1984) told courts to defer to reasonable agency interpretations of ambiguous statutes. In ***Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo*** (2024), the Court overruled *Chevron*: courts must exercise independent judgment about the best reading of a statute, though they may give weight to an agency's view under *Skidmore* respect; earlier holdings based on *Chevron* remain in force. In ***SEC v. Jarkesy*** (2024), the Court held that the Seventh Amendment requires a jury when the SEC seeks civil penalties for securities fraud. In *Corner Post v. Board of Governors* (2024), it held that an APA claim accrues when the plaintiff is injured.

The President's power to remove agency heads has also changed. *Humphrey's Executor v. United States* (1935) had upheld for-cause removal limits for FTC commissioners. *Seila Law* (2020) and *Collins v. Yellen* (2021) had already struck down such limits on single-director agencies. In ***Trump v. Slaughter*** (June 2026, 6 to 3), the Court overruled *Humphrey's Executor*: FTC commissioners are removable at will. The Court left open questions about the Federal Reserve, and in *Trump v. Cook* (2026) it refused to let the removal of a Federal Reserve governor take effect while that case continues.

On delegation, the Court in *FCC v. Consumers' Research* (2025) rejected a nondelegation challenge to the Universal Service Fund. And in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* (February 2026, 6 to 3), the Court held that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorise tariffs; a plurality also applied the **major questions doctrine** (*West Virginia v. EPA*, 2022).

title: Following the commerce power across the 1930s and 1940s
problem: Explain how the Court's treatment of congressional economic regulation changed from *Schechter* (1935) to *Wickard* (1942).
step: In *Schechter*, the Court struck the NIRA on nondelegation and commerce grounds, treating a local business as outside national power.
step: In 1937, *West Coast Hotel* and *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin* moved away from close review of economic regulation and upheld broad commerce power.
step: In *Wickard*, the aggregate-effects reasoning let Congress regulate even activity not itself sold in commerce.
step: Conclude: after 1937 the Court deferred to economic legislation, which supported the growth of the administrative state.

@mistakes
- Saying the Court-packing plan was enacted. It failed in Congress.
- Treating *Chevron* as still governing statutory interpretation. *Loper Bright* (2024) overruled it.
- Treating *Humphrey's Executor* as current law. *Trump v. Slaughter* (2026) overruled it.
- Assuming agencies are not subject to judicial review. The APA provides for it.
