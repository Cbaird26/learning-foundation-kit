@article
Every legal system answers the same practical question: when two people quarrel, who decides, by what rules, and with what proof? This course follows one answer, the **common law** of England and the United States, from the Middle Ages to the present. To see what was distinctive about it, you first need to see the traditions around it.

### Rome: written rules and legal science

Roman law began with custom and with public demands for certainty. According to Roman tradition, a body of rules called the **Twelve Tables** was set down around 450 BCE and displayed publicly, so that the rules no longer belonged only to a priestly or aristocratic few. Over the following centuries Roman magistrates, and then jurists, developed a sophisticated body of private law: property, contract, obligations, family and inheritance. Roman writers distinguished the **ius civile** (the law of Roman citizens) from the **ius gentium** (rules thought common to all peoples).

In the 6th century CE the Eastern Roman emperor Justinian ordered the whole inheritance gathered. The result, compiled between 529 and 534 CE, is the **Corpus Juris Civilis**: the **Code** (imperial enactments), the **Digest** or Pandects (extracts from the jurists), the **Institutes** (a teaching textbook), and later the **Novels** (new laws). Justinian's work was a deliberate act of **codification**: rules collected, ordered and issued with the ruler's authority.

### The revival of Roman law on the continent

After the Western empire collapsed, Roman law survived only in fragments. Around the end of the 11th century, scholars at Bologna began to study the Digest intensively, writing explanatory notes called glosses. From this scholarship, together with the church's law, grew a shared learned law of continental Europe, often called the **ius commune**. Its descendants are the **civil-law** systems of countries such as France and Germany, where the central source of law is a comprehensive code and judges apply it.

### Canon law

The Catholic Church ran its own courts and its own body of **canon law**, covering marriage, wills, clergy and church property. Around 1140 the scholar Gratian produced a systematic collection of church law, the Decretum. Canon law was learned law, written in Latin, and it influenced procedure and the law of marriage and wills in England too, even though English royal law kept its own course.

### Germanic custom in early England

Early English law looked very different. It was **customary**: rules lived in the memory of the community and were declared in assemblies such as the **hundred** and **shire** courts. From about 600 CE, kings issued written codes (often called dooms) that recorded customs and set payments. Instead of imprisonment, law relied heavily on **compensation**. The central figure is the **wergild**, a sum fixed by the victim's rank, paid to the victim's kin to end a feud.

Proof was not a weighing of evidence by judges. A defendant might clear himself by **compurgation**: swearing an oath together with a set number of **oath-helpers** who vouched for his oath. Where oaths failed, a party might face an **ordeal** (for example, carrying hot iron), understood as an appeal to God's judgment.

### Why this matters for the common law

England was conquered in 1066 (Week 2), and the Normans brought strong royal government but not a Roman code. The royal courts built their law case by case, out of local custom and royal writs. England therefore became the home of a judge-made tradition, while most of the continent became the home of the code. The contrast is a tendency, not an absolute: the common law borrowed from Roman and canon law at points, and modern civil-law systems rely on case reasoning too.

@mistakes
- Treating the Twelve Tables and Justinian's Corpus Juris Civilis as the same thing; they are roughly a thousand years apart.
- Assuming England adopted a Roman code. English royal law grew from custom, writs and decided cases.
- Calling the ordeal a trial by evidence. It was a proof by divine judgment, and compurgation was a proof by oath.
- Thinking "civil law" means non-criminal law. In this context it names the Roman-descended code tradition.
