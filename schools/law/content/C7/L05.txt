@article
**Magna Carta** (the "Great Charter") was sealed by King John in June 1215 at Runnymede, after a rebellion by powerful barons. John had raised heavy taxes to fund wars, had lost lands in France, and had used royal justice in arbitrary and profitable ways. The barons forced him to accept a written list of promises. It was a feudal bargain, not a modern constitution, and most of its clauses dealt with specific grievances about feudal dues, courts and the crown's officers.

### What happened to it

The charter did not settle the crisis. Within weeks John obtained a papal declaration annulling it, and civil war followed. After John died in 1216, the government of his young son Henry III reissued the charter in 1216 and again in 1217, and in 1225 it was reissued in a shorter form that became the standard text. In 1297 Edward I confirmed it and it was entered on the statute roll. Because it had been reissued so often, Magna Carta became a familiar point of reference, and monarchs were often asked to confirm it.

### Two famous clauses

Later editors numbered the clauses. In the common modern English translation, **clause 39** says that no free man is to be seized or imprisoned, or stripped of his rights or possessions, "except by the lawful judgment of his equals or by the law of the land." **Clause 40** says: "To no one will we sell, to no one deny or delay right or justice." (In the 1225 reissue, these were combined into one chapter, number 29.)

Read the words carefully. They limit the king: punishment or seizure must follow a lawful process, not the king's will. They promise that justice will not be sold or delayed. In 1215 "free men" were a limited group, so the promise did not reach everyone. And "judgment of his equals" meant judgment by fellow nobles of the same rank, not necessarily a modern jury; later readers linked it to trial by jury.

Other clauses addressed taxation. In the 1215 text, the king promised not to levy certain extraordinary payments (scutage and aid) without the general consent of the realm, with exceptions for traditional feudal occasions. Later readers would link this idea to Parliament's control of taxation. Clause 61 created a committee of twenty-five barons to enforce the charter, an arrangement that was short-lived.

### The rule of law

Magna Carta is cited for the principle of the **rule of law**: that government, including the monarch, is subject to law, and that official action against individuals must follow established legal process. This is a large claim for a charter that mainly protected barons' interests. The key historical point is that the charter's *meaning grew*. In the early 1600s Sir Edward Coke and others read it as a guarantee of the ancient rights of English subjects against the Stuart kings, and the Petition of Right (1628, Week 6) invoked it. Colonists later cited it, and several of its ideas reappear in American documents.

### Tracing the ideas forward

The words "law of the land" in clause 39 were later restated in English statutes as "due process of law." The Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution says that no person shall be "deprived of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law," and the Fourteenth Amendment repeats the phrase for the states. Clause 40's promise against denial and delay of justice is echoed in the Sixth Amendment's guarantee of "a speedy and public trial." Treat these as lines of descent, not exact copies: the context and the people protected changed dramatically.

@mistakes
- Treating Magna Carta as a modern democratic constitution. It was a negotiated settlement of feudal grievances.
- Saying the charter was sealed once and then stayed in force. It was annulled in 1215 and survived through later reissues.
- Reading "judgment of his equals" as a modern twelve-person jury. The link to jury trial was drawn later.
- Claiming the Fifth Amendment copies clause 39 word for word. The relationship is historical descent.
