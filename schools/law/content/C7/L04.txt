@article
By the 14th century, the common law had a problem. It was precise, but its rigid forms sometimes left plaintiffs without help, and its main remedy was money damages. A person who needed something other than money, or whose claim fit no writ, or who feared that a powerful opponent would intimidate a jury, might petition the king. The king referred such petitions to his **Chancellor**, and a new court grew: the **Court of Chancery**, administering **equity**.

### Law and equity

Equity did not claim to overrule the common law. It acted on the person of the defendant, ordering him to do or refrain from doing something, and enforced the order by contempt. Chancery judged according to **conscience**: where a defendant's legal right was being used to commit what a court of conscience would call a wrong, the Chancellor intervened. Chancery used its own procedure, and, characteristically, **it had no jury**.

### Equitable remedies

| Remedy | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Injunction** | orders a party to do or stop doing something |
| **Specific performance** | orders a party to perform a contract, usually for unique property such as land |
| **Rescission** | undoes a contract (for example for fraud or mistake) |
| **Reformation** | rewrites a written contract to match the parties' actual agreement |
| **Constructive trust** | imposes a trust on property that someone holds unjustly |

Equity also enforced the **trust** (originally the "use"), in which one person holds legal title for another's benefit; common-law courts did not recognise the beneficiary's interest, but Chancery did.

### Limits on equity: the maxims

Equity developed maxims that still shape remedies. Equity will not grant relief where the plaintiff has an **adequate remedy at law**: if money damages will fully compensate, there is no need for an injunction or specific performance. A plaintiff must come with **clean hands**; **equity aids the vigilant**, so unreasonable delay may bar relief (**laches**); and **he who seeks equity must do equity**.

### Law versus equity: a comparison

Take a seller who breaks a contract to sell a unique parcel of land. At law, the buyer's remedy is **damages**, a payment of money measured by the loss (for example, the difference between the contract price and market price). In equity, the buyer might obtain **specific performance**, an order that the seller convey the land. The legal remedy is money and follows a jury verdict; the equitable remedy is an order enforced by contempt and decided by a judge. Land is treated as unique, so damages are often inadequate.

### Conflict and merger

The common-law judges and the Chancellor sometimes clashed. In the **Earl of Oxford's Case** (1615), King James I settled that where law and equity conflict, equity prevails. In England, the **Judicature Acts** of 1873 to 1875 merged the administration of law and equity in a single court system, though the distinction in remedies remains.

In the United States, many states kept separate courts of chancery for a time. Federal courts retained separate sides ("law" and "equity") until the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure took effect in 1938, which created one form of action, the "civil action." The distinction still matters: the Seventh Amendment preserves the right to a jury in "suits at common law," and equitable claims are tried by a judge.

@example
title: Law or equity?
problem: Seller promised to sell Buyer a rare, one-of-a-kind painting for \$40,000, then refused to deliver. Buyer wants the painting itself. Which kind of remedy fits, and who decides?
step: Identify the legal remedy: damages, a sum of money, usually the difference between the market value and the contract price.
step: Ask whether money is adequate. A unique painting has no exact substitute, so damages may not make Buyer whole.
step: Identify the equitable remedy: specific performance, an order to deliver the painting on payment of the price.
step: Note the procedural difference: specific performance is decided by a judge in equity, with no jury, and enforced by contempt.

@mistakes
- Saying equity overruled the common law. Equity operated on the defendant personally and supplemented it.
- Forgetting the requirement that legal remedies be inadequate before equity will act.
- Treating the merger of law and equity as the end of the distinction. The distinction survives in remedies and the jury right.
- Assuming juries decide equitable claims. Equity traditionally has no jury.
