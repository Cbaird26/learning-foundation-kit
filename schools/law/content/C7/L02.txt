@article
The common law got its name from a simple idea: after the Norman Conquest, the king's courts developed one body of law **common to the whole realm**, in place of a patchwork of local customs. This lesson shows how that happened.

### 1066: conquest and continuity

In 1066 William of Normandy defeated King Harold at the Battle of Hastings and was crowned king of England. The Normans did not abolish English law. They kept the shire and hundred courts and much customary law, but they added a strong, centralised monarchy. In 1086 the king's commissioners compiled the **Domesday Book**, a survey of land and landholders. It shows a government able to count, tax and control property across the whole country.

Feudalism shaped the law of land. Land was held of the king, directly or through lords, in exchange for service. Disputes about land were therefore matters of royal concern, and that gave the crown a reason to supervise them.

### The king's court and the rise of royal justice

The king's advisers and officials formed a body often called the **Curia Regis** (the king's court). Over time its judicial work separated into three central courts:

| Court | Main concern |
|---|---|
| **Exchequer** | royal revenue, and disputes about debts owed to the crown |
| **Court of Common Pleas** | civil disputes between subjects, especially land and debt |
| **Court of King's Bench** | matters touching the crown's peace and, later, review of other courts |

Common Pleas was the court for ordinary civil litigation ("common" meaning between common persons, not the crown). Magna Carta (1215) provided that common pleas should be heard in a fixed place, which in practice became Westminster.

### Henry II and the travelling judges

Henry II ruled from 1154 to 1189, after a period of civil war. He strengthened royal justice by sending **itinerant justices** on circuits (the **eyre**) to hear cases in the counties and to bring back to the central courts what they found. His Assize of Clarendon (1166) required local people to report suspected serious criminals to the justices. In civil matters, **petty assizes** such as novel disseisin gave a person recently dispossessed of land a quick royal remedy in place of a long dispute in a lord's court. Litigants chose the royal route because it was faster and more reliable, so business flowed to the king's courts.

### Why "common"

As judges rode from county to county and then returned to Westminster, they compared decisions and applied the same rules. Local custom did not vanish, but the rules the royal judges applied came to be seen as the **common law** of the whole kingdom: one law for all, as opposed to the particular laws of a manor, borough or church court. Early treatises record the result. The work usually called Glanvill (written around the end of Henry II's reign) described royal procedure, and the later work attributed to Bracton (mid-13th century) gathered cases and principles.

### Common law as a method

Notice what kind of law this was. It was not issued as a code. It emerged from a **procedure** (writs, Week 3) and from **decisions** by judges applying consistent rules. That method, building law case by case, is the sense in which the common law is still different from code-based systems, and it is the reason a later chapter of this course is about writs, juries and precedent.

@mistakes
- Saying the Normans brought a new legal code. They kept much English custom and added strong royal institutions.
- Reading "common" as "ordinary" or "shared by commoners." In this phrase it means common to the whole realm.
- Confusing Common Pleas (civil disputes between subjects) with King's Bench (crown pleas).
- Thinking Henry II invented the jury in one stroke. His reforms used local inquiry, which later developed into the jury (Week 3).
