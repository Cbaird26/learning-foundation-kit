@article
Two features of the common law are familiar to every American law student: **trial by jury** and decision by **precedent**. Both grew slowly out of the medieval royal courts, and a third feature, the **writ system**, explains why the law looked so strange to later reformers.

### From inquiry to jury

Royal officials in the 12th century often wanted facts from the people who knew them. They summoned a group of local men and required them to answer under oath: who holds this land, who is suspected of this crime. This **inquest** or recognition was the seed of the jury.

Two juries grew from it. The **grand jury** (called the jury of presentment) reported suspected crimes to royal justices and, over time, decided whether there was enough accusation to require a trial. The **petit** or **trial jury** decided disputed facts at trial.

The trial jury became the usual mode of proof after the ordeal collapsed. In 1215 the Fourth Lateran Council forbade clergy from taking part in ordeals. Without priests to bless them, ordeals lost their religious basis, and the English royal courts turned to trial by a jury of twelve. Early juries were not impartial strangers: they were neighbours who knew the facts. Only later did the jury become a body that hears evidence in court and decides on it.

### Jury independence

A landmark episode is **Bushell's Case** (1670). Jurors who acquitted defendants against the judge's direction were fined and imprisoned; on a writ of habeas corpus, the Court of Common Pleas held that a jury could not be punished for its verdict. The case is remembered as a foundation of jury independence.

In the United States the jury is protected by Article III, the Sixth Amendment (criminal cases) and the Seventh Amendment (civil cases "at common law"). In *Ramos v. Louisiana* (2020), the Supreme Court held that the Sixth Amendment requires a unanimous verdict to convict of a serious offense, in state as well as federal court.

### Writs and forms of action

To start a suit in the king's courts, a plaintiff generally had to obtain an **original writ**, a written royal command, from the Chancery (the royal writing office). Each writ matched a particular claim, and each claim had a **form of action** with its own procedure and rules. Familiar forms included debt, detinue, covenant, replevin, trespass, trespass on the case, assumpsit and ejectment. Lawyers summarised the system: **remedies precede rights**. A claim existed only if some writ fitted it.

The system had two sides. It gave precision and predictability. But if you picked the wrong form, you lost, however strong your story. In the 13th century, the king's government tried to limit new writs, and the Statute of Westminster II (1285) allowed the Chancery to issue new writs in similar cases, which led to important new actions such as trespass on the case. In England the forms of action were largely abolished in the 19th century; their legacy is still present in the way law students learn contract, tort and property.

### Precedent

Common-law judges decided by looking at how earlier cases had been decided. Their discussions were recorded in the **Year Books** (reports of arguments from the late 13th century to the 16th) and later in named reports. Writers such as Coke and Blackstone systematised the law in the 17th and 18th centuries. The modern rule of **stare decisis** ("to stand by things decided") holds that courts follow earlier decisions on the same point, especially those of higher courts in the same jurisdiction. Precedent gives stability and equal treatment, while distinguishing and overruling allow the law to change.

@mistakes
- Assuming the medieval jury was impartial strangers hearing evidence. Early juries were local people who already knew the facts.
- Treating the grand jury and the trial jury as the same body. One accuses; the other decides guilt or liability.
- Thinking stare decisis means precedents can never change. Courts may distinguish or overrule.
- Believing modern jury trial is unaffected by *Ramos*. It requires unanimity to convict of a serious offense.
