@article
By 1787 the weaknesses of the Articles of Confederation were clear: no power to tax, no national executive, and no national courts. A convention met in Philadelphia that summer, ostensibly to revise the Articles, and produced a new Constitution, signed on September 17, 1787.

### Structure and ratification

The Constitution created three branches (Congress, President and federal courts), divided power between nation and states (**federalism**), and made the Constitution and federal laws "the supreme Law of the Land" (the **Supremacy Clause**, Article VI). Article VII provided that it would take effect among the states ratifying it once **nine of the thirteen** had done so. Supporters (Federalists) and opponents (Anti-Federalists) debated in state conventions and in print. The *Federalist Papers* were essays defending the new plan. New Hampshire became the ninth state in 1788 and the new government began in 1789.

### The Bill of Rights

Many Anti-Federalists objected that the Constitution lacked a list of rights. In 1789 the first Congress proposed amendments; ten were ratified by the states and took effect on December 15, 1791. These are the **Bill of Rights**. They protect, among other things, speech, religion, arms, protection against unreasonable searches, due process, the jury, and (in the Eighth Amendment) bail and punishment limits. The Bill of Rights was understood in 1791 to restrain the federal government only; in *Barron v. Baltimore* (1833) the Supreme Court held that the Fifth Amendment's takings clause did not apply to a state or city. Application to the states came later, through the Fourteenth Amendment (Weeks 9 and 12).

Article V sets the amendment process: proposal by two-thirds of both houses of Congress (or a convention called by two-thirds of the states), and ratification by three-fourths of the states.

### Judicial power and *Marbury v. Madison* (1803)

Article III vests "the judicial Power" in the Supreme Court and lower federal courts. It does not expressly say courts may declare statutes unconstitutional. Alexander Hamilton's *Federalist* No. 78 argued that courts, as the "least dangerous" branch, must treat the Constitution as fundamental law and prefer it to a conflicting statute. The Supreme Court adopted this in *Marbury*.

**Facts.** John Adams's administration appointed William Marbury a justice of the peace for the District of Columbia just before leaving office, but his commission was not delivered. The new Secretary of State, James Madison, withheld it. Marbury asked the Supreme Court directly for a writ of **mandamus**, an order to deliver the commission, relying on section 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789, which seemed to give the Court original jurisdiction to issue such writs.

**Reasoning.** Chief Justice Marshall asked three questions. First, did Marbury have a right to the commission? Yes: the appointment was complete. Second, if he had a right, did the law give him a remedy? Yes: the laws of the country must provide a remedy for the violation of a vested legal right. Third, was mandamus from the Supreme Court the proper remedy? Here the Court held that Article III lists the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction and Congress could not enlarge it; section 13, to the extent that it gave the Court original jurisdiction over mandamus, conflicted with Article III. When a statute conflicts with the Constitution, a court must choose, and the Constitution, as fundamental and paramount law, prevails. The Court wrote that it is "emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is."

**Result.** Marbury had a right and a remedy in principle, but the Court lacked jurisdiction to grant it. The decision established **judicial review** of federal statutes. Later cases extended review to state laws and executive acts. The idea of courts saying what the law is was invoked again in *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo* (2024), where the Court held that courts must exercise independent judgment on what a statute means rather than defer to agencies under *Chevron* (Week 11).

@example
title: Applying Marbury's three questions
problem: Summarise the reasoning of *Marbury v. Madison* by answering each of Marshall's three questions in turn.
step: Right: Marbury's appointment was complete, so he had a legal right to the commission.
step: Remedy: where there is a legal right there is a legal remedy; the executive's refusal could be reviewed by a court.
step: Jurisdiction: Article III set the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction, and Congress could not add to it by statute.
step: Conclusion: section 13 conflicted with Article III; the Constitution prevails over a conflicting statute, so the Court could not issue the writ, and judicial review was established.

@mistakes
- Saying *Marbury* ordered the commission delivered. The Court held it lacked jurisdiction to order it.
- Thinking the Bill of Rights originally applied to the states. *Barron v. Baltimore* held it did not.
- Believing that the Constitution expressly grants judicial review. The power comes from *Marbury*'s reasoning and from structure and practice.
- Confusing the amendment rules: proposal needs two-thirds of Congress, ratification needs three-fourths of the states.
