@article
After Reconstruction, southern states enacted segregation laws. In ***Plessy v. Ferguson*** (1896), the Supreme Court upheld a Louisiana law requiring separate railway cars for white and Black passengers, holding it consistent with the Fourteenth Amendment so long as the facilities were equal; a single justice dissented. The "separate but equal" doctrine shaped law for decades.

### Brown and the civil rights statutes

Lawyers challenged segregation case by case. In ***Brown v. Board of Education*** (1954), a unanimous Court held that separate public schools for Black and white children are inherently unequal and violate the Equal Protection Clause. In *Brown II* (1955), it directed desegregation "with all deliberate speed." Resistance was strong, and enforcement took years.

Congress then acted. The **Civil Rights Act of 1964** barred discrimination in public accommodations (Title II) and in employment (Title VII), among other things. The Court upheld Title II under the Commerce Clause in *Heart of Atlanta Motel v. United States* (1964). The **Voting Rights Act of 1965** enforced the Fifteenth Amendment with measures including federal review of changes to voting rules in covered jurisdictions. In *Shelby County v. Holder* (2013), a 5 to 4 Court held the coverage formula unconstitutional. More recently, in *Louisiana v. Callais* (2026), the Court held a congressional map was an unconstitutional racial gerrymander because the Voting Rights Act did not require the second majority-minority district.

### Incorporation and the Warren Court

Since *Barron* (1833), the Bill of Rights bound only the federal government (Week 8). Over the 20th century the Court applied most of its guarantees to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment's due process clause, a process called **incorporation**. Landmarks: *Mapp v. Ohio* (1961), applying the exclusionary rule to the states; *Gideon v. Wainwright* (1963), requiring counsel for indigent defendants in state felony cases; and *Miranda v. Arizona* (1966), requiring warnings before custodial interrogation. In *New York Times v. Sullivan* (1964), the Court held that public officials suing for defamation must show actual malice. In *Reynolds v. Sims* (1964), it required legislative districts of roughly equal population ("one person, one vote").

### Equality, liberty and privacy cases

*Loving v. Virginia* (1967) struck down bans on interracial marriage. *Griswold v. Connecticut* (1965) recognised a constitutional privacy right as to married couples' use of contraception. *Roe v. Wade* (1973) recognised a right to abortion; *Planned Parenthood v. Casey* (1992) reaffirmed its core; and *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization* (2022) overruled both, holding that the Constitution does not confer a right to abortion and returning the issue to elected representatives. The majority relied on history and tradition; the dissent argued for a broader reading of liberty and of precedent. *Obergefell v. Hodges* (2015) held that the Fourteenth Amendment requires states to license and recognise same-sex marriages.

### Recent developments

*Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard* (2023) held that race-conscious admissions at Harvard and UNC violate equal protection. *New York State Rifle & Pistol Ass'n v. Bruen* (2022) required gun regulations to fit the Second Amendment's text and the nation's historical tradition. In *Trump v. Barbara* (2026), the Court held that children born in the United States to parents who are unlawfully or temporarily present are citizens at birth under the Fourteenth Amendment. In *Ramos v. Louisiana* (2020), the Court required unanimous juries for serious state criminal convictions.

### Building a timeline

To place a case in its era, write the date, the case name, the legal question, the holding, and the era (Reconstruction, Lochner era, New Deal, civil rights era, or modern). A five-case timeline might read: *Marbury* (1803, early republic), *Dred Scott* (1857, antebellum), *Lochner* (1905, Progressive Era), *Wickard* (1942, New Deal), *Brown* (1954, civil rights era).

step: *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896), post-Reconstruction: upheld "separate but equal."

@mistakes
- Saying *Brown* overruled *Plessy* by name. It rejected "separate but equal" in public education.
- Forgetting that incorporation applied rights to the states selectively, case by case.
- Treating *Roe* and *Casey* as current law. *Dobbs* (2022) overruled both.
- Placing cases in the wrong era because of when they are later cited, not when they were decided.
