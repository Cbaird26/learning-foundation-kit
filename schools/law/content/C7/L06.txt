@article
In the 17th century, England fought over a basic question: could the king govern without Parliament and imprison or tax subjects on his own authority? The answers shaped American constitutional law.

### The Stuart conflict

Kings James I and Charles I claimed wide royal prerogative. Charles raised money without parliamentary consent through forced loans and, in the 1630s, **ship money**. He imprisoned those who refused to pay, and the courts at first backed him; in **Hampden's Case** (1637) the judges upheld ship money. The prerogative court of **Star Chamber** was also used to punish critics. Parliament responded in 1628 with the **Petition of Right**, which, citing Magna Carta and other statutes, declared that taxes require parliamentary consent, that no one should be imprisoned without cause shown, that soldiers should not be billeted on householders against their will, and that martial law should not be used in peacetime.

### Civil war and its aftermath

Charles ruled for eleven years without calling Parliament and then, needing money, called it again. In 1641 Parliament abolished Star Chamber. Civil war between king and Parliament began in 1642. Charles was tried by a special court and executed in January 1649, and England became a republic, later a protectorate under Oliver Cromwell. The monarchy was restored in 1660 under Charles II.

### Habeas corpus

The writ of **habeas corpus** ("you shall have the body") ordered a jailer to bring a prisoner before a court and explain the detention. It existed long before the 17th century, but royal officials could evade it. The **Habeas Corpus Act of 1679** strengthened it: it set time limits for jailers to respond and penalties for delay. The statute is the model for the American constitutional rule that "The Privilege of the Writ of Habeas Corpus shall not be suspended, unless when in Cases of Rebellion or Invasion the public Safety may require it" (Article I, section 9).

### The Glorious Revolution and the Bill of Rights

James II, a Catholic king, claimed power to suspend and dispense with laws. In 1688 leading Englishmen invited William of Orange to intervene; James fled. In early 1689 Parliament declared the throne vacant and offered the crown to **William III and Mary II** on conditions. The resulting **Bill of Rights (1689)** declared that the king could not suspend laws or levy money without Parliament, that subjects have the right to petition, that parliamentary elections and debates should be free, and, in words you should know, that "excessive bail ought not to be required, nor excessive fines imposed, nor cruel and unusual punishments inflicted." The Act of Settlement (1701) later provided that judges hold office during good behaviour, protecting judicial independence.

The settlement is often described as establishing **parliamentary sovereignty**: Parliament, with the monarch, became the supreme lawmaker. Americans would later argue that these rights belonged to them as Englishmen.

### Tracing a clause forward

Here is one chain, from Magna Carta's promise against seizure without lawful process:

| Document | Language or idea |
|---|---|
| Magna Carta (1215), clause 39 | "except by the lawful judgment of his equals or by the law of the land" |
| Petition of Right (1628) | no imprisonment without cause shown; due process language |
| Habeas Corpus Act (1679) | enforces the demand that the jailer justify detention |
| U.S. Constitution, Article I, section 9 | habeas privilege protected |
| Fifth Amendment (1791) | "deprived of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law" |

Another chain runs from the English Bill of Rights to the Eighth Amendment: "Excessive bail shall not be required, nor excessive fines imposed, nor cruel and unusual punishments inflicted." Almost the same words appear.

title: Tracing the Eighth Amendment
problem: Show how the Eighth Amendment's wording is connected to the English Bill of Rights of 1689.
step: Quote the 1689 declaration: "excessive bail ought not to be required, nor excessive fines imposed, nor cruel and unusual punishments inflicted."
step: Quote the Eighth Amendment: "Excessive bail shall not be required, nor excessive fines imposed, nor cruel and unusual punishments inflicted."
step: Compare: the phrases, order and structure nearly match; the American version is a command in the Constitution rather than a statement of rights in a parliamentary declaration.
step: Conclude: the Eighth Amendment is a direct descendant of the 1689 provision, which in turn reflects the long English effort to limit arbitrary royal punishment.

@mistakes
- Confusing the English Bill of Rights (1689) with the American Bill of Rights (1791).
- Thinking the Glorious Revolution involved a major civil war; it was largely bloodless in England.
- Assuming habeas corpus was invented in 1679. The Act strengthened an older writ.
- Treating a chain of documents as proof that each copied the one before. They show historical influence, not quotation.
