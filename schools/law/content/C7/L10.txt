@article
After the Civil War, railroads, steel, oil and factories transformed the country. Large corporations, urban poverty and labour conflict raised new legal questions that the old common law struggled to answer.

### New regulation

States and then Congress began to regulate economic power. In *Munn v. Illinois* (1877), the Supreme Court upheld state regulation of grain-elevator rates for businesses "affected with a public interest." The **Interstate Commerce Act** (1887) created the Interstate Commerce Commission to regulate railroad rates, a forerunner of the modern administrative agency (Week 11). The **Sherman Antitrust Act** (1890) prohibited contracts and combinations in "restraint of trade" and monopolisation; in *Standard Oil v. United States* (1911), the Court read it with a "rule of reason" and ordered the break-up of Standard Oil.

### Codification and organisation of law

Lawyers worried that case law was becoming vast and uncertain. One response was **codification**. In 1848 New York adopted a code of civil procedure drafted by David Dudley Field, and similar "Field Codes" were adopted in many states, simplifying pleading by abolishing the old forms of action (Week 3). Another response was educational: from 1870 the Harvard Law School introduced the **case method**, teaching law by reading appellate opinions. In 1923 the American Law Institute was founded and produced the **Restatements of the Law**, which summarise common-law rules (for example, of contracts and torts); courts cite them as persuasive. Later, the **Uniform Commercial Code**, promulgated in the 1950s and adopted in some form in nearly every state, codified the law of sales and other commercial transactions. The pattern in each case: the common law was not abandoned but organised.

### The Progressive Era

From the 1890s to the 1910s, reformers called Progressives sought to regulate industry, protect workers and clean up government. States enacted workers' compensation, wage and hours laws, and child labour limits. Constitutional amendments followed: the **Sixteenth** (1913) authorised a federal income tax; the **Seventeenth** (1913) provided for direct election of senators; the **Eighteenth** (1919) established national Prohibition (repealed by the Twenty-first in 1933); and the **Nineteenth** (1920) forbade denying the vote on account of sex.

### Lochner and liberty of contract

The Fourteenth Amendment's due process clause forbids states to deprive any person of "liberty" without due process of law. In ***Lochner v. New York*** (1905), the Court, by a vote of 5 to 4, struck down a New York law limiting bakers to ten hours a day and sixty hours a week. The majority held that the law interfered with the **liberty of contract** protected by the Fourteenth Amendment and was not a valid health measure. The dissenters argued that the Constitution does not adopt a particular economic theory and that legislatures should decide such matters. In *Muller v. Oregon* (1908), the Court upheld an Oregon limit on women's working hours in laundries, accepting evidence of health effects, reasoning that later courts and scholars have rejected. The period in which courts closely reviewed economic regulation is called the **Lochner era**; it ended in the 1930s (Week 11).

### Putting the amendments in context

The Reconstruction amendments (Week 9) were enacted to end slavery, to make citizens of the formerly enslaved, and to protect voting. From the late 19th century into the early 20th, courts applied the Fourteenth Amendment more in business cases such as *Lochner* than in cases protecting formerly enslaved people. Notice that the same text served different aims in different periods. That is a recurring feature of constitutional history.

title: Explain the purposes of the Thirteenth, Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments
problem: Write a summary that explains the purpose of each Reconstruction amendment and one limit on its enforcement.
step: Thirteenth (1865): abolish slavery and involuntary servitude, except as punishment for crime, throughout the United States.
step: Fourteenth (1868): define national citizenship, overturning *Dred Scott*, and forbid states to deny due process or equal protection of the laws.
step: Fifteenth (1870): forbid denying or abridging the right of citizens to vote on account of race, color or previous condition of servitude.
step: Limit: the Court read the Fourteenth narrowly in the *Slaughter-House Cases* (1873) and held in the *Civil Rights Cases* (1883) that its enforcement power reaches state action only.

@mistakes
- Saying *Lochner* upheld the New York bakers' law. It struck the law down, 5 to 4.
- Confusing the Restatements, which summarise common law, with statutes enacted by legislatures.
- Treating the Field Codes as abolishing the common law. They reorganised procedure and some substantive rules.
- Mixing up the Sixteenth (income tax) and Seventeenth (direct election of senators) Amendments.
