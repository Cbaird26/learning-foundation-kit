@article
The first English colonies in North America were founded under royal charters. The Virginia charter of 1606 promised the colonists the liberties and immunities of Englishmen, and the idea that the colonists carried English rights with them became central to colonial legal thought.

### Reception of English law

Colonies did not simply copy England's courts and rules. Settlers took the common law as the background law but adapted it to local needs. Some colonies were governed by compacts or early codes: the **Mayflower Compact** (1620) was an agreement among settlers to form a civil body politic, and the Massachusetts **Body of Liberties** (1641) set out rights and procedures. Colonial assemblies, such as Virginia's House of Burgesses (established 1619), made local law, while royal governors and the Privy Council supervised. Local lawyers often learned from English books, and Blackstone's *Commentaries on the Laws of England* (published 1765 to 1769) became a standard text in America.

Juries mattered greatly in the colonies. In **Zenger's trial** (1735), a New York printer was prosecuted for seditious libel for criticising the governor; under English law, truth was not a defence, but the jury acquitted, and the case became a symbol of jury independence and free press.

### The crisis after 1763

After the Seven Years' War, Britain sought to raise revenue in America and tighten control. The Sugar Act (1764) and the **Stamp Act** (1765) taxed the colonies directly. Colonists responded that, under English constitutional principles, they could be taxed only by their own representatives: the slogan was "no taxation without representation." Britain's answer was the theory of **virtual representation** and, in the Declaratory Act (1766), the assertion that Parliament could legislate for the colonies "in all cases whatsoever," a doctrine of parliamentary sovereignty.

Legal grievances multiplied. **Writs of assistance** gave customs officers broad search powers. **Vice-admiralty courts**, which sat without juries, heard many customs cases. The Townshend duties (1767), the Tea Act (1773) and the Coercive (or Intolerable) Acts (1774) intensified the conflict. The colonists claimed the rights of Englishmen; Parliament claimed supreme authority. Resistance led to the First Continental Congress (1774) and fighting at Lexington and Concord in 1775.

### Independence

The **Declaration of Independence**, adopted on July 4, 1776, justified separation. It claimed that "all men are created equal" and are "endowed by their Creator with certain unalienable Rights," among them "Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness," and that governments derive "their just powers from the consent of the governed." Much of the document is a list of charges against the king: depriving colonists of jury trial in many cases, imposing taxes without consent, and keeping standing armies. Slavery remained lawful in most of the new states, a contradiction that later chapters of this course take up.

### New governments

After independence, states wrote written constitutions, often with declarations of rights. Many provided for separation of powers, annual or frequent elections, and protections for jury trial and against general warrants. The national government operated under the **Articles of Confederation**, which Congress proposed in 1777 and the states ratified in 1781. It created a weak confederation with no power to tax and no national executive or court system. The war formally ended with the Treaty of Paris in 1783. The weaknesses of the Articles led to the Constitutional Convention of 1787 (Week 8).

### A seed of judicial review

Colonists also drew on the claim, associated with **Dr. Bonham's Case** (1610), that a court might refuse to apply an act contrary to fundamental law. That claim was disputed in England, where Parliament became sovereign, but it fed into American ideas of written constitutions that courts could enforce.

problem: A colonial pamphlet complains that customs cases are tried before a judge alone, in a court with no jury. Identify the institution and the legal principle at stake.
step: Identify the institution: vice-admiralty courts sat without juries and heard many customs and trade cases.
step: Identify the principle: colonists claimed the rights of Englishmen, including jury trial.

step: Link forward: the Sixth and Seventh Amendments later protected jury trial in federal courts.

@mistakes
- Treating the colonies as having identical legal systems. Each had its own charter, courts and practices.
- Reading "no taxation without representation" as a complaint about all British law. It was about taxation and consent.
- Confusing the Declaration of Independence (1776) with the Constitution (1787). The Declaration is not a legal framework for government.
- Forgetting that the Articles of Confederation preceded the Constitution.
