material: **Source 1: Magna Carta (1215), clauses 39 and 40, in the usual modern English translation (quotation).** Clause 39: "No free man shall be seized or imprisoned, or stripped of his rights or possessions, or outlawed or exiled, or deprived of his standing in any other way, nor will we proceed with force against him, or send others to do so, except by the lawful judgment of his equals or by the law of the land." Clause 40: "To no one will we sell, to no one deny or delay right or justice."
material: **Source 2: Assize of Clarendon (1166), clearly labeled summary (not a quotation).** Henry II's assize required sworn groups of local men from each area to report to the royal justices persons suspected of serious crimes such as robbery and murder. It is often cited as an origin of the grand jury (jury of presentment).
material: **Source 3: Fourth Lateran Council (1215), clearly labeled summary (not a quotation).** The Council forbade clergy from taking part in trials by ordeal. English royal courts then increasingly used trial by jury of local people in place of the ordeal.
material: **Source 4: Bushell's Case (1670), clearly labeled summary (not a quotation).** Jurors who had acquitted defendants contrary to the judge's direction were fined and imprisoned. On a writ of habeas corpus, the Court of Common Pleas held that jurors could not be punished for their verdict. The case is cited as a foundation of jury independence, and it also shows habeas corpus protecting liberty.
material: **Source 5: Habeas Corpus Act (1679), clearly labeled summary (not a quotation).** The statute strengthened the writ of habeas corpus by setting time limits within which a jailer had to bring a prisoner before a court and explain the detention, and by imposing penalties for delay.
material: **Source 6: English Bill of Rights (1689), excerpt (quotation).** "excessive bail ought not to be required, nor excessive fines imposed, nor cruel and unusual punishments inflicted."
material: **Source 7: United States Constitution, Article I, section 9, and Article III, section 2 (quotations).** Article I, section 9: "The Privilege of the Writ of Habeas Corpus shall not be suspended, unless when in Cases of Rebellion or Invasion the public Safety may require it." Article III, section 2: "The Trial of all Crimes, except in Cases of Impeachment, shall be by Jury."
material: **Source 8: Sixth and Seventh Amendments (1791), excerpts (quotations).** Sixth: "In all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right to a speedy and public trial, by an impartial jury of the State and district wherein the crime shall have been committed." Seventh: "In Suits at common law, where the value in controversy shall exceed twenty dollars, the right of trial by jury shall be preserved."
material: **Source 9: Zenger's trial (1735), clearly labeled summary (not a quotation).** A New York printer was prosecuted for seditious libel for publishing criticism of the colonial governor. Under the law as the judges stated it, truth was not a defence, but the jury acquitted. The case is remembered as an example of jury independence and of press freedom in colonial America.
material: **Source 10: Ex parte Milligan (1866) and Boumediene v. Bush (2008), clearly labeled summaries (not quotations).** In Milligan, the Supreme Court held that a civilian could not be tried by a military tribunal in a place where the civil courts were open and operating. In Boumediene, the Court held that foreign nationals detained at Guantanamo Bay have the constitutional privilege of habeas corpus and that a statute withdrawing it, without an adequate substitute, violated the Suspension Clause.
material: **Source 11: Ramos v. Louisiana (2020), clearly labeled summary (not a quotation).** The Supreme Court held that the Sixth Amendment's guarantee of an impartial jury requires a unanimous verdict to convict a defendant of a serious offense, and that this requirement applies to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment.

### Week by week

- **Week 1, Roman and medieval roots:** Twelve Tables, Justinian's Corpus Juris Civilis (Code, Digest, Institutes), civil law versus common law, canon law, wergild, compurgation and ordeal.
- **Week 2, Norman Conquest and royal courts:** 1066, Domesday Book, Curia Regis, Exchequer, Common Pleas and King's Bench, Henry II's itinerant justices; the common law is law common to the whole realm.
- **Week 3, juries, writs and precedent:** grand jury and trial jury, the Fourth Lateran Council (1215), Bushell's Case (1670), original writs and forms of action, stare decisis, *Ramos* (2020).
- **Week 4, equity:** Chancery, conscience, injunction, specific performance, rescission, reformation, adequate remedy at law, clean hands, laches, *Earl of Oxford's Case* (1615), Judicature Acts, merger.
- **Week 5, Magna Carta:** 1215, clauses 39 and 40, reissues and the 1297 confirmation, the rule of law, descent to due process.
- **Week 6, civil war and revolution:** Petition of Right (1628), Habeas Corpus Act (1679), English Bill of Rights (1689), Act of Settlement (1701).
- **Week 7, colonial law:** charters, Mayflower Compact, Stamp Act, vice-admiralty courts, Declaration of Independence, Articles of Confederation.
- **Week 8, Constitution:** ratification by nine states, Bill of Rights (1791), Article V, *Barron*, *Marbury v. Madison*.
- **Week 9, slavery and Reconstruction:** Three-Fifths Clause, *Dred Scott*, the Thirteenth, Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments, the Civil Rights Cases.
- **Week 10, industrialization:** Sherman Act, Field Codes, Restatements, *Lochner*, Progressive amendments.
- **Week 11, New Deal:** *Schechter*, 1937, *Wickard*, the APA, *Loper Bright* (2024), *Jarkesy* (2024), *Trump v. Slaughter* (2026).
- **Week 12, civil rights era:** *Plessy*, *Brown*, the 1964 and 1965 statutes, incorporation, *Dobbs* (2022).

### Common errors

- Treating *Chevron*, *Humphrey's Executor*, *Roe* and *Casey* as current law; each has been overruled.
- Confusing the English Bill of Rights (1689) with the American Bill of Rights (1791).
- Saying *Marbury* ordered the commission delivered.
- Treating the ordeal, compurgation and the jury as the same kind of proof.

prompt: Write a short essay (about 250 words) explaining how the courts changed their approach to economic regulation between *Lochner v. New York* (1905) and *West Coast Hotel v. Parrish* (1937), and how this shift helped the administrative state develop. Mention one recent change in administrative law.
hint: Start with the liberty-of-contract rule, then the 1937 shift, then Loper Bright or Slaughter.
solution: In Lochner (1905), the Court struck down a bakers' hours law, finding a liberty of contract in the Fourteenth Amendment's due process clause. During the Lochner era, courts closely reviewed economic regulation. After the Great Depression, early New Deal laws also met resistance, as in Schechter (1935). In 1937, West Coast Hotel upheld a state minimum-wage law and overruled Adkins, and NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin upheld national labor regulation under the Commerce Clause. From then, courts reviewed economic legislation deferentially, which allowed Congress to create and delegate to agencies such as the NLRB. Recent decisions reshape the state: Loper Bright (2024) overruled Chevron so that courts decide the best reading of a statute, and Trump v. Slaughter (2026) overruled Humphrey's Executor so FTC commissioners are removable at will.
