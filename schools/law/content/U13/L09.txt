@article
Most speech is protected, but a small set of historic categories receives no First Amendment protection or less protection. *United States v. Stevens* (2010) declined to recognize a new category by balancing the value of speech against its social cost; the exceptions are those with a long tradition, and a restriction within a category still may not discriminate on viewpoint (*R.A.V. v. St. Paul*, 1992).

### Incitement

*Brandenburg v. Ohio* (1969): advocacy of illegal conduct may be punished only if it is "directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action" and is "likely to incite or produce such action." Abstract advocacy of force, or a statement that violence may be needed at some future time, is protected (*Hess v. Indiana*, 1973). The earlier "clear and present danger" cases (*Schenck v. United States*, 1919) have been superseded.

### True threats

*Virginia v. Black* (2003) held that a state may ban cross burning carried out with the intent to intimidate, but not treat all cross burning as prima facie evidence of intent. *Counterman v. Colorado* (2023) held that to prosecute a true threat, the state must prove that the defendant had some subjective understanding of the statements' threatening nature, and that **recklessness** suffices: the defendant consciously disregarded a substantial risk that the communications would be viewed as threatening violence. A purely objective "reasonable listener" standard is insufficient. The majority reasoned that the chilling effect of liability for innocent misunderstanding required a mental-state requirement; the dissent would have retained the objective standard, as speech that a reasonable person would see as a threat causes the same harm regardless of the speaker's state of mind.

### Defamation

*New York Times v. Sullivan* (1964): a public official may recover for a defamatory falsehood relating to official conduct only with proof of **actual malice**, meaning knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for the truth, shown by clear and convincing evidence. *Curtis Publishing v. Butts* (1967) extended the rule to public figures. *Gertz v. Robert Welch* (1974): for private figures, states may not impose liability without fault (negligence suffices) and may not allow presumed or punitive damages without actual malice. *Dun & Bradstreet v. Greenmoss Builders* (1985): presumed and punitive damages are allowed without actual malice if the speech is on a private matter. *Hustler Magazine v. Falwell* (1988): a public figure cannot recover for intentional infliction of emotional distress from a parody unless it contained a false statement of fact made with actual malice. *Milkovich v. Lorain Journal* (1990): no separate opinion privilege, but statements that cannot reasonably be interpreted as stating actual facts are protected. *Snyder v. Phelps* (2011): speech on a public issue at a funeral is protected.

### Obscenity and sexual material

*Miller v. California* (1973): material is obscene if (1) the average person applying contemporary community standards would find that the work as a whole appeals to the prurient interest; (2) it depicts sexual conduct in a patently offensive way as defined by state law; and (3) it lacks serious literary, artistic, political or scientific value, judged by a reasonable-person standard. All three elements are required. *Stanley v. Georgia* (1969) protects private possession of obscene material at home, but not child pornography (*Osborne v. Ohio*, 1990). Child pornography depicting real children is unprotected (*New York v. Ferber*, 1982), but virtual images not using real children are protected (*Ashcroft v. Free Speech Coalition*, 2002). In *Free Speech Coalition v. Paxton* (2025), the Court applied intermediate scrutiny to a Texas law requiring age verification for websites with substantial material harmful to minors, and upheld it, reasoning that the law only incidentally burdens adults' protected access; the dissent would have applied strict scrutiny.

### Commercial speech

*Central Hudson Gas & Electric v. Public Service Commission* (1980): commercial speech receives intermediate protection. (1) The speech must concern lawful activity and not be misleading; if so, (2) the government interest must be substantial; (3) the regulation must directly advance the interest; and (4) it must be no more extensive than necessary. False or misleading advertising may be banned. Truthful price advertising is protected (*Virginia Pharmacy Board*, 1976).

### Other points

*Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire* (1942) defines **fighting words** as words that by their very utterance inflict injury or tend to incite an immediate breach of the peace, but the category is now narrow: *Cohen v. California* (1971) protected an offensive slogan on a jacket. *United States v. Alvarez* (2012) held that the Stolen Valor Act's ban on lying about military medals violated the First Amendment; false statements are not categorically unprotected.

### Applying it

Step 1: Does the statement fit a historic category? Step 2: Apply that category's test (imminence, mental state, actual malice, Miller). Step 3: If not, apply general content-based analysis. Step 4: Conclude.

@example
title: Heated words at a rally
problem: At a rally, Ravi tells a crowd, "Some day the people will have to take up arms against the police." Nothing happens, and no one is urged to act at a particular time. He is prosecuted for inciting violence. Is the speech protected?
step: Category: incitement. The test from Brandenburg asks whether the speech is directed to inciting imminent lawless action and is likely to do so.
step: Application: the statement is about a vague future time ("some day"), is not directed at producing immediate violence, and is unlikely to produce it. It is abstract advocacy.
step: Hess v. Indiana, which protected a statement about future action, is controlling.
step: Conclusion: the speech is protected and the prosecution fails.

@mistakes
- Using the Schenck "clear and present danger" test instead of Brandenburg's imminence and likelihood.
- Applying an objective reasonable-listener test alone to true threats after Counterman.
- Saying that all defamation of a private person requires actual malice. Only presumed and punitive damages on matters of public concern do (Gertz).
- Treating offensive speech as unprotected. Offensive speech is protected unless it fits a category.

@quiz mc
prompt: A state prosecutes a bookseller for selling a sexually explicit novel that a jury finds appeals to the prurient interest and is patently offensive under state law. The court finds, based on expert testimony, that the book taken as a whole has serious literary value. Under Miller v. California, what is the likely result?
choice*: The book cannot be obscene if, taken as a whole, it has serious literary value, because all three Miller elements must be met.
choice: The book is obscene, because prurient appeal and patent offensiveness suffice.
choice: The book is obscene if local community standards say it lacks value.
choice: The book is protected, because obscenity is not a recognized exception.
hint: The third Miller element is judged by a reasonable-person standard, not by community standards.
solution: Miller requires all three elements. The value prong is judged by whether a reasonable person would find serious value, not local standards. Obscenity is an unprotected category.
