@article
The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment says that no state shall "deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws." It applies to the federal government through the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause (*Bolling v. Sharpe*, 1954). Nearly every law classifies; the question is how closely a court examines the classification.

### The three tiers

| Tier | Classifications | Test |
|---|---|---|
| Strict scrutiny | race, national origin, alienage (state laws); laws burdening fundamental rights | necessary to a compelling interest; narrowly tailored |
| Intermediate scrutiny | sex; nonmarital birth | substantially related to an important interest |
| Rational basis | everything else (age, disability, wealth, most economic and social laws) | rationally related to a legitimate interest |

This lesson covers the base tier; the next covers the upper two.

### The rule

Under **rational basis review**, a classification is valid if it is rationally related to a legitimate government purpose. *FCC v. Beach Communications* (1993) states the defining features: a statute is presumed constitutional; the challenger must negate "every conceivable basis" that might support it; the legislature need not articulate its purpose, and the rationale need not have actually motivated the legislature; and rational speculation unsupported by evidence suffices. A classification is not invalid because it is **overinclusive** (burdens some it need not) or **underinclusive** (misses some it could reach). *Railway Express Agency v. New York* (1949) upheld a ban on advertising on delivery trucks that exempted trucks advertising the owner's own products, since the city could have thought the first kind distracting. *City of New Orleans v. Dukes* (1976) upheld a grandfather clause that exempted pushcart vendors in business at least eight years from a ban in the French Quarter. The Court has also upheld age-based mandatory retirement (*Massachusetts Board of Retirement v. Murgia*, 1976) since age is not a suspect class, and has held that wealth is not a suspect class and education is not a fundamental right for equal protection purposes (*San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez*, 1973).

### Rational basis with more bite

In several cases the Court has found laws invalid under the label of rational basis review when the law rested on animus. *United States Department of Agriculture v. Moreno* (1973) held that a "bare … desire to harm a politically unpopular group" is not a legitimate interest, and struck a food stamp rule aimed at "hippie communes." *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center* (1985) refused to treat intellectual disability as quasi-suspect but held that requiring a special permit for a group home for people with intellectual disabilities, when other group housing needed none, rested on an irrational fear and prejudice. *Romer v. Evans* (1996) struck down a state constitutional amendment that barred all legal protection against discrimination based on sexual orientation, concluding that it was so broad and so unrelated to any legitimate interest that it seemed motivated by animus. *United States v. Windsor* (2013) invalidated the federal definition of marriage in DOMA, relying on the Fifth Amendment. Critics of this line of cases argue that the Court is applying an unstated heightened standard; defenders say animus is never a legitimate interest.

### Class of one

*Village of Willowbrook v. Olech* (2000) held that a plaintiff who alleges she has been intentionally treated differently from others similarly situated, with no rational basis for the difference, states an equal protection claim even if she is not in a recognized class. *Engquist v. Oregon Department of Agriculture* (2008) held the class-of-one theory does not apply to public employment decisions, which are discretionary by nature.

### Applying it

Step 1: Identify the classification (on its face or in its application). Step 2: Select the tier. Step 3: State the government's purpose, actual or conceivable. Step 4: Ask whether the classification rationally relates to it, and whether the facts show animus. Step 5: Conclude.

@example
title: A grandfather clause for sidewalk food carts
problem: A city bans sidewalk food carts in its historic district but exempts carts that have operated there continuously for at least ten years. A newer vendor, Ben, claims the exemption denies him equal protection. Analyze.
step: Classification: older versus newer vendors, an economic classification involving no suspect class or fundamental right, so rational basis applies.
step: Purpose: the city may want to preserve the district's character by reducing congestion, while protecting the reliance interests of established vendors.
step: Fit: a grandfather clause is a rational way to protect reliance while phasing out the carts. Under Beach Communications and Dukes, the city need not prove that the line is perfect, nor that this was its actual purpose.
step: Conclusion: the ordinance is valid. Ben would need to show animus or irrationality, and nothing suggests that.

@mistakes
- Applying a "least restrictive means" or narrow tailoring analysis to an ordinary economic classification.
- Requiring the government to prove its purpose. The challenger must negate every conceivable basis.
- Assuming that rational basis review is always a win for the government. Animus (Moreno, Cleburne, Romer) can defeat a classification.
- Forgetting that class-of-one claims are unavailable in public employment (Engquist).
