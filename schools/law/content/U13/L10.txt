@article
The government has more power to control speech when it acts as employer or educator than when it acts as sovereign. But it cannot condition employment or school attendance on giving up all First Amendment rights.

### Public employees

Courts apply a sequence drawn from three cases.

1. **Citizen speech on a matter of public concern.** *Connick v. Myers* (1983) held that a government employee's speech is protected only if it addresses a matter of public concern, judged by content, form and context; a purely personal workplace grievance is not. *Garcetti v. Ceballos* (2006) held that when public employees speak **pursuant to their official duties**, they are not speaking as citizens and the Constitution does not insulate their communications from discipline. The majority reasoned that the government must control speech that it commissions, and that employees remain protected by whistleblower laws; the dissents worried about penalizing truthful reports of misconduct. *Lane v. Franks* (2014) held that sworn testimony in court, delivered outside the scope of ordinary job duties, is citizen speech even if it concerns information learned on the job.
2. **Pickering balance.** *Pickering v. Board of Education* (1968) weighs the employee's and public's interests in the speech against the government's interest in efficient public services, including disruption, loss of discipline or close working relationships. The teacher's letter to a newspaper about school funding was protected where it was not knowingly false and caused no disruption.
3. **Causation.** The employee must show the speech was a substantial or motivating factor, and the employer may show it would have taken the same action anyway (*Mt. Healthy v. Doyle*, 1977).

**Related rules.** Patronage dismissals of non-policymaking employees for political affiliation violate the First Amendment (*Elrod v. Burns*; *Branti v. Finkel*), but policymaking and confidential positions may require loyalty. *Heffernan v. City of Paterson* (2016) recognized a claim when an employer demotes someone based on a mistaken belief that she supported a political candidate. *Janus v. AFSCME* (2018) held that requiring nonmember public employees to pay agency fees violates the First Amendment, overruling *Abood*; the majority reasoned that the fees compel speech on matters of public concern, while the dissent stressed reliance and the government's interest in labor peace.

### Students

- ***Tinker v. Des Moines*** (1969): students do not "shed their constitutional rights … at the schoolhouse gate." The school may restrict speech only if it reasonably forecasts substantial disruption of school activities or invasion of the rights of others. Black armbands protesting war were protected.
- ***Bethel School District v. Fraser*** (1986): schools may punish lewd, vulgar and plainly offensive speech at a school assembly.
- ***Hazelwood v. Kuhlmeier*** (1988): schools may regulate school-sponsored speech (such as a school newspaper) if reasonably related to legitimate pedagogical concerns.
- ***Morse v. Frederick*** (2007): schools may restrict speech reasonably viewed as promoting illegal drug use at a school-supervised event.
- ***Mahanoy Area School District v. B.L.*** (2021): a school's authority over off-campus speech is diminished but not nonexistent. The Court held that suspending a cheerleader from the team for a vulgar off-campus social media post violated the First Amendment, without setting a categorical rule on off-campus speech. The Court noted that the school's interests (preventing bullying, threats, and disruption) might justify regulation in some off-campus circumstances.
- ***West Virginia v. Barnette*** (1943): schools may not compel students to salute the flag.

*Kennedy v. Bremerton School District* (2022) considered a public school coach's postgame prayer; the Court held that the coach's prayer was private religious expression, not speech pursuant to his official duties, so the *Garcetti* framework did not defeat his claim (see the next lessons).

### Applying it

For employees: (1) Was the speech part of official duties? (2) Public concern? (3) Pickering balance. (4) Causation. For students: identify the type of speech (political, vulgar, school-sponsored, drug-related, off-campus) and apply the matching case.

@example
title: A prosecutor's internal memo
problem: A deputy district attorney is assigned to review a search warrant. She writes a memo to her supervisor recommending dismissal of the case because the warrant affidavit contains misstatements. She is reassigned to a less desirable job. She sues, claiming retaliation for protected speech. Analyze.
step: Threshold: was she speaking as a citizen or pursuant to official duties? She wrote the memo because it was part of the task she was assigned.
step: Under Garcetti, speech made pursuant to official duties is not speech as a citizen for First Amendment purposes.
step: Because the first step fails, the court does not reach Pickering balancing.
step: Conclusion: no First Amendment claim; any remedy would come from whistleblower statutes or internal policies.

@mistakes
- Using Pickering balancing before deciding that the employee spoke as a citizen on a public concern.
- Saying that any speech about job-related information is automatically unprotected. Lane v. Franks protects sworn testimony outside ordinary duties.
- Applying Tinker to all school speech. Fraser, Hazelwood and Morse apply different tests.
- Reading Mahanoy as a rule that schools never regulate off-campus speech.

@practice self
prompt: A county employee who works in the permits office testifies under subpoena in a court trial that the county's director accepted payments from a developer, information the employee learned while working. The county then demotes him. Write an IRAC analysis of his First Amendment claim.
hint: Ask whether testifying was within his ordinary job duties, then whether it addressed a public concern, then apply Pickering.
solution: Issue: whether the demotion for sworn testimony violates the First Amendment. Rule: a public employee is protected when speaking as a citizen on a matter of public concern, unless the employer's interest in efficient operations outweighs the speech (Pickering; Connick). Speech pursuant to official duties is not citizen speech (Garcetti), but sworn testimony outside ordinary job duties is citizen speech even when it concerns information learned on the job (Lane v. Franks). Application: testifying under subpoena was not part of the employee's ordinary duties; public corruption is a matter of public concern. The county's interest in efficiency is weak, since truthful testimony in court is valued and the county has shown no disruption. Conclusion: the demotion likely violates the First Amendment, subject to causation (the county could show it would have taken the same action anyway).
rubric: Applies Garcetti and Lane to conclude that the testimony is citizen speech.
rubric: Finds a matter of public concern.
rubric: Conducts the Pickering balance.
rubric: Mentions causation or reaches a supported conclusion.

solution: Mahanoy held that the school's punishment violated the First Amendment on those facts, while noting that schools may have regulatory interests in some off-campus speech. It did not adopt a rule that off-campus speech is always beyond the school's reach.
