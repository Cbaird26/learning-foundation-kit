@article
### Strict scrutiny

**Race and national origin.** Any racial classification by government, whether burdening or benefiting a group, is reviewed under strict scrutiny (*Adarand Constructors v. Peña*, 1995, for the federal government; *City of Richmond v. J.A. Croson*, 1989, for states). The government must show a compelling interest and that the classification is narrowly tailored, ordinarily the least restrictive effective means. Remedying identified past discrimination by the government itself can be compelling; general societal discrimination cannot. *Korematsu v. United States* (1944) applied strict scrutiny but upheld the exclusion order; *Trump v. Hawaii* (2018) stated that it was wrongly decided. *Loving v. Virginia* (1967) struck down an anti-miscegenation law. The Fourteenth Amendment's guarantee of citizenship to persons born in the United States was applied in *Trump v. Barbara* (2026), which held that children born in the United States to parents who are unlawfully or temporarily present are citizens at birth.

**Alienage.** State classifications based on alienage get strict scrutiny (*Graham v. Richardson*, 1971, which involved lawful permanent residents; lower courts divide on noncitizens admitted only temporarily), with an exception for positions that go to the heart of self-government, such as police officers, probation officers and public school teachers (*Foley v. Connelie*, *Ambach v. Norwick*, *Cabell v. Chavez-Salido*). Federal classifications get rational basis review because of Congress's power over immigration (*Mathews v. Diaz*, 1976).

### Intermediate scrutiny

**Sex.** A sex classification must serve "important governmental objectives" and be "substantially related" to them (*Craig v. Boren*, 1976). *United States v. Virginia* (1996) required an "exceedingly persuasive justification," which the state must show rests on actual, not hypothesized, reasons and not on overbroad generalizations about the sexes; Virginia Military Institute's male-only admission failed. *Sessions v. Morales-Santana* (2017) struck down different citizenship transmission rules for unwed mothers and fathers, while *Nguyen v. INS* (2001) upheld a rule that required unwed fathers, but not mothers, to take steps to establish a link to the child. **Nonmarital children** are also subject to intermediate scrutiny (*Clark v. Jeter*, 1988).

### Discriminatory intent

A facially neutral law that has a disproportionate effect on a group violates equal protection only if adopted with discriminatory purpose (*Washington v. Davis*, 1976, a police test that disproportionately excluded Black applicants). *Village of Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Housing Development Corp.* (1977) lists evidence of intent: impact, historical background, sequence of events, departures from normal procedure, and legislative history. *Personnel Administrator v. Feeney* (1979) held that "discriminatory purpose" means the legislature acted "because of," not merely "in spite of," the adverse effect. Once improper purpose is shown, the burden shifts to the state to show that it would have enacted the law anyway.

### Recent developments

**Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard** (2023). The majority held that the race-conscious admissions programs of Harvard and the University of North Carolina violate equal protection. It reasoned that the programs' goals (such as educational benefits of diversity) were not sufficiently coherent or measurable to survive strict scrutiny, that race was used as a negative for some applicants and relied on stereotypes, and that the programs had no logical end point. The majority said universities may consider an applicant's discussion of how race affected her life, as long as the decision rests on the individual's experience and not on race. The dissents argued that the decision departs from decades of precedent, that the Fourteenth Amendment permits race-conscious efforts to achieve integration, and that ignoring race perpetuates inequality.

**United States v. Skrmetti** (2025). The Court upheld Tennessee's ban on puberty blockers and hormones to treat gender dysphoria in minors under rational basis review. The majority reasoned that the law classifies on the basis of age and medical use, not on sex or transgender status, and left open whether transgender status is a quasi-suspect class. The dissent would have applied heightened scrutiny, reasoning that the law classifies on the basis of sex.

**Little v. Hecox and West Virginia v. B.P.J.** (2026). The Court held that Idaho's law limiting girls' and women's teams by biological sex does not violate the Equal Protection Clause, and that Title IX allows schools to maintain separate male and female teams based on biological sex.

**Louisiana v. Callais** (2026). The Court held that a congressional map was an unconstitutional racial gerrymander because the Voting Rights Act did not require a second majority-minority district.

### Applying it

State the classification and whether it is on the face of the law or arises from purpose. Choose the tier. Apply the test: strict (compelling interest, narrow tailoring) or intermediate (important interest, substantial relation, exceedingly persuasive justification). Conclude.

@example
title: A state nursing program open only to women
problem: A state university runs a nursing school that admits only women, explaining that women are better suited to caring professions. A qualified man is denied admission and sues under equal protection. Analyze.
step: Classification: the admission rule classifies expressly by sex, so intermediate scrutiny applies under Craig v. Boren and United States v. Virginia.
step: The state must show an exceedingly persuasive justification: an important objective, and means substantially related to it, based on real reasons and not overbroad generalizations.
step: Application: the stated rationale is a stereotype about the sexes. It is not a legitimate objective, and no showing is made that excluding men serves any actual compensatory purpose.
step: Conclusion: the policy violates the Equal Protection Clause, and the man is entitled to admission or other relief.

@mistakes
- Applying strict scrutiny to sex classifications, or intermediate scrutiny to alienage classifications by states.
- Treating disparate impact alone as an equal protection violation. Washington v. Davis requires discriminatory purpose.
- Treating "benign" racial classifications as subject to a lower standard. Strict scrutiny applies to all racial classifications.
- Overstating Skrmetti or Hecox. Skrmetti did not decide whether transgender status is quasi-suspect.
