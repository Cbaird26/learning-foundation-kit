@article
Procedural due process asks a simple question: when the government takes away something important, did it use a fair procedure? The Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments forbid depriving a person of "life, liberty, or property, without due process of law." The analysis has two steps: (1) is a protected interest at stake, and (2) if so, what process is due?

### Step one: a protected interest

**Property.** *Board of Regents v. Roth* (1972) held that property is more than ownership: it includes a legitimate claim of entitlement, not a mere unilateral expectation. Such entitlements are created by independent sources such as state statutes, regulations, contracts, or mutually explicit understandings (*Perry v. Sindermann*, 1972). An untenured professor with a one-year contract and no right to renewal had no property interest in being rehired. A civil servant who may be removed only "for cause" has one. An at-will employee generally does not. Benefits such as welfare (*Goldberg v. Kelly*, 1970) and public education (*Goss v. Lopez*, 1975) can also be property. *Town of Castle Rock v. Gonzales* (2005) held there was no property interest in police enforcement of a restraining order.

**Liberty.** Liberty includes physical freedom and, in some contexts, other interests. Reputation alone is not liberty: *Paul v. Davis* (1976) requires "stigma plus," damage to reputation tied to a change in legal status. *Roth* recognized that if a government employer, in firing someone, publicly makes a stigmatizing charge such as dishonesty, the employee may be entitled to a name-clearing hearing, provided the employee claims the charge is false (*Codd v. Velger*, 1977). For prisoners, *Sandin v. Conner* (1995) looks for an atypical and significant hardship.

**Deprivation.** Negligence does not deprive anyone of due process (*Daniels v. Williams*, 1986). For random, unauthorized acts by state employees, *Parratt v. Taylor* (1981) and *Hudson v. Palmer* (1984) hold that an adequate postdeprivation state remedy satisfies due process, because the state could not have predicted the act. If the deprivation is authorized or predictable (*Zinermon v. Burch*, 1990), predeprivation process may be required.

### Step two: what process is due

Federal law, not state law, defines the process required. *Cleveland Board of Education v. Loudermill* (1985) rejected the argument that a state may grant a property right while limiting the procedures ("take the bitter with the sweet"). Process is "due" measured by *Mathews v. Eldridge* (1976), which weighs three factors:

1. the private interest affected;
2. the risk of an erroneous deprivation under current procedures and the probable value of additional safeguards;
3. the government's interest, including the fiscal and administrative burden of more process.

The core is notice and an opportunity to be heard "at a meaningful time and in a meaningful manner," before a neutral decisionmaker. *Goldberg* required an evidentiary hearing before welfare benefits end; *Mathews* held that a hearing after termination suffices for Social Security disability benefits, because the decision turns on medical records and the claimant's need is less immediate. A judge or adjudicator with a disqualifying financial or personal stake violates due process (*Caperton v. Massey Coal*, 2009).

### The public employee

*Loudermill* held that a public employee with a property interest in continued employment is entitled, before termination, to oral or written notice of the charges, an explanation of the employer's evidence, and an opportunity to present her side. This pretermination step is only an "initial check against mistaken decisions"; it need not be elaborate when a full post-termination hearing with back-pay is available promptly. *Gilbert v. Homar* (1997) held that a suspension without pay of an employee charged with a felony could occur without a pre-suspension hearing, followed by prompt post-suspension process.

**Deliverable method.** For any firing: (a) Is there property (for-cause or tenure rights) or liberty (stigma plus)? (b) What was the pretermination notice, evidence and chance to respond? (c) What happens after, and how soon? (d) Weigh Mathews.

@example
title: A tenured clerk fired after a timesheet audit
problem: A city charter allows removal of a civil service clerk only for cause. An audit shows Ana falsified her timesheets. The city fires her on the spot without notice or any chance to respond, and offers an appeal hearing before a neutral board 45 days later with back pay if she wins. Does the city's procedure satisfy due process?
step: Property: the charter's for-cause protection gives Ana a legitimate claim of entitlement to continued employment, a property interest under Roth and Loudermill.
step: Process is a federal question. Mathews factors: her private interest in a livelihood is strong; the risk of error from a one-sided audit is real and a brief opportunity to respond has value; the city's burden is small, since the pretermination step is not a full trial.
step: Loudermill requires, before termination, notice of the charges, an explanation of the evidence and a chance to respond. Ana received none.
step: The prompt, full post-termination hearing does not cure that omission. The firing violated procedural due process; under § 1983 the remedy turns on proof of injury caused by the lack of process (at least nominal damages, per Carey v. Piphus).

@mistakes
- Letting state law define the process. State law creates the property interest; the Constitution defines the process due.
- Assuming a full evidentiary hearing is required before every termination. Loudermill requires only an initial check if a full post-termination hearing follows.
- Treating damage to reputation alone as a liberty interest. Stigma must be accompanied by a change in legal status, such as termination.
- Forgetting that at-will employees ordinarily lack a property interest.
