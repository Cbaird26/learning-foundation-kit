@article
The Establishment Clause says "Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion," and it applies to the states (*Everson v. Board of Education*, 1947). It prohibits government from sponsoring religion, coercing religious participation, and preferring one denomination to another, and it is in tension with the Free Exercise Clause: government must avoid establishing religion but cannot penalize it.

### The test: from Lemon to history

*Lemon v. Kurtzman* (1971) asked whether a law had a secular purpose, a primary effect that neither advances nor inhibits religion, and no excessive entanglement; later cases added the "endorsement" test. In *Kennedy v. Bremerton School District* (2022), the Court said it had long ago abandoned *Lemon* and its endorsement offshoot, and that the Establishment Clause must be interpreted by "reference to historical practices and understandings." The majority also reasoned that the clause does not compel the government to suppress private religious expression, and that a coach's private prayer was not coercive on the record. The dissent read the record as showing that the coach's prayers at midfield pressured students to participate, and would have given more weight to the school district's concern about endorsement. After *Kennedy*, courts look to history and tradition, coercion, and the Court's precedents.

### Prayer and schools

- *Engel v. Vitale* (1962) and *Abington School District v. Schempp* (1963) invalidated state-sponsored prayer and Bible reading in public schools.
- *Lee v. Weisman* (1992) held that clergy-led prayer at a public school graduation is impermissibly coercive; *Santa Fe Independent School District v. Doe* (2000) invalidated a policy of student-led, student-elected prayer over the stadium loudspeaker at football games.
- *Edwards v. Aguillard* (1987) struck down a law requiring that creationism be taught when evolution is taught, for lack of a secular purpose.
- **Legislative prayer** is permitted because of its long history (*Marsh v. Chambers*, 1983; *Town of Greece v. Galloway*, 2014, upholding a town board's practice of opening meetings with prayers, mostly Christian, where the practice was not coercive).

### Displays

*Van Orden v. Perry* (2005) upheld a Ten Commandments monument on the state capitol grounds, while *McCreary County v. ACLU* (2005) invalidated courthouse displays whose purpose was religious. *Stone v. Graham* (1980) struck down a law posting the Ten Commandments in public school classrooms. *American Legion v. American Humanist Association* (2019) upheld a long-standing cross-shaped war memorial and applied a presumption of constitutionality for older monuments with religious elements.

### Denominational preference

*Larson v. Valente* (1982): a law that grants a denominational preference is subject to strict scrutiny. A Minnesota law that imposed registration requirements only on religious organizations that received less than half their funds from members failed it. *Catholic Charities Bureau v. Wisconsin* (2025) (unanimous) held that a state unemployment-tax exemption scheme that, as applied, distinguished among religious organizations based on theological characteristics violated the First Amendment's bar on denominational preference (applying *Larson*). The Court was evenly divided and issued no opinion in *Oklahoma Statewide Charter School Board v. Drummond* (2025), so that decision sets no precedent.

### Aid to religious institutions

Aid may flow to religious institutions under neutral programs of true private choice: *Zelman v. Simmons-Harris* (2002) upheld an Ohio voucher program where parents chose among religious and secular schools; *Agostini v. Felton* (1997) allowed public-school teachers to provide remedial services on religious school premises. The Free Exercise Clause now prohibits excluding religious schools from neutral programs (*Espinoza*; *Carson*), so the two clauses often point the same way. Direct funding of religious instruction remains contested.

### Religious exemptions

The government may accommodate religion if it does not impose burdens on third parties or prefer one denomination (*Corporation of Presiding Bishop v. Amos*, 1987, upholding the exemption of religious organizations from the Title VII ban on religious discrimination in hiring for secular jobs; *Cutter v. Wilkinson*, 2005, upholding RLUIPA).

### Combined analysis (deliverable method)

For a free-exercise challenge to a neutral, generally applicable law: (1) identify the burden; (2) ask whether the law is neutral (text, operation, hostility); (3) generally applicable (individualized exemptions? comparable secular exceptions per Tandon?); (4) if yes to both, rational basis, so the claim fails unless a statute such as RFRA applies; (5) if no, strict scrutiny; (6) check whether granting the exemption would raise an Establishment Clause problem.

@example
title: A town's prayer practice
problem: A town board opens each meeting with a prayer by a volunteer chaplain from a list that includes any congregation in town. Residents sometimes attend as petitioners; no one is required to participate. A resident sues, claiming an Establishment Clause violation. Analyze.
step: Test: after Kennedy v. Bremerton, the question is whether the practice fits within historical practices and understandings; Marsh and Greece address legislative prayer.
step: History: legislative prayer has a long tradition. The town allows any congregation to offer the prayer and does not edit the content.
step: Coercion: attendees are adults and are not required to join, and the town does not penalize those who do not.
step: Conclusion: the practice is likely valid under Town of Greece. A different result may follow if the town excludes minority faiths or pressures attendees.

@mistakes
- Applying the Lemon test or the endorsement test as the current standard.
- Treating school prayer cases as identical to legislative prayer. Schools involve students and coercion concerns (Lee), while legislative prayer has a historical tradition.
- Assuming the Establishment Clause forbids all aid reaching religious schools. Neutral programs of true private choice are permitted (Zelman).
- Treating a free-exercise exemption as always creating an establishment problem. Accommodations are allowed within limits.

@practice self
prompt: This is the deliverable for this block. A city's zoning code prohibits all gatherings of more than ten people in a home in a residential zone, with no exceptions. A family hosts a weekly prayer meeting of 25 people at home, and the city fines them. The family sues under the Free Exercise Clause. Analyze whether strict scrutiny applies. Then explain how the answer would change if the code allowed the city manager to grant discretionary waivers for secular events such as birthday parties and book clubs.
hint: Work through burden, neutrality, general applicability, and then the comparator analysis from Tandon.
solution: Burden: the fine burdens the family's religious exercise of group worship. Neutrality: the code applies to all gatherings, uses no religious terms and shows no hostility, so it is neutral. General applicability: with no exceptions, it applies to all gatherings, so it is generally applicable. Under Smith, such a law is reviewed under rational basis, and zoning limits on density, parking and noise are rationally related to legitimate interests. The claim likely fails under the Free Exercise Clause, though the family may have a claim under RLUIPA's land-use provisions or a state RFRA if available. If the code allows discretionary waivers for secular events, the law is no longer generally applicable (Fulton: individualized exemptions) and, if the secular events pose comparable risks, it treats comparable secular activity better (Tandon). Strict scrutiny would apply and the city would have to show that denying a waiver for worship is narrowly tailored; given the waivers, it likely cannot. Granting a religious exemption would not violate the Establishment Clause, as accommodation is permitted.

rubric: Applies Smith and notes statutory alternatives such as RLUIPA or a state RFRA.
rubric: Explains how discretionary waivers (Fulton) and comparable secular activity (Tandon) change the standard.
