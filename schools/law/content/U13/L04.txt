@article
Outside economic regulation, the Court has held that the Due Process Clauses protect certain unenumerated liberties. The analysis asks (1) whether the asserted interest is a **fundamental right**, and (2) what level of review applies. If a fundamental right is substantially burdened, the law is generally subject to **strict scrutiny**: it must be narrowly tailored to a compelling government interest. If not, **rational basis** applies.

### Rights recognized

- **Raising and educating children**: *Meyer v. Nebraska* (1923) (a ban on teaching foreign languages to young children); *Pierce v. Society of Sisters* (1925) (a requirement to attend public schools); *Troxel v. Granville* (2000) (a plurality held a visitation statute unconstitutional as applied to a fit parent's decision); *Moore v. City of East Cleveland* (1977) (extended family).
- **Marriage**: *Loving v. Virginia* (1967); *Zablocki v. Redhail* (1978), which gave "critical examination" (commonly read as strict scrutiny) to a law that significantly interfered with marriage while allowing reasonable regulations; *Obergefell v. Hodges* (2015) (same-sex couples may not be denied marriage; grounded in due process and equal protection).
- **Procreation and contraception**: *Skinner v. Oklahoma* (1942); *Griswold v. Connecticut* (1965); *Eisenstadt v. Baird* (1972).
- **Intimate conduct**: *Lawrence v. Texas* (2003), overruling *Bowers v. Hardwick* (1986).
- **Refusing medical treatment**: *Cruzan v. Director, Missouri Department of Health* (1990) assumed a competent person has a liberty interest in refusing life-sustaining treatment but upheld a state's clear-and-convincing evidence requirement for incompetent patients.

### The Glucksberg method

*Washington v. Glucksberg* (1997) rejected a claimed right to physician-assisted suicide. The Court said that (1) it requires a "careful description" of the asserted fundamental liberty interest, and (2) such rights are those "objectively, deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition" and "implicit in the concept of ordered liberty." The Court found a long tradition of prohibiting assisted suicide and upheld the ban under rational basis review. Concurring justices stressed the availability of palliative care. *Obergefell* applied a more flexible approach, reasoning that rights may rest on the Court's understanding of the central meaning of liberty and that history alone does not always define the scope of a right; the dissents argued that the majority substituted the Court's judgment for democratic debate.

### Abortion: from Roe to Dobbs

*Roe v. Wade* (1973) recognized a constitutional right to abortion framed by trimesters. *Planned Parenthood v. Casey* (1992) reaffirmed its "essential holding," replaced the trimester framework with the **undue burden** standard, and recognized state interest in potential life throughout pregnancy. In *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization* (2022), the Court upheld a Mississippi law banning most abortions after 15 weeks and **overruled Roe and Casey**.

**The majority** reasoned that abortion is not "deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition" and not part of ordered liberty under *Glucksberg*, that the right is distinct from other privacy cases because abortion involves what the Court called "potential life," and that *Roe* was egregiously wrong, with reasoning that damaged other areas of law, so stare decisis did not require adhering to it. Abortion regulations are therefore subject to rational basis review: they are valid if rationally related to legitimate state interests. The majority said that nothing in the opinion should be understood to cast doubt on precedents that do not concern abortion.

**A concurrence in the judgment** would have upheld the 15-week law without overruling *Roe* and *Casey*.

**The joint dissent** argued that the Court's decision took away a right women had relied upon for fifty years, that the Fourteenth Amendment's protection of liberty is not limited to rights recognized in 1868, that the majority's reasoning threatens other rights such as contraception and same-sex intimacy, and that stare decisis should have controlled. One concurrence urged the Court to reconsider other substantive due process precedents.

### Applying it

Step 1: State the asserted right at the right level of specificity. Step 2: Is it deeply rooted in history and tradition or already recognized as fundamental in a precedent? Step 3: If yes and substantially burdened, strict scrutiny; if not, rational basis. Step 4: Apply and conclude.

@example
title: A mandatory public school law
problem: A state requires all children to attend public schools and bars private schooling. Parents who want a religious private school sue, claiming a due process right to direct their children's education. Analyze.
step: Right: the liberty of parents to direct the upbringing and education of their children. Pierce v. Society of Sisters (1925) recognized this as protected, and later cases treat parental rights as fundamental.
step: Burden: the law prohibits all private schooling, so the burden is direct and substantial, unlike a regulation of curriculum or accreditation.
step: Level of review: strict scrutiny. The state has a compelling interest in educating children, but a ban on private schools is not narrowly tailored, since reasonable regulation of private schools would serve it.
step: Conclusion: the law is invalid, as in Pierce.

@mistakes
- Describing the asserted right too generally ("liberty") or too narrowly, which can decide the Glucksberg outcome.
- Applying the Casey undue burden test to abortion after Dobbs.
- Saying Dobbs overruled other privacy and family precedents. The majority said it did not, though one concurrence invited reconsideration.
- Applying strict scrutiny to every personal liberty claim without first deciding whether the right is fundamental.
