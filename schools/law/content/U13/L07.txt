@article
The First Amendment says "Congress shall make no law … abridging the freedom of speech," and it applies to the states through the Fourteenth (*Gitlow*). Speech doctrine begins with a classification question: what kind of law is this?

### Content-based laws: strict scrutiny

A law is **content-based** if it applies to speech because of the topic discussed, the idea expressed or its function or purpose. *Reed v. Town of Gilbert* (2015) held that a sign code that treated "ideological," "political" and "temporary directional" signs differently was content-based on its face, and that a benign motive or content-neutral justification does not change that. Content-based laws are presumptively unconstitutional and must satisfy **strict scrutiny**: the government must show the law is narrowly tailored to a compelling interest. *Police Department of Chicago v. Mosley* (1972) struck down an ordinance banning picketing near schools except labor picketing. *Texas v. Johnson* (1989) held that burning a flag in political protest is expressive conduct, that Texas's interest in preserving the flag as a symbol of national unity was related to the expression's message, and that the conviction was invalid; *United States v. Eichman* (1990) reached the same result for the federal statute. The dissents in *Johnson* argued that the flag has unique value and that the ban regulated a mode of expression leaving ample alternatives. *Brown v. Entertainment Merchants Ass'n* (2011) and *United States v. Stevens* (2010) invalidated restrictions on violent video games sold to minors and on depictions of animal cruelty.

**Viewpoint discrimination**, a subset of content discrimination, is regarded as especially egregious (*Rosenberger v. University of Virginia*, 1995; *R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul*, 1992).

*City of Austin v. Reagan National Advertising* (2022) held that a sign ordinance distinguishing on-premises from off-premises signs was content-neutral because it depended on location, not on the message, and so received intermediate scrutiny. The dissent would have found it content-based because a reader must look at the sign's message to know if it is on-premises.

### Content-neutral laws: intermediate scrutiny

A content-neutral law regulating the time, place or manner of speech, or expressive conduct, is valid if narrowly tailored to a significant government interest and leaves open ample alternatives (next lesson). **Symbolic conduct** is evaluated under *United States v. O'Brien* (1968), which upheld a conviction for burning a draft card: the regulation (1) is within the government's constitutional power, (2) furthers an important or substantial interest, (3) the interest is unrelated to suppression of free expression, and (4) the incidental restriction on speech is no greater than essential. If the government's interest is related to the message, as in *Johnson*, strict scrutiny applies. Narrow tailoring here does not require the least restrictive means (*Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 1989).

### Other core doctrines

- **Prior restraints** bear a heavy presumption of invalidity (*Near v. Minnesota*, 1931; *New York Times v. United States*, 1971).
- **Overbreadth**: a law is facially invalid if it prohibits a substantial amount of protected speech compared with its legitimate sweep; **vagueness** is a due process doctrine.
- **Compelled speech**: the government may not force a person to speak or display a message (*West Virginia v. Barnette*, 1943; *Wooley v. Maynard*, 1977). *303 Creative LLC v. Elenis* (2023) held that Colorado could not use its public accommodations law to require a website designer to create expressive websites celebrating same-sex weddings, which the majority treated as compelled speech; the dissent argued that the designer was selling a commercial service and that the law regulated discrimination in sales, not messages.
- **Campaign finance**: *Buckley v. Valeo* (1976) distinguished limits on contributions (reviewed less strictly) from limits on expenditures; *Citizens United v. FEC* (2010) held the government may not ban independent corporate expenditures. In *NRSC v. FEC* (2026), the Court held that limits on political parties' coordinated spending with candidates violate the First Amendment. An earlier decision, *FEC v. Colorado Republican Federal Campaign Committee* (2001), had upheld such limits.
- **Compelled disclosure**: *Americans for Prosperity Foundation v. Bonta* (2021) applied exacting scrutiny to a donor-disclosure requirement. In *First Choice Women's Resource Centers v. Davenport* (2026) the Court held that a group had standing to challenge a state subpoena for donor information.
- **Professional speech**: In *Chiles v. Salazar* (2026), the Court held that a state ban on conversion therapy, as applied to talk therapy, regulates speech based on content and viewpoint, and remanded for the correct scrutiny. The state had argued that it regulated professional conduct that only incidentally involves speech.

@example
title: A town sign code
problem: A town allows temporary signs up to 12 square feet announcing meetings of nonprofit groups, but limits temporary political signs to 6 square feet and temporary directional signs to 4 square feet. A resident challenges the 6-square-foot limit on her election sign. Analyze.
step: Classification: the code draws lines according to what the sign says (political, directional, nonprofit meeting). Under Reed, that is content-based on its face, regardless of the town's benign motives.
step: Standard: strict scrutiny. The town must show a compelling interest and narrow tailoring.
step: Application: aesthetics and traffic safety are legitimate and may be significant, but the town allows larger nonprofit signs, which harm aesthetics and safety equally (underinclusive), so the law is not narrowly tailored.
step: Conclusion: the restriction is invalid. A size cap applying to all temporary signs regardless of message would be content-neutral and reviewed under intermediate scrutiny.

@mistakes
- Treating a law as content-neutral because the legislature had a good motive. Reed says facial content discrimination triggers strict scrutiny anyway.
- Applying O'Brien where the government's interest relates to the message, as with flag burning.
- Requiring the least restrictive means for content-neutral laws. Narrow tailoring there is less demanding.
- Overstating NRSC or Chiles: NRSC concerned party-coordinated spending, and Chiles remanded for the correct scrutiny.

hint: Think of strict scrutiny and then of the four parts of O'Brien.
