@article
The First Amendment forbids laws "prohibiting the free exercise" of religion, and it applies to the states through the Fourteenth (*Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 1940). Belief is absolutely protected; conduct is not (*Reynolds v. United States*, 1879). The question is when a law that burdens religiously motivated conduct must be justified.

### From Sherbert to Smith

*Sherbert v. Verner* (1963) required a compelling interest to justify denying unemployment benefits to a Sabbath observer, and *Wisconsin v. Yoder* (1972) exempted Amish children from compulsory schooling after eighth grade. The Court rarely applied this strict test after that. *Employment Division v. Smith* (1990) held that the Free Exercise Clause does not require an exemption from a **neutral law of general applicability**: members of a church who were fired for using peyote could be denied unemployment benefits because the state's criminal ban on peyote applied to everyone. The majority reasoned that a contrary rule would make each person a law unto himself and that exemptions are a matter for legislatures; the concurring and dissenting justices argued that the clause protects against substantial burdens and that the compelling interest test should continue. A neutral, generally applicable law is reviewed under rational basis; a law that is not neutral or not generally applicable must satisfy **strict scrutiny**.

### Neutrality and general applicability

- **Neutral**: a law is not neutral if its object is to suppress religion, which can be shown by its text, operation, or the official hostility behind it. *Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye v. City of Hialeah* (1993) struck down ordinances prohibiting ritual animal sacrifice that exempted most secular killings of animals; their object was the suppression of a particular religion. *Masterpiece Cakeshop v. Colorado Civil Rights Commission* (2018) found that state officials' comments showed impermissible hostility toward the baker's religion.
- **Generally applicable**: a law is not generally applicable if it permits individualized exemptions, as in *Fulton v. City of Philadelphia* (2021), where the city's contract nondiscrimination requirement could be waived at the commissioner's discretion, so the city could not refuse to contract with a religious foster care agency without meeting strict scrutiny; the Court declined to overrule *Smith*. Under *Tandon v. Newsom* (2021) and *Roman Catholic Diocese v. Cuomo* (2020), a law also is not generally applicable whenever it treats **any comparable secular activity more favorably** than religious exercise; comparability depends on the risks the government's interest identifies, not on the reason for the activity.
- **Hybrid claims** (free exercise plus another right) were suggested in *Smith* and are not reliably applied.

*Kennedy v. Bremerton School District* (2022) held that a public school district's restriction on a coach's brief personal postgame prayer was neither neutral nor generally applicable and failed strict scrutiny. *Mahmoud v. Taylor* (2025) considered a claim by parents to opt their children out of instruction using storybooks that conflicted with their religious beliefs; the Court held, at the preliminary-injunction stage, that the board's refusal to allow opt-outs burdened the parents' religious exercise and applied strict scrutiny, relying on *Yoder*. The dissent argued that exposure to ideas in a public classroom does not coerce religious practice and that opt-outs would be administratively unworkable. *Groff v. DeJoy* (2023), a statutory case, held that "undue hardship" under Title VII means substantial increased costs in relation to the conduct of the employer's business.

### Statutes protecting religion

- **RFRA** (1993; 42 U.S.C. § 2000bb) restores the compelling interest test for federal government actions that substantially burden religion; the government must show a compelling interest pursued by the least restrictive means (*Gonzales v. O Centro*, 2006; *Burwell v. Hobby Lobby*, 2014). *City of Boerne v. Flores* (1997) held RFRA cannot be applied to the states; many states have their own RFRAs.
- **RLUIPA** (2000) applies a similar test to land-use regulation and to institutionalized persons (*Holt v. Hobbs*, 2015).

### Funding and church autonomy

*Trinity Lutheran v. Comer* (2017), *Espinoza v. Montana* (2020) and *Carson v. Makin* (2022) held that a state may not exclude religious institutions from an otherwise available public benefit program because of their religious status or use; *Locke v. Davey* (2004) allowed a state to refuse scholarships for devotional theology degrees. The **ministerial exception**, rooted in both religion clauses, bars employment discrimination claims by ministers against their religious employers (*Hosanna-Tabor v. EEOC*, 2012); *Our Lady of Guadalupe School v. Morrissey-Berru* (2020) applies it to teachers who perform vital religious functions.

### Applying it

Identify the burden. Is the law neutral? Is it generally applicable (individualized exemptions; comparable secular exceptions)? If both, rational basis. If not, strict scrutiny. Check for RFRA or a state RFRA.

@example
title: A city ban on ritual animal sacrifice
problem: A city bans "sacrificing" animals, a term defined to cover religious rituals, but exempts hunting, pest control, fishing and slaughter for food. A church that practices animal sacrifice sues. Analyze.
step: Burden: the ordinance prohibits an act that is part of the church's religious exercise.
step: Neutrality and general applicability: the ordinance's terms and operation show that it targets religious killings while exempting secular ones that cause comparable harm. It is neither neutral nor generally applicable (Lukumi).
step: Standard: strict scrutiny. The city's interests in animal welfare and public health are weighty, but the exemptions show that the law is underinclusive, so it is not narrowly tailored.
step: Conclusion: the ordinance is invalid under the Free Exercise Clause.

@mistakes
- Applying strict scrutiny to every burden on religion. After Smith, a neutral, generally applicable law gets rational basis review.
- Ignoring comparable secular exceptions. Tandon treats a single comparable exemption as defeating general applicability.
- Applying RFRA to state laws. Boerne limited it to the federal government.
- Saying Fulton overruled Smith. The Court declined to reach that question.
