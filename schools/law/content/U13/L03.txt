@article
**Substantive due process** is the doctrine that the Due Process Clauses protect some liberties from government interference no matter how fair the procedures are. It has two chapters: economic liberty, which the Court gave up on policing closely, and personal liberty (next lesson), which it still protects in limited ways.

### The Lochner era

In *Lochner v. New York* (1905), the Court struck down a law limiting bakers' working hours as an unreasonable interference with "liberty of contract," concluding the law was labor regulation, not a genuine health measure. Dissenting opinions argued the Constitution does not enact a particular economic theory and that the law was a rational health regulation. Over the next three decades the Court struck many wage, hour and price laws (for example, *Adkins v. Children's Hospital*, 1923, minimum wages for women), though it also upheld some (*Muller v. Oregon*, 1908, hours for women).

### The retreat

After the Great Depression and political conflict over the New Deal, the Court changed course. *Nebbia v. New York* (1934) upheld price regulation of milk, treating the legislature's judgment about economic policy as reasonable. *West Coast Hotel Co. v. Parrish* (1937) upheld a state minimum wage law and overruled *Adkins*, holding that liberty of contract is not absolute and that regulation reasonably related to the community's welfare is due process.

*United States v. Carolene Products Co.* (1938) upheld a federal ban on shipping filled milk and, in **footnote four**, suggested circumstances in which more exacting judicial scrutiny might be warranted: (1) legislation that appears on its face to violate a specific prohibition of the Constitution, such as one in the first ten amendments; (2) legislation that restricts the political processes ordinarily relied upon to repeal undesirable laws; and (3) statutes directed at particular religious, national or racial minorities, or prejudice against "discrete and insular minorities." Footnote four became a bridge to heightened scrutiny under the First and Fourteenth Amendments.

### The modern rule

Economic and social regulation that does not burden a fundamental right or use a suspect classification gets **rational basis review**: the law is valid if it is rationally related to a legitimate government interest. *Williamson v. Lee Optical* (1955) upheld an Oklahoma law that restricted who could fit and duplicate lenses, though it might be wasteful, because the legislature could have thought the rule would address a perceived problem; reform "may take one step at a time." *Ferguson v. Skrupa* (1963) said courts do not sit as a superlegislature to weigh the wisdom of economic laws. The challenger bears the burden of negating every conceivable basis. The Court did not at that point say that economic liberty receives no protection at all; rather, the standard became very deferential and laws rarely lose.

*Lochner* is now treated as a decision that went wrong and is not followed.

### Contracts Clause

Article I, § 10 says "No State shall … pass any … Law impairing the Obligation of Contracts." The modern test, from *Home Building & Loan Ass'n v. Blaisdell* (1934) and *Energy Reserves Group v. Kansas Power & Light* (1983), asks: (1) does the law substantially impair a contractual relationship? If so, (2) is there a significant and legitimate public purpose, and (3) are the adjustments reasonable and appropriate to that purpose? When the state is impairing its own contracts, as in *United States Trust Co. v. New Jersey* (1977), the Court gives less deference: the impairment must be reasonable and necessary to serve an important public purpose.

### Punitive damages

The Due Process Clause limits grossly excessive punitive awards. *BMW of North America v. Gore* (1996) set three guideposts: the reprehensibility of the defendant's conduct, the ratio between punitive damages and the actual harm, and comparable civil or criminal penalties. *State Farm v. Campbell* (2003) said few awards exceeding a single-digit ratio will satisfy due process, and that a 4-to-1 ratio may be close to the line, though ratios are not rigid.

@example
title: A state law reorganizing barbers' and stylists' licensing
problem: A state licenses hair stylists but exempts those who work only at licensed nursing homes. Stylists outside nursing homes argue the exemption lacks any justification and violates due process. What standard applies and what result is likely?
step: No fundamental right or suspect class is involved. Economic liberty to practice a trade gets rational basis review under Lee Optical and Ferguson v. Skrupa.
step: Rule: the law is valid if it is rationally related to a legitimate interest; courts may consider any conceivable justification, and the challengers must negate every one.
step: Application: the legislature could have believed nursing home residents have limited mobility and are served by a closely supervised setting, or that the exemption eases access to grooming services. Even if the line is imperfect, reform may proceed one step at a time.
step: Conclusion: the exemption is likely upheld.

@mistakes
- Applying strict scrutiny to ordinary economic regulation because the plaintiff invokes "liberty of contract."
- Treating Lochner as good law or as the current test.
- Using the ordinary Contracts Clause test when the state is impairing its own financial obligations, where review is less deferential.
- Treating a 4-to-1 punitive ratio as a constitutional limit rather than as a guidepost.
