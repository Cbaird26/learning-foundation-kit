@article
Suppose a city seizes your property for a public project and pays nothing. The Fifth Amendment's Takings Clause says that cannot happen, yet the Amendment is addressed, in its terms, to the federal government. How does it reach a city? **Incorporation** is the answer: the doctrine that the Fourteenth Amendment makes most of the Bill of Rights enforceable against state and local governments.

### The starting point and the detour

In *Barron v. Baltimore* (1833) the Court held that the Bill of Rights restrains only the federal government. After the Civil War the Fourteenth Amendment (1868) told the states that no state may "abridge the privileges or immunities of citizens of the United States," nor deprive any person of life, liberty or property without due process of law, nor deny equal protection. But in the *Slaughter-House Cases* (1873) the Court read the Privileges or Immunities Clause so narrowly that it did no incorporating work, and it has remained largely dormant since. That left the Due Process Clause as the vehicle.

### Selective incorporation

Two rival theories were debated in the mid-twentieth century. **Total incorporation** (a few justices) said the Fourteenth Amendment applied the whole Bill of Rights to the states. **Fundamental fairness** said states violated due process only when they offended basic notions of justice, whether or not a Bill of Rights guarantee was involved. The Court adopted a middle path, **selective incorporation**: it asks, guarantee by guarantee, whether the right is protected by the Due Process Clause.

The test has been phrased two ways. *Palko v. Connecticut* (1937) asked whether the right is "implicit in the concept of ordered liberty." *Duncan v. Louisiana* (1968), incorporating the Sixth Amendment jury right in serious criminal cases, asked whether the right is "fundamental to the American scheme of justice." *McDonald v. City of Chicago* (2010) asked whether the right is "deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition" and fundamental to our scheme of ordered liberty. Courts often use these formulations together.

**Jot-for-jot.** Once a guarantee is incorporated, the Court applies the same content to the states as to the federal government. There is no watered-down state version. *Malloy v. Hogan* (1964) (self-incrimination) and *Ramos v. Louisiana* (2020) (jury unanimity) are typical statements of this rule.

### What has been incorporated

Nearly everything: the First Amendment (speech in *Gitlow* (1925) assumed, and later firmly established; free exercise in *Cantwell v. Connecticut* (1940); establishment in *Everson v. Board of Education* (1947)), the Fourth Amendment and exclusionary rule (*Mapp v. Ohio*, 1961), most of the Fifth and Sixth Amendments, the Eighth Amendment's cruel and unusual punishments ban, the Second Amendment (*McDonald*, 2010), and the Excessive Fines Clause (*Timbs v. Indiana*, 2019). In *Ramos* the Court held that the Sixth Amendment requires a unanimous verdict to convict of a serious offense and that this applies to the states, overruling *Apodaca v. Oregon* (1972). On federal collateral review, *Edwards v. Vannoy* (2021) held that *Ramos* does not apply retroactively.

In *McDonald*, a plurality relied on the Due Process Clause; a separate concurrence would have used the Privileges or Immunities Clause. The dissenters argued that the right to keep and bear arms for self-defense was not part of the liberty protected by due process.

### What has not been incorporated

- The Fifth Amendment's **grand jury indictment** requirement (*Hurtado v. California*, 1884), so states may prosecute by information.
- The Seventh Amendment's **civil jury** guarantee (the Court has long held it inapplicable to state courts, e.g., *Minneapolis & St. Louis R.R. v. Bombolis*, 1916).
- The Third Amendment, which the Supreme Court has never squarely addressed (at least one federal circuit has treated it as incorporated).

### Applying it

For an IRAC answer, (1) identify the guarantee and whether the challenged actor is federal or state; (2) if state, say whether the Court has incorporated it; (3) if so, apply the federal doctrine as is; (4) if the Court has not decided, apply the selective incorporation test.

@example
title: A state prosecution without a grand jury or a unanimous jury
problem: A state charges Dana with a serious felony by prosecutor's information, with no grand jury. A jury votes 11 to 1 to convict, and state law permits that. Dana argues the grand jury and unanimity requirements of the Fifth and Sixth Amendments apply to the state. What result on each point?
step: Issue: whether each federal guarantee is incorporated against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment Due Process Clause. The state, not Congress, is the actor, so Barron's rule is not the end of the inquiry.
step: Grand jury: Hurtado v. California held the Fifth Amendment's indictment requirement does not bind the states, and the Court has not incorporated it. The prosecution by information is valid.
step: Unanimity: Ramos v. Louisiana held that the Sixth Amendment requires a unanimous verdict in serious criminal cases and applies to the states, overruling Apodaca. An 11 to 1 conviction is invalid on direct review.
step: Conclusion: Dana loses on the grand jury claim and wins on the unanimity claim. The conviction must be set aside and a new trial is available.

@mistakes
- Saying the Bill of Rights applies to the states "directly." After Barron it applies only through the Fourteenth Amendment.
- Assuming selective incorporation means states get a weaker version of a right. Incorporated rights apply jot-for-jot.
- Treating the Privileges or Immunities Clause as the working vehicle. The Court incorporates through the Due Process Clause.
- Forgetting that the grand jury and civil jury guarantees are the classic non-incorporated rights.
