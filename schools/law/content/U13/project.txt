material: **Riverton City Ordinance 24-17 (invented for this course).**

**Section 1. Assemblies in Harbor Park.** No assembly of more than 30 persons may occur in Harbor Park, the city's central park, without a permit. An application must be filed at least 72 hours before the event. The Parks Director may deny a permit if the assembly "would, in the Director's judgment, be likely to provoke a hostile reaction from members of the public" or if the park is already reserved. The permit fee is \$150 for all applicants, with no waiver.

**Section 2. Signs.** Temporary signs on private property may not exceed 8 square feet, except that (a) temporary signs announcing a garage sale or a community event may be up to 20 square feet, and (b) temporary signs concerning an election may be displayed for 30 days before the election only.

**Section 3. Employment.** All city employees, including probationary employees, may be dismissed by the City Manager "at any time and for any reason," except that permanent employees who have completed one year of service may be dismissed "only for cause." Under the ordinance, a permanent employee dismissed for cause receives a written notice on the day of the dismissal, and may request a hearing before the Personnel Board to be held within 90 days, with back pay if the dismissal is reversed. Dismissed employees are listed by name and reason on the city's public website.

**Section 4. Street vending.** Vending carts are prohibited on Harbor Street, except for carts operated by (a) persons who have operated on the street for at least five years, and (b) persons who are United States citizens residing in Riverton.

**Section 5. Employee speech.** No city employee may make a public statement criticizing a city policy or official "on any matter," whether or not the statement is made on duty.

**Background facts.** Mara organizes a rally of 200 people in Harbor Park to protest a city policy and is denied a permit under Section 1 because the Director predicted counter-protesters. Ben displays a 12-square-foot sign supporting a candidate 45 days before the election and is cited. Carl, a permanent employee of nine years in the Public Works Department, is dismissed for alleged theft, receives notice at 5 p.m. on the day of his dismissal and his name and reason appear on the website. Dina, a probationary employee in the same department, is dismissed after she speaks at a public council meeting about unsafe equipment. Omar, a lawful permanent resident (a green-card holder), is denied a vending permit under Section 4, while a citizen who moved to Riverton last month receives one.

@review
### Week by week

1. **Incorporation.** The Bill of Rights binds the states selectively through the Fourteenth Amendment Due Process Clause, jot-for-jot. Grand jury and civil jury guarantees are not incorporated; McDonald, Timbs and Ramos are.
2. **Procedural due process.** Is there property (entitlement from state law) or liberty (stigma plus)? Then Mathews balancing. Loudermill: pretermination notice, evidence and a chance to respond, then a prompt post-termination hearing.
3. **Economic substantive due process.** Lochner is gone; rational basis review applies. Carolene Products footnote four, the Contracts Clause and punitive damages guideposts.
4. **Fundamental rights.** Glucksberg: careful description and deeply rooted in history and tradition. Strict scrutiny for fundamental rights; Dobbs applies rational basis to abortion.
5. **Rational basis.** Any conceivable rational basis; under- and overinclusive lines allowed; animus (Moreno, Cleburne, Romer) and class-of-one claims.
6. **Suspect and quasi-suspect classes.** Strict scrutiny for race, national origin and state alienage; intermediate scrutiny for sex and nonmarital birth. Discriminatory purpose is required (Davis, Arlington Heights). SFFA, Skrmetti, Hecox and Callais.
7. **Content-based and content-neutral laws.** Reed, O'Brien, prior restraint, overbreadth, compelled speech, NRSC and Chiles.
8. **Forums and TPM.** Forum type, the Ward test, unbridled discretion, fees, government speech.
9. **Unprotected speech.** Brandenburg, Counterman, Sullivan and Gertz, Miller, Central Hudson, Paxton.
10. **Employees and students.** Garcetti, Pickering, Tinker line, Mahanoy.
11. **Free exercise.** Smith, Lukumi, Fulton, Tandon, RFRA and the ministerial exception.
12. **Establishment.** History and tradition, not Lemon; Greece, Santa Fe, Zelman, Larson.

### Self-check

- Can I state each test in words, and give a case for it?
- Can I say what each major case held and what the dissent argued, without taking a side?
- For any fact pattern, can I name the actor, the right, the classification, the standard and the best argument for each side?

### Common errors

- Skipping the threshold question (state action, protected interest, forum or classification).
- Using the wrong tier, for example intermediate scrutiny for alienage or strict scrutiny for age.
- Treating disparate impact as sufficient for equal protection.
- Teaching the old rule as current, such as Lemon, the Casey undue burden test or the objective-only true threat test.
- Requiring the least restrictive means for content-neutral rules.

@exam mc
prompt: A city fires Lena, a permanent employee who can be removed only for cause, two days after she tells a local newspaper, on her own time and not as part of her duties, that the city is mishandling funds. The city gave her no notice of charges or chance to respond before firing her. A hearing is available a month later. Which statement is most accurate?
choice*: She may have two independent claims: a First Amendment retaliation claim, assessed with Pickering balancing because she spoke as a citizen on a matter of public concern, and a procedural due process claim, because Loudermill requires pretermination notice and a chance to respond.
choice: She has only a First Amendment claim, because a prompt post-termination hearing satisfies due process.
choice: She has only a procedural due process claim, because Garcetti bars all speech claims by public employees.
choice: She has no claim, because a tenured employee who speaks publicly waives her property interest.
hint: Consider the First Amendment and due process questions separately.
solution: The speech claim turns on Garcetti, Connick and Pickering; speaking to a newspaper on her own time about funds is citizen speech on a public concern. The due process claim turns on her for-cause property interest and Loudermill, and the later hearing does not substitute for pretermination notice and a chance to respond.

choice*: A grant program that awards college scholarships only to applicants of one racial group
choice*: A rule that bans yard signs about candidates but permits signs about garage sales
choice*: A ban on state jobs for lawfully present noncitizens that covers clerical positions
choice: A mandatory retirement age of 70 for state judges

hint: Match each law to its classification: race, content, alienage, age, sex.
solution: Race, content-based speech rules and state alienage classifications generally get strict scrutiny. Age gets rational basis. A sex classification gets intermediate scrutiny, not strict scrutiny.

@exam self
prompt: A city ordinance provides: Section 1, no assembly of more than 30 persons may occur in the central park without a permit, applied for 72 hours ahead, which the Parks Director may deny if the assembly "would, in the Director's judgment, be likely to provoke a hostile reaction from members of the public," with a flat \$150 fee and no waiver; Section 2, temporary signs on private property may not exceed 8 square feet, except that garage-sale and community-event signs may be 20 square feet and election signs may be displayed only for 30 days before the election; Section 4, vending carts are prohibited on Harbor Street except carts run by (a) persons who have operated there at least five years and (b) United States citizens residing in the city. Mara was denied a permit for a 200-person protest rally because the Director predicted counter-protesters. Ben was cited for displaying a 12-square-foot candidate sign 45 days before the election. Omar, a lawful permanent resident (a green-card holder), was denied a vending permit while a citizen who moved to the city last month received one. Dina, a probationary employee who may be dismissed at will, was dismissed after speaking at a public council meeting about unsafe equipment in her department. Choose any two of the following claims and, for each, write a short IRAC analysis stating the standard and the best arguments for each side: Mara's challenge to Section 1; Ben's challenge to Section 2; Omar's challenge to Section 4; Dina's First Amendment claim.
hint: For each, identify the classification or forum before choosing the standard.
solution: Mara: the park is a traditional public forum; Section 1's permit rule is content-neutral in form, but its "hostile reaction" criterion gives the Director standardless discretion and permits a heckler's veto (Forsyth; Shuttlesworth), and the \$150 fee with no waiver adds a further concern; the rule is likely invalid as applied and on its face. The city argues that crowd safety is significant and a content-neutral permit rule is permissible under Thomas v. Chicago Park District; this argument works for the permit, not for the hostile-reaction criterion. Ben: Section 2 distinguishes signs by subject (garage sale, community event, election), so it is content-based under Reed and subject to strict scrutiny; the city's aesthetic and safety interests are weakened by larger exempt signs, so the rule is likely invalid. The city argues that the rules are content-neutral time limits that serve ordinary aesthetic goals, which Reed rejects. Omar: Section 4 imposes a state alienage classification, subject to strict scrutiny (Graham v. Richardson) unless it falls within the political-function exception, which does not cover street vending; the city's argument is that a residency preference is a rational local economic rule, but the citizenship criterion is the problem, so it is likely invalid. The five-year grandfather clause alone would get rational basis (Dukes). Dina: she is a probationary employee, but First Amendment protection does not depend on a property interest. Speaking at a public council meeting about unsafe equipment is citizen speech on a matter of public concern, unless it was part of her job duties (Garcetti). The city must show that its interest in efficient service outweighs the speech under Pickering. She likely has a claim; the city argues that she spoke as part of her safety duties or that the dismissal would have occurred anyway.
