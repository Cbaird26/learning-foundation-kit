@article
Speech on government property is not equally protected everywhere. The Court classifies the property as a type of **forum** and applies different tests.

### The forums

| Forum | Examples | Content-based restriction | Content-neutral restriction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Traditional public forum | streets, sidewalks, parks (*Hague v. CIO*, 1939) | strict scrutiny | time, place and manner test |
| Designated public forum | property opened for general expressive use, such as a municipal theater | strict scrutiny | time, place and manner test |
| Limited public forum | property opened for certain groups or topics, such as a school's meeting rooms | reasonable and viewpoint-neutral | same |
| Nonpublic forum | airport terminal, military base, jail | reasonable and viewpoint-neutral | same |

Viewpoint discrimination is forbidden in every forum (*Rosenberger*; *Good News Club v. Milford Central School*, 2001). *International Society for Krishna Consciousness v. Lee* (1992) held that an airport terminal is a nonpublic forum and upheld a ban on soliciting money there as reasonable. *Minnesota Voters Alliance v. Mansky* (2018) held that a ban on political apparel at polling places, a nonpublic forum, was unreasonable as applied because its standards were unclear and gave officials too much discretion.

### Time, place and manner (TPM) rules

In a public forum, the government may impose a content-neutral regulation that (1) is **narrowly tailored** to serve a **significant** government interest and (2) leaves open **ample alternative channels** (*Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 1989). The rule need not be the least restrictive means, but it may not burden substantially more speech than needed. *McCullen v. Coakley* (2014) held that a 35-foot buffer zone around abortion clinic entrances was content-neutral but not narrowly tailored, because the state had not tried less burdensome measures; *Hill v. Colorado* (2000) had upheld an eight-foot "floating" buffer, though *McCullen* did not overrule it. *Frisby v. Schultz* (1988) upheld a ban on picketing targeted at a specific residence, and *City of Ladue v. Gilleo* (1994) invalidated a ban on most residential signs because it left too few alternatives.

### Permit systems

- **Unbridled discretion**: a licensing rule that gives officials standardless discretion over who may speak is invalid (*Shuttlesworth v. Birmingham*, 1969). Permit criteria must be clear and objective.
- **Fees**: a modest fee to cover administrative costs is allowed (*Cox v. New Hampshire*, 1941), but a fee based on the content of the speech or the expected hostile reaction of listeners is not (*Forsyth County v. Nationalist Movement*, 1992), which rejected the "heckler's veto."
- **Procedural safeguards**: a content-based censorship scheme requires prompt decisions and prompt judicial review (*Freedman v. Maryland*, 1965), but *Thomas v. Chicago Park District* (2002) held that a content-neutral park permit scheme does not need all *Freedman* safeguards, though officials' discretion must be limited by objective standards and judicial review must be available.
- **Spontaneous speech**: courts are concerned about advance-notice rules applied to speech responding to news; many courts require an exception or a short notice period.

### Government speech

When the government speaks for itself, the Free Speech Clause does not apply (*Pleasant Grove City v. Summum*, 2009, monuments in a park; *Walker v. Texas Division, Sons of Confederate Veterans*, 2015, specialty license plates). The Court looks at history, whether the public would perceive the message as the government's, and how much control the government exercises. *Shurtleff v. City of Boston* (2022) held that Boston's flag-raising program was not government speech, because the city had never rejected a request among many, and its refusal to raise a Christian flag therefore abridged the applicant's free-speech rights as viewpoint discrimination. *Matal v. Tam* (2017) and *Iancu v. Brunetti* (2019) held that bans on registering disparaging and scandalous trademarks are viewpoint-based.

### Applying it

Step 1: Identify the property and forum. Step 2: Content-based or neutral? Step 3: Apply the test for that forum. Step 4: For permits, test discretion, fee, timing, alternatives.

@example
title: A park permit that depends on an official's judgment
problem: A city requires a permit for any rally in a downtown park. The Parks Director may grant a permit if she "finds the rally to be in the community's interest." A group is denied a permit to protest city policy. Analyze.
step: Forum: a park is a traditional public forum, so a content-neutral rule must satisfy TPM, and content-based denial must meet strict scrutiny.
step: The permit requirement is acceptable in principle (crowd management is significant), but the criterion "community's interest" gives the Director unbridled discretion over who speaks and invites viewpoint discrimination (Shuttlesworth, Forsyth).
step: Standards must be clear and objective, such as capacity, conflicting reservations, and safety logistics.
step: Conclusion: the permit scheme is facially invalid, and the group may challenge the scheme without first applying for a permit or being denied.

@mistakes
- Applying strict scrutiny to every restriction on government property. Content-neutral rules in public forums receive TPM review, and nonpublic forums only reasonableness.
- Requiring the least restrictive means for a TPM rule.
- Allowing a permit fee set by the expected hostility of the audience.
- Treating a program as government speech without looking at history, public perception and control.

@practice self
prompt: This is the deliverable for this block. A city code provides: "No assembly of more than 50 persons may occur in Lakeside Park without a permit. Applications must be filed at least 48 hours in advance. The Parks Director must grant a permit unless (a) another event is already scheduled for that time and place, (b) the number expected exceeds the park's posted capacity of 500, or (c) the applicant failed to pay the flat \$25 fee, which is waived for those who cannot afford it. Denials are reviewable promptly in court." Organizers want to hold a rally of 200 people on short notice in response to a news event. Analyze the rule under the First Amendment.
hint: Identify the forum, whether the rule is content-neutral, and then apply the three TPM elements and the permit-system rules.
solution: Forum: a park is a traditional public forum. Content: the rule applies to all assemblies regardless of subject, so it is content-neutral and receives TPM review. Interest and tailoring: crowd management, safety and avoiding conflicts among users are significant interests; a permit requirement for assemblies over 50 persons is narrowly tailored, since smaller groups are exempt, and it need not be the least restrictive means (Ward; Thomas v. Chicago Park District). Discretion: the criteria are objective (prior booking, capacity, fee), so there is no unbridled discretion (Shuttlesworth, Forsyth), the fee is flat and modest with an indigence waiver (Cox), and prompt judicial review exists. Alternatives: other parks and sidewalks remain available. The weak point is the 48-hour advance-notice rule as applied to a rally responding to breaking news; courts have been concerned that such rules suppress timely speech, and the city should be required to allow a shorter notice period for spontaneous events or the rule may be invalid as applied. Conclusion: the permit scheme is likely facially valid, but the 48-hour rule is vulnerable as applied to a spontaneous rally.
rubric: Classifies the park as a traditional public forum and the rule as content-neutral.
rubric: Applies narrow tailoring, significant interest and ample alternatives.
rubric: Analyzes discretion, fee and review under the permit-system cases.
rubric: Identifies the advance-notice problem for spontaneous speech and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

A counselor who wants to talk quietly with patients challenges the law. Under McCullen v. Coakley (2014), what is the likely result?
choice*: The law is content-neutral but not narrowly tailored, because less burdensome measures were available, so it is invalid.
choice: The law is valid, because it is content-neutral and a buffer zone need not be tailored at all.
choice: The law gets strict scrutiny because it applies only at abortion clinics, and fails.
choice: The law is valid, because a sidewalk near a clinic is a nonpublic forum.
hint: McCullen v. Coakley.
solution: McCullen found the buffer zone content-neutral but not narrowly tailored because the state had not shown that less restrictive measures would fail. Public sidewalks are traditional public forums.
