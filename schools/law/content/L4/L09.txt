@article
At common law, **first in time is first in right**: if an owner conveys the same land twice, the first grantee wins and the second takes nothing. That rule is unfair to a buyer who could not have known about an unrecorded earlier deed, so every state has a **recording act**. The act lets a later buyer who meets its requirements defeat an earlier **unrecorded** conveyance. An unrecorded deed remains **valid between the parties**; recording is a protection against third parties.

### Three kinds of statute

| Type | Who wins between a prior unrecorded grantee (A) and a later grantee (B)? |
|---|---|
| **Race** | The grantee who **records first** wins, even if that grantee knew of the earlier deed |
| **Notice** | B wins if B **took without notice** of A's interest, whether or not B records first |
| **Race-notice** | B wins only if B took **without notice and recorded before** A |

Most states use a notice or race-notice statute; pure race statutes are rare. A typical race-notice or notice statute reads, in substance, that an unrecorded conveyance is void against a subsequent **bona fide purchaser for value without notice**.

### The bona fide purchaser

To be protected, the later taker must be a **purchaser** who gives **value** and takes **without notice**.

- **Value.** A buyer qualifies, and so does a mortgage lender who lends money. A **donee** or heir does not, because she gave no value (though she can take under the shelter rule below).
- **Notice** comes in three kinds. **Actual notice**: the buyer knows of the earlier interest. **Record (constructive) notice**: the earlier interest is recorded in the **chain of title**, which the buyer is charged with searching. **Inquiry notice**: facts that would lead a reasonably prudent buyer to investigate, such as another person's **possession** of the land or a reference in a recorded document to an unrecorded interest. Buyers are charged with what an inquiry would have revealed.

### The chain of title

Most counties use **grantor-grantee indices**: a searcher starts with the current owner's name in the grantee index and works back to the root of title, then looks in the grantor index under each owner's name for conveyances during that owner's period of ownership. A deed recorded **outside** the chain, such as a deed from A recorded before A received title from O (a **wild deed**), gives no constructive notice to a later buyer who searches properly. A few counties use **tract indices** organized by parcel, and a few states use the **Torrens** system, which registers title with the state.

### The shelter rule

A person who takes from a bona fide purchaser steps into the purchaser's shoes, even with actual notice of the earlier deed, and even if she is a donee. The rule protects the marketability of the BFP's title: otherwise a BFP could not sell. A person cannot use the rule to repurchase land after knowingly holding it earlier.

### Estoppel by deed and after-acquired title

A grantor who conveys land by a warranty deed before owning it, and later acquires title, holds the title for the grantee: it passes to the grantee automatically under **estoppel by deed**.

### Marketable record title acts and title insurance

A **marketable record title act** extinguishes old interests (often more than 30 or 40 years old) that are not re-recorded within the statutory period, simplifying searches. **Title insurance** protects the owner against covered defects that exist at the date of the policy and were not excepted, including hidden defects such as forged deeds or unknown heirs, and the insurer must defend covered claims. Policies exclude matters of record that are listed as exceptions, matters the insured knew, and zoning and other governmental regulations; a lender's policy is separate from an owner's policy. Insurance covers defects, not later events, and the policy pays up to its stated amount.

### Policy

The acts reward diligence in recording, give buyers confidence in the records, and encourage the public recording of every interest. The notice statute protects the innocent buyer; the race-notice statute adds an incentive to record promptly.

### Applying the doctrine

Write down the order of events (who received a deed from whom, when, for value, and when each recorded), ask whether the later taker is a BFP, and then apply the statute type. If the later taker is not a BFP, the prior grantee wins under any statute. Check the shelter rule and the chain of title before you conclude.

@example
title: One chain, three statutes
problem: In January, O conveys land to A for value. A does not record. In February, O conveys the same land to B for value. B has no notice of A's deed. In March, A records. In April, B records. Who wins under a race statute, a notice statute and a race-notice statute?
step: Race: the first to record wins. A recorded in March, before B in April, so A wins.
step: Notice: B wins if she took without notice. When B bought in February, A had not recorded and B knew nothing, so B is a BFP and wins, whatever happened later.
step: Race-notice: B must also record first. A recorded first, so B loses and A wins.

@example
title: Inquiry notice
problem: A has lived openly on a farm for three years under an unrecorded deed from O. O then sells the farm to B, who never visits the property. B pays full value. Is B protected under a notice statute?
step: B had no actual notice and the deed is unrecorded, so there is no record notice.
step: A's open possession would have prompted a reasonable buyer to inspect and ask. B is charged with what a reasonable inquiry would have revealed.
step: B had inquiry notice and is not a BFP without notice, so A prevails.

@mistakes
- Saying an unrecorded deed is void; it is valid between the parties and against anyone who is not a BFP.
- Applying the notice rule to protect a donee, who gave no value.
- Ignoring inquiry notice, such as open possession by someone else.
- Reading race-notice as requiring only that the buyer record first; the buyer also must have taken without notice.

prompt: In a race-notice jurisdiction, O conveys land to Cal for value on June 1, and Cal does not record. On July 1, O conveys it to Dee for value, and Dee has no notice. Cal records on August 1. Dee records on September 1. Who owns the land?
choice*: Cal, because Dee did not record before Cal, which race-notice requires of a later purchaser.
choice: Dee, because she paid value without notice when she bought.
choice: Dee, because her deed was the later deed and the later deed controls.
choice: Cal, because Dee had constructive notice from her own recording.
hint: What two things must the later buyer show under a race-notice statute?
hint: She must have no notice and also record first.
solution: Race-notice protects a later purchaser only if she paid value without notice and recorded before the earlier grantee. Dee failed the second requirement because Cal recorded first, so the common-law first-in-time rule gives Cal the land.

prompt: In a notice jurisdiction, O conveys land to Eve for value, and Eve does not record. O then conveys it to Fay, who pays value and has no notice, and Fay records. Fay then gives the land as a gift to Gus, who knows of Eve's earlier deed. Who owns the land?
choice*: Gus, because he takes Fay's rights under the shelter rule despite his notice.

choice: Eve, because Gus gave no value and a donee is never protected.
choice: Fay, because a gift to Gus is ineffective against an earlier deed.
hint: What rights did Fay hold when she took?
hint: A taker from a bona fide purchaser holds the purchaser's rights, even a donee with notice.
solution: Fay was a BFP, so she held title free of Eve's claim. Gus stands in her shoes under the shelter rule, even though he is a donee with actual notice, so Gus owns the land and Eve loses.

@quiz mc
prompt: In a race-notice jurisdiction, Owner O conveys land to Al, who does not record. Al conveys to Bo, who records, but because Al's deed from O is unrecorded, Bo's deed is indexed only under Al's name. O later conveys the same land to Cy, who pays value, searches the grantor index under O's name, finds no conveyance, and records at once. Who owns the land?
choice*: Cy, because Bo's deed is a wild deed outside Cy's chain of title that gave no constructive notice.
choice: Bo, because he recorded his deed.
choice: Bo, because Al's deed was earlier in time than Cy's.
choice: Cy, but only if Bo never took possession.
hint: Does a search under O's name reveal Bo's deed?
hint: A deed recorded outside the chain does not give record notice to a proper searcher.
solution: A deed recorded before the grantor had record title is not in the chain, so Cy had no constructive notice. Cy paid value without notice and recorded first, so she wins under race-notice. Recording alone does not protect Bo, and his possession is irrelevant because the facts do not state that he took possession.

prompt: Hal has lived openly on a farm for seven years under an unrecorded deed from Owner. Owner then sells the farm to Ira, who pays full value, never visits the property, and finds nothing in the record. The jurisdiction has a notice statute. Who owns the farm?
choice*: Hal, because Ira is charged with inquiry notice from Hal's open possession.
choice: Ira, because she had no actual or record notice and paid value.
choice: Ira, because an unrecorded deed is void against later purchasers.

hint: What would a reasonable buyer have done about someone living on the land?
hint: A visible occupant by another puts a buyer on inquiry.
solution: A buyer is charged with notice of what a reasonable inspection or inquiry would reveal, and open possession by someone other than the seller does that, so Ira is not a bona fide purchaser without notice.
