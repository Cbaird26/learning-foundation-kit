@article
A **future interest** is a present right to possess land at a later time. Every conveyance that gives less than a fee simple absolute to the first taker leaves a future interest somewhere. The skill for the exam and for practice is to name every interest, in order, for each conveyance.

### Interests in the transferor

- **Reversion**: what the grantor keeps after granting an estate of shorter duration than the grantor held (O to A for life: A has a life estate, O has a reversion). Reversions are vested.
- **Possibility of reverter**: the grantor's interest after a fee simple determinable.
- **Right of entry** (power of termination): the grantor's interest after a fee simple subject to condition subsequent.

### Interests in a transferee

A **remainder** follows a natural end of the prior estate and is created in the same instrument; it cannot cut short the prior estate.

- **Vested remainder** (indefeasibly vested): created in an ascertained person with no condition precedent (O to A for life, then to B and her heirs).
- **Vested remainder subject to open** (a class gift with at least one member ready to take): O to A for life, then to the children of B, where B has living children and may have more.
- **Vested remainder subject to complete divestment**: vested but may be cut short by a condition subsequent (O to A for life, then to B, but if B dies before A, then to C).
- **Contingent remainder**: created in an unascertained person (B's unborn children, when none are alive) or subject to a condition precedent (to B if B graduates).

An **executory interest** is a future interest in a transferee that is not a remainder because it cuts short a prior estate or takes effect after a gap. It is **shifting** if it cuts short another transferee's estate (O to A, but if X then to B) and **springing** if it cuts short the grantor's estate or follows a gap (O to A when A marries). After a fee simple, a future interest in a third person can only be an executory interest.

Old common-law rules that survive in a few places: the **Rule in Shelley's Case** and the **Doctrine of Worthier Title** (nearly all states have abolished the first and many have converted the second to a rule of construction), and the **destructibility of contingent remainders** (abolished in nearly every state). Read the local law.

### The Rule Against Perpetuities

> No interest is good unless it must vest, if at all, not later than 21 years after some life in being at the creation of the interest.

At common law the rule applies to **contingent remainders, executory interests, vested remainders subject to open**, and commercial options in gross. It does **not** apply to reversions, possibilities of reverter, rights of entry (some states regulate those by statute), or vested remainders. The test looks at **possibilities**, not probabilities: if any conceivable sequence of events could cause the interest to vest too late, the interest is void from the start. Look for a **validating life**, someone alive at creation whose life and 21 years bound the vesting.

Classic traps: the **fertile octogenarian** (the law presumes anyone can have children at any age), the **unborn widow** ("to A for life, then to A's widow for life, then to A's children": the widow may be someone born after the grant), the **slothful executor** (a condition tied to the administration of an estate), and **age contingencies above 21** (to A for life, then to A's children who reach 25: an after-born child might turn 25 more than 21 years after A's death). A **class gift** is judged by the **all-or-nothing rule**: if the interest of any possible member can vest too late, the whole class gift is void. A **charity-to-charity** gift is exempt from the rule. The remedy at common law is to **strike** the offending interest and let the prior estates take as written: in "O to A so long as used for a school, then to B," B's executory interest is void, and A has a fee simple determinable and O a possibility of reverter, which the rule does not touch.

### Reform

Many states now follow the **wait-and-see** approach in the **Uniform Statutory Rule Against Perpetuities** (1986): an interest that would be void at common law is valid if it actually vests or terminates within **90 years** of creation. Courts may also reform a void interest to carry out the grantor's intent. About half the states have abolished or sharply limited the rule for trusts, which permits "dynasty trusts." The policy behind the rule is the limit on the dead hand: an owner should not control land for generations, and land should remain marketable.

### Applying the rules

Identify the present estate, then each future interest. Then for each interest that is contingent or an executory interest or a class gift, test the common-law rule by asking whether anything can vest later than 21 years after a life in being; if so, say whether the jurisdiction uses wait-and-see.

@example
title: Name the interests
problem: O conveys land "to A for life, then to B and her heirs, but if B dies before A, then to C." Name every interest.
step: A has a life estate.
step: B's remainder is created in an ascertained person but may be cut short by the condition that B die before A. It is a vested remainder subject to complete divestment.
step: C's interest cuts short B's vested remainder, so it is a shifting executory interest.
step: O has nothing left. The conveyance carried the entire fee simple.

@example
title: Rule Against Perpetuities at common law
problem: O conveys "to A for life, then to A's children who reach age 25." A is alive with no children. Is the interest valid at common law?
step: The children's interests are contingent remainders in a class and are subject to the rule, which asks whether every interest must vest within 21 years after a life in being.
step: After the conveyance, A could have a child and die the next day. That child is not a life in being at the conveyance, and A's life plus 21 years ends before the child's 25th birthday.
step: Under the all-or-nothing rule, because one possible member could vest too late, the whole class gift is void. A has a life estate and O has a reversion. Under USRAP, the interest is valid if it actually vests or fails within 90 years, which depends on what in fact happens.

@mistakes
- Calling an interest a remainder when it cuts short a prior estate; that is an executory interest.
- Applying the Rule Against Perpetuities to reversions, possibilities of reverter and rights of entry.
- Looking at what will probably happen instead of what could happen under the common-law rule.
- Forgetting that a class gift is void if any possible member could vest too late.
