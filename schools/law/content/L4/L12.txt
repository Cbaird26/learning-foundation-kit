@article
Government can regulate land use under the **police power** (to protect public health, safety, morals and general welfare), and it can **take** land under eminent domain if it pays. The line between regulation, which needs no compensation, and a taking, which does, is the central question of this week.

### Zoning

*Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co.* (1926) upheld comprehensive zoning as a valid exercise of the police power: a land use regulation is valid if it is not arbitrary and bears a substantial relation to public health, safety, morals or general welfare. Modern review is deferential, rational-basis review (*Village of Belle Terre v. Boraas*, 1974, upheld a limit on unrelated occupants, while *Moore v. City of East Cleveland*, 1977, struck a limit that barred a grandmother from living with grandchildren). Zoning is authorized by a state enabling act, in many states modeled on the Standard State Zoning Enabling Act.

- A **variance** allows a departure from the zoning rule. A **use variance** requires unnecessary hardship caused by unique conditions of the land and not self-created; an **area variance** (setbacks, height) generally requires lesser "practical difficulties." Wanting more profit is not hardship.
- A **special exception** (conditional use permit) is a use the ordinance allows if stated conditions are met.
- A **nonconforming use** is a lawful use that predates a zoning change. It is protected (grandfathered) but generally cannot be expanded, and it ends on abandonment, destruction or discontinuance beyond the period set by the ordinance; some jurisdictions permit **amortization**.
- **Spot zoning** is a rezoning of a small parcel that benefits one owner and conflicts with the comprehensive plan; it is invalid if arbitrary.
- **Exclusionary zoning.** *Southern Burlington County NAACP v. Township of Mount Laurel* (1975) held that under the New Jersey constitution a developing municipality must provide a realistic opportunity for low- and moderate-income housing. Under the federal Equal Protection Clause, a plaintiff must show discriminatory purpose (*Arlington Heights*, 1977).

### Eminent domain

The Fifth Amendment says private property shall not "be taken for public use, without just compensation." The **public use** requirement is read broadly: *Kelo v. City of New London* (2005) held that a taking for economic development under a comprehensive plan was a public use, and many states responded with statutes limiting that use. **Just compensation** is the fair market value of the property at the time of the taking.

### Regulatory takings

When a regulation, not a formal condemnation, costs an owner value, *Pennsylvania Coal Co. v. Mahon* (1922) says a regulation that "goes too far" is a taking. Courts now sort claims into categories.

1. **Permanent physical occupation** is a per se taking, however small (*Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp.*, 1982: a cable box on a roof). *Cedar Point Nursery v. Hassid* (2021) held that a regulation giving union organizers a recurring right to enter farmland is a per se physical taking, even though the access is intermittent, because it appropriates the right to exclude; *Horne v. Department of Agriculture* (2015) applied per se rules to personal property (raisins).
2. **Total loss of all economically beneficial use** is a per se taking unless the restriction inheres in the title under background principles of state property or nuisance law (*Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council*, 1992).
3. **Everything else**: the ad hoc *Penn Central* balancing (*Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City*, 1978), weighing the **economic impact**, interference with **distinct investment-backed expectations**, and the **character of the government action**. Landmark designation of Grand Central was upheld under it. A diminution in value alone, even a large one, does not establish a taking. *Tahoe-Sierra* (2002) applied *Penn Central* to a temporary moratorium; *Palazzolo* (2001) held a later purchase does not bar a claim; and *Murr v. Wisconsin* (2017) treated the "parcel as a whole" question under a multifactor test.
4. **Exactions.** A permit condition must have an **essential nexus** to the harm the project causes (*Nollan v. California Coastal Commission*, 1987) and be **roughly proportional** to it (*Dolan v. City of Tigard*, 1994). *Koontz* (2013) extended this to demands for money and to permit denials, and *Sheetz v. County of El Dorado* (2024) held that conditions imposed by legislation are not exempt from the test (the Court left open whether a fee applied to a whole class of properties is treated differently, and remanded on whether that fee satisfied the test).

**Procedure and remedy.** *Knick v. Township of Scott* (2019) lets owners sue in federal court under § 1983 without first seeking compensation in state court. *Tyler v. Hennepin County* (2023) held that a county takes property when it sells a tax-delinquent home and keeps the surplus over the debt.

### Policy

Compensation spreads the cost of public projects across the public and protects owners against bearing burdens that should be borne by all, while the police power lets government protect the public without paying every owner it restricts. Courts balance the two by categories.

### Applying the doctrine

First ask whether the government physically occupied the land or imposed an exaction; if neither, ask whether the owner lost all economically beneficial use; if not, run *Penn Central*.

@example
title: Penn Central analysis
problem: A city bars large-scale development of a landowner's vacant lot to protect a historic district. The lot keeps substantial value for a smaller building. Is it a taking?
step: Is there a physical occupation? No. Is there a total loss of value? No, because substantial value remains. So the *Penn Central* test governs.
step: Economic impact: the owner loses development potential but keeps substantial value. Investment-backed expectations: depends on what the owner knew when buying. Character: a general land-use rule serving a public interest.
step: A court would likely find no taking, because a partial loss in value, standing alone, does not establish one.

@example
title: Nollan and Dolan after Sheetz
problem: A county requires every builder of a new house to pay a fixed \$20,000 traffic fee, set by legislation, to receive a permit. Is it exempt from Nollan and Dolan?
step: Before 2024 some courts exempted legislatively imposed conditions.
step: *Sheetz* held that legislative conditions get no exemption.
step: The fee must satisfy the essential-nexus and rough-proportionality tests with respect to the traffic the house will cause; Sheetz itself left that question to the lower court.

@mistakes
- Treating a large loss in value as automatically a taking; only a total loss is a per se taking under Lucas.
- Forgetting that a permanent physical occupation is a taking however small.
- Applying a strict test to ordinary zoning, which gets deferential rational-basis review.
- Saying legislatively imposed fees are exempt from Nollan and Dolan; Sheetz rejected that.
