@article
An **estate** is an interest in land measured by time: it answers how long the holder's right to possess lasts. A **present estate** gives a right to possess now; a **future interest** (Week 4) gives a right that may become possessory later. Classifying a conveyance means reading its words of creation and naming both the present estate and the interest that follows.

### Fee simple absolute

The fee simple absolute is the largest estate: it is potentially infinite in duration, freely alienable, devisable and inheritable. At common law the words were "to A **and her heirs**"; "heirs" were words of **limitation** (they described the estate, they did not make heirs owners). Today a conveyance "to A" or "to A in fee simple" is presumed to convey a fee simple absolute unless the instrument shows a lesser intent.

### Defeasible fees

A defeasible fee is a fee simple that may end early on a stated event. Three kinds matter, and the words of creation decide which.

| Estate | Typical words | What happens at the event | Interest held after it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fee simple determinable** | "to A **so long as** ...", "until", "while", "during" | Ends **automatically** | **Possibility of reverter** in the grantor |
| **Fee simple subject to condition subsequent** | "to A, **but if** ...", "on condition that", "provided that, and the grantor may re-enter" | Continues until the grantor **exercises** the right | **Right of entry** (power of termination) in the grantor |
| **Fee simple subject to executory limitation** | "to A, but if ... then to B", "so long as ..., then to B" | Ends automatically in favor of a **third party** | **Executory interest** in B |

The difference between the first two is the consequence of the event. In a determinable fee, A's estate ends the moment the event happens and the grantor is owner without doing anything; the statute of limitations on the grantor's claim starts at that moment. In a fee simple subject to condition subsequent, A remains owner after the event until the grantor re-enters or sues; the grantor can waive the breach, and delay may bar the right. Courts construe ambiguous language to favor the fee subject to condition subsequent, because forfeitures are disfavored. Conditions that violate public policy, such as a racial restriction, are void, and several states limit the life of reverters and rights of entry by statute.

### Fee tail

The **fee tail** ("to A and the heirs of his body") was an estate that passed to lineal descendants and ended when the line failed. Almost every state has abolished it by statute, most commonly converting it to a fee simple absolute and a few converting it to a life estate with a remainder to the heirs. Read the local statute before applying it.

### Life estate

A **life estate** lasts for the life of the holder ("to A for life") or for the life of another (a life estate **pur autre vie**). The grantor keeps a reversion unless a remainder is created in a third party (Week 4). A life tenant may use and enjoy the land, lease it, and transfer her interest, but the buyer's interest still ends at the measuring life. The life tenant owes the future-interest holders three main duties:

- **Do not commit waste.** *Voluntary* (affirmative) waste is an act that damages the land, such as demolishing a building or cutting timber for sale. *Permissive* waste is failing to maintain the land, such as letting the roof rot. *Ameliorative* waste changes the land in a way that increases its value; some modern courts allow it when conditions have changed so much that the old use no longer makes economic sense. Under the **open mines doctrine**, a life tenant may continue to take minerals from a mine already opened before the life estate began, but may not open new ones.
- **Pay ordinary taxes and the interest on any mortgage**, up to the income or reasonable rental value the land produces; the holder of the remainder bears the principal and the extraordinary costs.
- **Not injure the future interest** by mortgaging or selling more than the life estate she holds.

A remainderman or reversioner may sue for damages or an injunction against waste.

### Policy

The law of estates balances **alienability** against the grantor's control over the land. Defeasible fees let a grantor impose conditions, but courts read conditions narrowly and legislatures limit old restrictions so that land is not tied up by stale claims. Life estates are common in family planning, while trusts have largely replaced them for complex transfers.

### Applying the rules

Read the words of creation, name the present estate, then name the future interest. If the language is "so long as", expect a determinable fee. If it is "but if" with a re-entry clause, expect a condition subsequent. If the grantor wanted a third person to take on the event, the estate is subject to an executory limitation.

@example
title: Determinable or conditional
problem: O conveys land "to the Town so long as it is used as a public park." Twenty years later the Town stops using it as a park and builds a parking garage. Who owns the land?
step: Name the estate. "So long as" is durational language, so the Town holds a fee simple determinable, and O retains a possibility of reverter.
step: Apply the event. The park use ended when the Town began the garage; the estate ends automatically.
step: Conclusion. O (or O's successors) owns the land without taking any action, and the garage is built on O's land. If the words had been "but if the land is not used as a park, O may re-enter," the Town would remain owner until O re-entered.

@example
title: Mortgage payment during a life estate
problem: A holds a life estate in a house; B holds the remainder. The mortgage payment is \$14,000 for the year, of which \$9,000 is interest and \$5,000 is principal. The house produces income exceeding \$9,000. How much should A bear?
step: Use the rule that the life tenant pays ordinary taxes and mortgage interest, up to the income produced.
step: A bears the interest of \$9,000. The principal reduces the debt on land that B will inherit, so B bears \$5,000.

@mistakes
- Treating "so long as" and "but if" as interchangeable; the first ends the estate automatically, the second requires the grantor to act.
- Forgetting that the grantor keeps the possibility of reverter or right of entry after a defeasible fee, because the grantor did not transfer everything.
- Applying the common-law fee tail without checking whether the state has abolished it.
- Saying that a life tenant may do anything she likes with the land; she cannot commit waste or injure the remainder.
