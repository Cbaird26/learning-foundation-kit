@article
A **mortgage** is a security interest in land that secures payment of a debt, usually evidenced by a **promissory note**. The note creates the borrower's personal liability; the mortgage lets the lender take the land if the borrower defaults.

### Forms and theories

In a **mortgage**, the borrower (mortgagor) gives the lender (mortgagee) a lien on the land. In a **deed of trust**, the borrower conveys title to a **trustee** who holds it as security for the lender and may sell the land on default under a **power of sale**. Under the **lien theory** (most states) the borrower keeps title and the lender holds only a lien. Under the **title theory** (a few states) the lender holds title until the debt is paid, though the borrower keeps possession.

A deed that is absolute on its face but was intended as security is an **equitable mortgage**, and courts will treat it as a mortgage if the parties intended security. An **installment land contract** leaves title in the seller until the buyer pays in full; many courts limit the seller's right to forfeit the buyer's payments, and some require foreclosure.

### Foreclosure

The lender's remedy on default is **foreclosure**, which sells the land to pay the debt. **Judicial foreclosure** is the standard method in many states. **Nonjudicial foreclosure** by power of sale is permitted in states that use deeds of trust, with statutory notice. The sale is a public auction. Federal law adds borrower protections: the Truth in Lending Act's ability-to-repay rule for residential mortgages (added in 2010) and servicing rules under the Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act. In *BFP v. Resolution Trust Corp.* (1994), the Supreme Court held that the price at a regularly conducted foreclosure sale is "reasonably equivalent value" for purposes of the Bankruptcy Code's fraudulent-transfer provision.

### Redemption

Before the sale, the borrower has the **equity of redemption**: the right to redeem by paying the full debt, with interest and costs. It cannot be waived in the original mortgage, because courts will not allow a lender to **clog** the equity of redemption ("once a mortgage, always a mortgage"). Some states also give a **statutory right of redemption** for a period after the sale.

### Transfers of mortgaged land

If the borrower sells the land, the buyer either **assumes** the mortgage, becoming personally liable on the debt (the seller then stands as a surety), or takes **subject to** it, in which case the buyer is not personally liable and risks only the land. In either case the original borrower remains liable to the lender unless the lender releases the borrower. A **due-on-sale clause**, which lets the lender accelerate the debt if the property is sold, is enforceable under the **Garn-St Germain Depository Institutions Act** (1982), except for specified transfers of residential property, such as a transfer to a spouse or child, a transfer on the borrower's death to a relative, a transfer incident to divorce, or a transfer into a living trust in which the borrower remains the beneficiary and occupant.

### Priority and the sale

Priority generally follows first in time, as modified by the recording acts (Week 9), so that a later lender or buyer who qualifies as a bona fide purchaser can take ahead of an earlier unrecorded interest. A **purchase-money mortgage** (one that finances the purchase) takes priority over liens created earlier against the buyer. A foreclosure sale **extinguishes** the foreclosing mortgage and all **junior** interests whose holders were **joined** in the suit; **senior** interests survive, and a junior interest whose holder was **not joined** also survives the sale. Proceeds are paid in this order: (1) costs of sale, (2) the foreclosing lender's debt, (3) junior liens in order of priority, (4) the **surplus** to the borrower. If the proceeds do not cover the debt, the lender may seek a **deficiency judgment** against the borrower on the note. Some states limit deficiencies by anti-deficiency statutes or by requiring a fair-value credit.

### Chain of title

Week 9's statutes apply to every deed in a chain of title. To apply them, list each conveyance, who paid value, who had notice, and the order of recording, then test each statute type.

### Applying the doctrine

For a foreclosure problem, write each claim in priority order and subtract. For a transfer problem, ask whether the buyer assumed or took subject to the debt.

@example
title: Surplus after foreclosure
problem: A first mortgage of \$200,000 is foreclosed with all junior holders joined. A second mortgage secures \$60,000. The sale brings \$290,000 and costs are \$10,000. Who receives what?
step: Pay costs first: \$290,000 - \$10,000 = \$280,000.
step: Pay the first mortgage: \$280,000 - \$200,000 = \$80,000 remains.
step: Pay the second mortgage in full: \$80,000 - \$60,000 = \$20,000 remains.
step: The \$20,000 surplus goes to the borrower.

@example
title: Assumption or subject to
problem: Owner sells to Buyer, who takes the land "subject to" an existing \$150,000 mortgage. Buyer defaults and the sale brings only \$120,000 net. Who is liable for the deficiency?
step: A buyer who takes subject to the mortgage is not personally liable on the note.
step: The original borrower remains liable on the note to the lender, so the lender may collect the \$30,000 deficiency from Owner, not from Buyer.

@mistakes
- Allowing a lender to waive the equity of redemption in the mortgage itself.
- Making a "subject to" buyer personally liable for a deficiency; only an assuming buyer is liable.
- Forgetting that a junior mortgagee who is not joined survives foreclosure.
- Giving the surplus to the junior lienor before paying costs and the senior debt.

@practice self
prompt: Apply race, notice and race-notice statutes to this chain of title and decide who owns the land under each. In January, Owner conveys to Ben for value; Ben does not record until December. In March, Owner conveys the same land to Ann for value; Ann has no notice of Ben's deed. Ben records in December. Ann does not record until the following February.
solution: Order of events: Ben bought first (January), Ann bought second (March) without notice, Ben recorded in December, and Ann recorded the following February, after Ben. Race statute: the first to record wins, so Ben wins. Notice statute: Ann bought for value without notice of Ben's unrecorded deed, so she is a bona fide purchaser and wins, whether or not she recorded first. Race-notice statute: Ann must have taken without notice and recorded first; she recorded after Ben, so she loses and Ben wins.

rubric: Explains why the notice statute does not require recording first.
