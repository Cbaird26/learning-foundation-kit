@article
When two or more people own the same land at the same time, the law must say what each owns and what happens when one dies, conveys or quarrels with the others. The main forms of **concurrent ownership** are the tenancy in common, the joint tenancy and the tenancy by the entirety.

### Tenancy in common

Each **tenant in common** owns an **undivided share** of the whole. The only unity required is **possession**: each has the right to possess the whole. Shares may be unequal (if the instrument says nothing, equal shares are presumed). A tenant in common may sell, mortgage or devise her share, and there is **no right of survivorship**: at death her share passes by will or intestacy. Because of that, courts presume a **tenancy in common** whenever the instrument does not clearly create a joint tenancy; a conveyance "to A and B" is a tenancy in common today in nearly every state.

### Joint tenancy

A **joint tenancy** requires the **four unities**: **time** (the interests vest at the same moment), **title** (they come from the same instrument), **interest** (equal, undivided shares of the same duration) and **possession** (equal right to the whole). Its defining feature is the **right of survivorship**: when one joint tenant dies, her interest ends and the survivors share the whole, so she cannot leave it by will. Most states require the instrument to say so ("to A and B as joint tenants with right of survivorship").

**Severance.** A joint tenant can destroy the joint tenancy without the others' consent:

- **Conveyance.** If A conveys her interest, the buyer becomes a **tenant in common** as to A's share, and the **remaining** joint tenants stay joint tenants as to the rest. If A, B and C hold one-third each and A conveys to D, then D owns one-third as a tenant in common, and B and C own two-thirds as joint tenants with survivorship between them. A secret conveyance still severs.
- **Partition** (below), **mutual agreement**, and **murder**: a joint tenant who kills another cannot profit from the killing.
- **Mortgage.** In states following the **lien theory** (most states), a mortgage by one joint tenant does not sever, and if the borrower dies first the surviving tenant takes the whole free of the lien (*Harms v. Sprague*, 1984). In a few **title-theory** states, a mortgage can sever.
- A **will does not sever**: a joint tenant cannot devise her interest, because it ends at death.

### Tenancy by the entirety

Spouses may hold property as **tenants by the entirety**, which adds the unity of **marriage** (a fifth unity) to the four. It is available in a number of states. Neither spouse can sever it alone or defeat the other's survivorship by conveying, and in the majority rule **a creditor of only one spouse cannot reach it**, though creditors of both spouses can. Divorce converts it to a tenancy in common; death passes the whole to the survivor.

### Rights and duties among co-tenants

- **Possession.** Each may possess the whole. A co-tenant who occupies the property alone owes no rent to the others in the majority rule, unless she **ousts** them (wrongfully excludes them) or the others demanded entry and were refused (*Spiller v. Mackereth*, 1976). A few states by statute require payment of fair rental value for exclusive use.
- **Rents from third parties** are shared in proportion to ownership, after expenses.
- **Contribution.** A co-tenant who pays more than her share of necessary expenses (mortgage payments, property taxes, needed repairs) may seek contribution from the others in proportion to ownership; improvements are normally compensated only in a partition accounting.
- **Adverse possession by a co-tenant** requires an ouster, because each has a right to possess.

### Partition

Any co-tenant may demand **partition**, either **in kind** (a physical division) or **by sale** with division of proceeds. Courts prefer partition in kind and order a sale when division would be impractical or greatly prejudice the owners. Under the **Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act** (2010), now enacted in many states, when co-owners inherited the land from a common source, the other co-tenants have a chance to buy out the one who forces partition at an appraised value, and courts must consider a range of factors before ordering a sale.

### Applying the doctrine

Identify the form of ownership from the instrument, ask whether any act severed it, and then resolve the dispute under the rules on possession, contribution or partition.

@example
title: Fractions after a severance
problem: A, B and C hold land as joint tenants, equal shares. A conveys her interest to D by a deed delivered to D. B then dies, leaving a will that devises "all my property" to E. Who owns what?
step: A's conveyance severed as to A's share: D is a tenant in common of one-third.
step: B and C remain joint tenants of the other two-thirds, with a right of survivorship between them.
step: B's will cannot pass B's interest, because a joint tenant's interest ends at death. C takes B's share by survivorship.
step: Result: C owns two-thirds in fee simple, D owns one-third, and E takes nothing.

@example
title: Contribution among tenants in common
problem: A (70%) and B (30%) own a duplex as tenants in common. A pays \$6,000 for a necessary roof repair. How much can A recover from B?
step: A necessary repair is a shared cost, in proportion to ownership.
step: B owes 30% of \$6,000.
step: 0.30 times \$6,000 is \$1,800, so A can recover \$1,800 from B.

@mistakes
- Treating a will as severing a joint tenancy; a joint tenant cannot devise the interest.
- Saying that a conveyance by one of three joint tenants destroys the joint tenancy among all three; it severs only as to the share conveyed.
- Awarding rent to a co-tenant who was not ousted, merely because the other co-tenant used the property.
- Assuming a mortgage by one joint tenant always severs the joint tenancy; most states follow the lien theory.
