@article
Every property course starts with a simple question: how does anyone come to own anything? This week covers three answers that need no earlier owner's cooperation or that depend on a gift: **first possession**, **find**, and **gift**.

### First possession

The default rule is that an unowned thing (a wild animal, a fish, a whale, unclaimed oil in the ground) belongs to the first person to **possess** it. Everything turns on what counts as possession. In *Pierson v. Post* (1805), Post chased a fox with hounds across a beach; Pierson, who knew of the chase, killed the fox and carried it off. The majority held that Post acquired no property by mere pursuit. Ownership of a wild animal (*ferae naturae*) requires actual bodily seizure, or at least wounding or trapping that places the animal under the hunter's certain control. The dissent would have deferred to the custom of hunters. The majority's reasons are the classic policies of the doctrine: a bright-line rule gives certainty, it rewards actual labor and capture rather than a race to start, and it avoids litigation over who "almost" had the animal.

Courts sometimes accept an industry custom that supplies a clear rule. In *Ghen v. Rich* (1881), a whaler killed a whale with a bomb lance and it sank and washed ashore; the custom of the fishery gave the whale to the first to kill it, and the court enforced the custom against the finder and the buyer who took it from the finder. In *Keeble v. Hickeringill* (1707), a decoy-pond owner won damages when a neighbor maliciously frightened away the ducks: even without ownership of the birds, intentional interference with another's lawful business is actionable. In *Popov v. Hayashi* (2002), two claimants had competing claims to a record home-run baseball; the trial court split the ball's value equally. *Johnson v. M'Intosh* (1823) applied a discovery theory to land: the Court held that title derived from the discovering sovereign, and that private purchases of land from tribes could not support title against a grant from the United States.

### Finders

A finder has title good against everyone **except the true owner** (*Armory v. Delamirie*, 1722: a chimney sweep's boy found a jewel and the goldsmith refused to return it; the goldsmith was liable). The harder question is a contest between the finder and the **owner of the place** where the item was found. The answer depends on how the item is classified.

| Category | Meaning | Who prevails over the other claimant |
|---|---|---|
| **Lost** | Owner parted with it unintentionally and does not know where it is | Finder, generally, if lawfully present (*Bridges v. Hawkesworth*, 1851: notes on the floor of a public part of a shop) |
| **Mislaid** | Owner intentionally put it somewhere and forgot it | Owner of the place, who holds it for the true owner (*McAvoy v. Medina*, 1866: pocketbook left on a barber-shop table) |
| **Abandoned** | Owner voluntarily gave up ownership | The first taker |
| **Embedded or attached** | In or under the soil, or part of the premises | Owner of the land (*South Staffordshire Water Co. v. Sharman*, 1896) |

A trespasser-finder generally cannot claim against the landowner, and courts sometimes award employees' finds to employers. *Hannah v. Peel* (1945) went the other way on the facts: a soldier billeted in a house found a brooch on a window ledge, and the owner, who had never lived in the house, lost to the finder. Many states now have **lost-property statutes**: the finder reports the item, holds it for a period, and then owns it if no one claims it. **Treasure trove** (coins or bullion concealed long ago) is handled differently from state to state, often by statute.

### Gifts

A completed **inter vivos gift** needs three elements: **donative intent** (a present intent to transfer ownership now, not a promise), **delivery**, and **acceptance** (presumed when the gift is beneficial). A promise to give is an unenforceable gratuitous promise. Delivery may be actual (handing it over), **constructive** (handing over the key to the box that holds the item) or **symbolic** (a writing), and courts accept the lesser forms when manual delivery is impractical. *Gruen v. Gruen* (1986) held that a father made a valid present gift of a remainder in a painting by a letter, even though he kept possession for life. Delivery of a donor's own check is not complete until the check is paid, because the donor can revoke it before then, and death revokes it. A **gift causa mortis** is made in anticipation of imminent death from a present peril, must also be delivered, and is revoked automatically if the donor survives the peril or may be revoked by the donor before death. A completed gift is irrevocable.

### Applying the rules

Write it as IRAC. State the issue (who has the better claim?), the rule and its elements, apply each element to the facts, and conclude. For finds, begin by classifying the item and then identify the competing claimants; for gifts, test intent, delivery and acceptance in turn.

@example
title: Who gets the ring?
problem: Mia, a customer in a hair salon, finds a diamond ring on the floor of the public waiting area. The true owner is unknown. The salon owner demands the ring. Who has the better right as between Mia and the salon owner?
step: Classify the item. A ring on the floor of a public area, with no sign anyone put it there deliberately, looks **lost**, not mislaid and not embedded.
step: State the rule. Between a finder and the owner of the premises, a lawful finder of lost property in a public part of a place generally prevails; the premises owner prevails for mislaid or embedded items.
step: Apply. Mia was lawfully present, found it in an area open to customers, and the facts show no intent by anyone to place it there.
step: Conclude. Mia has the better right as against the salon, and the true owner still outranks both. Many states would also require her to follow the lost-property statute.

@example
title: A gift of a watch
problem: Lee tells his niece Ana, "The watch in my desk drawer is yours as of today." He keeps the watch in the drawer and never gives her the drawer key. Lee dies a month later. Does Ana own the watch?
step: Identify the elements: donative intent, delivery and acceptance. Intent looks present because the words speak of ownership "as of today."
step: Test delivery. Nothing was handed over, no key was given, and no writing transferred it. There was neither actual, constructive nor symbolic delivery.
step: Conclude. Without delivery there is no completed gift. The words are at most an unenforceable promise, so the watch passes through Lee's estate.

@mistakes
- Treating pursuit of an animal as possession; under the majority rule in Pierson v. Post, pursuit alone gives no title.
- Awarding every found item to the finder or to the landowner without first classifying it as lost, mislaid, abandoned or embedded.
- Forgetting that the true owner beats every finder, so "the finder wins" only means the finder wins against others.
- Calling a statement of intent a completed gift when nothing was delivered.
