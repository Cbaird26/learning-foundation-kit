@article
**Servitudes** are nonpossessory interests that give one landowner rights over, or restrictions on, another's land. The three main types are **easements**, **real covenants** and **equitable servitudes**.

### Easements

An **easement** is a nonpossessory right to use another's land for a limited purpose. An **affirmative** easement permits an act (a right of way); a **negative** easement prevents the servient owner from doing something, and traditionally only four are recognized: light, air, support and the flow of an artificial stream. An easement is **appurtenant** if it benefits the owner of a neighboring (dominant) parcel and passes automatically with it, even if the deed is silent. It is **in gross** if it benefits a person rather than land (a utility's pole line); commercial in-gross easements are generally assignable. A **license** is permission to enter, revocable at will, and creates no property interest, though a license made irrevocable by the licensee's reliance on it may become an easement by estoppel.

### Creation

- **Express**: a grant or reservation in a writing that satisfies the Statute of Frauds.
- **Implied from prior use** (quasi-easement): when one owner divides a parcel, an easement passes for a use that existed before the division, was **apparent** and **continuous**, and is **reasonably necessary** to enjoyment of the part conveyed.
- **By necessity**: when a division leaves a parcel landlocked, an easement across the other part arises if it was **strictly necessary** at the time of division; it ends when the necessity ends.
- **By prescription**: use that is **open and notorious, adverse (without permission), and continuous** for the statutory period, resembling adverse possession but for use rather than possession. Permission defeats it.
- **By estoppel**: a license that the licensor allowed the licensee to spend money relying on.

### Scope, relocation and termination

The use is limited to what was granted or reasonably necessary; the burden may not be increased without consent. The traditional rule bars the servient owner from relocating an easement without the dominant owner's consent; the **Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes** allows relocation if it does not significantly lessen the easement's utility or increase its burden (*M.P.M. Builders v. Dwyer*, 2002, adopted it). An easement ends by **release** (written), **merger** (one person owns both parcels), **abandonment** (nonuse plus intent to abandon), **estoppel**, **prescription** by the servient owner, expiration of its necessity, or condemnation.

### Real covenants

A **real covenant** is a promise about land that is enforceable at law, with damages as the remedy. To bind a successor, it must satisfy: (1) a **writing** (Statute of Frauds); (2) **intent** that it run; (3) it must **touch and concern** the land; and (4) **privity**. **Horizontal privity** (some relationship between the original parties apart from the covenant, such as grantor-grantee or landlord-tenant, in states that require it) and **vertical privity** (the successor holds the whole estate of the original party) are needed for the **burden** to run, while only vertical privity is required for the **benefit**. *Spencer's Case* (1583) is the classic rule for covenants in leases.

### Equitable servitudes

An **equitable servitude** is enforceable by **injunction** against a successor who had **notice**. The elements are: (1) a **writing** (or a **common scheme** of development, which implies a reciprocal servitude on lots sold without the restriction, as in *Sanborn v. McLean*, 1925), (2) **intent** that it run, (3) **touch and concern**, and (4) **notice** to the successor (actual, record or inquiry). **No privity is required.** *Tulk v. Moxhay* (1848) first enforced a negative covenant in equity against a buyer with notice. Homeowners' associations enforce servitudes through recorded declarations (*Neponsit Property Owners' Ass'n v. Emigrant Industrial Savings Bank*, 1938, held that an affirmative covenant to pay assessments runs).

### Limits and termination

*Shelley v. Kraemer* (1948) held that judicial enforcement of a racially restrictive covenant is state action that violates the Equal Protection Clause; the Fair Housing Act now independently bars such discrimination. Courts also refuse to enforce a servitude that violates public policy. The **Restatement (Third)** would drop touch and concern in favor of a rule that invalidates illegal, unconstitutional or arbitrary servitudes and those imposing unreasonable burdens on rights. A covenant may end by release, merger, abandonment or acquiescence in widespread violations, or **changed conditions** that make its purpose impossible to achieve.

### Applying the doctrine

For an easement, ask how it was created, whether it is appurtenant or in gross, and whether it has ended. For a covenant, ask who is suing whom and for what: damages (real covenant) or injunction (equitable servitude), then check each requirement.

@example
title: Implied easement from prior use
problem: Owner has a house at the front of a lot and a cottage at the back. For years the cottage's tenants have used an open gravel driveway across the front yard to reach the street. Owner sells the cottage to Buyer by deed that mentions no easement. Can Buyer use the driveway?
step: Identify the type. No writing grants it, so look to implication from prior use.
step: Check the elements: unity of ownership before the sale, a use that existed before severance, apparent and continuous, and reasonably necessary to reach the street.
step: Conclusion. The driveway use meets each element, so Buyer holds an easement implied from prior use over the front yard.

@example
title: Equitable servitude against a successor
problem: In a subdivision, Seller sold every lot, including lot 1 to Ann and another lot to Cal, subject to the same recorded covenant restricting use to a single-family home for the benefit of all lots. Ann's successor Bea, who had record notice, plans to open a dental office. Ann's neighbor Cal, who also bought in the subdivision, seeks to stop her. What remedy and what elements?
step: Cal wants an injunction, so the action is an equitable servitude. No privity is required.
step: Elements: a writing (the recorded covenant), intent to bind successors (stated), touch and concern (a use restriction does), and notice (record notice to Bea).
step: Conclusion. Cal can enjoin Bea's office use.

@mistakes
- Requiring strict necessity for an easement implied from prior use; it requires only reasonable necessity.
- Requiring privity for an equitable servitude; privity is for real covenants.
- Treating permission as compatible with a prescriptive easement.
- Forgetting that an appurtenant easement passes with the dominant parcel even if the deed does not mention it.
