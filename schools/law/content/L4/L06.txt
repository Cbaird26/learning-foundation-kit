@article
Marriage changes who owns property and what a person may do with it. American states use two basic systems. The rules differ at three moments: **during** marriage, at **divorce**, and at **death**.

### Common-law (title) states

In a **common-law** property state, each spouse owns what he or she earns or acquires, and title controls during the marriage. The law adds protections at the ends of the marriage:

- **At divorce**, courts use **equitable distribution**: they classify property as **marital** (generally acquired during the marriage, whoever holds title) or **separate** (generally owned before marriage, or received by gift or inheritance), and divide marital property fairly, which is not always equally. Courts consider the length of the marriage, each spouse's contribution (including homemaking) and economic circumstances. Separate property can become marital by **commingling** or **transmutation** (for instance, putting an inherited sum into a joint account). Most states treat a pension as marital to the extent earned during the marriage. Few states have treated a professional degree or license as divisible property; New York did (*O'Brien v. O'Brien*, 1985) until a 2016 statute ended that rule, and most states instead consider a spouse's contributions to the degree in awarding support or reimbursement.
- **At death**, a spouse cannot be completely disinherited. The **elective share** lets the survivor take a statutory fraction of the decedent's estate instead of what the will gives. The traditional fraction is one-third, and the **Uniform Probate Code** (as revised in 2008) bases the percentage on the length of the marriage, applied to an **augmented estate** that includes certain nonprobate transfers (such as revocable trusts) so that a spouse cannot be defeated by moving assets out of the probate estate. Older **dower** and **curtesy** rights (a life interest in a share of the spouse's land) have been abolished or replaced in most states.

### Community property states

Nine states (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington and Wisconsin) use **community property**. Property acquired **during marriage** by the labor of either spouse is **community property**, owned equally and in undivided halves by both, whoever holds title. **Separate property** is what a spouse owned before marriage and what he or she receives during marriage by **gift, devise or inheritance**; the rules for income from separate property vary by state. At death, each spouse may dispose of only his or her half of the community, and at divorce most community states divide the community equally (Texas divides it as the court finds "just and right"). A spouse's separate property can also be transmuted to community by commingling.

### Nonmarital cohabitants

Unmarried couples have no automatic property rights from living together. Under *Marvin v. Marvin* (1976), many courts enforce express or implied-in-fact contracts and apply quasi-contract and trust remedies between cohabitants, so an agreement to share earnings is not void for being "meretricious"; a few states refuse to enforce such agreements. Since *Obergefell v. Hodges* (2015), same-sex spouses have the same marital property rights as other spouses.

### Concurrent ownership between spouses

Spouses may hold land as **joint tenants** or as **tenants by the entirety** where the form exists (Week 5). A deed from one joint tenant severs as to that tenant's share, with the buyer becoming a tenant in common, and the remaining owners stay joint tenants. A deed from one spouse alone cannot sever an entirety estate. **Divorce** converts an entirety estate to a tenancy in common, and many states treat a divorce decree as severing an ordinary joint tenancy between the spouses.

### Policy

Marriage is treated as an economic partnership: community property says so from the start, while equitable distribution and the elective share achieve a similar result at divorce and death. Critics of title systems note that the nonearning spouse has no rights during the marriage, and critics of equitable distribution note that it makes results less predictable.

### Applying the rules

Classify the property as separate or marital, decide which moment is in play (marriage, divorce or death), and apply that system's rule. For numerical questions, use only the percentages and definitions the question supplies.

@example
title: Classifying inherited property
problem: In a community property state, Wendy inherits a lakeside cabin from her aunt during her marriage and pays the property tax only from a separate account that holds her own inheritance. On divorce, her husband claims half of the cabin. Who prevails?
step: Classify the property. Property received by inheritance during the marriage is separate property, not community property.
step: Check for transmutation. Wendy used only separate funds, so there was no commingling that would convert the cabin to community property.
step: Conclusion. The cabin remains Wendy's separate property, and her husband has no claim to half of it.

@example
title: Elective share arithmetic
problem: Assume a statute giving a surviving spouse one-third of the augmented estate, defined as the probate estate plus a revocable trust. A decedent's probate estate is \$300,000 and the revocable trust holds \$120,000. His will leaves everything to his brother. What is the elective share?
step: Add the probate estate and the revocable trust: \$300,000 + \$120,000 = \$420,000.
step: Multiply by the statutory fraction: one-third of \$420,000 is \$140,000.

@mistakes
- Treating all property acquired during marriage as community property without excluding gifts and inheritances.
- Ignoring nonprobate assets such as a revocable trust when computing an elective share under an augmented-estate statute.
- Applying community property rules in a common-law state, where title controls during the marriage.
- Believing that a deed by one spouse can sever a tenancy by the entirety.

@practice self
prompt: Four years ago Ann, Bea and Cy bought a building as joint tenants with right of survivorship. This year Ann sold her interest to Dee by a deed that Dee recorded. Explain who owns what now and what happens if Bea dies next without a will, naming the rule that applies.
solution: A conveyance by one joint tenant severs the joint tenancy as to that tenant's share. Dee now owns Ann's one-third as a tenant in common; Bea and Cy remain joint tenants as to the other two-thirds. If Bea dies, her interest ends and Cy takes it by survivorship, so Cy owns two-thirds and Dee one-third. Dee's share does not pass to Cy, and Bea's lack of a will makes no difference because a joint tenant's interest does not pass by will or intestacy.
