@article
A sale of land runs in two stages: a **contract** of sale, then a **closing** at which the seller delivers a **deed**. Most disputes arise between the stages, so the law protects both parties' reliance on the signed contract and the buyer's right to receive good title.

### The contract

**Statute of Frauds.** A contract for the sale of an interest in land must be in a writing signed by the party to be charged. The writing must identify the parties, describe the land and state the essential terms (price and, in many states, other terms). Courts relax the writing requirement in two ways: **part performance**, where in the majority rule the buyer's possession plus either partial payment or substantial improvements makes the oral contract enforceable by specific performance (payment alone is not enough in most states), and **estoppel**, where reliance makes denial of the contract unjust.

**Marketable title.** Unless the contract says otherwise, the seller impliedly promises to convey **marketable title**: title free from reasonable doubt, without encumbrances such as undisclosed mortgages, easements and restrictions, and not dependent on a risky adverse-possession claim. Marketability is measured at **closing**, not at signing, so a seller who can clear a mortgage with the sale proceeds can still perform. If title is unmarketable, the buyer may rescind and recover the deposit, or sue for damages.

**Equitable conversion.** Once a valid contract is signed, equity treats the buyer as the owner of the land (equitable title) and the seller as holding legal title as security for the price. In the majority rule, the **risk of loss** therefore falls on the buyer if the property is destroyed without fault before closing, and the buyer must still pay unless the contract provides otherwise. A minority of states and the Uniform Vendor and Purchaser Risk Act put the risk on the seller until the buyer takes legal title or possession.

**Other contract points.** Buyers' financing contingencies, "time is of the essence" clauses and liquidated damages in a deposit are enforced if reasonable. Remedies for breach include specific performance (land is unique) and damages. A seller who knows of a latent material defect that the buyer cannot discover by reasonable inspection must disclose it in most states (*Johnson v. Davis*, 1985), and the old rule of caveat emptor for used houses has eroded. Builders of new homes impliedly warrant good quality in most states.

### The deed

A valid deed requires a **writing** signed by the grantor that identifies the grantor and grantee, contains words of conveyance, and describes the land, plus **delivery** and acceptance. Consideration is not required for validity. An acknowledgment (notarization) is usually required for recording, not for validity. **Delivery** is the grantor's intent that the deed take effect now, shown by words or conduct; physical transfer is neither required nor sufficient. Recording by the grantee raises a presumption of delivery. A deed given to a third party under conditions is held in **escrow**. A deed left in a drawer to take effect "at my death" is not delivered, and the transfer fails as an invalid will unless the grantor irrevocably gave up control; about half the states now authorize a **transfer-on-death deed** by statute. A forged deed is void, while a deed obtained by fraudulent inducement is voidable, so a bona fide purchaser from the defrauded owner's grantee takes good title.

**Descriptions.** A description by metes and bounds, government survey or recorded plat must identify the land. Where its parts conflict, courts prefer **natural monuments** over **artificial monuments**, then **courses** and **distances**, and last **area** or quantity.

### Covenants for title

A **general warranty deed** carries six covenants. Three are **present covenants**, breached when the deed is delivered, if at all: **seisin**, **right to convey**, and **against encumbrances**. Three are **future covenants**, breached only when the buyer is disturbed: **quiet enjoyment**, **warranty**, and **further assurances**. A **special warranty deed** warrants only against defects arising during the grantor's ownership. A **quitclaim deed** conveys whatever interest the grantor has and carries no covenants. The contract's terms **merge** into the deed at closing, except for collateral promises.

### Constructive eviction

Constructive eviction (Week 7) lets a tenant who is not actually removed treat the lease as ended when the landlord's conduct makes the premises unusable. The elements are: (1) a **substantial interference** with the tenant's use and enjoyment; (2) **caused by the landlord**, or by the landlord's failure to act when the lease or law imposed a duty; (3) **notice and a reasonable chance** for the landlord to fix it; and (4) the tenant **vacates within a reasonable time**. If all four are met, the tenant owes no further rent and may recover damages; if the tenant stays, the claim fails.

### Applying the doctrine

Walk through each stage in order: is there an enforceable contract, can the seller convey marketable title at closing, was the deed validly delivered, and which covenant (if any) protects the buyer?

@example
title: Risk of loss
problem: Seller contracts to sell a house to Buyer for \$300,000, with closing in 60 days. The contract is silent about risk of loss. Thirty days later, lightning destroys the house without any fault. Must Buyer close?
step: Identify the rule. Equitable conversion treats Buyer as owner from the signing of the contract.
step: Apply. In the majority rule, the risk of loss falls on Buyer, so Buyer remains bound to pay the price.
step: Conclusion. Buyer must close and pay \$300,000 unless a minority rule, the Uniform Vendor and Purchaser Risk Act or the contract places the risk on Seller, and insurance may soften the loss.

@example
title: Delivery of a deed
problem: Owner signs a deed naming her daughter as grantee, puts it in her desk, and tells the daughter, "It is yours when I die." Owner keeps control of the deed until she dies. Does the daughter own the land?
step: The deed is signed, has a grantee and describes the land, so the writing requirements are met.
step: Test delivery. Owner kept control and intended the deed to operate at her death, not now.
step: Conclusion. There was no delivery, so no transfer. A transfer effective at death must meet the requirements of a will, so the land passes through Owner's estate (unless a transfer-on-death statute was followed).

@mistakes
- Saying marketable title must exist at signing; it is measured at closing.
- Treating payment of a deposit alone as enough part performance to enforce an oral land contract.
- Applying the covenants of a general warranty deed to a quitclaim deed, which carries none.
- Counting a deed as delivered because the grantee has a copy; delivery turns on the grantor's present intent to be bound.

prompt: Analyze a constructive-eviction claim element by element. A tenant's apartment has had sewage back up into the bathroom and kitchen for three months. The tenant told the landlord in writing four times, and the landlord did nothing. The tenant moved out after three months and the landlord sued for the rent for the remaining 8 months of the lease. Decide the claim.
solution: Constructive eviction requires (1) substantial interference with use and enjoyment, (2) caused by the landlord or by the landlord's failure to act when under a duty, (3) notice and a reasonable opportunity to cure, and (4) vacating within a reasonable time. (1) Recurring sewage backup makes the bathroom and kitchen unusable, a substantial interference. (2) The landlord has a duty to repair a plumbing defect under the lease and the implied warranty of habitability, so inaction suffices. (3) Four written notices over three months gave ample opportunity to cure. (4) The tenant moved out after three months of waiting; courts allow a reasonable period for the landlord to repair, and the tenant did not remain long past the point where it was clear the landlord would not act. A court could question whether three months is too long to stay, but the repeated written notices, each met with inaction, support reasonableness. Conclusion: constructive eviction is likely established, so the tenant owes no rent after leaving, and the landlord's suit for the remaining 8 months fails. The tenant may also claim damages.
