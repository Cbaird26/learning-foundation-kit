@article
**Adverse possession** lets a person who occupies land that belongs to someone else become its owner after a statutory period. It rests on a statute of limitations: when the true owner's claim to recover possession (ejectment) expires, title vests in the possessor by operation of law.

### The elements

The possessor must prove that the possession was, for the entire statutory period:

1. **Actual.** The claimant used the land as a reasonable owner of that type of land would: built on it, fenced or cultivated it, or, for a vacation property or timberland, used it in the seasonal or occasional way an owner of that land normally would.
2. **Open and notorious.** The use was visible enough that a reasonably diligent owner who inspected would learn of it. The law presumes an owner who ignores an obvious encroachment has slept on the claim.
3. **Exclusive.** The claimant did not share possession with the true owner or the public. Joint possession with other adverse claimants can still count for them as co-owners.
4. **Hostile (adverse).** Without the owner's permission. Hostility does not require anger.
5. **Continuous.** Without a significant break for the whole period. Continuity is judged by what an owner would do with that kind of land.

The **statutory period** varies by state, commonly between 10 and 20 years.

### Hostility: majority and minority rules

Most states use an **objective** test: the claimant's state of mind is irrelevant, and a mistaken boundary line (a fence built in the wrong place) is enough. *Mannillo v. Gorski* (1969) is the leading statement of that view: the claimant's intent to claim land that is not hers is not an element, though she must occupy the whole disputed area as an owner would. A minority of states require a **good-faith** claim of right (the claimant must honestly believe the land is hers), and a few require **knowing, bad-faith** possession, an "aggressive trespasser" intent. Some statutes, such as New York's, also require a "claim of right" and that the land be cultivated, improved or protected by a substantial enclosure (*Van Valkenburgh v. Lutz*, 1952, is the usual case on the point). Permission, whether express or implied, defeats hostility; a tenant's possession is permissive as against the landlord.

### Tacking and privity

A claimant may combine her possession with predecessors' possession (**tacking**) if there is **privity** between them, meaning a voluntary transfer of the possession: by deed, by will, by inheritance, or by an express or implied transfer of the occupied land. *Howard v. Kunto* (1970) allowed tacking where successive buyers occupied land outside the deed's description, because each seller handed over the actual possession of the house and land they used. Without privity (a squatter followed by a different squatter), the periods cannot be tacked.

### Disabilities, color of title and future interests

If, when the claim accrues, the true owner is a minor, mentally incompetent, or (in some states) imprisoned, most statutes toll the period until the disability ends; disabilities arising later do not toll, and successive disabilities are not tacked. **Color of title** is a written instrument that purports to convey the land but is defective. Some states require color of title or payment of property taxes, and where a claimant occupies part of the land described in the instrument, she is in **constructive possession** of the whole described tract. The statute does not run against the government. It also does not run against the owner of a **future interest** until that interest becomes possessory: if a life tenant's land is taken adversely, the possessor can acquire only the life tenant's interest for the life of the tenant, and the remainderman's period begins when the life estate ends.

### Effect and policy

When the period ends, title vests in the possessor without any court order, and the former owner's title is extinguished. The possessor typically brings a **quiet title** action to obtain a marketable record title. The policies are repose of old claims, protection of settled expectations, and a penalty on owners who neglect land; critics say it rewards wrongdoers and sometimes takes a landowner's property for an innocent mistake. Recording acts do not protect an owner against adverse possession.

### Applying the doctrine

Take the elements one at a time against the facts, state the majority rule on hostility, and then compute the period, remembering tacking and tolling.

@example
title: Tacking across a sale
problem: In a state with a 10-year statutory period and the objective test, Ann builds a fence in 2010 that encloses a 20-foot strip of Bo's land that she believes is hers. In 2013, Ann sells her lot to Cy by a deed describing only her own lot but tells Cy, "the fence is the line," and Cy occupies the fenced area. In 2021, Bo sues to remove the fence. Has title to the strip passed?
step: Check each element during the whole period. Fencing and living on the strip is actual, visible (open and notorious) and exclusive. Continuity is satisfied if no break occurred.
step: Hostility. Under the objective test, Ann's mistaken belief does not matter; she did not have Bo's permission.
step: Tacking. Ann held for 3 years (2010 to 2013) and Cy for 8 years (2013 to 2021). The period reaches 11 years if the two can be combined, and privity requires a transfer of possession. Ann transferred the fenced area to Cy when she sold and pointed out the fence line.
step: Conclusion. 3 + 8 = 11, which is at least 10, so the period ran. Title to the strip vested in Cy (with Ann's period tacked), and Bo's suit fails. Cy should bring a quiet title action.

@mistakes
- Requiring the claimant to know the land is not hers; under the majority objective test, mistake or knowledge is irrelevant.
- Tacking without privity, or counting the periods of two unrelated squatters together.
- Applying the statute against a remainderman while a life tenant is still in possession.
- Treating permission as irrelevant; a permissive use is not hostile however long it lasts.

prompt: In a state with a 10-year statutory period and the objective test of hostility, Pat builds a shed that extends 3 feet over the line onto Quinn's lot, believing the line is farther over. Eleven years later Quinn discovers the encroachment and sues to remove it. Pat has used the area openly and exclusively throughout. Who prevails?
choice*: Pat, because under the objective test Pat's mistaken belief does not defeat hostility and the period has run.
choice: Quinn, because a possessor who is mistaken about the boundary lacks the intent required for hostility.
choice: Quinn, because Quinn had no actual knowledge of the encroachment during the period.
choice: Quinn, because adverse possession cannot be used for a strip as small as 3 feet.
hint: Which test of hostility does the state use?
hint: Under the objective test, only the absence of permission matters, and an open, visible structure puts the owner on notice whether or not he actually knew.
solution: Under the objective test, mistake does not matter. The shed was open and notorious, and 11 years exceeds the 10-year period, so title to the strip vested in Pat. Actual knowledge by the owner is not required, and the doctrine has no minimum size.

choice*: Ned, because Ray's use was with Ned's permission and was not hostile.

hint: Hostility means the possession is without the owner's consent.
solution: Permission, express or implied, defeats hostility, however long the use continues. The statutory period never began to run. An oral permission is enough, and adverse possession does not depend on whether the land was built on.

@practice self
prompt: Analyze element by element. In a state with a 10-year period and an objective test, Tom fenced and gardened a strip of Uma's vacant lot in 2012, believing it was his own. In 2018, Tom sold his house to Vera, telling her, "the garden and fence are part of what you are buying," though the deed described only his lot. Vera kept using the strip. In 2023 Uma sues for possession. Decide who prevails.
solution: Actual: gardening and fencing is the use an owner would make. Open and notorious: a fence and garden are visible to an inspecting owner. Exclusive: Tom and then Vera kept others out and shared with no one. Hostile: under the objective test, Tom's mistake is irrelevant and he had no permission. Continuous: use continued without break. Period: Tom held 6 years (2012 to 2018) and Vera 5 (2018 to 2023); the combined 11 years exceeds 10 if the periods can be tacked. Privity: Tom told Vera the fence and garden were included and gave her possession of the strip, and most states accept an oral or implied transfer of possession as privity even where the deed omits the strip (Howard v. Kunto). Conclusion: title to the strip vested in Vera (with Tom's period), and Uma loses. A court in a state that required the deed to describe the strip might reach the opposite result.

@quiz mc
prompt: Olga conveys land "to Al for life, then to Bea." Cal enters the land during Al's life estate and occupies it openly and adversely, in a state with a 10-year period, for 12 years until Al dies. After Al's death, Bea demands possession. Cal refuses. Who prevails?
choice*: Bea, because the statute did not begin to run against her remainder until it became possessory at Al's death.
choice: Cal, because 12 years of adverse possession ran against every owner of the land, including Bea.
choice: Cal, because Bea could have sued the moment Cal entered.
choice: Bea, because adverse possession can never be asserted against land subject to a life estate.
hint: Think about when Bea had a right to possess.
hint: A future interest holder cannot sue for possession until her interest is possessory.
solution: The period ran against Al's life estate, so Cal acquired at most Al's interest for Al's life. Bea's right to possession began only at Al's death, so her period had not run. Adverse possession against a life tenant is possible, but it does not bind the remainderman.
