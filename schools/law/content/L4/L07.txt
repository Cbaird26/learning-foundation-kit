@article
A lease is both a **conveyance** of a possessory estate (the leasehold) and a **contract** with covenants. Traditional law treated it almost entirely as a conveyance, so that the tenant's duty to pay rent was independent of the landlord's duties. Modern residential law treats a lease as a contract too, with obligations that depend on one another.

### The leasehold estates

- **Term of years**: a lease for a fixed period, however short or long, that ends automatically at the end of the term without notice. A lease of more than one year generally must be in writing under the Statute of Frauds.
- **Periodic tenancy**: a lease for successive periods (month-to-month, year-to-year) that renews automatically until one party gives notice. Notice must usually equal the length of the period (up to six months at common law), and many states set a statutory notice period such as 30 days.
- **Tenancy at will**: no fixed period; either party may end it. Many states require notice by statute.
- **Tenancy at sufferance**: a tenant who stays after the lease ends without the landlord's consent. The landlord may evict, or elect to treat the holdover as a new periodic tenancy (the period of the new tenancy varies by state and is often set by statute).

### The landlord's duties

- **Deliver possession.** Under the **American rule** (the majority), the landlord must deliver actual possession on the first day; the English rule requires only the legal right to possession.
- **Quiet enjoyment.** Every lease contains an implied covenant not to disturb the tenant's possession. A substantial interference by the landlord can amount to a **constructive eviction** (Week 8).
- **Implied warranty of habitability** (residential leases). In *Javins v. First National Realty* (1970), the court implied into urban residential leases a warranty that the premises meet the housing code standards, and treated the tenant's duty to pay rent as dependent on it. Most states now follow, and in most the warranty cannot be waived. Remedies include **rent abatement**, **repair and deduct**, **rent withholding** or payment into escrow, damages, and termination. Some courts apply an implied warranty of suitability to commercial leases (*Davidow v. Inwood North Professional Group*, 1988).
- **No retaliation.** A landlord may not evict or raise rent to punish a tenant who complains to a housing authority. The Fair Housing Act (1968) also bars refusals to rent based on race, color, religion, sex, national origin, familial status or disability.

### The landlord's remedies

- **Evict only through the courts.** Most states prohibit **self-help** eviction of residential tenants (changing locks, removing belongings) even when the tenant has breached; the landlord must use the statutory summary eviction process (*Berg v. Wiley*, 1978).
- **Abandonment.** If the tenant leaves before the term ends, the landlord may accept the surrender, or hold the tenant to the lease. In the majority rule for residential leases, the landlord has a **duty to mitigate** by making reasonable efforts to relet, and the tenant owes rent for the vacancy and any shortfall (*Sommer v. Kridel*, 1977). A minority of states still follow the traditional rule that the landlord may leave the property vacant and sue for rent, especially in commercial leases.
- **Security deposits** are regulated by statute.

### Assignment and sublease

If a tenant transfers the **entire remaining term**, the transfer is an **assignment**; if she keeps any part of the term (even a day) or a right of reentry, it is a **sublease**. The consequences differ.

| | Assignment | Sublease |
|---|---|---|
| Landlord and transferee | **Privity of estate**: the assignee is liable to the landlord on covenants that run with the land (including rent) | **No privity**: the sublessee is not liable to the landlord on the lease covenants |
| Landlord and original tenant | **Privity of contract**: the original tenant stays liable on the lease covenants | Original tenant stays liable and is also the sublessee's landlord |

Leases often restrict transfer. Courts enforce such clauses, though some require a landlord not to withhold consent unreasonably in commercial leases (*Kendall v. Ernest Pestana*, 1985).

### Landlord's tort liability

At common law a landlord was not liable for injuries on the leased premises. Exceptions now cover latent defects the landlord knows about, common areas, premises leased for public use, negligent repairs, and a growing number of courts apply ordinary negligence principles to landlords (*Sargent v. Ross*, 1973).

### Applying the doctrine

Name the estate, ask what each side promised and did, and choose the remedy the law gives for the breach. For transfers, count how much of the term was transferred before deciding between assignment and sublease.

@example
title: Assignment or sublease
problem: Tess has three years left on a five-year lease. She transfers all three remaining years to Raj and Raj stops paying rent. Who is liable to the landlord?
step: Compare what Tess transferred with what she had. Three years remain, and she transferred all three. This is an assignment, not a sublease.
step: Raj, as assignee, is in privity of estate with the landlord and is liable on the covenant to pay rent.
step: Tess remains liable on the lease because privity of contract continues unless the landlord released her. The landlord can sue either.

@example
title: Mitigation after abandonment
problem: In a state following the duty to mitigate, a residential tenant abandons an apartment with 10 months left at \$1,200 per month. The landlord relets it after 4 months at \$1,200 per month. What are the landlord's damages?
step: The tenant owes the rent for the period the unit was vacant despite reasonable efforts.
step: 4 months at \$1,200 is \$4,800. After reletting at the same rent, there is no shortfall.

@mistakes
- Treating a transfer of the entire remaining term as a sublease; any transfer of the whole term is an assignment.
- Using self-help (locking out a tenant) because the tenant breached the lease.
- Applying the traditional independent-covenants rule to a residential lease, where the landlord's breach of habitability excuses rent in whole or in part.
- Assuming a lease for more than one year can be oral; the Statute of Frauds requires a writing.
