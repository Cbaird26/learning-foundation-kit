@article
The Reading Comprehension section has four passages (one is a pair of comparative passages, covered next week) and about 27 questions in 35 minutes. That is roughly **8 to 9 minutes per passage**, reading and questions together. The passages are dense academic prose in law, the humanities, the natural sciences and the social sciences. You do not need background knowledge, and you should not use any: the correct answer is the one the passage supports.

### Read for structure, not for detail

On a first read, most test takers get lost in details. Instead, read to answer four questions:

1. **What is the topic and scope?** What is being discussed, and what exactly is the author's claim about it?
2. **What is the main point?** The author's conclusion, usually stated near the start or end, or signalled by words like *however*, *in fact*, *thus*, *this suggests*.
3. **What is the structure?** Each paragraph does a job: introduces a debate, gives a view, gives evidence, raises an objection, qualifies, concludes.
4. **What is the author's attitude?** Enthusiastic, critical, skeptical, balanced, or neutral and descriptive. Look for evaluative words ("regrettably," "persuasive," "naive").

### Voice: who is speaking?

Separate the **author's voice** from the views the author **reports**. "Some critics argue X" is not the author's claim unless the author endorses it. Many wrong answers attribute a reported view to the author.

### Marking and the passage map

Mark as you read: circle transition words (*but, yet, however, although*), underline the main point, bracket opposing views, and note strength words (*always, may, rarely*). After the passage, jot a **passage map** of one short line per paragraph, such as "P1: common view that X. P2: evidence against X. P3: author's alternative, qualified." The map is the tool for finding detail later: go back to the paragraph whose job matches the question.

### The common question types

- **Main idea:** the correct answer covers the whole passage and is neither too narrow (one paragraph) nor too broad.
- **Primary purpose:** use a verb that describes what the author does: *argues, proposes, describes, evaluates, reconciles, challenges*.
- **Function of a detail:** why does the author mention this? Return to the sentence and read the lines around it.
- **Attitude and tone:** match the author's strength: "qualified approval" differs from "enthusiastic."
- **Structure:** how the passage is organized (traditional view, then objection, then synthesis).
- **Detail:** answers are supported by specific lines; ignore outside knowledge.

### Timing practice

For your deliverable, do two timed passages, 8.75 minutes each, then map their structure and compare the maps with the passage. If your map does not match the passage's flow, practice marking transition words before you answer a question. Aim to finish the first read-through of a passage in two to three minutes, leaving the rest for questions.

### The answer-choice pattern

The right answer to a main-idea question matches the passage's scope and strength. Wrong answers are usually **too narrow**, **too broad**, **opposite** (reversing the author's view), **distorted** (stronger or weaker than the passage), or **outside the passage** (true in the real world but unsupported).

@example
title: Mapping a short passage
problem: "Many people assume that sleep is a passive state. Research on memory suggests otherwise. During sleep the brain replays recent experience, strengthening some memories and discarding others. Sleep is therefore better described as active maintenance than as rest." Map the passage and state its main point.
step: Sentence 1 reports a common view ("many people assume"). The author's voice begins with "Research... suggests otherwise."
step: Sentences 2 and 3 are the evidence: research shows the brain replays and sorts memories.
step: Sentence 4 begins with "therefore," so it is the conclusion: sleep is better described as active maintenance than as rest.
step: Map: common view, then evidence, then conclusion. Main point: sleep is an active process of maintenance, not passive rest. A correct main-idea answer mentions memory as support but does not make it the main point.

@mistakes
- Treating a view the author reports as the author's own.
- Reading every sentence with equal attention and losing the main point.
- Choosing an answer that is true but covers only one paragraph (too narrow) or goes beyond the passage (too broad).
- Using outside knowledge instead of what the passage says.
