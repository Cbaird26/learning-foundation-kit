@article
An **argument** on the LSAT is a set of statements in which some (the **premises**) are offered as reasons to accept another (the **conclusion**). Every Logical Reasoning question begins with reading the stimulus as an argument, and the first job is always to find the conclusion. If you misidentify it, nearly every later step goes wrong.

### Finding the conclusion

Use two tools together.

1. **Indicator words.** Conclusion words: *therefore, thus, hence, so, it follows that, consequently, clearly*. Premise words: *because, since, for, given that, after all, as shown by*.
2. **The "why?" test.** Put a candidate sentence in front of "why should I believe that?" The statement that the others answer is the conclusion. The statement that is only answered by others but answers nothing itself is a premise.

The conclusion can come first, last or in the middle. LSAT writers often hide it behind an opening of background or an opposing view, for example: "Many commentators think X. But this is mistaken, because Y." Here *Many commentators think X* is a **counterpoint**, "X is mistaken" is the conclusion, and Y is a premise.

### Pieces other than premise and conclusion

- **Background** sets the scene and supports nothing: "The city council held a vote last week."
- **Opposing viewpoint** is a view the author rejects or concedes; words such as *although*, *some argue*, *it is often said* mark it.
- **Intermediate conclusion** is a statement that is supported by other statements and itself supports the main conclusion. It is a conclusion with respect to what is below and a premise with respect to what is above.

### Main-point and role questions

A **main-point question** ("Which one of the following most accurately expresses the main conclusion?") has a correct answer that restates the conclusion, not a premise and not a detail. Wrong answers are usually premises, intermediate conclusions or statements that go beyond the author. A **role-of-a-statement question** ("The claim that ... figures in the argument as ...") asks what job one sentence does: main conclusion, intermediate conclusion, premise, opposing view or background. For these, label every sentence first, then match the claim to its label.

### Strength matters

Pay attention to hedges and strong words. If the author says "may," an answer saying "will" is wrong. If the author says "some," an answer saying "all" is wrong.

### Practice with timing

Aim for about a minute and a half per question on average. Spend the first ten seconds underlining the indicator words and bracketing the conclusion. Then predict what a correct main-point answer would say before reading the choices. This is the habit that will carry through every later week: **read the stimulus as an argument, predict, then evaluate the choices.**

### Your log for the diagnostic

Your timed diagnostic from Week 1 gets the second half of its value now. For every miss in a Logical Reasoning section, record the question type and cause. Misidentifying the conclusion is the most common cause of errors on main-point, assumption, flaw and strengthen/weaken questions, so tag every such miss "wrong conclusion" and count them. If the count is high, drill conclusion-spotting before anything else.

@example
title: Hidden conclusion
problem: "Some say the new bus lane will speed up commutes. But the lane takes space from cars, which already crawl at rush hour, and buses will have to merge back into traffic at every intersection. So the lane will not speed up commutes." What is the main conclusion?
step: Mark the indicator words. "Some say" signals a counterpoint, and "So" signals a conclusion.
step: Apply the why test. Why believe the lane will not speed up commutes? Because the lane takes space from cars and buses must merge back at every intersection. Those are premises.
step: The sentence "Some say ... will speed up commutes" is the opposing view the author rejects.
step: The main conclusion is that the new bus lane will not speed up commutes. A correct answer to a main-point question restates exactly this, with nothing stronger added.

@mistakes
- Choosing the first or the most interesting sentence as the conclusion instead of applying the "why?" test.
- Treating an opposing view the author rejects as the author's own conclusion.
- Picking a premise because it appears in the answer choices word for word.
- Ignoring hedge words and choosing an answer that is stronger than the author's claim.

@practice mc
prompt: "The museum's attendance fell this year because ticket prices rose and the nearby parking lot closed." What is the conclusion of this argument?
choice: The museum's ticket prices rose.
choice*: The museum's attendance fell this year because of the price increase and the parking closure.
choice: The nearby parking lot closed.
choice: Ticket prices should be lowered.
hint: Ask what the author is trying to get you to believe, and what the other statements are offered to support.
solution: The "because" clause gives reasons; the claim they support is that attendance fell for those reasons. No recommendation to lower prices is made.

@practice mc
prompt: "Residents argue that the new ordinance will cut noise. However, the ordinance exempts the loudest sources, namely construction and aircraft. Thus the ordinance will not cut noise much." Which statement best expresses the main conclusion?
choice: Residents argue that the ordinance will cut noise.
choice: The ordinance exempts construction and aircraft.
choice*: The ordinance will not cut noise much.
choice: The loudest sources of noise are construction and aircraft.
hint: Find the conclusion word and the counterpoint word.
solution: "Thus" marks the conclusion. The residents' claim is the rejected view, and the exemptions and loudness claim are premises.

@practice mc
prompt: "Plants grown in the lab's new soil mix grew taller. Therefore the mix is better for growth. Because it is better for growth, farmers should adopt it." In the argument, the claim that the mix is better for growth plays which role?
choice: It is the main conclusion.
choice*: It is an intermediate conclusion, supported by the lab result and supporting the recommendation to adopt it.
choice: It is background information.
choice: It is the opposing view that the argument rejects.
hint: Check what supports the claim and what it supports in turn.
solution: The claim is supported by the height result and in turn supports the final recommendation, so it is intermediate. The main conclusion is that farmers should adopt the mix.

@practice ms
prompt: Which of the following words or phrases usually signal a premise rather than a conclusion?
choice*: because
choice*: since
choice: therefore
choice: it follows that
hint: Premise indicators introduce reasons.
solution: "Because" and "since" introduce reasons for a claim. "Therefore" and "it follows that" introduce a claim drawn from reasons.

@practice self
prompt: Write a four-sentence argument with a background sentence, an opposing view, one premise and a conclusion. Label each sentence, then write the conclusion in your own words as a correct main-point answer.
hint: Plan your answer against the rubric before you write, then compare.
solution: Background: The school district is considering a longer school day. Opposing view: Supporters claim extra hours will raise test scores. Premise: However, studies of tired students show that learning drops sharply after the sixth hour. Conclusion: Therefore a longer school day will not raise test scores. Main-point answer: The proposed longer school day will fail to raise test scores.
rubric: Each of the four sentences is labeled correctly.
rubric: The conclusion contains an indicator word or passes the "why?" test.
rubric: The main-point answer restates the conclusion without adding a stronger claim.

@quiz mc
prompt: "The town has argued that the old bridge must be demolished. But demolition costs twice as much as repair, and repair would meet the safety code. So the bridge should be repaired." The claim that demolition costs twice as much as repair figures in the argument as
choice: the main conclusion.
choice*: a premise offered against the view that the bridge must be demolished.
choice: an opposing view the author rejects.
choice: background with no support role.
hint: Label every sentence before you assign roles.
solution: The cost claim is a reason to reject demolition and support repair, so it is a premise. The main conclusion is that the bridge should be repaired.

@quiz mc
prompt: "Critics claim that the new reading program failed because it was expensive. Yet the program's cost per student was lower than the old program's, and test scores rose in every grade where it was used. Hence the program did not fail." What is the main conclusion?
choice: The new reading program failed because it was expensive.
choice: Test scores rose in every grade where the program was used.
choice*: The new reading program did not fail.
choice: The program cost less per student than the old program.
hint: Look for the conclusion word and the claim the critics make.
solution: The critics' claim is the rejected view. "Hence" introduces the main conclusion, and the cost and score claims are premises.
