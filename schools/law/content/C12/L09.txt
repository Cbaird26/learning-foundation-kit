@article
By now you know the question types. Timing turns knowledge into score. Each scored section lasts 35 minutes, and a Logical Reasoning section contains roughly 24 to 26 questions, so you have about **1.4 minutes per question** on average. Reading Comprehension, with about 27 questions across four passages, allows about **8 to 9 minutes per passage**. These are averages, and you will not spend them evenly.

### Not all questions are equal

Every question is worth one point regardless of difficulty, and the questions are roughly ordered from easier to harder, though not strictly. A hard question that takes four minutes costs you the time to answer three easier ones. A good pacing plan is therefore:

1. **First pass:** work through the section in order, answering what you can in the time you expect to spend (about 1 minute on early questions).
2. **Flag and move on** any question that stalls you at about 90 seconds. Mark your best guess so something is entered.
3. **Second pass:** return to flagged questions with the time you saved.
4. **Last minute:** make sure every question has an answer.

A checkpoint pace for Logical Reasoning: about 10 questions answered by the 12-minute mark and about 17 by the 22-minute mark. Adjust to your own results from timed practice.

### Guessing and expected value

There is **no penalty for wrong answers**, so leaving a question blank can never raise your score. Each question has five choices, so a blind guess has a $1/5$ chance of being right. The **expected number correct** from $n$ guesses with probability $p$ each is $np$. Eliminating wrong choices raises $p$: with two eliminated, $p = 1/3$; with three, $p = 1/2$.

This is why elimination matters even for questions you cannot solve. A mechanical rule: never spend time on a question after you have eliminated what you can and have a better-than-chance pick; move on. In the last minute, guess on every unanswered question, even with no elimination.

### Process habits under time

- Read the question stem first to know the task (weaken, assumption, main point), then read the stimulus with that task in mind.
- Predict before the choices.
- Cross out wrong choices actively, and don't rewrite an entire argument.
- On Reading Comprehension, don't linger on a detail you can look up later.
- Don't carry frustration forward; reset after each question.

### Building the practice routine

1. **Untimed accuracy first**, so you can analyze reasoning without clock pressure.
2. **Timed sections** as your score approaches the target, with your error log updated after each.
3. **Full tests** at the real start time of your test, with proper breaks, every week or two near test day.
4. **Review** takes longer than the test: for each miss, write why the right answer is right and why yours is tempting.

### Test-day and logistics

The LSAT is delivered digitally. Registration, testing options (test center or remote), accommodations, score-release timing, fees, fee waivers and limits on retakes are set by LSAC and change; read LSAC's current information before you choose a date, and plan so a retake would still fit inside the application cycle of the schools you plan to apply to. **Check each school's current requirements**, including whether it accepts other tests and how it treats multiple scores.

@example
title: Pacing with a checkpoint
problem: A Logical Reasoning section has 26 questions in 35 minutes. You plan to reach question 14 at the 17-minute mark. How many minutes per question does that leave for the remaining questions, assuming you finish all 26?
step: Time already used at the checkpoint is 17 minutes for 13 questions (you are starting question 14), about 1.3 minutes each.
step: Time left: $35 - 17 = 18$ minutes.
step: Questions left: $26 - 13 = 13$, which include the harder end of the section.
step: $18/13 \approx 1.4$ minutes per question. The checkpoint keeps your average on budget, and a flagged-and-return question is the tool for staying on it.

@mistakes
- Leaving questions blank when there is no penalty for guessing.
- Sinking three or four minutes into one question because you "almost" have it.
- Skipping a question without entering a provisional answer, then running out of time.
- Doing only untimed practice, so that pace collapses on test day.

@quiz num
prompt: A section has 24 questions in 35 minutes. You finish the first 14 in 17 minutes. How many minutes per question can you spend on the remaining questions, on average?
answer: 9/5
check: (35 - 17) / (24 - 14)
unit: minutes
hint: Find the time left and the number of questions left.
solution: Time left is $35 - 17 = 18$ minutes for $24 - 14 = 10$ questions, so $18/10 = 9/5 = 1.8$ minutes per question.
