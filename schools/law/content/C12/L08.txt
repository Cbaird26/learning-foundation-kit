@article
One of the four Reading Comprehension sets is a **comparative pair**: Passage A and Passage B on related topics, followed by questions about each and about both. The pair requires the same structural reading as a single passage, with one more task: relating the two.

### Map each passage separately

Read Passage A and write its one-line main point and attitude. Read Passage B and do the same. Resist reading B through A's eyes. Many errors come from attributing one author's view to the other.

### State the relationship

After mapping, say in one sentence how B relates to A. The common relationships are:

- **Agreement:** the passages reach the same conclusion, perhaps with different evidence.
- **Disagreement:** they reach opposing conclusions about the same issue.
- **Extension:** B applies, qualifies or builds on A's point.
- **Partial agreement:** B accepts part of A (a fact or a concern) but disagrees about its significance or what to do.
- **Different aspects:** A and B examine different sides of one subject.

Test the relationship by asking whether one author would accept the other's central claim, and which parts.

### Questions about both passages

- **What both passages share:** the answer is supported by *each* passage. A choice true of A only is wrong.
- **Relationship:** pick the verb that describes B's stance toward A ("accepts the premise but rejects the conclusion").
- **How would the author of A respond to B?** Base your answer on A's own claims. The best answer extends A's logic to B's point, in A's strength and tone.
- **Differences in method or emphasis:** one passage may use an example, a case study or a general principle while the other uses another.

### Inference questions in Reading Comprehension

An RC **inference question** ("it can be inferred from the passage that...", "the author would most likely agree...") asks for the choice **most strongly supported** by the text. As in Logical Reasoning, the best choice is modest, a restatement or a combination of two things the text says. Watch for:

- **Extreme words** (*always, never, all, only*) that the passage does not support.
- **Reversals** of a stated relationship.
- **Outside knowledge** that happens to be true.
- **Scope shifts:** the passage is about one city or one period, and the choice is about all of them.

### The timed-passage deliverable

This block's deliverable is two timed passages (8.75 minutes each, or a comparative pair), followed by structural maps. After finishing, compare your map to the passage: did each paragraph's job match? Then write the reason for each missed question in your error log. Passage questions you answered correctly for the wrong reasons count as misses in the log.

### The midterm and your error log

The midterm covers Weeks 1 to 7, including the first half of the Reading Comprehension toolkit. Whether you take it just before or just after this lesson, review your error-log patterns, redo every miss untimed, and then retry it timed.

@example
title: Relating two short passages
problem: Passage A: "Cities should require every new building to include bike parking, because commuters will not cycle without a safe place to leave a bike." Passage B: "Bike parking helps, but commuters cite unsafe roads, not parking, as the main barrier to cycling; the money would be better spent on protected lanes." State the relationship, and what both passages agree on.
step: Map A: main point is that new buildings should include bike parking, because parking is a prerequisite for cycling.
step: Map B: main point is that parking helps but is not the main barrier; funds should go to protected lanes.
step: Relationship: B partly agrees (parking helps) but disagrees with A about priority, arguing the barrier is road safety.
step: What both accept: bike parking has some value for cycling. A's author would probably reply that parking is also essential, while B's author would say it is secondary. Neither passage claims that parking is useless.

@mistakes
- Attributing one passage's view to the other, or choosing an answer true of only one passage when the question asks about both.
- Choosing an extreme inference ("never," "only," "all") when the passage supports a modest claim.
- Answering "how would A's author respond to B?" with the answer you would give instead of what A's logic implies.
- Using outside knowledge about the topic to justify an answer.

Passage B: "Standardized grading is fair only if the standards themselves fit the students; otherwise it fixes the same unfairness everywhere." hint: Plan your answer against the rubric before you write, then compare.
solution: A: Standardized grading is fair because it keeps standards consistent. B: Standardized grading is fair only when the standards fit students; consistency alone can spread an unfair standard. Relationship: B accepts A's point that consistency is a fairness value but adds a condition that A overlooks.
rubric: Each map states the author's claim in one line.
rubric: The relationship names agreement and disagreement parts.
rubric: The answer does not attribute B's condition to A.
