@article
This week covers three question types that all ask you to think about an argument's **structure** rather than its subject matter: **principle**, **parallel reasoning** and **method of argument**. Each rewards abstraction: replace the topic with letters and look at the skeleton.

### Principle questions

A **principle** is a general rule that covers specific cases. Principle questions come in two forms.

- **Identify the principle** ("Which of the following principles most helps to justify the reasoning?"): the principle is a broad statement that, added to the premises, supports the conclusion. It works like a sufficient assumption stated generally. The right answer is general enough to cover the case, but not so broad that it says more than the argument needs.
- **Apply the principle** ("Which of the following judgments most closely conforms to the principle?"): you are given a rule and must pick the case it governs. Focus on the rule's **trigger** (what conditions activate it) and its **result** (what it requires or forbids). A wrong answer typically matches the topic but not the trigger, or triggers the rule but gives the wrong result.

### Parallel reasoning

A **parallel-reasoning question** asks for the argument whose reasoning is **most similar in structure** to the one in the stimulus. Topic is irrelevant. Compare, in order:

1. The **type of conclusion** (a must-be-true claim, a recommendation, a prediction, a causal claim).
2. The **form of the premises**: conditionals, "most," "some," "no."
3. The **strength** of the words: certain ("must"), probable ("probably") or possible ("could").
4. If the original is **flawed**, the correct answer has the *same flaw*; an answer that is logically valid is wrong, however similar its surface.

Replace each term with a letter: "All A are B. x is B. So x is A" is a famous flawed pattern (the converse). Then compare every choice to it.

### Method of argument

A **method question** ("The argument proceeds by...") asks you to describe how the author gets from premises to conclusion, in abstract language. Common methods: offering a **counterexample** to a general claim, drawing an **analogy**, offering an **alternative explanation** for evidence, pointing out that a **premise conflicts with the conclusion**, or arguing from a **general principle** to a case. In two-speaker stimuli ("Dev says... Mia replies..."), the question often asks how the second speaker responds to the first: by questioning the evidence, by challenging an assumption or by changing the subject.

### Linking to your diagrams

Conditional reasoning appears everywhere here. When a principle is a conditional ("A person who ... must ..."), diagram it, take the contrapositive and test each case against it. Your deliverable for this block is a diagram of 15 conditional inference questions: for each, write the arrows, the contrapositives and the chain, then check each choice. Time yourself; with practice, translation should take under a minute.

### Strategy

Always predict. For a principle question, state the principle in your own words before the choices. For parallel reasoning, write the skeleton in letters. For method, write one verb ("counterexample," "alternative explanation") and match it. If two choices seem close, check strength words.

@example
title: Matching the skeleton
problem: "Every dish cooked by Chen is gluten-free. This dish was cooked by Chen. So this dish is gluten-free." Which is most parallel? (a) Every map drawn by Kim is to scale. This map was drawn by Kim. So this map is to scale. (b) Every map drawn by Kim is to scale. This map is to scale. So Kim drew this map.
step: Write the skeleton of the original: All A are B. x is A. So x is B. The reasoning is valid.
step: Choice (a) has the form All A are B; x is A; so x is B. Same structure and same validity.
step: Choice (b) is All A are B; x is B; so x is A. This reverses the conditional and is flawed.
step: Choose (a). The topic (cooking versus maps) is irrelevant; the form decides.

@mistakes
- Matching topic or wording instead of logical structure in parallel questions.
- Picking a valid answer when the original is flawed, or a flawed answer when the original is valid.
- Choosing a principle that is too broad (it overreaches) or too narrow (it fits only the facts).
- Describing an argument's method in terms of its subject matter instead of in abstract terms.

prompt: Principle: "An employer may not penalize an employee for taking leave to which the employee is entitled." Which action violates the principle?
choice*: A manager lowers Dana's performance rating because she took leave she was entitled to.
choice: A manager denies leave to an employee who is not entitled to any.
choice: A manager lowers Dana's rating because she missed deadlines unrelated to any leave.
choice: A company reduces every employee's pay during a recession.
hint: Check the trigger (penalty for entitled leave), not just the topic of leave.
solution: Only the choice in which a manager lowers Dana's rating because she took entitled leave penalizes an employee for that leave. The others involve no entitlement, an unrelated reason or a general measure.

hint: Write the skeleton in letters, then compare quantifiers and the direction of the reasoning.

@practice self
prompt: Diagram each of these as an arrow with its contrapositive: (a) "Every participant who consents is enrolled." (b) "No enrolled participant may be under 18." (c) "A participant is paid only if the form is signed." (d) "The study continues unless funding ends." (e) "No unsigned form is accepted."
hint: Plan your answer against the rubric before you write, then compare.
solution: (a) consent $\to$ enrolled; not enrolled $\to$ no consent. (b) enrolled $\to$ not under 18; under 18 $\to$ not enrolled. (c) paid $\to$ signed; unsigned $\to$ not paid. (d) funding does not end $\to$ study continues; study does not continue $\to$ funding ends. (e) unsigned $\to$ not accepted; accepted $\to$ signed.
rubric: Arrows go from sufficient to necessary term in each line.
rubric: Each contrapositive is correctly reversed and negated.
rubric: "Unless" and "only if" are translated correctly.

@quiz num
prompt: You plan to diagram 18 conditional inference questions. You expect to spend 3 minutes diagramming and 1.5 minutes checking each. How many minutes do you need in all?
answer: 81
check: 18 * (3 + 1.5)
unit: minutes
hint: Add the time spent on one question.
hint: Multiply by the number of questions.
solution: Each question takes $3 + 1.5 = 4.5$ minutes, and $18 \times 4.5 = 81$ minutes.

choice: Every licensed driver passed a test. Lena is a licensed driver. So Lena passed a test.
choice: Most licensed drivers passed a test. Lena is a licensed driver. So Lena probably passed a test.
