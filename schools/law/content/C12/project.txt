@review
### Week by week

- **Week 1, the LSAT and scoring.** Two scored Logical Reasoning sections, one scored Reading Comprehension section and one unscored section (replaced from August 2027 by embedded unscored questions); a separate unscored Argumentative Writing task that schools receive; raw score converted to 120 to 180; no penalty for wrong answers. Take a diagnostic and keep an error log.
- **Week 2, arguments.** Find the conclusion with indicator words and the "why?" test; separate premises, background and opposing views; main-point and role questions.
- **Week 3, assumption and flaw.** Find the gap; necessary versus sufficient assumptions; negation test; common flaws.
- **Week 4, strengthen, weaken, evaluate.** Act on the assumption; rule out or offer rival causes; evaluate by asking which answer cuts both ways; resolve a paradox with a fact that explains both sides.
- **Week 5, inference.** Conditional arrows, contrapositives, chains; "some," "most" and "all."
- **Week 6, principle, parallel, method.** Match structure, strength and flaw; describe method in abstract terms.
- **Week 7, passage structure.** Map main point, paragraph jobs, voice and attitude.
- **Week 8, comparative passages.** Map each passage; state the relationship; modest inferences.
- **Week 9, timing and guessing.** About 1.4 minutes per Logical Reasoning question; flag and return; guess on every question.
- **Week 10, Argumentative Writing.** Prewrite, state a thesis, give reasons from the prompt, answer the best objection.
- **Week 11, schools and money.** Reach, target and likely tiers; outcomes data; net cost; loan growth $P(1+r)^n$; check current federal loan rules.
- **Week 12, application materials.** Specific personal statement, one-page résumé, early and informed recommendation requests, honest addenda.

### Self-check

- Can I state the conclusion of any stimulus in under ten seconds?
- Can I write the negation of "all" and "some," and say what "most" means?
- Can I write the contrapositive of a conditional with "only if" or "unless"?
- Can I describe a passage in a one-line-per-paragraph map?
- Do I know my pace checkpoints and my guessing rule?
- Does my school list have a written reason for every school?

### Common errors

- Choosing a premise as the conclusion.
- Strengthening when the stem says weaken.
- Using the converse of a conditional.
- Treating a reported view as the author's.
- Leaving questions blank.
- Comparing sticker prices instead of net cost.
- Writing a personal statement that repeats the résumé.

Always check each school's current requirements and LSAC's current rules for registration, scoring and retakes.

@exam num
prompt: On a timed set you answered 58 questions correctly out of 66 that you answered with an actual attempt. For the remaining 9 questions you eliminated two of the five choices and guessed at random among the remaining three. Using expected values for the guesses, what is your expected raw score?
answer: 61
check: 58 + 9 * (1 / 3)
hint: Your 58 correct answers are fixed.
hint: Each guess has a one-in-three chance of being right.
solution: Expected correct guesses are $9 \times \tfrac{1}{3} = 3$, so the expected raw score is $58 + 3 = 61$.

@exam mc
prompt: "Applicants who took the Ridgeway prep course scored higher on the LSAT than applicants who did not. So the Ridgeway course raises LSAT scores." Which of the following, if true, most weakens the argument?
choice: Many applicants who took the course said they enjoyed it.
choice*: Applicants who enrolled in the course had already scored higher on a diagnostic test than other applicants before they enrolled.
choice: The Ridgeway course costs more than other prep courses.
choice: Some applicants who took the course did not improve.
hint: Think about the assumption that the groups were similar before the course.
solution: If course takers were already stronger, their higher scores can be explained without the course. Enjoyment, cost and a few non-improvers do not undercut the average difference.

@exam self
prompt: An applicant has an LSAT score of 166 and a GPA of 3.7. A school's LSAT range is 163 to 168 and GPA range 3.6 to 3.9. It charges \$60,000 tuition and \$22,000 living costs per year, and offers a scholarship of \$18,000 per year that requires a 3.2 GPA after the first year, which about 40 percent of recipients lose. (a) Classify the school for the applicant. (b) Compute the three-year net cost if the scholarship is kept, and if it is lost after year one. (c) Write one sentence for a school-list reason and one question you would ask the school about the scholarship.
hint: Compare each number to the school's ranges, then compute yearly cost with and without the scholarship.
solution: (a) Both numbers fall inside the school's ranges, so it is a target school. (b) Kept: $3 \times (60{,}000 + 22{,}000 - 18{,}000) = 192{,}000$. Lost after year one: $(82{,}000 - 18{,}000) + 2 \times 82{,}000 = 228{,}000$. (c) Reason: a target school whose ranges match the applicant's, with strong placement in the region (to be verified in the school's disclosures). Question: What share of scholarship recipients met the GPA condition in each of the last three years, and is there an appeal process?
rubric: Classification uses both LSAT and GPA against the ranges.
rubric: Both net-cost figures are computed correctly.
rubric: The reason is specific, and the question addresses the scholarship condition.
