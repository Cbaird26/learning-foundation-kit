@article
Your numbers get a file read; your essays and letters get it understood. The application materials answer a question the LSAT cannot: who are you, and what will you contribute to a law school and the profession?

### The personal statement

Most schools ask for a personal statement of about two pages double-spaced, and your project sets a target of about **650 words**; **check each school's instructions for length, format and topic**. It is a short essay, not a résumé in paragraphs and not a statement of purpose that says "I want to help people."

**Choose a focus.** One theme with one or two concrete episodes beats a tour of your life. Good focuses include a problem you worked on, a challenge that shaped how you think, an experience that changed your mind or a skill you built. The topic matters less than the quality of the thinking.

**Show, then reflect.** Open with a specific moment (a conversation, a task, a decision) rather than a generalization. Then reflect: what did you learn and how did it change what you do? Admissions readers want evidence of judgment, maturity and writing, the same abilities the Argumentative Writing task samples.

**Avoid common traps.** Clichés ("I have always been passionate about justice"), a list of achievements already on your résumé, quotations in the first line, legal jargon you cannot support, and overstated claims that your experience prepared you for the courtroom.

**Identity and experience.** In *Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard* (2023), the Court held that race-conscious admissions programs at Harvard and UNC violate the Equal Protection Clause. Chief Justice Roberts's opinion also said that a university may consider an applicant's discussion of how race has affected their life, as long as it is tied to a quality of that applicant's character or ability and treated as an individual quality. Describe your own experience and what you took from it; schools' instructions and policies vary, so follow each one.

**Honesty and tools.** Every fact must be true. Many schools have policies on the use of generative AI in application materials, and some prohibit it; read each school's policy. Whatever your tools, the essay must be your own thinking in your own voice.

### The résumé

Keep it to **one page** unless a school allows more. Use reverse chronological order within sections (education, work, activities, honors, skills). Start bullets with strong verbs, state **results with numbers** where they are true ("trained 12 volunteers"), and use consistent formatting. Do not pad with high-school items if you have college experience, and do not list responsibilities when you can show outcomes.

### Recommendations

Most schools ask for two or three letters, and academic recommenders are often preferred for applicants still in college; a recommender who knows your work well, such as a professor who graded your papers, writes a stronger letter than a famous person who barely knows you. **Ask at least six to eight weeks before the earliest deadline**, in person or by a polite message, and ask whether the person can write a strong letter. Supply a packet: your résumé, personal statement draft, a list of the schools and deadlines, and a reminder of what you did in their course. Letters are usually submitted through LSAC's system; verify the process and your waiver of access (a standard choice that tells schools you will not read the letter).

### Addenda, character and fitness

An **addendum** is a short, factual explanation of a one-time problem (a low semester GPA due to illness, a gap in your record). Keep it brief, without blame. **Answer every character and fitness question truthfully**, including past discipline or arrests, because an omission is treated far more seriously than the underlying event. Bar examiners later ask the same questions.

@example
title: Revising a vague opening
problem: Revise this first sentence of a personal statement: "I have always been passionate about justice and helping people."
step: Diagnose the problem: it is a cliché, states a trait without evidence and could be written by anyone.
step: Find a specific moment from your own experience, for instance helping a neighbor read a lease. Write the scene in one or two sentences with a concrete detail.
step: Revised opening: "The lease was eleven pages long, and my neighbor had signed it without turning a single one."
step: Then reflect in the next lines: what the moment taught you about the gap between rights on paper and rights in practice. The scene shows the trait without announcing it.

@mistakes
- Opening with a cliché or a quotation instead of a specific moment.
- Repeating the résumé in the personal statement instead of showing judgment and reflection.
- Asking for a recommendation only two weeks before the deadline, or choosing a well-known person who does not know your work.
- Leaving out or minimizing a fact on a character and fitness question.

@quiz mc
prompt: A student's GPA dropped sharply in one semester because of a serious illness. How should she handle this in her applications?
choice: Say nothing and hope that readers overlook it.
choice*: Include a short, factual addendum that explains the cause and shows later recovery, without blaming others.
choice: Write a three-page personal statement about the semester.
choice: Ask her recommenders to hide it.
hint: Which approach is brief, honest and informative?
solution: A short factual addendum gives context without drama, and the transcript shows recovery. Silence invites guesses, and asking recommenders to hide it would be dishonest.
