@article
Most LSAT arguments are **gappy**: the premises, even if true, do not guarantee the conclusion, because something connecting them is left unsaid. That unstated connection is the **assumption**. Assumption and flaw questions are two sides of one skill, finding the gap. An assumption question asks what must fill the gap; a flaw question asks you to describe the mistake the gap represents.

### Find the gap first

After you bracket the conclusion and premises, ask: "What would have to be true for these premises to establish this conclusion?" The gap is often a **term shift**: the premises talk about one thing (test scores) and the conclusion about another (learning), or the premises talk about one group (survey respondents) and the conclusion about another (all residents). The assumption bridges the two.

### Necessary versus sufficient assumptions

- A **necessary assumption** is something the argument cannot do without. If it is false, the argument fails. The wording is usually "The argument depends on which of the following assumptions?" Necessary assumptions tend to be weak and cautious ("not entirely," "at least some").
- A **sufficient assumption** is something that, if added to the premises, guarantees the conclusion. The wording is usually "Which of the following, if assumed, enables the conclusion to be properly drawn?" Sufficient assumptions tend to be strong.

### The negation test

To test a candidate for a necessary assumption, negate it. If the negation destroys the argument, the candidate is necessary. Negate carefully: the negation of "all" is "at least one is not"; the negation of "some are" is "none are"; the negation of "will" is "will not necessarily."

### The common flaws

1. **Correlation versus causation.** Two things go together, so one is said to cause the other, ignoring reverse causation, a common cause or coincidence.
2. **Unrepresentative sample.** A conclusion about a population rests on a sample that may differ from it, or is too small or self-selected.
3. **Part and whole.** What is true of the parts is said to be true of the whole, or conversely.
4. **Equivocation.** A word shifts meaning between premise and conclusion.
5. **Necessary versus sufficient.** The author treats a requirement as a guarantee, or the reverse.
6. **Attacking the source** instead of the claim, or accepting a claim only because an authority said it.
7. **False dichotomy.** Only two options are offered when others exist.
8. **Circularity.** The conclusion is restated as a premise.
9. **Assuming that what is possible is certain, or that a lack of proof of a claim proves its negation.**

On a flaw question, the correct answer describes the mistake in abstract terms, such as "fails to consider that the correlation could be explained by a third factor." Match the description to the argument; do not choose a flaw that sounds familiar but does not occur.

### Working a set

Your deliverable for this block is 30 assumption and flaw questions with an error log. Do each question in order: conclusion, premise, gap, prediction, choices. After scoring, tag each miss: wrong conclusion, missed gap, attractive trap (too strong, out of scope, reversed), or timing. The patterns tell you what to drill.

@example
title: Negation test on a causal argument
problem: "A district gave every student a tablet. Test scores in the district then rose by 8 points. So the tablets raised the scores." Test the candidate assumption: "The rise in scores was not due entirely to something other than the tablets."
step: Identify the gap. The argument moves from a change that followed the tablets to the claim that the tablets caused it.
step: Negate the candidate carefully: "The rise in scores was due entirely to something other than the tablets."
step: If the entire rise came from another source, the tablets did not cause it, so the conclusion collapses. The negation destroys the argument.
step: The candidate is therefore a necessary assumption. It is weak ("not entirely"), which is typical. A stronger candidate such as "Tablets always raise scores" would fail the test, because the argument only needs the claim for this district.

@mistakes
- Choosing an answer that is true and relevant but that the argument does not need (a plausible background fact, not a necessary link).
- Negating "all" as "none," so that the test gives the wrong verdict.
- Picking an answer that is too strong for a necessary assumption question (words like "always," "only," "every").
- Naming a flaw that sounds familiar when the argument contains a different gap.

prompt: "The council surveyed people leaving the city's farmers' market and found that 90 percent support raising the market's funding. So a large majority of city residents support raising the market's funding." What does the argument assume?
choice: Most residents have visited the market at some point.
choice*: The people surveyed leaving the market are not substantially different from city residents in their views on funding.
choice: The market's funding is lower than that of other city programs.
choice: Residents who dislike the market would not answer a survey.
hint: Compare the group in the premise with the group in the conclusion.
solution: The sample is people leaving the market, the conclusion is about all residents. The argument assumes the sample is representative on this question.

@practice self
prompt: Write a four-sentence argument whose conclusion depends on a necessary assumption. State the assumption, apply the negation test, and say whether it is necessary or sufficient and why.
hint: Plan your answer against the rubric before you write, then compare.
solution: Argument: The bakery added a loyalty card in March. Profits in April were higher than in February. So the loyalty card raised profits. Assumption: The rise in profits was not entirely due to something other than the card, such as seasonal demand. Negation: The rise was entirely due to something other than the card. With that negation the card did no work, so the conclusion collapses; the assumption is necessary. It is not sufficient, because it does not itself show that the card caused any increase.
rubric: The argument has an identifiable gap between premises and conclusion.
rubric: The assumption is stated in cautious wording and bridges the gap.
rubric: The negation is correct and its effect on the argument is explained.

@quiz mc
prompt: "The theater's revenue rose after it began selling tickets online. So online ticketing increased revenue." The argument depends on which assumption?
choice: Customers prefer online ticketing to buying at the box office.
choice*: The rise in revenue was not due entirely to some other change made at about the same time.
choice: Every theater that adopts online ticketing sees revenue rise.
choice: Online ticketing is cheaper to operate than the box office.
hint: Look for the claim whose negation would explain the revenue rise without the tickets.
solution: If another change caused the whole rise, online tickets did not. The other choices are either too strong, irrelevant to revenue growth, or not required.

@quiz mc
prompt: "Only staff who completed the safety course may use the saw. Rosa completed the safety course. So Rosa may use the saw." Which is the flaw?
choice: It attacks Rosa instead of the claim about the saw.
choice: It generalizes from a single case to all staff.
choice*: It treats a requirement as if it were a guarantee: it reasons from the necessary condition being met to the permission it is required for.
choice: It confuses "some staff" with "all staff."
hint: Restate the rule as a requirement and check which way the reasoning runs.
solution: The rule makes the course necessary for using the saw, not sufficient. The argument concludes that Rosa may use the saw from the fact that she met the requirement, but other requirements may also apply.
