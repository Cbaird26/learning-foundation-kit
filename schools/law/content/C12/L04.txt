@article
Once you can find an argument's assumption, strengthening and weakening are easy to understand: you **act on the assumption**. A correct strengthen answer makes the assumption more likely to be true; a correct weaken answer makes it less likely. Neither proves or refutes the argument. The answer only has to move the conclusion's likelihood, so a good strengthener is often modest.

### Strengthen

The standard moves are:

- **Support the assumption.** Add a fact that rules out a gap, for example "the two groups were similar in every other respect."
- **Rule out an alternative explanation.** In a causal argument, show that a rival cause was not present.
- **Add a new premise** that gives another reason for the conclusion.
- **Show the correlation holds in other cases** (for a causal argument).

### Weaken

- **Attack the assumption.** Show that the premises do not lead to the conclusion because of the gap.
- **Provide an alternative explanation.** A rival cause, reverse causation or coincidence.
- **Show the sample is unrepresentative.**
- **Provide a counterexample** to a general claim.

A weakener does not have to destroy the argument. "The evidence is less convincing than it appears" is enough. Beware the **half-right answer**: one that attacks a premise the author never needed.

### Principle for scale

Wrong answers are usually **irrelevant** (out of scope), **reversed** (they weaken when you needed strengthen), or **too extreme**. When choices all seem plausible, read the stem again: *strengthen* or *weaken*? Reversal is the single most common careless error on these questions.

### Evaluate

An **evaluate-the-argument question** asks, "Which of the following would be most useful to know in order to evaluate the argument?" The best answer is a question whose possible answers cut both ways: if the answer is yes, the argument gets stronger; if no, weaker. Test each choice by supposing both answers. If both outcomes do not move the conclusion, the choice is irrelevant.

### Resolve-the-paradox

A **paradox question** presents two facts that seem to conflict ("sales rose although prices rose"). The correct choice is a new fact that makes both possible. It does not weaken or strengthen anything and does not pick a side; it explains. Wrong answers explain one fact only, deepen the conflict, or are irrelevant.

### Time and approach

Predict first. Before the choices, write one line: "Assumption: X." Then evaluate each choice by asking, "Does this make X more likely or less?" For causal arguments, the standard rival explanations (third factor, reversed causation, coincidence) are the first things to check. As you finish your 30-question set, rework every miss by writing the assumption in your own words. That one-line habit often fixes more misses than additional volume.

### A word on proportion

Strengthen and weaken answers are rarely dramatic. A fact that changes a claim from "possible" to "likely" counts. When two choices both help, choose the one that more directly addresses the gap you identified.

@example
title: Weaken or strengthen by the same assumption
problem: "A gym introduced a free trainer session for new members. Over the next year its member retention rose from 60 to 70 percent. So the free sessions improved retention." Which of these would most weaken the argument? (a) The gym also cut its monthly fee by 20 percent during the same year. (b) The gym's trainers are certified. (c) Some members did not use the free session.
step: Find the assumption: the rise in retention was due to the free sessions, not to other changes.
step: Choice (a) offers a rival explanation, a price cut, that could raise retention by itself. It attacks the assumption directly.
step: Choice (b) is irrelevant to retention. Choice (c) is weak: members who skipped the session may be consistent with the effect being smaller, but it does not give a reason the sessions failed.
step: Choose (a). If instead the stem asked you to strengthen, you would look for a fact that rules out rival explanations, such as "the gym made no other changes that year."

@mistakes
- Reading "weaken" as "strengthen" or the reverse, and picking a correct-sounding answer for the wrong task.
- Picking an answer that merely restates a premise or adds irrelevant detail.
- On evaluate questions, choosing an answer that would help only if the answer is yes, without checking the other outcome.
- On paradox questions, choosing an answer that explains just one of the two facts.

@practice mc
prompt: "After the city added bike lanes, the number of cyclist injuries rose. So bike lanes make cycling more dangerous." Which of the following, if true, most weakens the argument?
choice*: The number of people cycling in the city tripled after the lanes were added, while the injury rate per cyclist fell.
choice: Most of the injured cyclists were not wearing helmets.
choice: The bike lanes cost more than the city originally expected.
choice: Injuries to drivers in the city also rose slightly.
hint: Compare the total number of injuries with injuries per cyclist.
solution: If many more people cycle, total injuries can rise while the risk per cyclist falls, which undermines the claim that the lanes make cycling more dangerous.

@practice mc
prompt: "A survey found that employees who work from home report higher job satisfaction. So allowing remote work will raise satisfaction at the firm." Which of the following most strengthens the argument?
choice: Remote workers in the survey were paid slightly more than their office counterparts.
choice*: Employees in the survey were assigned to work from home or in the office at random.
choice: Many employees say they would like to try remote work.
choice: The firm's satisfaction scores have been stable for years.
hint: What would eliminate the possibility that happier people simply chose remote work?
solution: Random assignment removes self-selection as an alternative explanation, strengthening the causal conclusion.

@practice mc
prompt: "The number of items Highland Pharmacy sold rose even though it raised prices on most items." Which fact would best resolve the apparent conflict?
choice: Other pharmacies in the area also raised prices.
choice*: A nearby pharmacy closed, sending many of its customers to Highland.
choice: Highland's prices are still above the national average.
choice: Highland's costs for most items rose.
hint: Find a fact that explains why customers kept coming despite higher prices.
solution: The extra customers from the closed pharmacy explain higher sales despite the price increase. The other choices do not explain why sales grew.

@practice ms
prompt: Which of the following moves would strengthen a causal argument that a new fertilizer increased crop yields?
choice*: Showing that the treated and untreated fields had the same soil quality.
choice*: Showing that the yield increase appeared in several different farms.
choice: Showing that the fertilizer is more expensive than the old one.
choice: Showing that the farmers who used it were more experienced.
hint: Strengthen by ruling out rival causes or repeating the effect.
solution: Equal soil quality removes a rival explanation and repetition across farms supports the causal claim. Cost is irrelevant, and greater farmer experience offers a rival explanation, which weakens.

@practice self
prompt: Take this argument: "The only difference between the two classes is that one used flash cards; that class scored higher, so flash cards improve scores." Write one fact that would strengthen it, one that would weaken it, and one question that would help you evaluate it, each with a sentence explaining why.
hint: Plan your answer against the rubric before you write, then compare.
solution: Strengthen: Students were assigned to the two classes at random; this rules out the explanation that stronger students chose flash cards. Weaken: The flash-card class was taught by a more experienced teacher; this gives a rival explanation. Evaluate: Did the two classes score the same on a pretest? If yes, the argument is stronger; if the flash-card class already led, it is weaker.
rubric: The strengthener acts on the assumption of no rival cause.
rubric: The weakener offers an alternative explanation or attacks the assumption.
rubric: The evaluating question could cut both ways.

@quiz mc
prompt: "Every year more people visit the lake park. So the park's new picnic area must have attracted them." Which of the following most weakens the argument?
choice: The picnic area is large and has many tables.
choice*: Visitor numbers had been rising by roughly the same amount each year before the picnic area opened.
choice: Some visitors said they liked the picnic area.
choice: The park also has hiking trails.
hint: Consider whether the trend would have continued without the picnic area.
solution: If attendance was already rising at the same rate, the picnic area was not needed to explain the increase.

@quiz mc
prompt: "A town banned plastic bags. A year later, litter collected on its streets fell by 15 percent. So the ban reduced litter." Which of the following would be most useful to know in evaluating the argument?
choice: Whether plastic bags are recyclable.
choice: Whether shoppers in the town liked the ban.
choice*: Whether litter also fell by a similar amount in nearby towns that had no ban.
choice: Whether the town's streets were cleaned on a regular schedule before the ban.
hint: Ask which answer, either way, would change your view of the ban's effect.
solution: If litter fell similarly elsewhere, something else may explain the decline; if it did not, the ban looks more likely to be the cause.
