@article
The **Law School Admission Test (LSAT)** is a standardized test administered by the Law School Admission Council (**LSAC**) and used by nearly all U.S. law schools as one measure of how well you read closely and reason under time pressure. It tests no legal knowledge. Everything you need is on the page, so the test rewards a skill you can train: careful reading, then disciplined reasoning.

### The current format

Since August 2024 the scored portion of the LSAT has had three sections: **two Logical Reasoning sections** and **one Reading Comprehension section**. A fourth section is unscored (LSAC uses it to try out new questions), and you are not told which section it is, so treat every section as if it counts. Logic games (Analytical Reasoning) are no longer on the test. Each section is timed at 35 minutes. Logical Reasoning sections contain short arguments, each followed by one question; Reading Comprehension contains four passages (one is a pair of comparative passages) with a set of questions each.

Separately, the **LSAT Argumentative Writing** task is an unscored writing exercise taken on your own computer, usually on a different day from the multiple-choice test. LSAC sends it to the schools you apply to, and it is required before your score is released. We treat it in Week 10.

LSAC has announced that from August 2027 the separate unscored section will be replaced by unscored questions embedded within the three scored sections. Either way, your job is the same: answer every question as if it counts.

### Scoring

Each scored question is worth one point. Your **raw score** is the number of scored questions you answer correctly. **There is no penalty for a wrong answer**, so a blank is always worse than a guess. LSAC converts the raw score to a **scaled score from 120 to 180** using a conversion table for that test form. The conversion adjusts for small differences in difficulty between forms, so the same scaled score means the same level of performance across test dates. The score report also shows a **percentile**, the share of recent test takers you outscored; because percentiles depend on the recent applicant pool, look at LSAC's current figures rather than relying on a table from a book.

### Why it matters and how schools use it

Schools publish the median LSAT and GPA of their enrolled classes, and admissions offices weigh the score alongside your GPA, essays, recommendations and experience. Many schools also report scholarship patterns tied to score. LSAC limits how many times you may test in a given period, and policies on score reporting, GRE acceptance and fee waivers change, so **check each school's current requirements and LSAC's current rules** before registering.

### The diagnostic and the error log

Start with a **timed diagnostic**: a full official practice test from LawHub under real time limits. Do not study first; you want an honest baseline. Then build an **error log**: a spreadsheet with one row per missed or guessed question, recording the section, question type, the correct answer, why you missed it (misread the stimulus, wrong-answer trap, timing, content gap) and the fix. Patterns in this log, not your total score, tell you what to practice.

@example
title: Reading a diagnostic result
problem: On a timed diagnostic you answered 18 of 25 Logical Reasoning questions correctly in the first section, 20 of 25 in the second, and 17 of 27 Reading Comprehension questions. Find your raw score, your overall accuracy to the nearest whole percent, and the section with the lowest accuracy.
step: The raw score is the total of correct answers: $18 + 20 + 17 = 55$.
step: The number of scored questions is $25 + 25 + 27 = 77$, so accuracy is $55/77 \approx 0.714$, or 71%.
step: Section accuracies are $18/25 = 72\%$, $20/25 = 80\%$ and $17/27 \approx 63\%$.
step: Reading Comprehension is the weakest section, so its error log rows get the first look. Remember that only the raw score is converted to the scaled score; the percentages are for your own planning.

@mistakes
- Treating the unscored section as a throwaway and coasting, when you cannot tell which section it is.
- Leaving questions blank when there is no penalty for wrong answers.
- Studying from a book's old conversion table or percentile chart instead of LSAC's current information.
- Logging only your score after a practice test, not why each question was missed.
