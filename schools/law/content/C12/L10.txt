@article
The **LSAT Argumentative Writing** task is a separate, unscored writing exercise. You take it online, on your own computer, in a window LSAC opens in its own system, usually on a different day from the multiple-choice test. LSAC's current format gives a period for **prewriting (about 15 minutes)** and then a period for **writing (about 35 minutes)**; check LSAC's current directions for exact timing and technical requirements. The writing is not part of the 120 to 180 score. It is sent with your score report to the law schools you apply to, and completing it is required for score release. Treat it as a writing sample that a reader at an admissions office may actually read.

### What the prompt looks like

The prompt presents a **decision** with two reasonable options and a set of **criteria** or facts that pull in different directions. There is no correct answer. You are asked to **choose one option** and **argue for it**. What is evaluated is the quality of your reasoning and writing, not the option you pick.

### The prewriting period

Use it. A strong plan is half the essay.

1. **List the criteria** the prompt gives and decide which matter most and why.
2. **Pick an option** and write your thesis in one sentence that names the choice and the main reason.
3. **Draft two or three reasons**, each tied to a criterion and a fact in the prompt.
4. **Anticipate the best objection** to your choice and decide how you will answer it.

### Structure of a strong essay

- **Introduction:** state the decision, your choice and the reason that matters most. Two to four sentences.
- **Body paragraphs:** each begins with a topic sentence that states one reason, then supports it with facts from the prompt, then ties back to the thesis.
- **Counterargument:** state the strongest case for the other option fairly, then explain why your choice still wins (the other option's benefit is smaller, less certain, or can be recovered by another means).
- **Conclusion:** restate the choice and the weighing that supports it. Do not add new arguments.

### Reasoning habits that carry over from Logical Reasoning

Everything you learned this semester applies. State the conclusion clearly; support it with premises that are actually in the prompt; surface your own assumptions ("this assumes enrollment stays stable") and say why they are reasonable; do not treat correlation as causation; do not claim certainty where the facts only support probability; and weigh trade-offs honestly instead of pretending your option has no costs.

### Style

Aim for plain, precise sentences, a logical order and a professional tone. Avoid slang, emotional appeals unrelated to the criteria, and new facts that the prompt does not give. Leave time to reread: a minute or two for missing words and unclear sentences is better than a new paragraph.

### Scoring yourself

Law schools do not publish a rubric of the kind that applies to the multiple-choice test, so we use a practical one: (1) a clear thesis, (2) reasons tied to the prompt's criteria, (3) a fair, answered counterargument, (4) organization and (5) clarity and correctness of language. For your deliverable, write a timed essay, then score it from 1 to 4 on each criterion and note one fix for each criterion below 3. Revise the weakest paragraph untimed to see the difference.

@example
title: From prompt to plan
problem: A library can fund one project this year: extended weekend hours (cost \$40,000) or a new children's reading room (cost \$40,000). The facts say weekday visits are steady, weekend visits are limited by hours, and the children's section is crowded on afternoons. Plan an essay that chooses one option.
step: List the criteria: reaching new users, relieving crowding, and the same cost for both options. Decide which matters most; here it is relieving a documented problem.
step: Thesis: the library should build the reading room, because it addresses a crowding problem that the facts document.
step: Reasons: (1) crowding is described in the prompt; (2) the crowding falls on afternoons, when families use the children's section most; (3) the cost is the same, so the benefit decides.
step: Counterargument: weekend hours would reach new users, but the prompt gives no evidence of unmet weekend demand beyond limited hours, so that benefit is less certain. Close with a sentence that restates the weighing.

@mistakes
- Spending almost no prewriting time and starting the essay without a thesis.
- Presenting the other option as foolish or ignoring its strongest point.
- Adding facts that are not in the prompt, or assuming facts without saying so.
- Ending with a new argument instead of a conclusion, or running out of time before the counterargument.

choice: Both options are good, and the town should decide soon.
choice*: The town should repave the road, because the documented damage claims show a current cost that the pool's benefit does not yet match.
choice: The town should build the pool because everybody likes swimming.
choice: Roads are more important than pools in every town.
hint: A strong thesis names the choice and the main reason, tied to the facts.
solution: The correct choice commits to an option and gives a reason from the prompt. The others avoid a choice, rely on an unsupported claim or overgeneralize.

@practice num
prompt: You plan an essay of one introduction of 60 words, three body paragraphs of 110 words each, a counterargument paragraph of 90 words and a conclusion of 50 words. How many words in all?
answer: 530
check: 60 + 3 * 110 + 90 + 50
hint: Add the body paragraphs together before adding the rest.
solution: $60 + 3 \times 110 + 90 + 50 = 530$ words.

@practice self
prompt: Take 15 minutes of prewriting and 35 minutes of writing to produce a timed essay on this prompt: "A museum can spend its annual acquisition budget on one painting by an established artist, or on three works by emerging artists. Visitor numbers are stable, the museum's collection has few recent works, and the single painting would be the most valuable item it owns. Choose one option and argue for it." Then score it from 1 to 4 on each of five criteria: thesis, reasons tied to the prompt, counterargument, organization, language. Note one fix for each criterion below 3.
hint: Plan your answer against the rubric before you write, then compare.
solution: Model essay outline: Thesis: buy the three emerging works, because the collection has few recent pieces and three works address that gap more than one painting would. Reason 1: the gap in recent work is stated in the prompt. Reason 2: visitor numbers are stable, so a single blockbuster is less needed to draw visitors. Reason 3: spreading the budget lowers risk if one work disappoints. Counterargument: the single painting would be the museum's most valuable item and may become a centerpiece; however, value is not need, and a centerpiece does not repair the gap. Conclusion: restate the weighing. A full essay would expand each point into a paragraph in a formal tone.
rubric: The essay has a clear thesis that chooses one option.
rubric: Each reason uses a fact or criterion from the prompt.
rubric: The counterargument is fair and answered.
rubric: The self-score is honest and each low score has a specific fix.
