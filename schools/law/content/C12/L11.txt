@article
A law school list is a decision under uncertainty, so build it from evidence. Start with what you want: practice area, region, setting and the kind of career you imagine. Then compare schools on four dimensions: **admission odds, outcomes, cost and fit**.

### Admission odds

Schools publish the **median LSAT and GPA** of their entering class and often the **25th to 75th percentile range** for each. A quick map:

- **Reach:** your numbers are below the 25th percentile of one or both.
- **Target:** your numbers fall inside the school's ranges.
- **Likely or safer:** your numbers are above the 75th percentile on both, though no admission is guaranteed.

Ranges describe past classes, not a formula, and schools weigh essays, experience, recommendations and the strength of your undergraduate program. Applying in several tiers protects you. Many schools use rolling admissions, so earlier applications often face less competition for seats and funds, but submit a polished file rather than a rushed one. Policies on early decision, binding commitments, accepted tests (some schools take the GRE) and deadlines vary; **check each school's current requirements**.

### Outcomes

The ABA requires accredited law schools to publish standardized employment and bar passage data. Look for:

- **Bar passage:** the share of first-time takers who pass in the school's main jurisdiction, compared with the state's overall rate.
- **Employment:** the share working in full-time, long-term jobs requiring a law license, with sector breakdowns (large firm, small firm, government, public interest, clerkships).
- **Geography:** schools place most graduates in their home region, and where you want to practice will shape which bar you take.

Rankings are one input. They combine factors that may not match your goals, so read the underlying data.

### Cost and financing

Sticker price is not what you pay. **Net cost** equals tuition and fees plus living costs minus grants and scholarships. Compare **three-year totals**. Scholarship offers may carry **conditions** (for example, a minimum GPA or class rank); ask what share of recipients keep them each year. Many schools will consider competing offers, so ask politely.

Federal student loans for graduate and professional students were restructured by law in 2025: Grad PLUS loans closed to new borrowers beginning July 1, 2026, and annual and lifetime limits now apply to federal graduate and professional borrowing, so some students will need private loans for any gap. Interest and repayment rules, including income-driven plans and Public Service Loan Forgiveness, have been changing, so read the federal student aid office's current information before you borrow. Interest generally accrues while you are in school; a loan of $P$ at annual rate $r$ compounding annually grows to

$$P(1 + r)^n$$

after $n$ years. Work through that figure for every loan scenario you consider.

### Your school list

For the deliverable, produce a list of 8 to 12 schools across reach, target and likely tiers, with a one- or two-sentence **reason** for each: region, program strength, outcomes, scholarship expectation and fit. A school with no stated reason does not belong on the list.

@example
title: Net cost over three years
problem: A school charges \$58,000 tuition and fees per year, expects \$21,000 per year in living costs, and offers a scholarship of \$20,000 per year. What is the three-year net cost, and what is the net cost if the scholarship is lost after the first year?
step: Annual net cost with the scholarship: $58{,}000 + 21{,}000 - 20{,}000 = 59{,}000$.
step: Three years: $3 \times 59{,}000 = 177{,}000$.
step: If the scholarship is lost after year one, years two and three cost $58{,}000 + 21{,}000 = 79{,}000$ each: $59{,}000 + 2 \times 79{,}000 = 217{,}000$.
step: The difference of \$40,000 is why you ask what share of recipients keep their awards.

@mistakes
- Comparing sticker prices instead of three-year net cost.
- Ignoring scholarship conditions, so a scholarship disappears after the first year.
- Treating median LSAT and GPA as cutoffs rather than as ranges from past classes.
- Choosing schools by rank alone without checking bar passage in the state where you plan to practice.

prompt: Assume you borrow \$40,000 at 7% annual interest, compounding annually, with no payments for three years. What is the balance after three years, to the nearest dollar?
answer: 49002
check: 40000 * (1 + 0.07) ** 3
tol: 0.5
unit: dollars
hint: Use $P(1 + r)^n$.
hint: Here $P = 40000$, $r = 0.07$ and $n = 3$.
solution: $40{,}000 \times 1.07^3 = 40{,}000 \times 1.225043 = 49{,}001.72$, which rounds to \$49,002.
