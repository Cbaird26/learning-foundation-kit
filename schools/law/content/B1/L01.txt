@article
Before you study a single rule, learn the instrument you will be measured on. Bar review is not a second year of law school: it is preparation for a specific timed test, and every study decision (what to memorize, how to practice, how to pace) flows from that test's format.

### Who controls what

The NCBE writes and scores the common exam components. **Each jurisdiction** (usually through its supreme court or a board of bar examiners) decides whether to use them, sets the passing score, sets application deadlines, and adds its own requirements: the Multistate Professional Responsibility Examination (MPRE), character and fitness review, and sometimes a local-law component. A passing score in one jurisdiction is not a passing score in another.

### The legacy UBE

The legacy Uniform Bar Examination has three components:

| Component | Format | Time | Weight |
|---|---|---|---|
| Multistate Bar Examination (MBE) | 200 multiple-choice questions, 25 of them unscored pretest items | 6 hours (two 3-hour sessions of 100) | 50% |
| Multistate Essay Examination (MEE) | 6 essays | 30 minutes each | 30% |
| Multistate Performance Test (MPT) | 2 closed-universe tasks (a file and a library) | 90 minutes each | 20% |

The MBE covers seven subjects: Civil Procedure, Constitutional Law, Contracts (including UCC Article 2 sales), Criminal Law and Procedure, Evidence, Real Property and Torts. Each subject supplies 25 scored questions, so each is worth the same on the MBE; this course covers four of them (Contracts, Torts, Real Property and Civil Procedure). The MEE draws on those subjects and others, including business associations, conflict of laws, family law, trusts and estates and secured transactions. The MPT tests lawyering skills with a supplied file and library, so no outside law is needed.

Component scores are combined and scaled to a total on a 0 to 400 scale. The UBE is **portable**: a score earned in one UBE jurisdiction can be transferred to another that accepts it, if the score meets the destination's minimum and you satisfy its other requirements and time limits. The jurisdictions' minimum scores differ.

### The NextGen exam

The NextGen bar exam was first administered in July 2026 in ten jurisdictions. More jurisdictions join in July 2027, and nearly all UBE jurisdictions are scheduled to switch by July 2028. The legacy UBE (MBE, MEE and MPT) is administered through February 2028. So for a given sitting, the exam you take is the one **your jurisdiction** offers on that date.

NextGen is designed to test foundational legal concepts and principles together with foundational lawyering skills, such as legal research, legal writing, issue spotting and analysis, investigation and evaluation, client counseling and advising, and negotiation and dispute resolution. Questions come in integrated sets built on a common fact pattern and resources, using several formats rather than only multiple choice. It covers a more limited set of doctrinal subjects at a foundational level (including civil procedure, contract law, evidence, real property and torts). Check the NCBE's current content scope for the full list, because it is the authority.

### What this means for your preparation

The black-letter rules you review in this course serve both exams. What changes is the packaging: for the legacy exam, drill single-question speed and 30-minute essays; for NextGen, also practice working from supplied materials and answering several linked questions about one situation. Either way, the habits are the same: read the call of the question first, identify the subject, state the rule, apply it to the facts, and choose the best answer.

### Pace is a rule too

Pace is arithmetic. One MBE session is 100 questions in 180 minutes, which is $180 \times 60 / 100 = 108$ seconds per question on average. Knowing your number lets you notice at once when you are behind.

@example
title: Budgeting a multiple-choice session
problem: In one 3-hour MBE session of 100 questions, you want 15 minutes at the end to revisit flagged questions. How many seconds per question can you spend on the first pass?
step: Total time is 180 minutes. Reserve 15 for review, leaving $180 - 15 = 165$ minutes for the first pass.
step: Convert to seconds: $165 \times 60 = 9900$ seconds.
step: Divide by 100 questions: $9900/100 = 99$ seconds per question.
step: Sanity check: 99 seconds is under the raw average of 108, which is exactly why the review reserve is paid for by moving a little faster on easy questions.

@example
title: Which exam am I taking?
problem: Ana will apply in a UBE jurisdiction for the sitting after the next one. How should she find out whether she will take the legacy UBE or NextGen?
step: Identify the controlling body: the jurisdiction's board of bar examiners decides which exam it administers and when.
step: Check the jurisdiction's announcement for her sitting date, and compare it with the NCBE's published schedule (July 2026 first NextGen; more jurisdictions July 2027; nearly all by July 2028; legacy through February 2028).
step: Record the exam, the application deadline, the passing score and the MPRE requirement in the study plan, so every later choice is tied to a real format.

@mistakes
- Assuming the NCBE sets the passing score or the application rules; those belong to each jurisdiction.
- Treating NextGen as "the same exam with a new name" and never practicing integrated question sets.
- Studying all seven MBE subjects by gut feeling instead of the equal 25 scored questions each carries.
- Ignoring pace until the real exam, when 108 seconds per question is a number you have never felt.
