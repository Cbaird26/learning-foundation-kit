### The elements

**Negligence** requires (1) a **duty** of reasonable care owed to the plaintiff, (2) **breach**, (3) **actual cause**, (4) **proximate cause**, and (5) **damages**. The standard is the **reasonable person** under like circumstances: an objective test that takes account of physical characteristics (a blind person is held to the care of a reasonable blind person) but not mental limitations. A **child** is judged against a child of like age, intelligence and experience, except that a child engaged in an **adult activity** (such as driving) is held to the adult standard. A professional must exercise the skill and knowledge of a reasonable member of the profession. Under the **Hand formula** (*United States v. Carroll Towing Co.* (1947)), a precaution is required when its burden is less than the probability of harm times the gravity of the loss; custom is evidence but not conclusive.

**Negligence per se**: violation of a statute establishes breach if the statute was designed to protect a class of persons that includes the plaintiff against the type of harm that occurred (most states; a minority treat the violation as only evidence of negligence). An excuse (incapacity, emergency, compliance more dangerous) defeats it, and the plaintiff must still prove causation. **Res ipsa loquitur** permits an inference of negligence when (1) the event is of a kind that ordinarily does not occur without negligence, (2) the instrumentality was in the defendant's exclusive control, and (3) the plaintiff did not cause it (*Byrne v. Boadle* (1863)).

### Duty limits

There is **no general duty to aid** a stranger in peril. Exceptions: a **special relationship** (carrier and passenger, innkeeper and guest, business and customer, custodian and ward), a person who **created** the danger, a statute, and a **voluntary undertaking**, after which one must use reasonable care and cannot leave the victim worse off. Duty to the unforeseeable plaintiff: under *Palsgraf v. Long Island R.R.* (1928), the majority rule is that a duty is owed only to foreseeable plaintiffs in the zone of danger; the minority (Andrews) view is that a duty is owed to all.

**Premises** (traditional majority): to **trespassers**, refrain from willful or wanton misconduct (and warn known trespassers of known, hidden artificial dangers that are highly dangerous); to **licensees**, warn of known concealed dangers; to **invitees**, inspect, and make safe or warn of dangers the owner knows or should know. A possessor owes children a duty of reasonable care for **attractive nuisances** (a dangerous artificial condition the possessor knows children are likely to trespass on, where the children cannot appreciate the risk and the burden is slight). A growing minority, following *Rowland v. Christian* (1968), applies a single duty of reasonable care to lawful entrants.

**Negligent infliction of emotional distress**: with physical injury, distress damages come with the claim. Without it, most states require the **zone of danger** (threat of physical impact plus fear for one's own safety), and permit a **bystander** to recover when a close relative is injured, the bystander was present at the scene and contemporaneously perceived it, and suffers serious distress.

### Causation

**Actual cause** is shown by the **but-for** test; where two independent forces each suffice, the **substantial factor** test applies, and where it is unknown which of several negligent defendants caused harm, burden shifting may apply (*Summers v. Tice* (1948)). **Proximate cause** limits liability to harms within the scope of the foreseeable risk. A defendant takes the plaintiff as found (the **eggshell plaintiff**): the extent of harm need not be foreseeable. **Foreseeable intervening forces** do not supersede: negligent medical treatment, rescue attempts and foreseeable reactions. **Superseding** causes are unforeseeable and extraordinary, typically intentional criminal acts of third parties, unless the defendant's negligence increased the risk of that very act.

### Defenses and apportionment

**Contributory negligence** bars recovery entirely (only Alabama, Maryland, North Carolina, Virginia and the District of Columbia retain it); **last clear chance** lets a plaintiff avoid the bar. Most states use **comparative fault**: **pure** (recovery reduced by the plaintiff's percentage, however high) or **modified** (the plaintiff is barred if fault is 50% or more in some states, or **more than 50%** in others). **Express assumption of risk** by a valid exculpatory contract bars recovery unless it violates public policy; implied assumption merges into comparative fault. Under traditional **joint and several liability**, each tortfeasor is liable for the entire indivisible damages and may seek **contribution**; many comparative-fault states have abolished it or limited it, so each defendant pays only its percentage. A **settlement** with one tortfeasor does not release the others, though the nonsettling defendants usually receive a credit.

@example
title: Comparative fault with two defendants
problem: Pat suffers \$300,000 in damages. A jury finds Pat 20% at fault, Dan 50% at fault and Eve 30% at fault. The jurisdiction uses pure comparative fault. How much can Pat recover, and how much does Dan owe under (a) joint and several and (b) several liability?
step: Pat's recovery is reduced by Pat's own fault: $300000 \times (1 - 0.20) = 240000$.
step: Under (a) joint and several liability, Pat may collect the full \$240,000 from either defendant; Dan and Eve then sort it out by contribution.
step: Under (b) several liability, each defendant pays only its own share of the total damages: Dan pays $300000 \times 0.50 = 150000$ and Eve pays $300000 \times 0.30 = 90000$.
step: Check: \$150,000 plus \$90,000 equals \$240,000, Pat's reduced recovery.

@example
title: Negligence per se and proximate cause
problem: A statute requires every apartment building to have working smoke alarms to protect tenants from fire injuries. Owner's building has none. A tenant is overcome by smoke from a fire a visitor negligently started. Evidence shows a working alarm would have woken the tenant in time to escape. Has the tenant proved breach and causation?
step: Check the statute: it protects tenants against fire injuries, so tenant and harm are within the class and risk.
step: The violation is negligence per se (majority rule), absent an excuse; none appears.
step: But-for cause: a working alarm would have allowed escape.
step: The visitor's negligence is a foreseeable intervening cause, not a superseding one, so Owner remains liable (the visitor may share fault).

@mistakes
- Treating negligence per se as automatic liability without checking the protected class, the type of harm and causation.
- Calling every intervening cause superseding; negligent medical care and rescue attempts are foreseeable.
- Applying the modified comparative bar as "more than 50%" when the jurisdiction uses "50% or more," or vice versa.
- Treating a general duty to rescue as the rule; it is the exception rather than the rule.
