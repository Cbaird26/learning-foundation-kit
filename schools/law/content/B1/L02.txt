@article
Most candidates who fail a bar exam did study; they studied by rereading, which feels productive and produces little. The evidence from learning research is consistent: **retrieval practice** (answering questions from memory), **spacing** (revisiting material at widening intervals) and **feedback** (knowing exactly why you were wrong) beat passive review. This lesson turns those three ideas into a plan.

### Start with a timed diagnostic

A diagnostic is a realistic, timed set of mixed questions taken before heavy review. A 50-question mixed set at the MBE pace of 108 seconds per question takes 90 minutes. Take it closed-book, in one sitting, in the conditions you expect on the exam. Its purpose is not a score to be proud of; it is **data**: which subjects, which topics and which kinds of mistakes cost you points.

### Plan backward from the exam date

Count the weeks to the exam, subtract fixed commitments, and state a weekly budget in hours. For example, 10 weeks at 5 study days and 6 hours a day is $10 \times 5 \times 6 = 300$ hours. Then divide the hours by purpose:

- **Learn or relearn rules** (outlines, rule statements, flashcards) early.
- **Do questions** from the first week, not the last: questions are where rules become usable.
- **Take timed, mixed sets** weekly, because the exam mixes subjects and interleaving trains you to identify the issue yourself.
- **Reserve a repair block** each week for the error log.
- **Rest.** A plan with no rest days is a plan you will abandon.

Weight your time by two things: the exam's structure (each MBE subject supplies the same number of scored questions, so a weak, heavily tested subject is worth fixing) and your own data (the largest loss per hour of work, not the subject you enjoy).

### The error log

An **error log** is a running table with one row per missed or guessed question. Useful columns: date, source and question number, subject and topic, the call of the question, your answer and the right answer, **error type**, the **correct rule in one sentence**, the **trap** (why the wrong choice looked attractive), and a **retest date**. Five error types cover nearly everything:

| Type | Meaning | Typical fix |
|---|---|---|
| Knowledge | You did not know the rule | Rebuild the rule statement; add to flashcards |
| Application | You knew the rule but misapplied it to the facts | Practice mapping each element to a fact |
| Reading | You misread the call, a fact or a qualifier ("least likely", "unless") | Read the call first; mark qualifiers |
| Timing | You rushed or ran out of time | Set per-question checkpoints |
| Judgment | You narrowed to two choices and picked the wrong one | Compare choices against each element of the rule |

Log guesses that turned out right, too: a lucky guess hides a gap.

### Spaced retesting

Rewrite each logged rule in your own words, then retest it after a gap: for example 1, 3, 7 and 15 days after you logged it. Each time you get it right, lengthen the gap; each time you miss, reset it. A question you have missed twice earns a dedicated drill.

### From log to plan

After the diagnostic, sort topics by points lost, then by how fixable each is. A topic with many misses of the "knowledge" type is fixed by rule-building; a topic with "reading" misses is fixed by technique, which is cheaper and faster. Write the plan as dated, checkable tasks ("Tuesday: 15 contracts questions, timed; log every miss") rather than intentions ("review contracts").

@example
title: Reading a diagnostic
problem: On a 50-question diagnostic, you answered 31 correctly. Of your 19 misses, 8 were knowledge errors, 6 were application errors, 3 were reading errors and 2 were timing errors. What percent did you score, and which error type should you attack first?
step: Accuracy is $31/50 = 62\%$.
step: Count the misses by type to check the log: $8 + 6 + 3 + 2 = 19$, which equals $50 - 31$, so nothing is missing from the log.
step: Knowledge errors are the largest group (8 of 19), so rebuilding rule statements has the largest expected return. Application errors (6) come next and call for element-by-element practice.
step: The 3 reading errors are the cheapest points: a technique change (read the call first, circle qualifiers) can recover them within days.

@example
title: One row of an error log
problem: A question asks which party "is least likely to prevail." You pick the party who would win, because you overlooked "least." Write the log entry.
step: Error type: reading, because you knew the rule and applied it correctly to the wrong question.
step: Rule: write the substantive rule anyway (one sentence), so the row also serves as review.
step: Trap: the superlative at the end of the call of the question.
step: Fix and retest: circle "least," "most," "unless" and "except" before reading the facts; retest with five questions in 3 days.

@mistakes
- Logging only wrong answers and ignoring correct guesses.
- Writing "didn't know it" as the whole entry, instead of the correct rule and the trap.
- Planning by subject names ("study torts") instead of dated, measurable tasks.
- Spending most hours rereading outlines and few hours on timed, mixed questions.

He did not notice that this state follows the "greater than 50%" version. How should the error be logged?
choice: Knowledge error, because he did not know the rule of comparative fault.
choice: Timing error, because he must have spent too long on the question.
choice*: Application error, because he knew the general rule but misapplied the state's version of the bar to the facts.
choice: Judgment error, because comparative fault is a minority approach and he should have eliminated it.
hint: Ask whether Ben knew the rule and whether he misread the question.
hint: He knew the rule family but attached the wrong threshold to the facts.
solution: The error is an application error: the rule was known, but the threshold was matched incorrectly to the facts. Rereading the full rule statement (including which version of the bar applies) is the repair.

@quiz mc
prompt: Cara misses a contracts question about a firm offer. She cannot state the rule. In her review she realizes she never learned that a merchant's signed written assurance can make an offer irrevocable without consideration. Which is the best classification and repair?
choice: Reading error; she should read the call of the question twice.
choice: Timing error; she should practice with a countdown clock.
choice: Judgment error; she should avoid narrowing to two choices.
choice*: Knowledge error; she should write the rule and retest it on a spaced schedule.
hint: Could she state the rule even with unlimited time?
hint: The fix should match the cause.
solution: She did not know the rule, so it is a knowledge error. Rebuilding the rule statement (a merchant's signed writing that gives assurance it will be held open is irrevocable for the stated time, not longer than three months, without consideration) and retesting it on a spaced schedule repairs it.
