### Intentional torts

Intent means desire to cause the result or knowledge to a substantial certainty that it will follow. **Transferred intent** applies among battery, assault, false imprisonment, trespass to land and trespass to chattels. **Battery**: intent to cause a harmful or offensive contact (or imminent apprehension of one), and a contact results. **Assault**: intent to cause imminent apprehension of such contact, and the plaintiff has a reasonable apprehension (words alone are generally not enough). **False imprisonment**: intent to confine within bounds, actual confinement without a reasonable means of escape, and the plaintiff is aware or harmed. **Intentional infliction of emotional distress**: extreme and outrageous conduct, intent or recklessness, and severe distress. **Trespass to land**: an intentional physical invasion; no harm is needed. **Conversion**: serious interference with another's chattel, with damages equal to its fair market value at the time of conversion; trespass to chattels requires lesser interference and actual damage.

**Defenses**: **Consent** (express or implied, within its scope). **Self-defense**: reasonable force when the defendant reasonably believes force is about to be used; deadly force only against a threat of death or serious bodily harm (a minority require retreat). **Defense of others** matches the person defended. **Defense of property**: reasonable, non-deadly force, after a request to desist; no deadly force and no spring guns. **Necessity**: **public** necessity is a complete privilege; **private** necessity is a privilege but the defendant owes for actual damage (*Vincent v. Lake Erie Transportation Co.* (1910)).

### Strict liability

An **abnormally dangerous activity** (Restatement (Second) §§ 519–520; *Rylands v. Fletcher* (1868)) involves a risk of serious harm that cannot be eliminated with reasonable care and is not a matter of common usage (blasting, storing explosives, certain toxic materials). The § 520 factors are a high degree of risk of some harm, likelihood of great harm, inability to eliminate the risk by reasonable care, the extent to which the activity is not a matter of common usage, inappropriateness of the place, and the activity's value to the community. Liability covers only harm from that dangerous quality, to foreseeable plaintiffs; comparative fault and assumption of risk apply. **Wild animals**: the keeper is strictly liable; **domestic animals**: only when the keeper knew of a dangerous propensity.

### Products liability

Under **strict liability** (Restatement (Second) § 402A), a commercial **seller** of a product in a defective condition unreasonably dangerous is liable for physical harm to users, consumers and bystanders, even if it exercised all possible care; **privity** is not required, and all commercial sellers in the chain are liable, but not casual sellers. Defects: **manufacturing** (departs from its intended design); **design** (most states: risk-utility, so the plaintiff shows a **reasonable alternative design** that would have reduced the risk, Restatement (Third) § 2; some use consumer expectations); **warning** (failure to give reasonable warnings or instructions). In negligence, *MacPherson v. Buick Motor Co.* (1916) abolished the privity bar. Defenses: comparative fault, assumption of risk, and misuse that was unforeseeable. The **economic loss rule** generally bars tort recovery for purely economic loss caused by a defective product.

### Defamation and privacy

**Defamation** requires a defamatory statement of fact about the plaintiff, **publication** to a third person, and reputational harm. A **public official** or **public figure** must prove **actual malice**, meaning knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for truth (*New York Times Co. v. Sullivan* (1964)). A **private figure** suing on a matter of **public concern** must prove at least **negligence** as to falsity, and presumed or punitive damages require actual malice (*Gertz v. Robert Welch, Inc.* (1974)). A private figure on a private matter may recover presumed damages without actual malice. Statements that cannot reasonably be read as asserting facts (hyperbole, parody) are not actionable. The four **privacy** torts are intrusion on seclusion, public disclosure of private facts, false light and appropriation of name or likeness.

### Misrepresentation, nuisance and vicarious liability

**Fraud**: false statement of material fact, knowledge (scienter), intent to induce reliance, justifiable reliance and damages. **Negligent misrepresentation** is generally limited to commercial settings and to plaintiffs the speaker knew would rely. **Private nuisance**: a substantial, unreasonable interference with another's use and enjoyment of land. **Respondeat superior**: an employer is liable for an employee's torts within the **scope of employment**; a minor deviation (**detour**) stays within scope, a substantial one (**frolic**) does not; intentional torts are within scope if motivated by the job. Employers generally are not liable for **independent contractors**, except for nondelegable duties and inherently dangerous activities, but are liable for their own negligent hiring or supervision.

@example
title: Self-defense and proportionality
problem: Amy shoves Ben in a bar argument. Amy is unarmed and makes no other threat, but Ben draws a knife and stabs her. Amy sues Ben for battery. What result?
step: Ben committed a battery unless a privilege applies; the privilege is self-defense.
step: Force may be used in response to a reasonably perceived threat, but only proportional force: deadly force is justified only against a threat of death or serious bodily harm.
step: A shove threatens non-deadly force, so a stabbing exceeds what is reasonable.
step: Ben cannot rely on self-defense and is liable for battery.

@example
title: Defamation by plaintiff type
problem: A local news site falsely reports that Rae, a private shop owner, was arrested for tax fraud (a matter of public concern). The reporter did not check the arrest record. Rae sues for defamation seeking presumed damages. What must Rae show?
step: Rae is a private figure and the statement concerns a matter of public concern, so *Gertz* applies.
step: Rae must prove falsity and at least negligence as to the truth; the careless failure to check the record supports negligence.
step: Presumed (and punitive) damages require actual malice; there is no sign of knowledge of falsity or recklessness.
step: Rae can recover for proven actual injury from the false report but not presumed damages.

@mistakes
- Requiring actual malice for every plaintiff; it applies to public officials and public figures, and for presumed or punitive damages to private figures on public concern.
- Applying strict liability to harm outside the abnormally dangerous quality, such as a negligent collision of a truck carrying explosives.
- Treating an employee's minor detour as outside the scope of employment.
- Allowing deadly force to defend property or to answer a non-deadly threat.

solution: Issue: whether Quarry Co. is strictly liable for foundation damage from blasting. Rule: one who carries on an abnormally dangerous activity, one involving a risk of serious harm that cannot be eliminated by reasonable care and that is not a matter of common usage, is strictly liable for harm resulting from that dangerous quality (Restatement (Second) §§ 519–520; *Rylands v. Fletcher*). Application: blasting near homes creates a risk of serious property damage that remains even with careful methods, and it is not common in residential areas. rubric: States the abnormally dangerous activity test.
