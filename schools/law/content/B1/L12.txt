@article
Repair is the step most candidates skip. They take practice sets, look at the score, feel bad or good, and take another set. A repair cycle turns the score into a plan: **diagnose, rank, rebuild, drill, retest**.

### Diagnose: read the data, not the mood

Gather your error log and the results of your last two or three timed sets. For every topic (not just every subject), count questions seen, questions missed and the dominant error type. A topic is a unit such as "UCC § 2-207", "recording acts", "personal jurisdiction", "negligence per se" or "Rule 12 waiver". Subject totals hide the real problem: a 70% in civil procedure can be 95% on pleading and 40% on jurisdiction.

### Rank: points lost, sample size, fixability

Rank topics by **points lost** (questions missed), because that is what you can recover. Weight each by **sample size**: a miss rate on 5 questions is noise, but 20 misses on 40 questions is a pattern. Then consider **fixability**: reading and timing errors are cheap to repair; knowledge errors in a rule with a short statement (the mailbox rule) are cheap; knowledge errors in large, detailed areas (the Rule Against Perpetuities) are expensive and may yield fewer points per hour. Choose **three topics** for the next two weeks, mixing one quick win with two larger gains. Each MBE subject supplies the same number of scored questions, so gains in your weakest subjects count the same as gains in your strongest.

### Rebuild: match the repair to the error type

| Error type | Repair |
|---|---|
| Knowledge | Write a one-sentence rule statement with elements and the main exception from memory; check it against your outline; make flashcards |
| Application | Take a fact pattern and label each fact with the element it satisfies or defeats; practice with questions where only one element is contested |
| Reading | Read the call first; circle qualifiers; underline the party the question asks about |
| Timing | Set per-question and per-block checkpoints; practice abandon-and-flag |
| Judgment | After a miss, write why the right answer is right and the wrong choice wrong, naming the distractor pattern |

A **repair sheet** for a topic fits on one page: (1) the rule statement; (2) the two facts that most often flip the answer; (3) the minority or exception you confuse with it; (4) the trap that caught you; (5) three practice questions to retest. For example, for "UCC § 2-207": rule: an acceptance with additional terms is still an acceptance unless expressly conditional; between merchants, additional terms become part unless the offer limits acceptance, they materially alter, or the offeror objects; flipping facts: both parties merchants, a warranty disclaimer versus interest on overdue invoices; trap: applying mirror-image acceptance.

### Drill and retest

Drill each topic in small sets (10 to 15 questions), untimed first and then timed, logging every miss. Retest after a delay (for example 2 days and then a week) with **new** questions, preferably **mixed** with other subjects, because getting the same question right a second time proves memory of that question rather than the rule. A topic is repaired when you reach your target accuracy (say 80%) on unseen, mixed questions after a delay. If it stalls, change the method (rebuild the rule, or move from reading to doing) rather than repeating it.

### Know when to stop

Two weeks of focused repair on three topics is a good cycle. Do not abandon strong areas: keep them alive with a few mixed questions a day. Near the exam, shift time from repair to mixed timed sets and rest.

### Estimating the payoff

If a subject supplies 25 scored questions and your accuracy there rises from 50% to 70%, you gain $25 \times 0.20 = 5$ questions. Small, concrete gains justify the effort and show you what to do next.

@example
title: A repair sheet for the recording acts
problem: You keep missing recording-act questions by applying the wrong statute type. Write a short repair sheet.
step: Rule: a bona fide purchaser gives value and takes without notice. Notice statute: a later BFP wins over a prior unrecorded interest. Race-notice: the later BFP must also record first. Race: first to record wins.
step: Flip facts: whether the later taker paid value and had notice, and who recorded first.
step: Trap: treating the first deed in time as always prevailing, or applying a notice rule in a race-notice state.
step: Retest: three new fact patterns, one for each statute type, in two days.

@mistakes
- Repairing the subject you enjoy rather than the topics that cost the most points.
- Retesting with the same questions and treating the improvement as mastery.
- Using a content cure (more outlines) for a reading or timing error.
- Giving up all practice on strong subjects during the repair weeks.
