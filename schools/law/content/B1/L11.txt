@article
On the exam, subjects arrive shuffled. A torts question may follow a civil procedure question, and a real property question may turn on a contract rule. Mixed practice trains the two skills the exam rewards most: **identifying the issue yourself**, and **doing it fast enough**.

### A method for every question

1. **Read the call of the question first.** Note who is the party, what the question asks (liability, admissibility, best defense, "least likely") and any qualifier.
2. **Read the facts for the issue, not the story.** Mark who did what, when, with what words, and which rule is being tested.
3. **Name the rule before you read the choices.** State the element or test in your head.
4. **Predict the answer, then compare.** Each choice is a conclusion plus a reason: the conclusion and the reason must both be right.
5. **Eliminate.** Cross out choices that misstate the rule, add a fact that is not there, apply the minority rule, or reach the right conclusion for the wrong reason.
6. **Choose, mark, move.** If two remain after about 90 seconds, choose one, flag it, and move on. There is no penalty for a wrong answer, so never leave one blank.

### Distractor patterns

Best-answer distractors tend to be: **(a) the right rule, wrong conclusion** (misapplied to the facts); **(b) a correct conclusion with a false reason**; **(c) a rule from the wrong subject or the wrong source** (common law instead of the UCC); **(d) the minority or older rule** (for example, applying a pure-notice recording act in a race-notice state); **(e) an irrelevant fact** that sounds legal; **(f) a rule stated too broadly** ("always", "never"). Train yourself to name which pattern you are rejecting.

### Pace and checkpoints

A 100-question session in 180 minutes gives an average of 108 seconds per question. Set checkpoints: after every **25 questions you should be at or under 45 minutes**, so by question 50 you are at 90 minutes and by question 75 at 135 minutes. If you are over at a checkpoint, do not rush the next question; simply cut the time you spend on questions you would only guess at anyway. Practice with these checkpoints in every timed set.

### Cross-subject rules to recognize

- **Contracts and real property.** An agreement to sell land needs a signed writing; part performance (possession plus payment or improvements) can support specific performance. After a contract of sale, equitable conversion in most states puts the risk of loss on the buyer.
- **Contracts and torts.** The **economic loss rule** generally bars tort recovery of purely economic loss from a defective product; the remedy is in contract or warranty. **Negligent misrepresentation** requires a duty and justifiable reliance; **fraud** requires scienter. **Promissory estoppel** can enforce a promise that induced reliance when there was no bargain.
- **Torts and civil procedure.** A plaintiff aggregates all claims against one defendant for the \$75,000 amount in controversy. Supplemental jurisdiction under § 1367(a) supports related claims, but § 1367(b) bars a plaintiff's claims against Rule 20 defendants that would destroy complete diversity. A judgment bars a later suit on the same claim (claim preclusion).
- **Real property and civil procedure.** A mortgagee's foreclosure only cuts off junior interests that are joined; a nonparty is not bound by a judgment to which it was not a party.
- **Torts and property.** The possessor's duty to entrants depends on their status in most states; a landlord's duty to repair is shaped by the lease and by the implied warranty of habitability in residential leases.

### Exam-length fact patterns

Longer fact patterns (and essays) often combine several issues. Spot them by asking, at each fact: *what claim, what defense, what court, what remedy?* Write a one-line issue list before you answer (for essays) or before you read the choices (for multiple choice).

title: A checkpoint calculation
problem: You are 55 questions into a 100-question, 180-minute session and 105 minutes have elapsed. At what average pace (in seconds per question) must you finish the remaining questions to end on time?
step: Remaining questions: $100 - 55 = 45$.
step: Remaining time: $180 - 105 = 75$ minutes, which is $4500$ seconds.
step: Required pace: $4500/45 = 100$ seconds per question.
step: Compared with the 108-second average, you are behind (at 108 seconds each, 55 questions would have taken 99 minutes, not 105), so you must now go about 8 seconds per question faster than average.

@example
title: A mixed fact pattern
problem: Ana contracts to buy Cal's house for \$300,000 in a signed writing. Before closing, a tree Cal negligently left leaning falls on a neighbor's car. The neighbor sues Cal in federal court (both in the same state) for \$20,000 in property damage. Identify the issues.
step: Contracts and property: the contract is within the statute of frauds and signed; on signing, Ana is the equitable owner and (in most states) bears casualty risk; risk of loss does not affect the neighbor's claim against Cal.
step: Torts: Cal is the possessor; a duty exists to keep the property reasonably safe for those outside it, and the leaning tree is a foreseeable hazard; breach and causation are plausible.
step: Civil procedure: no federal question, no diversity (same state) and less than \$75,000, so there is no subject-matter jurisdiction in federal court.
step: Conclusion: the case belongs in state court; the contract and the tort issues are independent.

@mistakes
- Spending three minutes on a question you would only guess at, then rushing five easy questions.
- Choosing an answer because its rule is correct without checking that its conclusion fits the facts.
- Skipping the call of the question and answering a different question than the one asked.
- Leaving questions blank; there is no penalty for guessing.

@practice self
prompt: Ana signs a written contract with Cole Paving to repave her driveway for \$10,000. The writing states that it is the entire agreement. Before signing, Cole orally promised to finish by June 1. Cole finished July 1. On June 20, Ned, a pedestrian on the public sidewalk, slipped on loose gravel that Cole's crew left on the sidewalk and was hurt. Write a short issue list and brief analyses of (1) Ana's claim about the delay and (2) Ned's claim against Ana.
hint: Look for the parol evidence rule and the status of Cole (employee or independent contractor).
hint: Ask what claim, what defense and what remedy for each.
solution: (1) Contract: the writing is completely integrated by its merger clause, so the earlier oral promise to finish by June 1 is a prior agreement that adds a term and is barred by the parol evidence rule, absent fraud, mistake or ambiguity, so Ana cannot rely on it; if the writing is silent on time, the court may imply a reasonable time. (2) Tort: Cole is an independent contractor (a separate business doing a job), so Ana is generally not vicariously liable for its negligence; the exceptions are nondelegable duties and inherently dangerous work, and some courts treat work that endangers a public sidewalk as a nondelegable duty of the hirer (Restatement (Second) of Torts § 417), so Ana's exposure depends on the jurisdiction; otherwise she is liable only if she negligently hired or controlled Cole. Ned's clearest claim lies against Cole.
rubric: Lists issues: parol evidence, implied reasonable time, independent contractor, exceptions.
rubric: States the parol evidence rule and applies it to the merger clause.
rubric: States the general rule of no vicarious liability for independent contractors with exceptions.
rubric: Reaches conclusions on both claims.
