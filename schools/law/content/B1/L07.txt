@article
### Present estates

A **fee simple absolute** is the largest estate ("to A and his heirs," or simply "to A"). A **fee simple determinable** ends automatically when a stated event occurs ("to A so long as the land is used as a school"; "until," "while"); the grantor keeps a **possibility of reverter**. A **fee simple subject to condition subsequent** ends only if the grantor elects to terminate ("to A, but if the land is used for sale of liquor, the grantor may re-enter"); the grantor holds a **right of entry** (power of termination). A **fee simple subject to executory limitation** shifts to a third party on the event ("to A, but if liquor is sold, then to B"). A **life estate** lasts for the holder's life (or another person's life, a life estate **pur autre vie**); the life tenant must not commit **waste**, and must pay ordinary taxes and interest on the mortgage up to the income from the land. The **fee tail** survives in a few states only.

### Future interests

The grantor's future interests: a **reversion** (after a life estate or term of years), a **possibility of reverter** and a **right of entry**. A third party's future interests: **remainders** (follow a natural end of the preceding estate, with no gap) and **executory interests** (**shifting** interests cut off a prior holder; **springing** interests cut off the grantor). A remainder is **vested** if the taker is ascertained and no condition precedent remains; it is **contingent** if the taker is unascertained or a condition precedent remains ("to A for life, then to B if B graduates"). A vested remainder may be **subject to open** (class gifts), **subject to complete divestment** or indefeasibly vested. The **Rule in Shelley's Case** and the **doctrine of worthier title** have been abolished in nearly all states.

### Rule Against Perpetuities

At common law: **no interest is good unless it must vest, if at all, not later than 21 years after some life in being at the creation of the interest.** It applies to **contingent remainders, executory interests, vested remainders subject to open** and options not connected to a lease. It does **not** apply to reversions, possibilities of reverter or rights of entry, or to a transfer from one charity to another. Validity is tested at creation by what **might** happen, not what does happen; a **class gift** is valid only if the interest of every possible member must vest within the period (**all-or-nothing**). Many states now apply **wait-and-see**, usually the Uniform Statutory Rule Against Perpetuities with a **90-year** period; an interest that is not valid at common law is valid if it actually vests within 90 years, and otherwise may be reformed. The remedy for a violation is to strike the offending interest.

### Co-ownership

A **joint tenancy** requires the four unities (time, title, interest and possession) and carries a **right of survivorship**. A joint tenant may sever by conveying his interest: the grantee becomes a **tenant in common** of that share while the others remain joint tenants among themselves. A **tenancy in common** has equal rights of possession and no survivorship. A **tenancy by the entirety** exists only for married persons (in states that retain it) and cannot be severed by one spouse's conveyance. A co-tenant in possession owes no rent to the others unless he **ousts** them, but a co-tenant who pays more than a share of taxes or mortgage payments may seek **contribution** (an accounting) from the others; a co-tenant may seek **partition** in kind or by sale.

### Landlord and tenant

Tenancy types: **term of years** (fixed end; a lease over a year needs a writing), **periodic tenancy** (renews automatically; notice is required to end it), **tenancy at will** and **tenancy at sufferance** (a holdover; the landlord may evict or elect a new periodic tenancy). The tenant may **assign** (transfer the whole remaining term) or **sublease** (transfer less than the whole term or keep a reversion) unless the lease forbids it; the assignee is in **privity of estate** with the landlord, the sublessee is not, and the original tenant remains liable on the lease. Under the majority (English) rule, the landlord must deliver actual possession at the start of the term; under the minority (American) rule, the landlord need deliver only the legal right to possession. The **covenant of quiet enjoyment** is breached by actual or **constructive eviction**: a substantial interference caused by the landlord, notice and a failure to fix, and the tenant vacating within a reasonable time. Most states imply a **warranty of habitability** in residential leases (*Javins v. First National Realty Corp.* (1970)), which cannot be waived and supports rent abatement, repair-and-deduct and termination. Landlords may not retaliate against a tenant who asserts those rights. Most states require a landlord whose tenant abandons to **mitigate** damages.

@example
title: Classifying a conveyance
problem: O conveys "to A for life, then to B and his heirs if B survives A." What interests do A, B and O hold?
step: A holds a life estate, since the grant is "to A for life."
step: B's remainder follows A's life estate naturally. It is contingent, because B must survive A, which is a condition precedent.
step: If B fails to survive A, no one takes after the life estate, so O holds a reversion in fee simple (the grantor keeps what is not given away).
step: The Rule Against Perpetuities is satisfied: B's interest vests, if at all, at A's death, and A is a life in being.

@example
title: Severing a joint tenancy
problem: A, B and C hold land as joint tenants. A conveys her interest to D. B then dies. Who owns what?
step: A's conveyance severs the joint tenancy as to A's share: D holds one-third as a tenant in common.
step: B and C remain joint tenants as to the other two-thirds, between themselves.
step: B's death triggers survivorship: C takes B's share, so C now holds two-thirds.
step: D, as a tenant in common, has no survivorship and keeps one-third.

@mistakes
- Treating a possibility of reverter as requiring an entry; only a right of entry does.
- Applying the Rule Against Perpetuities to reversions, possibilities of reverter and rights of entry.
- Forgetting that a severance by one joint tenant converts only that share to a tenancy in common.
- Treating a sublessee as in privity of estate with the landlord.
