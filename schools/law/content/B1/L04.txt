@article
### Parol evidence and interpretation

When parties adopt a writing as the final expression of their agreement (an **integration**), the **parol evidence rule** bars evidence of **prior or contemporaneous** agreements that contradict it. A **completely integrated** writing also bars consistent additional terms; a **partially integrated** writing bars only contradictory ones. The rule does **not** bar evidence of **fraud, duress, mistake**, illegality or other invalidating causes, of a **condition precedent** to the contract's effectiveness, of **subsequent** modifications, or evidence used to **interpret ambiguous** terms. Under UCC § 2-202, even a fully integrated writing may be explained or supplemented by **course of performance, course of dealing and usage of trade**; consistent additional terms are admissible unless the writing was intended as complete and exclusive.

### Defenses

**Misrepresentation** (a false assertion of material fact, justifiably relied on) makes a contract voidable; fraud adds knowledge and intent to induce reliance. **Duress** (an improper threat leaving no reasonable alternative) and **undue influence** also make a contract voidable. **Unconscionability** requires procedural and substantive unfairness measured at formation (UCC § 2-302). **Mutual mistake** about a basic assumption, where the adversely affected party did not bear the risk, makes a contract voidable; **unilateral mistake** is a defense only if the other party knew of it, or enforcement would be unconscionable. **Impracticability** (Restatement (Second) § 261; UCC § 2-615) excuses a party when an unforeseen event, whose non-occurrence was a basic assumption, makes performance extremely burdensome; increased cost alone is not enough. **Frustration of purpose** excuses when a supervening event destroys the principal purpose both parties understood.

### Conditions, performance and breach

A **condition** must occur before a duty arises. **Constructive conditions** of exchange mean each party's performance is conditioned on the other's. In services and land contracts, **substantial performance** satisfies the condition, and the other party may offset the cost of the defects. Under the UCC's **perfect tender rule** (§ 2-601), a buyer may reject goods that fail in any respect to conform, but the seller may **cure** within the contract time (§ 2-508(1)), or beyond it if the seller reasonably believed the tender would be acceptable (§ 2-508(2)). In an **installment contract**, the buyer may reject an installment only if the nonconformity substantially impairs its value and cannot be cured (§ 2-612).

**Anticipatory repudiation** is an unequivocal statement or voluntary act showing that the party will breach. The non-repudiating party may suspend performance and wait a commercially reasonable time (UCC § 2-610) or sue at once. The repudiator can retract until the other party materially changes position or says the repudiation is final. If **reasonable grounds for insecurity** arise, a party may demand **adequate assurance** in writing (UCC § 2-609) and may suspend performance until receiving it; failure to give it within a reasonable time, not over 30 days, is a repudiation.

### Remedies

**Expectation damages** put the plaintiff where full performance would have, plus incidental and foreseeable consequential damages (*Hadley v. Baxendale* (1854)), less costs avoided. **Reliance** and **restitution** are alternatives. Damages must be proved with reasonable certainty and are reduced by what the plaintiff could reasonably have avoided (**mitigation**). **Liquidated damages** are enforceable if reasonable in light of anticipated or actual loss and the harm is hard to estimate; a penalty is not. **Specific performance** is available when damages are inadequate, as for land or unique goods.

Under the UCC, a buyer who covers recovers the **cover price minus the contract price** (§ 2-712), or if the buyer does not cover, **market minus contract price** (§ 2-713), plus incidental and consequential damages, less expenses saved. A seller who resells in good faith and in a commercially reasonable manner recovers **contract price minus resale price** plus incidental damages, less expenses saved (§ 2-706). The **lost-volume seller** may recover lost profit (§ 2-708(2)).

### Warranties

An **express warranty** arises from an affirmation of fact or promise, a description, or a sample that is part of the basis of the bargain (§ 2-313). A **merchant** impliedly warrants **merchantability** (§ 2-314). **Fitness for a particular purpose** arises when the seller has reason to know the purpose and that the buyer is relying on the seller's judgment (§ 2-315). A disclaimer of merchantability must mention the word and be conspicuous; "as is" disclaims implied warranties (§ 2-316). The buyer must notify the seller within a reasonable time after discovering a breach (§ 2-607(3)(a)).

### Third parties

An **assignment** transfers rights; a **delegation** transfers the performance of duties, and the delegator **remains liable**. Rights are assignable unless the assignment materially changes the obligor's duty, increases the burden or risk, or is validly prohibited. A **third-party beneficiary** may enforce a contract if intended (the promisee intended to benefit the beneficiary, or the performance runs to the beneficiary); **incidental** beneficiaries cannot. An intended beneficiary's rights **vest** when the beneficiary relies on, assents to or sues on the promise, after which the parties may not modify them.

@example
title: UCC cover damages
problem: Seller promises to deliver 1,000 units at \$12 each. Seller repudiates. Buyer promptly and reasonably buys substitute units at \$15 each and incurs \$500 in incidental costs. What are Buyer's damages?
step: Buyer covered, so § 2-712 applies: damages are cover price minus contract price.
step: The per-unit difference is $15 - 12 = 3$, so on 1,000 units the difference is \$3,000.
step: Add the incidental damages: $3000 + 500 = 3500$.
step: No expenses were saved, so Buyer recovers \$3,500.

@example
title: Parol evidence and a collateral oral promise
problem: A written lease says it is the "entire agreement." Before signing, Landlord orally promised to repaint the unit. Tenant sues for failing to repaint. Is the evidence barred?
step: Identify the writing: a merger clause suggests complete integration.
step: A prior oral promise that adds a term to a completely integrated writing is barred by the parol evidence rule.
step: Check exceptions: there is no claim of fraud, mistake or ambiguity, and the promise does not go to a condition on effectiveness.
step: The evidence is barred; Tenant's only route is to prove an exception, such as fraud in inducing the lease.

@mistakes
- Using the parol evidence rule to exclude evidence of fraud, mistake or a later modification.
- Applying the perfect tender rule to a services contract (substantial performance governs there).
- Forgetting incidental costs, or subtracting expenses saved, when computing UCC damages.
- Treating increased cost alone as impracticability.

solution: Mailbox rule: an acceptance sent by a reasonable medium is effective on dispatch, unless the offer says otherwise or is an option.
