@article
### Which law governs

The **common law** governs contracts for services, land and employment. **UCC Article 2** governs the sale of **goods** (movable things). In a mixed contract, most courts ask what the **predominant purpose** is. Article 2 loosens several common-law rules and imposes special rules on **merchants**, so identifying the governing law is always the first step.

### Offer and termination

An **offer** is a manifestation of willingness to be bound that is definite enough and communicated so that the offeree has the power to accept. Advertisements are usually invitations to bargain, but an ad that is specific and leaves nothing to negotiate can be an offer (*Lefkowitz v. Great Minneapolis Surplus Store* (1957)). Under the UCC, a contract can be formed even if terms are open, if the parties intended to contract and there is a reasonably certain basis for a remedy (§ 2-204); quantity is the one term the statute of frauds requires.

An offer ends by **revocation** (effective when the offeree **receives** it), rejection or counteroffer (effective on receipt), lapse, or the death or incapacity of the offeror (at common law). An offer is **irrevocable** only if (1) it is supported by consideration (an **option**), (2) it is a **firm offer** under § 2-205 (a merchant's signed writing giving assurance it will be held open, irrevocable for the stated time, at most three months), or (3) the offeree's reliance makes it irrevocable, as when a subcontractor's bid is relied on by a general contractor (*Drennan v. Star Paving Co.* (1958)).

### Acceptance

At common law acceptance must mirror the offer. Under the **mailbox rule**, an acceptance sent by a reasonable medium is effective on **dispatch**, even if lost, unless the offer provides otherwise or the offer is an option (effective on receipt). If an offeree sends a rejection and then an acceptance, whichever the offeror receives first controls. Under § 2-206, an offer to buy goods for prompt shipment invites acceptance by a promise or by shipment; a nonconforming shipment is an acceptance and a breach unless the seller seasonably notifies the buyer that it is only an accommodation.

### UCC § 2-207: the battle of the forms

Section 2-207(1): a definite and seasonable expression of acceptance operates as an acceptance **even though it states additional or different terms**, unless acceptance is **expressly made conditional on assent to** those terms. Additional terms are then **proposals**. Between **merchants**, they become part of the contract unless (a) the offer **expressly limits** acceptance to its terms, (b) they **materially alter** the contract (for example, a clause disclaiming implied warranties), or (c) the offeror objects. Interest on overdue invoices is the classic non-material term. For **different** (conflicting) terms, most courts apply the **knockout rule**: the conflicting terms cancel and gap fillers apply; a minority treat different terms like additional ones. If the writings do not form a contract but the parties perform, § 2-207(3) finds a contract on the terms on which the writings agree, plus UCC gap fillers.

### Consideration, modification, promissory estoppel

Consideration is a **bargained-for exchange** of a legal detriment (*Hamer v. Sidway* (1891)); courts do not weigh adequacy. Past consideration and illusory promises (a promise that leaves the promisor free to perform or not) do not suffice. Under the common-law **pre-existing duty rule**, a promise to do what one is already bound to do is not consideration, so a modification needs new consideration, except where unanticipated circumstances make the modification fair (Restatement (Second) § 89). Under **§ 2-209(1)**, a modification of a sale-of-goods contract needs **no consideration** but must meet the **good faith** standard. **Promissory estoppel** (Restatement (Second) § 90): a promise the promisor should reasonably expect to induce action or forbearance, which does induce it, is enforceable if injustice can be avoided only by enforcement; relief may be limited to reliance.

### Statute of frauds

Contracts within the statute need a signed writing: **marriage** consideration, performance not possible **within one year** of making, **land** interests, **executor** promises to pay estate debts personally, **goods of \$500 or more** (§ 2-201), and **suretyship** (the main-purpose rule: a promise to answer for another's debt). The writing must be signed by the **party to be charged** and reasonably identify the subject matter and essential terms. Under § 2-201 it must indicate a contract and **state a quantity**. Exceptions to the goods rule: specially manufactured goods (with a substantial beginning), an admission in pleadings or testimony, **payment or acceptance** (to that extent), and a **merchant's confirmation** that the recipient does not object to in writing within **10 days** of receipt, which removes the defense but does not prove a contract was made. For land, part performance (possession plus payment or improvements) can support specific performance.

@example
title: Additional terms in an acceptance
problem: Buyer, a merchant, sends Seller, a merchant, a signed offer to buy 200 chairs at \$40 each; the offer says nothing about warranty disclaimers or limits on acceptance. Seller sends a definite acceptance that adds "Seller disclaims all implied warranties" and "1% monthly interest on overdue invoices." Buyer does not respond. What is the contract?
step: Both are merchants and the sale is of goods, so § 2-207 applies. The acceptance is definite and not expressly conditional on assent to the added terms, so a contract exists on the offer's terms.
step: The added terms are only proposals. Test each between merchants: not limited by the offer, not objected to, so the question is materiality.
step: The disclaimer of implied warranties materially alters the contract, so it does not become part of the contract.
step: Interest on overdue invoices is the standard example of a non-material term, so it becomes part of the contract.

@example
title: A modification without new consideration
problem: Contractor agreed to build a deck for Owner for \$20,000. Halfway through, Contractor demanded \$24,000 because the job was "more tiresome than expected," and Owner agreed. Contractor finished and sued for the extra \$4,000. What result?
step: A construction contract is a services contract, so the common law governs, not § 2-209.
step: Contractor's promise to finish was already owed, so under the pre-existing duty rule there was no new consideration for the extra \$4,000.
step: The § 89 exception requires unanticipated circumstances; tedium Contractor should have foreseen is not one.
step: Owner is not bound to pay the extra \$4,000; Contractor recovers the original \$20,000.

@mistakes
- Applying common-law mirror-image acceptance to a sale of goods between merchants instead of § 2-207.
- Treating a merchant's confirmation as proof that a contract existed rather than only as removing the statute-of-frauds defense.
- Applying the pre-existing duty rule to a goods modification (§ 2-209 needs no consideration, only good faith).
- Treating a revocation as effective on dispatch rather than on receipt.
