@article
### Contract of sale and closing

A contract for the sale of land must satisfy the **statute of frauds** (a signed writing identifying the parties, the land and the price). Unless the contract provides otherwise, the seller impliedly promises **marketable title** (free from reasonable doubt and from encumbrances) **at closing**, not at signing. Under **equitable conversion**, the buyer is treated as the equitable owner on signing, so in most states the **buyer bears the risk of casualty** loss between signing and closing (a minority and the Uniform Vendor and Purchaser Risk Act place it on the seller until legal title or possession passes). When the deed is delivered at closing, the contract terms **merge** into the deed, except for collateral terms.

### Deeds and title covenants

A valid deed needs a writing signed by the grantor, identification of the grantee, words of intent to convey and a description of the land, plus **delivery** with present intent to pass title; acceptance is presumed. A **general warranty deed** carries six covenants. The **present covenants** are **seisin**, **right to convey** and **against encumbrances**; they are breached, if at all, at delivery and in most states do not run to remote grantees. The **future covenants** are **warranty**, **quiet enjoyment** and **further assurances**; they are breached on eviction by paramount title and run with the land. A **special warranty deed** covers only defects arising from the grantor's own acts; a **quitclaim deed** gives no covenants.

### Recording acts

A recording act protects a **bona fide purchaser (BFP)**, a person who gives **value** and takes **without notice** (actual, constructive from the record, or inquiry notice from a visible possession), against a prior unrecorded interest. Under a **notice** statute, a later BFP prevails over a prior unrecorded conveyance whether or not the BFP records first. Under a **race-notice** statute, the later taker must be a BFP **and record first**. Under a **race** statute, the first to record wins, regardless of notice. A grantee who takes from a BFP is protected by the **shelter rule**. A deed outside the chain of title (a **wild deed**) gives no record notice.

### Mortgages

Most states are **lien theory** states: the mortgagee holds a lien and the mortgagor keeps title. A buyer who **assumes** a mortgage becomes personally liable on the debt; one who takes **subject to** it is not, but the land remains security, and the original mortgagor remains liable. **Due-on-sale** clauses are generally enforceable. Priority among mortgages follows the recording act, with a **purchase-money mortgage** taking priority over other liens against the buyer. In **foreclosure**, the sale proceeds go first to the costs of sale, then to the senior mortgage, then to junior liens in order, and any surplus to the mortgagor; a junior interest not joined in the foreclosure is not cut off. The mortgagor may redeem by paying the debt before the sale (**equity of redemption**).

### Easements and servitudes

An **easement** is a nonpossessory right to use another's land. Creation: express grant (a writing); **implication from prior use** (on severance of a common ownership, the prior use was apparent, continuous and reasonably necessary); **necessity** (strict necessity when a parcel is severed, ending when the necessity ends); and **prescription** (use that is open, notorious, adverse, and continuous for the statutory period). An easement **appurtenant** benefits a particular parcel and passes with it; one **in gross** benefits a person. Termination: release, merger of the parcels, abandonment shown by intent, estoppel, or the end of necessity. A **real covenant** runs at law if it is in a writing, the parties intended it to run and it **touches and concerns** the land; for the burden to run there must also be **horizontal and vertical privity** and notice to the successor, while the benefit needs only vertical privity; the remedy is damages. An **equitable servitude** is enforced by injunction against successors with **notice** if it is in a writing (or arises from a common scheme, an implied reciprocal servitude), is intended to run and touches and concerns the land; privity is not required.

### Adverse possession

A possessor becomes the owner if possession is **actual, open and notorious, exclusive, hostile (without the owner's permission) and continuous for the statutory period** (commonly 10 to 20 years). Most states do not ask what the possessor believed. **Tacking** of successive possessors requires privity. A disability of the true owner when the claim accrues extends the period. Title vests at the end of the period without a judgment, though the possessor may obtain one to clear title.

@example
title: A recording-act priority problem
problem: O conveys Blackacre to A, who does not record. O then conveys Blackacre to B, who pays full value and has no notice of A's deed. B records. Then A records. The jurisdiction has a race-notice statute. Who owns Blackacre?
step: Identify the statute: race-notice protects a later taker who is a BFP and who records first.
step: B gave value and had no notice (a BFP).
step: B recorded before A recorded, satisfying the race component.
step: B owns Blackacre. Under a pure notice statute B would also win; under a race statute B also wins because B recorded first.

@example
title: An easement implied from prior use
problem: Owner owns one parcel with a house in front and a cottage behind, served by a gravel driveway across the front lot that Owner has long used. Owner sells the cottage lot to Ana and keeps the front lot. Is there an easement over the driveway for the cottage?
step: There was a unified title, then a severance by sale.
step: Before severance, the driveway use was apparent and continuous.
step: It is reasonably necessary for the cottage's use because there is no equal alternative.
step: An easement implied from prior use arises in favor of the cottage lot over the driveway.

@mistakes
- Treating a race-notice statute as a pure notice statute and ignoring who recorded first.
- Treating a present covenant of title (seisin) as running with the land to remote grantees.
- Allocating foreclosure surplus to junior lienholders before the senior mortgage is paid in full.
- Requiring bad faith or knowledge of the true owner for the hostility element of adverse possession.

solution: A real covenant runs at law when (1) the promise is in a writing, (2) the original parties intended it to bind successors, (3) it touches and concerns the land, (4) there is horizontal privity (a relationship such as grantor and grantee between the original parties) and vertical privity (a successor taking the relevant estate), and (5) the successor has notice (in most states, by recording). An equitable servitude requires a writing (or an implied reciprocal servitude from a common scheme), intent to bind successors, touch and concern, and notice to the successor (actual, record or inquiry); privity is not required.
