@article
A criminal law answer has three layers: the **act** (and any required result), the **mental state**, and the **causation** link, then any **defense**. The common law and the Model Penal Code (MPC) differ on several points; on the bar, state the majority rule first.

### Mental states
Common law separates **specific intent** (a further purpose, such as larceny's intent to permanently deprive) from **general intent**, plus malice and strict liability. The MPC uses four culpability levels (MPC § 2.02): purposely, knowingly, recklessly (conscious disregard of a substantial and unjustifiable risk) and negligently (a gross deviation from reasonable care the person should have perceived). Voluntary intoxication can negate specific intent but not general intent or recklessness; involuntary intoxication can support an insanity-type defense. A reasonable mistake of fact negates general intent only if the mistake is reasonable; any honest mistake negates specific intent.

### Homicide
**Murder** is the unlawful killing of a human being with malice aforethought: intent to kill, intent to inflict serious bodily harm, a depraved-heart (extreme recklessness) state, or commission of a felony (**felony murder**). Many states divide murder into degrees: premeditation and deliberation is first degree, as are killings during enumerated felonies. **Felony murder** applies to deaths caused in the course of a dangerous felony (traditionally burglary, arson, rape, robbery, kidnapping); the death must be a foreseeable result and the felony must be independent of the killing (the merger rule bars assault as the predicate). Most courts apply the agency theory: a co-felon is not liable for a killing committed by a non-felon, such as police shooting a bystander.

**Voluntary manslaughter** is a killing that would be murder but for adequate provocation (heat of passion): provocation that would arouse sudden and intense passion in a reasonable person, no cooling-off period in fact or in law, and a causal link. **Involuntary manslaughter** is a killing by criminal negligence or recklessness, or in the course of an unlawful act (misdemeanor-manslaughter). Causation requires the defendant's act be the but-for and proximate cause; intervening forces break the chain only if unforeseeable.

### Inchoate crimes and accomplice liability
**Attempt** requires intent to commit the offense plus an act beyond mere preparation (the MPC: a substantial step corroborating the intent). Impossibility: factual impossibility is not a defense; pure legal impossibility is. Attempt merges into the completed offense. **Solicitation** is asking another to commit a crime with intent that the crime occur; it merges into the completed offense if the other person commits it. **Conspiracy** requires an agreement between two or more people (a bilateral rule in most states; the MPC accepts a unilateral approach) with intent to achieve an unlawful objective; the MPC and many states require an overt act, the common law does not (the MPC drops the requirement for first- and second-degree felonies, § 5.03(5)). At common law and in federal law, conspiracy does **not** merge into the completed crime, so both can be punished (the MPC, § 1.07, bars conviction of both a conspiracy and its target offense). A conspirator is liable for the reasonably foreseeable crimes of co-conspirators committed in furtherance (*Pinkerton*). **Accomplice** liability requires aiding, abetting or encouraging the principal with the intent to promote the crime; presence alone is not enough.

### Property crimes and others
**Larceny**: trespassory taking and carrying away of personal property of another with intent to permanently deprive. **Embezzlement**: fraudulent conversion of property already lawfully held. **False pretenses**: obtaining title by a knowing false statement of past or present fact. **Robbery**: larceny from the person or presence by force or fear. **Burglary** (common law): breaking and entering the dwelling of another at night with intent to commit a felony inside; most statutes drop the nighttime and dwelling elements. **Assault** is attempted battery or intentionally placing another in apprehension of imminent harm; **battery** is an unlawful application of force.

### Defenses
**Self-defense**: a reasonable belief in imminent unlawful force; deadly force only against a threat of death or serious harm; the initial aggressor generally loses the defense unless he withdraws. Most states have no duty to retreat; a minority impose it before deadly force if a safe retreat exists. **Duress**: an unlawful threat of imminent death or serious injury; not a defense to murder. **Insanity**: under *M'Naghten*, the defendant, because of a mental disease, did not know the nature of the act or that it was wrong; the MPC asks about lack of substantial capacity to appreciate wrongfulness or conform conduct. Insanity is an affirmative defense in most jurisdictions. **Entrapment** looks to predisposition (subjective test) in the federal system and most states.

### Applying the rules
In bar questions, circle the mental state, then check each charge against its elements and merger. Remember that the Constitution requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every element of the crime.

@example
title: Felony murder and a firefighter
problem: Cara sets fire to an occupied apartment building to collect insurance money. A firefighter dies when a ceiling collapses. Is Cara guilty of murder?
step: Identify the theory: no intent to kill, so consider felony murder (arson is an enumerated dangerous felony).
step: State the rule: a death caused during an inherently dangerous felony is murder if the death was a foreseeable result and the felony is not a merger-barred offense.
step: Apply: the fire in an occupied building made a death reasonably foreseeable, and the firefighter's death occurred during the felony.
step: Conclude: Cara is guilty of felony murder.

@example
title: Conspiracy and merger
problem: Ed and Fay agree to rob a store, and Ed commits the robbery with Fay's help. They are convicted of both conspiracy and robbery. Under the common law, is the conspiracy conviction barred by merger?
step: Identify the doctrine: merger of inchoate offenses into the completed offense.
step: Rule: at common law, attempt and solicitation merge with the completed crime; conspiracy does not.
step: Apply: the agreement is a separate offense from the robbery.
step: Conclude: both convictions stand, and each may be punished.

@mistakes
- Applying the common-law felony-murder rule to a death caused by someone else's act outside the agency rule.
- Saying voluntary intoxication is a defense to every crime, rather than only to specific intent crimes.
- Merging conspiracy into the completed crime.
- Forgetting that duress is not a defense to intentional murder.

@practice mc
prompt: Dina breaks into an occupied apartment at night to steal electronics, and the resident dies of a heart attack from fright when Dina enters. Dina did not intend to harm anyone. What is the best analysis of murder liability?
choice*: Dina may be guilty of felony murder, because the burglary was a dangerous felony and the death was caused in the course of it.
choice: Dina cannot be guilty of homicide because she lacked intent to kill.
choice: Dina is guilty of voluntary manslaughter because she acted in the heat of passion.
choice: Dina is guilty only of burglary because the victim's heart condition was a superseding cause.
hint: Is there a dangerous felony and a death caused in the course of it?
solution: Felony murder dispenses with intent to kill when a death is caused during a dangerous felony and was foreseeable. A pre-existing condition does not break causation (the victim is taken as found), and heat-of-passion manslaughter requires provocation.

@practice mc
prompt: Gil, who is heavily and voluntarily drunk, takes a neighbor's bicycle from the lawn, believing it was his own. He is charged with larceny. Which defense is best?
choice*: He lacked the intent to permanently deprive another of property, which voluntary intoxication and his honest mistake can negate.
choice: Insanity, because intoxication is a mental disease.
choice: Duress, because alcohol impaired his choices.
choice: No defense, because larceny is a general-intent crime.
hint: Larceny requires intent to permanently deprive. What do intoxication and honest mistake do to that intent?
solution: Larceny is a specific-intent crime. Voluntary intoxication can negate specific intent, and an honest claim of right negates it as well. Intoxication is not insanity and not duress.

@practice mc
prompt: Hal and Ivy agree to burn a warehouse for insurance money. Hal sets the fire and the warehouse burns. Both are convicted of arson and of conspiracy to commit arson. Hal argues that the conspiracy conviction merges into the arson. Apply the common-law rule. What is the best response?
choice: He is right, because all inchoate offenses merge into the completed crime.
choice*: He is wrong, because conspiracy does not merge into the completed offense, so both convictions stand.
choice: He is right, because conspiracy merges unless an overt act was proved.
choice: He is wrong, because Ivy cannot be convicted of arson without a separate agreement.
hint: Which inchoate offenses merge with the target crime?
solution: Attempt and solicitation merge with the completed offense; conspiracy does not, and each is separately punishable.

@practice ms
prompt: Which of the following crimes require a specific intent beyond the intent to do the act?
choice*: Larceny (intent to permanently deprive).
choice*: Common-law burglary (intent to commit a felony inside).
choice: Battery committed by a reckless act.
choice*: Attempt (intent to commit the target offense).
choice: Depraved-heart murder.
hint: Look for a further purpose beyond the act itself.
solution: Larceny, burglary and attempt each require a further intent. Reckless battery and depraved-heart murder rest on recklessness, not a specific purpose.

@practice self
prompt: Write an IRAC analysis: Jo is attacked by Max, who is unarmed and threatens only to slap her. Jo shoots and kills Max. Jo's jurisdiction has no duty to retreat. May she claim self-defense?
hint: State the rule first, then apply it to the facts, then conclude.
solution: Issue: Was deadly force justified? Rule: self-defense requires a reasonable belief in imminent unlawful force, and deadly force is permitted only in response to a threat of death or serious bodily harm; the force used must be proportionate. Application: a slap threatens neither death nor serious harm, so deadly force was disproportionate; the absence of a duty to retreat is irrelevant to proportionality. Conclusion: self-defense fails, and Jo is likely guilty of murder (or of voluntary manslaughter in a state that recognizes imperfect self-defense).
rubric: States the proportionality rule for deadly force.
rubric: Notes that retreat is irrelevant here.
rubric: Applies the rule and reaches a conclusion.

@quiz mc
prompt: Lou, enraged after finding his spouse in bed with another person, grabs a gun and shoots the other person immediately. He is charged with murder. What is the best analysis?
choice: Lou is guilty of murder, because adultery is never adequate provocation.
choice*: Lou may be guilty only of voluntary manslaughter, because the provocation would arouse sudden passion in a reasonable person and no cooling off occurred.
choice: Lou is guilty of involuntary manslaughter, because he acted without malice.
choice: Lou has a complete defense of insanity because he lost control.
hint: What elements reduce murder to voluntary manslaughter?
solution: Heat of passion requires adequate provocation, sudden passion, no cooling off and causation. Intentional killing under those conditions is voluntary manslaughter. Involuntary manslaughter is not intentional, and loss of temper is not insanity.

@quiz mc
prompt: Meg agrees to drive Ned to a bank so that he can rob it and get away, and she waits outside with the engine running. Ned robs the bank and returns. Meg did not enter the bank. What is the best analysis of Meg's liability?
choice: Meg is not liable, because she did not enter the bank.
choice*: Meg is liable as an accomplice, because she aided Ned with the intent to promote the robbery.
choice: Meg is liable only for larceny, because she did not use force.
choice: Meg is liable only as an accessory after the fact.
hint: Did she act, with what intent, before the crime was complete?
solution: An accomplice aids the principal with the intent to promote the crime; Meg's aid before and during the robbery makes her liable for it. Presence alone would not suffice, but her assistance and intent do.
