@article
Constitutional law questions on the bar usually turn on one move: identify **who is acting** (Congress, the President, a state) and **which constitutional source of power or limit** applies. Start every analysis there, then apply the rule, then state the conclusion.

### Congress: enumerated powers
Congress has only enumerated powers. The Commerce Clause lets it regulate (1) the channels of interstate commerce, (2) the instrumentalities of, and persons or things in, interstate commerce, and (3) activities that substantially affect interstate commerce. For category (3), Congress may aggregate the effects of a class of **economic** activity (*Wickard v. Filburn* (1942); *Gonzales v. Raich* (2005)). Purely noneconomic, local activity is different: *United States v. Lopez* (1995) and *United States v. Morrison* (2000) struck down statutes regulating gun possession near schools and gender-motivated violence, in part because the regulated conduct was not economic. Congress cannot use the Commerce Clause to compel individuals to enter commerce (*NFIB v. Sebelius* (2012)).

The **taxing power** reaches any measure that raises revenue, even one with a regulatory purpose. Under the **spending power**, Congress may attach conditions to federal funds if the spending serves the general welfare, the conditions are unambiguous, they relate to a federal interest in the program, they do not require unconstitutional action, and they are not so coercive that they become compulsion (*South Dakota v. Dole* (1987); *NFIB*). Congress may also enact laws that are necessary and proper to carry out an enumerated power.

### Federalism limits on Congress
The Tenth Amendment is the basis for the **anticommandeering doctrine**: Congress may not order a state legislature to enact a law or order state officers to administer a federal program (*New York v. United States* (1992); *Printz v. United States* (1997); *Murphy v. NCAA* (2018)). Congress may regulate states as participants in an activity if the law applies generally. Sovereign immunity under the Eleventh Amendment bars most private damages suits against a state in federal court, subject to exceptions (congressional abrogation under Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment, state waiver, and suits for prospective relief against a state officer under *Ex parte Young*).

### State power: the dormant Commerce Clause and preemption
A state law that **discriminates** against interstate commerce, in purpose or effect, is almost always invalid unless it is necessary to a legitimate local interest that no nondiscriminatory alternative can serve. A neutral state law is valid unless its burden on interstate commerce is clearly excessive in relation to local benefits (*Pike v. Bruce Church* (1970)). Two exceptions: **congressional authorization** and the **market-participant** doctrine (a state buying or selling in the market may prefer its own citizens). **Preemption** arises under the Supremacy Clause: express preemption, field preemption, or conflict preemption (impossibility or obstacle).

### Separation of powers
*Youngstown Sheet & Tube v. Sawyer* (1952) frames presidential power by reference to congressional authorization. Congress must provide an intelligible principle when delegating; the Court upheld the Universal Service Fund against a nondelegation challenge in *FCC v. Consumers' Research* (2025). Under the **major questions doctrine** (*West Virginia v. EPA* (2022); *Biden v. Nebraska* (2023)), an agency needs clear congressional authorization for decisions of vast economic and political significance. In *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* (2026, 6-3), the Court held that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorize tariffs; the majority relied on the statutory text, and a plurality also applied the major questions doctrine. Under *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo* (2024), courts decide the best reading of a statute themselves and give an agency's view only *Skidmore* respect; *Chevron* is overruled, though earlier *Chevron*-based holdings remain in force.

**Removal.** The President may remove executive officers at will unless Congress validly limits removal. *Trump v. Slaughter* (June 2026, 6-3) overruled *Humphrey's Executor*: the for-cause protection for FTC commissioners is unconstitutional, so they are removable at will. The Court left open the Federal Reserve (in *Trump v. Cook* (2026) it refused to let a Federal Reserve governor's removal take effect while the case continues) and offices outside the traditional executive branch. *Seila Law* (2020) and *Collins v. Yellen* (2021) had already invalidated removal limits on single-director agencies. *SEC v. Jarkesy* (2024) requires a jury when the SEC seeks civil penalties for securities fraud.

**Remedies.** *Trump v. CASA, Inc.* (2025): universal injunctions likely exceed the equitable authority Congress gave federal courts; relief is limited to what is needed to give the plaintiffs complete relief.

### Justiciability
A federal court needs **standing** (injury in fact that is concrete and particularized, caused by the defendant, redressable by the court), **ripeness**, **no mootness** (subject to exceptions for conduct capable of repetition yet evading review and voluntary cessation), and no **political question**. Generalized grievances and taxpayer status do not confer standing. A claim under the APA accrues when the plaintiff is injured (*Corner Post v. Board of Governors* (2024)).

### Applying the rules
For each question: (1) identify the actor; (2) name the source of power; (3) name the limit; (4) apply to the facts; (5) conclude with one best answer.

@example
title: A federal statute on local conduct
problem: Congress enacts a statute making it a crime to possess a laser pointer within 500 feet of any school, with no finding or jurisdictional element. Ben is charged. Under the Commerce Clause, is the statute valid?
step: Identify the actor and source: Congress, relying on the commerce power's third category (substantial effects).
step: State the rule: for category three, Congress may regulate economic activity in the aggregate; noneconomic local conduct is much harder to reach (*Lopez*, *Morrison*).
step: Apply: simple possession near a school is not itself economic activity, and the statute has no jurisdictional element linking it to interstate commerce.
step: Conclude: the statute is likely beyond the commerce power as applied, unless the government can show the possession is part of a larger economic regulatory scheme.

@example
title: An at-will removal
problem: The President removes a member of an independent agency that wields substantial executive power over a for-cause protection enacted by Congress. The member sues for reinstatement. Result?
step: Identify the issue: a statutory limit on the President's removal of an executive officer.
step: State the rule: after *Trump v. Slaughter* (2026), for-cause protection for FTC commissioners is unconstitutional; *Seila Law* and *Collins* had already struck limits for single-director agencies.
step: Apply: an agency wielding substantial executive power falls within the reasoning of those cases. The Federal Reserve and non-executive-branch offices were left open.
step: Conclude: the member likely loses, and the statute's for-cause limit is unenforceable as applied.

@mistakes
- Treating all local activity as unreachable: economic activity may be aggregated under *Wickard*; only noneconomic local conduct faces *Lopez* limits.
- Applying the anticommandeering doctrine to generally applicable federal laws that happen to bind states, or to conditions on federal funds.
- Teaching *Humphrey's Executor* or *Chevron* as current law.
- Forgetting that a state law that discriminates against out-of-state commerce is analyzed under a far stricter standard than a neutral law.

choice: No, because Congress may regulate firearms only if the purchase is itself an interstate sale.
choice: Yes, because the statute is a temporary measure and temporary commands do not infringe state sovereignty.

solution: The statute directs state officers to carry out a federal program, which is prohibited commandeering under the Tenth Amendment (*Printz*). The commerce power does not override that limit, and its temporary nature is irrelevant.

choice*: The condition must be related to the federal interest in the funded program.

@practice self

solution: Issue: May a federal court enter a universal injunction? Rule: *Trump v.
