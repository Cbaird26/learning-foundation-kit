@article
Individual-rights questions start with **state action** (the Constitution restrains government, not private actors, unless a private party performs a traditional public function or the government is entangled in the conduct), then ask which **right** is at stake and which **standard of review** follows.

### Due process
**Substantive due process** protects fundamental rights: government infringement gets strict scrutiny; other liberty interests get rational-basis review. A right must be deeply rooted in history and tradition (*Washington v. Glucksberg* (1997)). *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization* (2022) overruled *Roe* and *Casey*: there is no constitutional right to abortion, and abortion regulation is reviewed under rational basis. **Procedural due process** applies when government deprives a person of life, liberty or property; the process due is balanced under *Mathews v. Eldridge* (1976) (private interest, risk of error and value of added safeguards, government interest). The Takings Clause requires just compensation when government takes private property for public use. A physical occupation is a per se taking; a regulation that eliminates all economically beneficial use is a categorical taking unless background principles of property or nuisance law forbid the use (*Lucas* (1992)); other regulations use the *Penn Central* factors.

### Equal protection
Race or national origin classifications get **strict scrutiny** (necessary to a compelling interest), whether they burden or benefit a group, but discriminatory purpose is required for a facially neutral law. Sex classifications get **intermediate scrutiny** (exceedingly persuasive justification; substantially related to an important interest). Most other classifications get **rational basis** (state alienage classifications get strict scrutiny, and classifications based on nonmarital birth get intermediate scrutiny). *Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard* (2023): race-conscious admissions at Harvard and UNC violate equal protection. *United States v. Skrmetti* (2025): Tennessee's ban on puberty blockers and hormones to treat gender dysphoria in minors gets rational-basis review and is upheld. *Little v. Hecox* (2026): Idaho's law limiting girls' and women's teams by biological sex does not violate the Equal Protection Clause; *West Virginia v. B.P.J.* (2026): Title IX allows schools to keep separate male and female teams based on biological sex. *Louisiana v. Callais* (2026): a congressional map was an unconstitutional racial gerrymander because the Voting Rights Act did not require the second majority-minority district. *Trump v. Barbara* (2026): children born in the United States to parents who are unlawfully or temporarily present are citizens at birth under the Fourteenth Amendment.

### First Amendment: speech
A **content-based** restriction (one that depends on the topic or message) gets strict scrutiny (*Reed v. Town of Gilbert* (2015)). A **content-neutral** time, place or manner restriction must be narrowly tailored to a significant interest and leave open ample alternative channels. Unprotected categories include incitement (*Brandenburg*: directed to and likely to produce imminent lawless action), true threats, obscenity, and defamation under *New York Times v. Sullivan*'s actual-malice rule for public officials. In *Counterman v. Colorado* (2023), a true-threats prosecution requires proof of at least recklessness. *303 Creative LLC v. Elenis* (2023): a state cannot compel a designer to create expressive speech conveying a message she disagrees with. *Free Speech Coalition v. Paxton* (2025): intermediate scrutiny applies to age verification for sexual content harmful to minors. *Chiles v. Salazar* (2026): a ban on conversion therapy as applied to talk therapy regulates speech based on content and viewpoint; the Court remanded for the correct scrutiny. *NRSC v. FEC* (2026): limits on political parties' coordinated spending with candidates violate the First Amendment. *First Choice Women's Resource Centers v. Davenport* (2026): standing to challenge a state subpoena for donor information.

### First Amendment: religion
*Kennedy v. Bremerton School District* (2022): the Establishment Clause is read by reference to historical practices and understandings; the *Lemon* test is abandoned. A law that is neutral and generally applicable is reviewed under rational basis for free exercise (*Employment Division v. Smith*), but a law that is not neutral or generally applicable gets strict scrutiny. *Mahmoud v. Taylor* (2025): parents had a free-exercise claim to opt their children out of instruction.

### Second Amendment
*New York State Rifle & Pistol Ass'n v. Bruen* (2022): if the Amendment's plain text covers the conduct, the government must show the regulation is consistent with the nation's historical tradition of firearm regulation; there is no interest-balancing. *United States v. Rahimi* (2024): upheld the federal ban for persons under domestic-violence restraining orders. *Wolford v. Lopez* (2026): Hawaii's default ban on licensed carry on private property open to the public, without the owner's express permission, violates the Second Amendment. *United States v. Hemani* (2026): the federal ban on gun possession by drug users was unconstitutional as applied without a showing of dangerousness or impairment.

### Other tests to know
Also review: privileges or immunities, contracts clause, ex post facto, bills of attainder, the right to travel, and the Fourteenth Amendment's citizenship rule. Present contested questions neutrally: state the holding, then the majority's reasoning and the dissent's.

@example
title: A content-based sign ordinance
problem: A town ordinance allows large signs for temporary events but limits signs directing the public to a religious service to a smaller size. A church challenges it. What standard applies and what result is likely?
step: Identify the right and actor: free speech against a municipality (state action).
step: Rule: a law that applies differently depending on the topic or message is content-based and receives strict scrutiny (*Reed*).
step: Apply: the size limit depends on whether the sign directs people to a religious service, so it is content-based.
step: Conclude: the town must show the rule is narrowly tailored to a compelling interest; it likely cannot, so the ordinance is probably invalid.

@mistakes
- Applying intermediate scrutiny to every discrimination claim; only sex classifications receive it, and race gets strict scrutiny.
- Treating *Dobbs* as a case about equal protection for sex classifications; it concerned substantive due process and rational-basis review.
- Teaching the *Lemon* test or the pre-*Bruen* interest-balancing test as current.
- Saying the First Amendment applies to private employers or private clubs without state action.

@practice mc
prompt: A state allows women to buy a type of beverage at age 18 but requires men to be 21. The state argues that statistics show young men commit more alcohol-related offenses. A male 19-year-old challenges the law. What is the best analysis?
choice: Rational-basis review applies because age is not a suspect class, so the law is valid.
choice: Strict scrutiny applies because the law classifies by sex, and the law fails.
choice*: Intermediate scrutiny applies because the law classifies by sex, and generalized statistics do not make it substantially related to an important interest.
choice: The law is valid because the Equal Protection Clause does not protect men.
hint: What type of classification appears on the face of the statute?
solution: A sex classification gets intermediate scrutiny, and crude generalizations are insufficient (*Craig v. Boren*). The Equal Protection Clause protects both sexes, and strict scrutiny is for race and national origin.

@practice mc
prompt: A town ordinance prohibits signs outside a person's home that urge a political candidate's election but allows signs announcing garage sales. Ana, a homeowner, is cited for a campaign sign. What is the best analysis?
choice: The ordinance is valid because it is a content-neutral time, place or manner regulation of signs.
choice*: The ordinance is content-based because it treats signs differently depending on their message, so strict scrutiny applies and it likely fails.
choice: The ordinance is valid because residential property is not a public forum.
choice: The ordinance is invalid only if it prevents all speech in the town.
hint: Does enforcement depend on what the sign says?
solution: The ordinance distinguishes signs by subject matter, so it is content-based and gets strict scrutiny (*Reed*); the limits on speech outside a public forum do not alter that rule for a message-based ban on private speech.

@practice mc
prompt: Dan sends many hostile messages to a stranger. He is prosecuted under a state statute that criminalizes statements a reasonable person would regard as threatening. The state offers no evidence of Dan's awareness that the messages could be viewed as threatening. A jury convicts. What is the best argument on appeal?
choice: The conviction must stand because true threats are unprotected speech and the objective standard is enough.
choice*: The conviction cannot stand because a true-threats prosecution requires proof that the defendant acted at least recklessly regarding the threatening nature of the statements.
choice: The conviction cannot stand because only intent to carry out the threat satisfies the First Amendment.
choice: The conviction stands only if the message incited imminent lawless action.
hint: Recall the mens rea the Supreme Court requires in 2023.
solution: *Counterman v. Colorado* (2023) requires at least recklessness; an objective standard alone is not enough. Purposeful intent to carry out the threat is not required, and incitement is a separate category.

@practice mc
prompt: A state regulation bans all construction on a landowner's coastal lot, which she bought before the regulation was adopted. The ban leaves the lot with no economically beneficial use. State law has never treated construction on the lot as a nuisance. What is the best analysis?
choice: There is no taking because the owner retains title to the land.
choice: There is a taking only if the state physically occupies the lot.
choice*: There is a categorical taking requiring compensation, unless background principles of the state's property or nuisance law would have forbidden the use.
choice: There is a taking only if the diminution in value exceeds 50 percent under the *Penn Central* test.
hint: When a regulation eliminates all economically beneficial use, which rule applies instead of ad hoc balancing?
solution: Total deprivation triggers *Lucas*: compensation unless background principles bar the use. Retaining title does not defeat a regulatory taking, and no fixed 50 percent rule exists.

@practice self

(d) Rational basis after *Dobbs*. (e) Balance the private interest, risk of erroneous deprivation and the government's interest under *Mathews*.
rubric: States the correct standard for each chosen issue.
rubric: Names the controlling case where applicable.
rubric: Avoids overbroad descriptions of recent cases.

@quiz mc
prompt: A state law bans puberty blockers and cross-sex hormone treatment for minors for the purpose of treating gender dysphoria. Parents of a minor challenge it under equal protection. What is the best analysis under current law?
choice: Strict scrutiny applies and the law fails.
choice: Intermediate scrutiny applies and the law fails because it classifies by sex.
choice*: Rational-basis review applies and the law is upheld.
choice: The law is invalid because of the Fourteenth Amendment's citizenship rule.
hint: Recall the 2025 holding in *Skrmetti*.
solution: *United States v. Skrmetti* (2025) applied rational-basis review to a Tennessee law of this type and upheld it. The other choices misstate the standard or the clause involved.
