@article
Criminal procedure questions follow a sequence: **Was there government action? Which amendment? Was the right violated? What is the remedy and who may seek it?** Always finish with the remedy, since the bar often asks whether evidence is admissible.

### The Fourth Amendment
A **search** occurs when the government intrudes on a reasonable expectation of privacy (*Katz*) or physically trespasses to gather information (*United States v. Jones*). A **seizure** of a person occurs when a reasonable person would not feel free to leave. *Chatrie v. United States* (2026): obtaining a person's location data from Google with a geofence warrant is a Fourth Amendment search. A search generally requires a warrant supported by probable cause and particularity, issued by a neutral magistrate; exceptions include:

- **Search incident to arrest**: of the person and the area within immediate control; vehicles only if the arrestee can reach the passenger compartment or evidence of the offense of arrest may be found there.
- **Automobile exception**: probable cause to believe the vehicle holds contraband or evidence permits a search of any container that could hold it.
- **Plain view**: lawful vantage point, lawful access, and the incriminating character is immediately apparent.
- **Consent**: voluntary, by someone with actual or apparent authority.
- **Exigent circumstances**, including hot pursuit and emergency aid. *Case v. Montana* (2026): police may enter a home without a warrant when they have an objectively reasonable basis to believe someone inside needs emergency aid.
- **Inventory** searches under standard procedures, and **administrative** or special-needs searches.

A **stop and frisk** (*Terry v. Ohio* (1968)) requires reasonable suspicion of crime for the stop, and reasonable suspicion that the person is armed and dangerous for the frisk. Probable cause is required for an arrest. A warrantless arrest in public needs probable cause; a home arrest needs a warrant (*Payton*).

*Barnes v. Felix* (2025): excessive-force claims under the Fourth Amendment are judged on the totality of the circumstances, not only the "moment of threat."

### The exclusionary rule
Illegally obtained evidence, and its derivative fruits, is generally excluded from the prosecution's case-in-chief (*Mapp v. Ohio*). Only a person whose own Fourth Amendment rights were violated may seek exclusion (standing). Exceptions: **independent source**, **inevitable discovery**, **attenuation**, **good-faith reliance** on a warrant later found invalid (*Leon*), and use for **impeachment** of the defendant. The rule does not apply in grand jury proceedings or civil cases generally.

### The Fifth Amendment and Miranda
*Miranda v. Arizona* (1966) warnings are required before **custodial interrogation** (custody: a reasonable person would feel a restraint on freedom of movement like formal arrest; interrogation: questioning or its functional equivalent). The suspect may waive knowingly, intelligently and voluntarily. Invoking the right to remain silent must be honored; invoking the right to counsel requires questioning to stop until counsel is present or the suspect reinitiates (*Edwards*); the presumption ends after a 14-day break in custody (*Maryland v. Shatzer* (2010)); the invocation must be unambiguous. Statements are admissible for impeachment if voluntary. A public-safety exception exists. *Vega v. Tekoh* (2022): a *Miranda* violation alone does not support a § 1983 damages claim. Involuntary confessions violate due process and are inadmissible for any purpose.

### The Sixth Amendment
The right to counsel attaches at the **initiation of formal charges** and applies at critical stages (the arraignment, post-charge interrogation, a lineup after charge). Statements deliberately elicited from a charged defendant without counsel violate the right (*Massiah*), even through an informant; it is offense-specific. *Strickland v. Washington* (1984) requires deficient performance and prejudice for ineffective assistance. A unanimous jury is required for serious offenses (*Ramos*), and any fact increasing the maximum penalty must be found by a jury (*Apprendi*). Double jeopardy bars successive prosecutions for the same offense (*Blockburger*: same elements test) after acquittal, conviction or a mistrial without necessity. Confrontation Clause issues appear in the evidence lessons.

### Timing a criminal set
At the legacy bar-exam pace (200 questions in 360 minutes), the budget is 1.8 minutes per question. For this block's deliverable, time a 30-question criminal law and procedure set and log each question's minutes and error type.

@example
title: Entry for emergency aid
problem: Officers arrive at a house after a neighbor reports screaming. Through the open door they see a man holding a bloody towel to his arm, and he does not respond to questions. They enter without a warrant and find drugs in plain view. Is the evidence admissible?
step: Identify the Fourth Amendment issue: warrantless entry into a home.
step: Rule: police may enter without a warrant when they have an objectively reasonable basis to believe someone inside needs emergency aid (*Case v. Montana*).
step: Apply: the screaming, the blood and the unresponsive man give an objectively reasonable basis.
step: Conclude: the entry was lawful, and drugs in plain view are admissible.

@example
title: Charged defendant and an informant
problem: After Lee is indicted and has a lawyer, police place an informant in his cell who deliberately gets Lee to describe the offense. Lee has not been given Miranda warnings. Is the statement admissible in the prosecution's case?
step: Identify the amendment: the right to counsel attached at indictment, so analyze the Sixth Amendment.
step: Rule: deliberately eliciting statements from a charged defendant without counsel violates the right (*Massiah*), even if the questioner is an informant.
step: Apply: the informant was a government agent who deliberately elicited the statement after indictment.
step: Conclude: the statement is inadmissible in the case-in-chief. Miranda is not the issue.

@mistakes
- Requiring Miranda warnings for every police question; they apply only to custodial interrogation.
- Treating *Vega v. Tekoh* as making Miranda statements admissible; it only bars a § 1983 damages claim.
- Letting a defendant assert another person's Fourth Amendment rights.
- Applying the Sixth Amendment before charges are filed, or across offenses not yet charged.

prompt: Without a warrant, police obtain from a technology company the stored location history of Pam's phone, which places her near a burglary scene. Pam moves to suppress. What is the best analysis?
choice: There was no search, because Pam voluntarily shared the data with the company.
choice*: Obtaining her location data this way is a Fourth Amendment search, so the police needed a valid warrant.

solution: *Chatrie v. United States* (2026) held that obtaining a person's location data from Google with a geofence warrant is a search. Under *Carpenter v. United States* (2018), the third-party doctrine does not defeat a privacy interest in detailed location records, so acquiring such stored location history with no warrant at all is a search that requires probable cause and a warrant. The sharing and no-content arguments do not avoid the Fourth Amendment.

@quiz mc
prompt: Rob is arrested for a bank robbery, receives warnings, and says "I want a lawyer." The officers stop. Two days later, a different officer, without Rob's lawyer present, gives Rob fresh warnings and Rob signs a waiver and confesses. What is the best analysis?
choice: Admissible, because Rob was warned and waived in writing.
choice*: Inadmissible, because after invoking counsel Rob could not be re-approached without counsel unless he himself reinitiated contact.

solution: Under *Edwards*, questioning must cease until counsel is present unless the suspect reinitiates; fresh warnings do not cure it. Change of officer does not matter. The *Edwards* presumption ends only after a break in custody of at least 14 days (*Maryland v. Shatzer*); two days is too short.
