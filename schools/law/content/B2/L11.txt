@article
A mixed set does not announce the subject. Your first task on each question is to **name the subject and the issue in under 15 seconds**, then recall the rule and test the choices. Choices on the exam are conclusions with reasons, so a wrong choice is usually wrong for one nameable reason: a misstated rule, a missing element, or the right rule applied to the wrong fact.

### A quick method
1. **Read the call of the question first**, then the facts.
2. **Name the subject and the issue** (for example, "Evidence: impeachment of a defendant with an unwarned statement").
3. **Predict the answer** before reading the choices.
4. **Eliminate** choices with a wrong rule, a wrong reason or an unsupported fact.
5. **Choose the best answer**, mark it if uncertain, and move on.

### Pacing
Under the legacy Multistate Bar Examination format, 200 questions are given in two three-hour sessions, a pace of 1.8 minutes per question. The NextGen exam uses integrated question sets and a different structure, and some questions use supplied law; check the NCBE exam information for the format in your jurisdiction. In either format: do not spend more than about two minutes on any one multiple-choice question; guess on every question, since there is no penalty for wrong answers; and check the clock at regular intervals (for example, every ten questions).

### Cross-subject rules to have ready
- **Impeachment with an unwarned statement.** A voluntary statement obtained in violation of *Miranda* may be used to impeach the defendant's contrary testimony (*Harris v. New York*), but not in the prosecution's case-in-chief. An involuntary statement may not be used for any purpose.
- **Corporate privilege.** The privilege belongs to the corporation; an employee's communication with corporate counsel for the purpose of legal advice is privileged (*Upjohn*); the employee cannot waive it.
- **Statements by corporate agents.** A statement by a party's employee on a matter within the scope of employment, made during the employment, is non-hearsay against the party (FRE 801(d)(2)(D)).
- **Federal statutes and the Second Amendment.** Apply *Bruen*'s text-and-history test; recall *Rahimi* and *Hemani* as the latest applications.
- **Agency actions.** After *Loper Bright*, courts decide the best reading of a statute; after *Slaughter*, removal limits for the FTC are invalid; after *Learning Resources*, IEEPA does not authorize tariffs.
- **Remedies.** *Trump v. CASA* limits universal injunctions.

### Error log
For each missed question, record: subject, topic, whether the error was **knowledge** (you did not know the rule), **application** (you knew the rule but misapplied it), **reading** (you misread the facts or the call) or **timing** (you rushed or ran out of time), and the fix. Patterns in the log decide what to review next, as in the next lesson.

### Block deliverable
Complete a timed mixed set. Use 1.8 minutes per question as your budget, check the clock every ten questions, log every miss, and total your errors by type and subject.

@example
title: Mixed set: a statement used to impeach
problem: Without Miranda warnings, police question Gus in custody and he says he was in the city on the night of a robbery. At trial, Gus testifies that he was out of state. The prosecutor offers the earlier statement to impeach him. The statement was voluntary. Is it admissible?
step: Identify the subjects: criminal procedure (Miranda) and evidence (impeachment).
step: Rule: a voluntary statement obtained without warnings may be used to impeach the defendant's inconsistent testimony.
step: Apply: the statement was voluntary and contradicts Gus's testimony.
step: Conclude: admissible for impeachment only, not in the case-in-chief.

@mistakes
- Spending four minutes on one question and rushing the next ten.
- Reading the choices before predicting the answer, which makes plausible distractors more attractive.
- Leaving a question blank instead of guessing.
- Logging "careless" as the error type without naming the rule that was missed.

@practice mc
prompt: A corporation's general counsel interviews a sales manager, at the CEO's direction, about the manager's dealings with a competitor, in order to give the company legal advice. In a later civil suit, the plaintiff asks the manager to testify about what the manager told the general counsel. The manager is willing to answer. What is the best analysis?
choice: The communication is not privileged because the manager is not the client.
choice*: The communication is privileged, and the corporation, not the manager, controls whether it is waived.
choice: The communication is privileged only if the manager consents to its disclosure.
choice: The communication is not privileged because the general counsel questioned the manager.
hint: Who holds the corporation's privilege?
solution: Under *Upjohn*, an employee's communication to corporate counsel at management's direction to secure legal advice is privileged, and the corporation holds the privilege.

@practice mc
prompt: Lee is charged under a federal statute that bans gun possession by any person who is an unlawful drug user. The government shows only that Lee regularly used marijuana at home and kept a handgun in a closet. It offers no evidence that Lee was impaired when possessing the gun or that Lee was dangerous. Lee moves to dismiss on Second Amendment grounds. What is the best analysis?
choice: The motion fails because Lee's drug use makes the statute valid in every application.
choice*: The motion should be granted as applied, because the government has made no showing of dangerousness or impairment.
choice: The motion fails because the Second Amendment protects only possession for hunting.
choice: The motion should be granted because the Court applies strict scrutiny to every firearm law.
hint: Recall the 2026 as-applied holding about drug users.
solution: *United States v. Hemani* (2026) held the federal ban unconstitutional as applied without a showing of dangerousness or impairment. The Court's test is text and historical tradition, not strict scrutiny.

@practice mc
prompt: A passenger in a car, who owns nothing in it, is a friend of the driver. Police lawfully stop the car for a speeding violation and then search the glove box without a warrant, consent or probable cause, finding drugs. The passenger is charged with possession of the drugs. The passenger moves to suppress the drugs as the product of an illegal search. What is the best analysis?
choice: The motion should be granted because the glove box search was unlawful and the passenger was seized in the stop.
choice*: The motion should be denied, because the passenger has no legitimate expectation of privacy in the glove box and cannot assert the driver's Fourth Amendment rights, even though the passenger was seized in the stop.
choice: The motion should be denied because passengers are never seized in a traffic stop.
choice: The motion should be granted because the glove box search was not incident to arrest.
hint: Fourth Amendment rights are personal: whose privacy interest did the glove box search invade?
solution: A passenger is seized in a traffic stop, but the stop here was lawful, and a challenge to the search of a container requires the passenger's own reasonable expectation of privacy in it. The passenger has none, so the motion to suppress likely fails.

@quiz mc
prompt: The SEC brings an enforcement action seeking civil penalties for securities fraud against a company, before the agency's own administrative law judge. The company objects and demands a jury. What is the best analysis?
choice: The company has no right to a jury because the SEC is an executive agency.
choice*: The Seventh Amendment requires a jury when the SEC seeks civil penalties for securities fraud, so the claim must be heard in court.
choice: The company has a right to a jury only if it seeks equitable relief.
choice: The Seventh Amendment applies only to private disputes.
hint: Recall *SEC v. Jarkesy* (2024).
solution: *Jarkesy* holds that the Seventh Amendment requires a jury when the SEC seeks civil penalties for securities fraud.

@quiz mc
prompt: In a civil trial against a store owner, a plaintiff offers an employee's statement made the day after the accident: "I knew that mat was loose and told the manager." The employee works on the floor and was speaking to a coworker about the store's cleaning practices. The employee is available to testify. What is the best analysis?
choice: Inadmissible hearsay, because the employee is available.
choice*: Admissible against the store as a statement by an employee on a matter within the scope of employment made during the employment, if the matter was within the employee's job.
choice: Inadmissible, because only the store owner's statements bind the store.
choice: Admissible only as an excited utterance.
hint: Which opposing party statement rule covers employee statements?
solution: FRE 801(d)(2)(D) treats an employee's statement on a matter within the scope of employment, made during the relationship, as non-hearsay against the employer. The employee's availability does not matter.
