@article
Business associations answers start with the **form** of the business (agency, partnership, LLC or corporation), then ask **who may bind it, who is liable, and what duties are owed.**

### Agency
An agent acts for a principal with consent and subject to the principal's control. **Actual authority** is express or implied (what the agent reasonably believes the principal wants). **Apparent authority** arises from the principal's manifestation to a third party that the agent is authorized; the third party must reasonably rely. **Ratification** adopts an unauthorized act with knowledge of the material facts. A principal is liable in contract if the agent acts within actual or apparent authority. A principal is liable in tort under **respondeat superior** for an employee's torts within the scope of employment (a frolic, as opposed to a detour, is outside it); a principal is generally not liable for an independent contractor's torts, subject to non-delegable duties and inherently dangerous work. An agent owes the principal duties of loyalty, obedience and care, and must not profit secretly.

### Partnerships
A **general partnership** forms by the association of two or more people to carry on a business for profit as co-owners, with no formalities; sharing profits is evidence of it. Under the Revised Uniform Partnership Act (RUPA), partners are jointly and severally liable for partnership obligations, but a judgment creditor must exhaust partnership assets first; each partner is an agent for apparently ordinary-course business; partners owe fiduciary duties of loyalty and care (limited to avoiding gross negligence and reckless or intentional misconduct); and partners share profits equally, and losses in proportion to profits, unless otherwise agreed. A partner's dissociation does not always dissolve the business. A **limited partnership** has at least one general partner (fully liable) and limited partners who are not liable beyond their contribution as long as they do not take part in control (under older law). A **limited liability partnership** shields partners from personal liability for the torts of other partners and, under the full-shield statutes of most states, for all partnership obligations (a partner remains liable for his own torts).

### LLCs
A limited liability company is created by filing articles with the state. Members are not personally liable for LLC obligations, and may manage directly (member-managed) or through managers. The operating agreement governs internal affairs; fiduciary duties exist by default but are modifiable within limits (the implied covenant of good faith cannot be eliminated).

### Corporations
**Formation** requires filing articles of incorporation; a de facto corporation or corporation by estoppel can protect participants in some cases. **Promoters** are personally liable on pre-incorporation contracts until the corporation adopts them and a novation occurs. **Limited liability** is the default, but courts may **pierce the corporate veil** when the shareholders disregarded corporate formalities, commingled funds, undercapitalized the company, or used the corporation to commit fraud or injustice, and the plaintiff shows an unfair result. Courts are generally more willing to pierce for tort creditors, who did not choose to deal with the corporation, than for contract creditors, who could have bargained for protection.

**Directors** manage the corporation; **officers** run day-to-day affairs. The **duty of care** is judged by the **business judgment rule**: courts presume directors acted on an informed basis, in good faith and in the honest belief that the action served the corporation. The presumption is rebutted by fraud, illegality, conflict of interest or a lack of informed decisions. The **duty of loyalty** forbids self-dealing: an interested-director transaction is not voidable merely because of the conflict if it is approved by disinterested directors or shareholders after disclosure, or is fair to the corporation. The **corporate opportunity doctrine** bars a fiduciary from taking an opportunity within the corporation's line of business unless it is first offered to the corporation and rejected.

**Shareholders** vote on directors and fundamental changes (mergers, sale of substantially all assets, dissolution, and charter amendments). A quorum is generally a majority of shares entitled to vote, and action requires approval by a majority of the shares represented at a meeting where a quorum is present; directors are elected by a plurality. **Cumulative voting**, where authorized, lets a shareholder cast votes equal to shares times the number of seats. Dissenting shareholders in a merger usually have **appraisal rights**. Shareholders may inspect books and records for a proper purpose. A **derivative suit** enforces a corporate claim; the plaintiff must be a shareholder at the time of the wrong (contemporaneous ownership) and throughout the suit, must make a demand on the board or show it is futile, and must fairly represent the corporation; any recovery goes to the corporation. A **direct suit** enforces the shareholder's own rights. Under federal securities law, Rule 10b-5 prohibits fraud in connection with securities trading, and *SEC v. Jarkesy* (2024) requires a jury when the SEC seeks civil penalties for securities fraud.

### Computing cumulative voting
To guarantee electing $r$ directors out of $d$ seats with $S$ shares voting, a shareholder needs

$$\left\lfloor \frac{r\,S}{d+1} \right\rfloor + 1 \text{ shares}.$$

@example
title: Apparent authority
problem: Owner tells Agent not to sign any lease over \$10,000 without approval. Agent, who has long handled Owner's leases, signs a \$15,000 lease with Lessor, who knows nothing of the restriction. Is Owner bound?
step: Identify authority: actual authority is limited by the instruction, so look at apparent authority.
step: Rule: apparent authority arises from the principal's manifestations; a third party reasonably relies on them.
step: Apply: Owner allowed Agent to handle leases in the past, a manifestation, and Lessor had no notice of the limit.
step: Conclude: Owner is bound; Owner may have a claim against Agent for breaching the instruction.

@example
title: Cumulative voting
problem: A corporation has 6,000 shares voting for 5 directors under cumulative voting. How many shares guarantee election of one director?
step: Use the formula $\lfloor rS/(d+1)\rfloor + 1$ with $r = 1$, $S = 6000$, $d = 5$.
step: Compute $6000/6 = 1000$.
step: Add one share: $1001$.

@mistakes
- Treating partners as protected by limited liability; general partners are jointly and severally liable.
- Piercing the veil whenever a corporation is undercapitalized, without the other elements and an unfair result.
- Overlooking the contemporaneous-ownership requirement in derivative suits.
- Ignoring apparent authority when actual authority is limited.
