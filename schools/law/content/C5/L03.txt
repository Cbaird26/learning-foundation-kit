@article
The law of demand tells you the *direction* of the response to a price change. **Elasticity** tells you the *size*. It is a ratio of percentage changes, so it has no units and does not depend on whether quantity is measured in pounds or tons.

### Price elasticity of demand

$$E_d = \frac{\%\Delta Q_d}{\%\Delta P}.$$

Because demand slopes downward, $E_d$ is negative; economists usually quote its absolute value. To avoid getting a different answer depending on which price you call "starting", use the **midpoint method**, which measures each change relative to the average of the old and new values:

$$E_d = \frac{(Q_2 - Q_1)/\tfrac{1}{2}(Q_1 + Q_2)}{(P_2 - P_1)/\tfrac{1}{2}(P_1 + P_2)}.$$

For a linear demand curve $Q = a - bP$, the **point elasticity** at a price $P$ is

$$|E_d| = \frac{bP}{Q}.$$

It is larger at higher prices on the same straight line, so a straight demand curve is elastic in its upper part and inelastic in its lower part. Slope and elasticity are different things.

### Classification

- $|E_d| > 1$: **elastic**; quantity changes proportionally more than price.
- $|E_d| < 1$: **inelastic**; quantity changes proportionally less than price.
- $|E_d| = 1$: **unit elastic**.
- $|E_d| = 0$ (vertical demand) is **perfectly inelastic**; an infinite elasticity (horizontal demand) is **perfectly elastic**.

### Total revenue test

Total revenue is $TR = P \times Q$. When demand is **elastic**, price and total revenue move in **opposite** directions. When demand is **inelastic**, they move in the **same** direction. At unit elasticity a small price change leaves revenue about unchanged. This is why a firm facing inelastic demand can raise revenue by raising price, and why a firm facing elastic demand gains revenue by cutting price.

### What makes demand more elastic?

- Many close **substitutes** (more elastic).
- A **luxury** rather than a necessity.
- A large share of the buyer's **budget**.
- A **longer time horizon**, since people adjust over time.
- A narrowly defined market ("one brand of cereal" is more elastic than "food").

### Other elasticities

**Income elasticity** is $\%\Delta Q / \%\Delta(\text{income})$. It is positive for normal goods (above 1 for luxuries) and negative for inferior goods. **Cross-price elasticity** is the percentage change in the quantity of good A divided by the percentage change in the price of good B: positive for substitutes, negative for complements. **Price elasticity of supply** is defined the same way as for demand, with quantity supplied in the numerator, and it is higher when producers can expand output easily, for example over a longer period. Elasticities matter in law and policy because who bears a tax, and how many trades a regulation prevents, depend on them (the next lesson uses this).

@example
title: Midpoint elasticity and revenue
problem: When the price of a good rises from \$10 to \$12, quantity demanded falls from 100 to 80. (a) Find the midpoint price elasticity of demand. (b) Linear demand is $Q = 120 - 2P$. Compute the point elasticity at $P = 20$ and say what happens to revenue when the price rises from 20 to 22.
step: Quantity change: $-20$ relative to the average $90$, so $\%\Delta Q = -20/90 = -2/9$. Price change: $+2$ relative to the average $11$, so $\%\Delta P = 2/11$.
step: $|E_d| = (2/9)/(2/11) = 11/9 \approx 1.22$, which is greater than 1, so demand is elastic over this range.
step: For $Q = 120 - 2P$ at $P = 20$: $Q = 80$ and $|E_d| = 2 \times 20/80 = 1/2$, so demand is inelastic there.
step: Revenue test: at $P = 20$, $TR = 20 \times 80 = 1600$; at $P = 22$, $Q = 76$ and $TR = 22 \times 76 = 1672$. Revenue rises with price, as the test predicts for inelastic demand.

@mistakes
- Equating elasticity with slope; a straight demand line has a different elasticity at every point.
- Computing percentage changes from the starting value in one direction and the ending value in the other, which gives two different answers (the midpoint method avoids this).
- Forgetting that revenue rises with price only when demand is inelastic.
- Reading a negative cross-price elasticity as "substitutes"; negative means complements.

prompt: The price of a good rises from \$5 to \$7 and quantity demanded falls from 90 to 70. Using the midpoint method, find the absolute value of the price elasticity of demand. Give an exact fraction.
answer: 3/4
check: (20 / 80) / (2 / 6)
hint: Average quantity is 80 and average price is 6.
hint: $\%\Delta Q = 20/80$ and $\%\Delta P = 2/6$ in absolute value.
solution: $(20/80)/(2/6) = (1/4)/(1/3) = 3/4$. Demand is inelastic over this range.

@practice num
prompt: When a household's income rises from \$40,000 to \$44,000, its quantity of restaurant meals rises from 50 to 56 per year. Using percentage changes measured from the starting values, what is the income elasticity of demand? Give an exact fraction.
answer: 6/5
check: ((56 - 50) / 50) / ((44000 - 40000) / 40000)
hint: Income rose by 10 percent.
hint: Meals rose by 6 out of 50, which is 12 percent.
solution: $12\% / 10\% = 1.2 = 6/5$. It exceeds 1, so restaurant meals are a luxury for this household.

@practice num

answer: 1/3
check: 5 * 10 / (200 - 5 * 10)

@practice num
prompt: Demand is $Q = 200 - 5P$, with price in dollars. By how many dollars does total revenue change when the price rises from \$10 to \$12?
answer: 180
check: 12 * (200 - 5*12) - 10 * (200 - 5*10)
unit: dollars
hint: Compute $P \times Q$ at each price.
hint: At \$10, revenue is 1500; at \$12, the quantity is 140.
solution: $TR(10) = 10 \times 150 = 1500$ and $TR(12) = 12 \times 140 = 1680$, a change of $+180$ dollars. At $P = 10$ demand is inelastic (elasticity 1/3), so revenue rises with price.

@quiz num

check: (20 / 40) / (10 / 25)
