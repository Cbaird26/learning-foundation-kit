@article
Competitive models assume that buyers and sellers know what they are trading. Often one side knows more. With **asymmetric information**, one party to a transaction has relevant knowledge that the other lacks: a used-car seller knows the car's history, an applicant for insurance knows more about her own health or driving than the insurer does. The problems fall into two families.

### Adverse selection

**Adverse selection** is a problem of **hidden characteristics before the contract**. The people most eager to accept an offer are disproportionately those for whom it is worst for the other side. The classic example is the **market for lemons**: buyers cannot tell good used cars from bad ones ("lemons"), so they will pay only the average value. At that price owners of good cars decline to sell, the average quality of cars on the market falls, buyers lower their offers again, and the market may shrink to the worst cars or disappear.

Insurance has the same structure. If an insurer charges a premium based on the average risk, low-risk customers find it a poor deal and leave, so the insurer's pool becomes riskier, the premium must rise, and more low-risk customers leave: an **adverse-selection spiral** or "death spiral".

### Moral hazard

**Moral hazard** is a problem of **hidden action after the contract**. Once insured, a person has less incentive to take care, because someone else bears part of the loss: a homeowner may skip fire precautions, a patient may use more care than needed. Insurers and the law respond by sharing the risk with the insured: **deductibles** (the insured pays the first part of any loss), **coinsurance**, coverage caps, monitoring, and exclusions for intentional acts.

### Insurance basics

Insurance pools many people's risks. The **actuarially fair premium** equals the expected payout:

$$\text{fair premium} = \text{probability of loss} \times \text{amount paid on a loss.}$$

Risk-averse people value insurance even above its expected cost, because a certain small payment is preferable to a small chance of a large loss; this is why insurance can make both sides better off. Real premiums add a loading for costs and profit. With a deductible $d$ and loss $L$, the insurer pays $L - d$ on a loss, so the fair premium is $p(L - d)$.

### Responses to information problems

- **Signaling:** the informed party takes a costly action that distinguishes it: a long warranty, a credential, a brand reputation, a money-back guarantee.
- **Screening:** the uninformed party offers a menu that sorts types: an insurer offers a low-premium, high-deductible plan alongside a high-premium, low-deductible plan, and low-risk customers choose the former.
- **Disclosure and legal rules:** fraud and misrepresentation law penalizes lies; the implied warranty of merchantability (UCC § 2-314) assures that goods sold by a merchant are fit for ordinary purposes; state "lemon laws" give remedies for defective new cars; disclosure statutes require sellers to reveal known defects; insurance applications require accurate answers, and material misrepresentations can allow the insurer to rescind. Mandatory insurance and group pooling reduce selection. Whether the best remedy is disclosure, warranties or regulation depends on the costs of each and the market in question.

@example
title: A death spiral and a market for lemons
problem: (a) An insurer covers a \$10,000 loss. High-risk people have a 20 percent chance of loss and low-risk people 5 percent; the population is half and half. Low-risk people will buy only if the premium is at most \$700. What happens if the insurer charges the pool-average fair premium? (b) Half of used cars are good (worth \$10,000 to a buyer, \$8,000 to the seller) and half are lemons (worth \$4,000 to a buyer, \$3,000 to the seller). Buyers cannot tell them apart. What happens?
step: Fair premiums by type: high risk $0.20 \times 10{,}000 = 2000$, low risk $0.05 \times 10{,}000 = 500$. The pool average is $0.5(2000) + 0.5(500) = 1250$.
step: At 1250, low-risk people (willing to pay at most 700) drop out. The insurer is left with high-risk customers whose expected cost is 2000, so it must raise the premium to 2000 to break even. The market has unraveled to the high-risk group only.
step: For cars, buyers' expected value of a random car is $0.5(10{,}000) + 0.5(4{,}000) = 7000$, so that is the most they will pay.
step: Good-car owners value their cars at 8000 > 7000, so they will not sell. Only lemons are offered, and rational buyers, knowing this, will pay at most 4000. A mutually beneficial trade (a good car is worth more to the buyer than to the seller) fails to happen.

@mistakes
- Using "adverse selection" and "moral hazard" interchangeably; adverse selection is about who enters the contract, moral hazard about behavior after it.
- Calculating a fair premium from the loss alone and forgetting to multiply by the probability.
- Forgetting that a deductible reduces the insurer's expected payout.
- Assuming that asymmetric information always destroys a market; signaling, screening, warranties and regulation can restore trade.

@practice num
prompt: An insurer cannot distinguish its customers. Forty percent are low risk with a 4 percent chance of a \$10,000 loss, and sixty percent are high risk with a 14 percent chance of the same loss. What is the fair premium for the whole pool, in dollars?
answer: 1000
check: (0.4 * 0.04 + 0.6 * 0.14) * 10000
unit: dollars
hint: Find the average probability of loss, weighting by group size.
hint: $0.4(0.04) + 0.6(0.14) = 0.10$.
solution: Average probability $= 0.016 + 0.084 = 0.10$, so the fair pool premium is $0.10 \times 10{,}000 = 1000$ dollars.

prompt: Which of the following are signaling or screening devices that help overcome an information problem?
choice*: A seller offering a long warranty on a used car
choice*: An insurer offering a low-premium high-deductible plan alongside a high-premium low-deductible plan
choice*: A job applicant earning a professional credential
choice: An insurer charging every applicant the pool average premium without asking any questions
hint: Signals are costly actions by the informed party; screens are menus offered by the uninformed party.
hint: A single price for everyone sorts no one.
solution: A warranty is a credible signal of quality (it costs more for sellers of bad cars), a menu of plans lets customers reveal their risk through their choice, and a credential signals ability. A single pooled premium does neither, and it invites adverse selection.

@quiz mc
prompt: A health plan charges everyone the same premium based on the average cost of its members. Healthy people find it expensive and many leave. What is the likely consequence if the plan sticks to the same pricing rule?
choice: The premium falls, because fewer members file claims.
choice: Premiums stay the same, because average costs do not change.
choice*: The remaining members are sicker on average, so the break-even premium rises, which can lead more healthy people to leave.
choice: Moral hazard disappears, because only people who need care remain.
hint: Who leaves, and what happens to the average cost of those who stay?
hint: Follow the premium through two or three rounds.
solution: When healthy people leave, the average cost of the remaining pool rises, forcing a higher premium and encouraging further exits. That feedback loop is an adverse-selection spiral.
