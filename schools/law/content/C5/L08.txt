@article
An **oligopoly** is a market with a few large sellers. Each firm's best choice depends on what the others do, so the tools of perfect competition and monopoly are not enough. **Game theory** studies this kind of strategic interdependence.

### Games and equilibrium

A **game** has players, strategies and payoffs. A **dominant strategy** is best for a player whatever the others do. A **Nash equilibrium** is a set of strategies, one per player, in which each player's strategy is a best response to the others', so no one gains by changing alone. A game may have no dominant strategies, one Nash equilibrium, or several.

### The prisoner's dilemma

In a **prisoner's dilemma** each player has a dominant strategy, and when both use it they end up worse off than if both had cooperated. Two rival firms each choose a high or low price. Cheating on a high-price understanding is tempting: if the rival keeps the high price, undercutting wins market share, and if the rival cuts price, cutting too protects you. Low price is dominant for each, yet both are better off at (High, High). The tension between individual and joint interest is why cartels tend to collapse.

### Cournot duopoly

In **Cournot** competition, firms choose quantities simultaneously. With inverse demand $P = a - Q$, $Q = q_1 + q_2$ and constant marginal cost $c$, firm 1's profit is $q_1(a - q_1 - q_2 - c)$. Maximizing gives its **best-response (reaction) function**

$$q_1 = \frac{a - c - q_2}{2},$$

and symmetrically for firm 2. At the Nash equilibrium the two reaction functions hold together; with identical firms $q_1 = q_2 = q$ and

$$q = \frac{a - c}{3}, \qquad Q = \frac{2(a - c)}{3}.$$

For comparison, a monopolist produces $(a - c)/2$ and perfect competition produces $a - c$. The duopoly outcome lies between them: price is above marginal cost but below the monopoly price. As the number of Cournot firms rises, the outcome approaches the competitive one.

### Repetition and collusion

If the game is played repeatedly with no known end, firms can sustain cooperation by threatening punishment. In a **grim-trigger** strategy a firm cooperates until the rival cheats and then plays the Nash (punishment) strategy forever. With per-period payoffs $C$ for cooperating, $D$ for cheating against a cooperator and $N$ for mutual punishment, and discount factor $\delta$ (the weight on a payoff one period later), cooperation lasts if

$$\frac{C}{1 - \delta} \ge D + \frac{\delta N}{1 - \delta} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \delta \ge \frac{D - C}{D - N}.$$

Patient firms (high $\delta$), few firms and transparent prices make collusion easier to sustain.

### The legal side

Section 1 of the Sherman Act prohibits contracts, combinations and conspiracies in restraint of trade. Agreements among competitors to fix prices or restrict output are treated as per se unlawful, meaning no showing of harm is needed. Economics explains why the problem arises: each cartel member profits from cheating on the agreement, and so the law targets the agreement itself. Merely recognizing that rivals will react to one another (**conscious parallelism**) is not itself an agreement, which is why courts require something more than parallel prices to find a conspiracy. This is a general description; the rule has details that law courses cover.

@example
title: Cournot competition versus collusion
problem: Two firms face $P = 100 - Q$ with $Q = q_1 + q_2$ and constant marginal cost of \$10. (a) Find the Cournot equilibrium quantities, price and profits. (b) Find the monopoly (cartel) output and total profit. (c) Show that cheating on an equal split of the cartel output is profitable.
step: Reaction function: $q_1 = (90 - q_2)/2$. By symmetry $q = 90/3 = 30$ for each firm, so $Q = 60$ and $P = 100 - 60 = 40$. Each firm's profit is $(40 - 10) \times 30 = 900$, total 1800.
step: A monopolist sets $MR = 100 - 2Q = 10$, so $Q = 45$, $P = 55$ and profit $= (55 - 10) \times 45 = 2025$. Splitting evenly gives each firm 22.5 units and 1012.5, more than the Cournot 900.
step: Suppose rival produces its quota of 22.5. Firm 1's best response is $(90 - 22.5)/2 = 33.75$. Then $Q = 56.25$, $P = 43.75$, and firm 1's profit is $(43.75 - 10) \times 33.75 = 1139.06$, above 1012.5.
step: So each firm gains by exceeding its quota, and when both do the outcome slides toward the Cournot 900 each. The same structure as a prisoner's dilemma.

@mistakes
- Treating a Nash equilibrium as the outcome that is best for the players; in a prisoner's dilemma it is not.
- Checking only one player's incentive to deviate; every player's strategy must be a best response.
- Using monopoly output as the Cournot quantity, or forgetting to add both firms' quantities when finding price.
- Assuming that parallel pricing alone proves an unlawful agreement.

prompt: Two firms play a repeated price game with a grim-trigger strategy. Each period, cooperating pays 10, cheating on a cooperator pays 14, and mutual punishment pays 2. What is the lowest discount factor $\delta$ at which cooperation can be sustained? Give an exact fraction.
answer: 1/3
check: (14 - 10) / (14 - 2)
hint: Cooperating forever is worth $10/(1 - \delta)$; cheating is worth $14 + 2\delta/(1 - \delta)$.
hint: Use $\delta \ge (D - C)/(D - N)$.
solution: $10 \ge 14(1 - \delta) + 2\delta = 14 - 12\delta$, so $\delta \ge 4/12 = 1/3$.

@quiz num
prompt: In a game each player chooses L or R. Payoffs are identical for both players: both choose L, 2 each; both choose R, 1 each; if they choose differently, 0 each. How many pure-strategy Nash equilibria does the game have?
answer: 2
check: (lambda d: sum(1 for r in 'LR' for c in 'LR' if d[(r, c)] >= max(d[(x, c)] for x in 'LR') and d[(r, c)] >= max(d[(r, y)] for y in 'LR')))({('L', 'L'): 2, ('R', 'R'): 1, ('L', 'R'): 0, ('R', 'L'): 0})
hint: Check each of the four outcomes: can either player gain by switching alone?
hint: Mismatched outcomes give 0 to both, and either could switch to match and gain.
solution: (L, L) and (R, R) are equilibria: switching alone to a mismatch lowers one's payoff to 0. Both mismatched outcomes fail, since a player gains by switching to match. So there are 2 equilibria, and the game is a coordination game.

choice: Because the output restriction lowers each firm's revenue.
choice*: Because each firm earns more by exceeding its quota if the other keeps to its quota, so cheating is a dominant strategy.
