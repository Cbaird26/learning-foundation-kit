@article
A **monopoly** is a single seller of a product with no close substitutes, protected by **barriers to entry**: control of a key resource, legal protection (patents, licenses), economies of scale that make a **natural monopoly**, or conduct that deters rivals. Unlike a price taker, a monopolist faces the **entire market demand curve**: to sell more it must lower its price.

### Marginal revenue

Because selling an extra unit means cutting the price on all units sold, a monopolist's marginal revenue is below its price. For linear inverse demand $P = a - bQ$,

$$TR = aQ - bQ^2, \qquad MR = a - 2bQ.$$

The $MR$ curve starts at the same intercept as demand but falls **twice as steeply**.

### Profit maximization

The monopolist, like every firm, expands output until $MR = MC$. It then reads the **price from the demand curve**, not from the $MR$ curve:

1. Find $Q_m$ where $MR = MC$.
2. Set $P_m = a - bQ_m$.
3. Profit is $(P_m - ATC) \times Q_m$.

The monopoly price exceeds marginal cost: $P_m > MC$. The competitive benchmark sets $P = MC$ and produces more.

### Deadweight loss

Relative to the competitive outcome the monopolist produces less ($Q_m < Q_c$) and charges more. The units between $Q_m$ and $Q_c$ are worth more to buyers than they cost to make, but are not produced. With constant marginal cost the deadweight loss is a triangle:

$$DWL = \tfrac{1}{2}(P_m - MC)(Q_c - Q_m).$$

Monopoly is therefore inefficient because of reduced output (a transfer from consumers to the monopolist is not itself a loss). Reasons the law restricts it include this efficiency loss and concerns about market power. Section 2 of the Sherman Act prohibits monopolization, which courts have understood to be the acquisition or maintenance of monopoly power through exclusionary conduct, as opposed to growth through a superior product, business skill or historical accident. Merely having a monopoly is not unlawful. For a natural monopoly, where one firm is the cheapest way to serve the market, governments often use price regulation instead, though setting price at marginal cost may leave the firm with a loss, which regulators must solve some other way.

### Price discrimination

**Price discrimination** means charging different customers different prices for the same good where the differences are not explained by cost. It requires:

- **Market power** (the seller faces a downward-sloping demand curve);
- the ability to **separate** customers by willingness to pay (age, location, time of purchase, coupon use); and
- **limits on resale** (otherwise low-price buyers resell to high-price buyers).

In **third-degree** discrimination the seller splits customers into groups and sets $MR = MC$ separately in each, charging a higher price to the group with **less elastic** demand. In **perfect (first-degree)** discrimination the seller charges each customer exactly their willingness to pay: output rises to the competitive level, there is **no deadweight loss**, and the monopolist captures all the surplus. Price discrimination can raise or lower total welfare in general, but it often expands output to buyers who would be priced out under a single price. In U.S. law, the Robinson-Patman Act reaches certain price discrimination in the sale of commodities where it may harm competition; discrimination in ordinary consumer pricing is generally lawful unless another statute (such as a civil rights law) prohibits it.

@example
title: Monopoly pricing and the value of discrimination
problem: Demand is $P = 100 - 2Q$ (price in dollars) and marginal cost is a constant \$20, with no fixed cost. (a) Find the monopoly output, price and profit. (b) Find the competitive output and the deadweight loss of monopoly. (c) Find the profit if the monopolist could perfectly price-discriminate.
step: $MR = 100 - 4Q$. Set $MR = MC$: $100 - 4Q = 20$, so $Q_m = 20$. The price comes from demand: $P_m = 100 - 40 = 60$. Profit is $(60 - 20) \times 20 = 800$.
step: The competitive output sets $P = MC$: $100 - 2Q = 20$, so $Q_c = 40$.
step: $DWL = \tfrac{1}{2}(60 - 20)(40 - 20) = \tfrac{1}{2}(40)(20) = 400$.
step: With perfect discrimination the seller produces 40 units and each unit sells at its buyer's maximum price. Profit is the triangle between demand and marginal cost: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 40 \times (100 - 20) = 1600$, twice the uniform-price profit and equal to the entire total surplus, so there is no deadweight loss.

@mistakes
- Reading the monopoly price off the marginal revenue curve rather than the demand curve.
- Setting $P = MC$ for a monopolist; that is the competitive rule.
- Calling the monopolist's higher price a deadweight loss; the loss comes from the output that is not produced.
- Overlooking resale: without limits on arbitrage, price discrimination collapses.

answer: 450
check: 0.5 * ((70 - root(lambda q: (70 - 2*q) - 10, 0, 100)) - 10) * (root(lambda q: (70 - q) - 10, 0, 100) - root(lambda q: (70 - 2*q) - 10, 0, 100))
unit: dollars
hint: Find both the monopoly output ($MR = MC$) and the competitive output ($P = MC$).

solution: $Q_m = 30$, $P_m = 40$, $Q_c = 60$. $DWL = \tfrac{1}{2}(40 - 10)(60 - 30) = 450$ dollars.

@practice ms
prompt: Which of the following conditions are needed for a firm to practice price discrimination profitably?
choice*: The firm has some market power (it faces a downward-sloping demand curve).
choice*: The firm can identify or separate customers with different willingness to pay.
choice*: Customers who buy at a low price cannot easily resell to those charged more.
choice: All customers have perfectly inelastic demand.
hint: Without any one of these, the seller is back to a single price.
hint: A price taker cannot choose its price at all.
solution: Discrimination needs market power, a way to separate groups, and limits on resale. Perfectly inelastic demand for everyone is not needed (and would leave nothing to discriminate on).

@quiz mc
prompt: A seller splits customers into two groups and sets $MR = MC$ separately in each. Which group is charged the higher price, and why?
choice: The group with more elastic demand, because it is more sensitive to price.
choice*: The group with less elastic demand, because its quantity falls less when the price rises.
choice: The group that buys more, because of volume discounts.
choice: Both pay the same price, because marginal cost is the same.
hint: Recall the markup rule: the markup over marginal cost is larger when demand is less elastic.
hint: Which customers respond least to a price increase?
solution: With the same marginal cost, setting $MR = MC$ gives a higher price where demand is less elastic. That group is the less price-sensitive one, so the seller can charge it more.
