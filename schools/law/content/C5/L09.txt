@article
Markets are efficient when buyers and sellers bear all the costs and receive all the benefits of their transactions. An **externality** is a cost or benefit that falls on someone who is not a party to the transaction. A **negative externality** (pollution, noise) imposes a cost on others; a **positive externality** (vaccination, basic research) confers a benefit on others.

### Negative externalities

With a negative externality the firm's private marginal cost understates the true cost to society:

$$MSC = MPC + MEC,$$

where $MEC$ is the marginal external cost. The market produces where demand (marginal private benefit) equals $MPC$, but the efficient quantity is where demand equals $MSC$. The market quantity is **too high**. Between the efficient and market quantities each unit costs society more than it is worth to buyers, and the area between $MSC$ and demand over those units is the **deadweight loss**.

### Fixing externalities

- **Pigouvian tax:** a per-unit tax equal to the marginal external cost at the efficient quantity. It makes the producer **internalize** the externality, shifting $MPC$ up to $MSC$.
- **Quantity regulation:** standards or output limits ("command and control"). They can achieve a target but do not necessarily allocate the cutbacks to the lowest-cost reducers.
- **Tradable permits (cap-and-trade):** the government caps total emissions and issues tradable permits. Firms with low cutback costs sell permits to firms with high cutback costs, so the cap is met at the lowest total cost. The sulfur dioxide trading program created under the 1990 amendments to the Clean Air Act is the standard example.
- **Private law and bargaining:** nuisance and tort liability, covered with the Coase theorem in the next lesson.

A policy debate runs alongside: supporters of taxes and permits point to cost-effectiveness, while others prefer direct regulation for certainty of outcomes or worry that prices are hard to set correctly when the marginal external cost is uncertain. The economics identifies the efficiency trade-offs; how to weigh them is a policy judgment.

### Positive externalities

If a good creates external benefit, $MSB = MPB + MEB$, the market quantity is **too low**, and a **subsidy** equal to the marginal external benefit moves output to the efficient level.

### Public goods

Goods are classified by two features:

| | **Excludable** | **Not excludable** |
|---|---|---|
| **Rival** | Private goods (pizza) | Common resources (open fishery) |
| **Nonrival** | Club goods (a streaming service) | Public goods (national defense) |

A **public good** is nonrival (one person's use does not reduce another's) and nonexcludable (nonpayers cannot be kept from using it). Because people can benefit without paying, each has an incentive to be a **free rider**, and the market undersupplies the good. Common resources suffer a related problem, the **tragedy of the commons**: because each user ignores the cost imposed on others, the resource is overused.

The efficient quantity of a public good sets marginal social cost equal to the **vertical sum** of individual marginal benefit curves (everyone consumes the same unit, so their values add), unlike a private good, where we add quantities horizontally. Governments usually provide public goods and pay for them through taxes, because voluntary contributions fall short.

@example
title: Externality and public good
problem: (a) Demand is $P = 60 - Q$ and private marginal cost is $MPC = 10 + Q$. Production creates an external cost of \$10 per unit. Find the market quantity, the efficient quantity, the corrective tax and the deadweight loss. (b) Two residents value a public good with marginal benefits $12 - Q$ and $8 - Q$, and it costs \$10 per unit to provide. Find the efficient quantity, and the quantity one resident would provide alone.
step: Market: $60 - Q = 10 + Q$ gives $Q = 25$ and $P = 35$.
step: Efficient: $MSC = 20 + Q$, so $60 - Q = 20 + Q$ gives $Q = 20$. The Pigouvian tax equals the external cost of \$10 per unit: with the tax the sellers' cost shifts to $20 + Q$ and the market quantity falls to 20.
step: Deadweight loss: at $Q = 25$, $MSC = 45$ and demand is 35, a gap of 10, so $DWL = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 10 \times (25 - 20) = 25$.
step: Public good: the vertical sum of marginal benefits is $(12 - Q) + (8 - Q) = 20 - 2Q$. Setting it equal to the cost of 10 gives $Q = 5$. Check: marginal benefits at $Q = 5$ are 7 and 3, which sum to 10.
step: The first resident alone would stop where $12 - Q = 10$, so $Q = 2$, less than the efficient 5. The second resident would provide nothing, since $8 < 10$ even at zero. This is the free-rider problem.

@mistakes
- Adding public goods' individual benefit curves horizontally (as for a private good) instead of vertically.
- Taxing at the market price or the market quantity rather than at the marginal external cost at the efficient quantity.
- Believing the efficient level of pollution is zero; the efficient level balances marginal benefits against marginal social cost.
- Confusing "public good" (nonrival and nonexcludable) with "good provided by the government" (a public school is publicly provided but rival).

@practice num
prompt: Demand is $P = 80 - Q$, private marginal cost is $MPC = 20 + Q$, and each unit imposes an external cost of \$12 on third parties. Find the deadweight loss, in dollars, of leaving the externality uncorrected.
answer: 36
check: 0.5 * 12 * (root(lambda q: (80 - q) - (20 + q), 0, 100) - root(lambda q: (80 - q) - (32 + q), 0, 100))
unit: dollars
hint: Find the market quantity (demand equals $MPC$) and the efficient quantity (demand equals $MSC$).
hint: The market quantity is 30 and the efficient quantity is 24.
solution: Market: $Q = 30$. Efficient: $Q = 24$. The gap between $MSC$ and demand is 12 dollars at $Q = 30$ and zero at $Q = 24$, so $DWL = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 12 \times 6 = 36$ dollars.

prompt: Two residents have marginal benefits of $20 - 2Q$ and $10 - Q$ from a public good (price in dollars). Each unit costs \$9 to provide. What is the efficient quantity?
answer: 7
check: root(lambda q: (20 - 2*q) + (10 - q) - 9, 0, 100)
hint: Add the marginal benefits vertically.
hint: $30 - 3Q = 9$.
solution: The sum of marginal benefits is $30 - 3Q$. Setting it equal to 9 gives $Q = 7$. Check: marginal benefits at $Q = 7$ are 6 and 3, and $6 + 3 = 9$.

@quiz num
prompt: Demand is $P = 90 - Q$, private marginal cost is $MPC = 30 + Q$, and each unit imposes an external cost of \$20. Find the deadweight loss, in dollars, of the unregulated market.
answer: 100
check: 0.5 * 20 * (root(lambda q: (90 - q) - (30 + q), 0, 100) - root(lambda q: (90 - q) - (50 + q), 0, 100))
unit: dollars
hint: Compare the quantity where demand equals $MPC$ with the quantity where demand equals $MSC = 50 + Q$.
hint: The market quantity is 30; the efficient quantity is 20.
solution: Market: $90 - Q = 30 + Q$, so $Q = 30$. Efficient: $90 - Q = 50 + Q$, so $Q = 20$. $DWL = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 20 \times (30 - 20) = 100$ dollars.
