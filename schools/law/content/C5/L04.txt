@article
Equilibrium tells you the price and quantity. **Surplus** tells you who gains and how much. It is the main tool economists use to judge whether a market outcome is efficient, and it is the quantitative core of the law-and-economics analysis of taxes, regulations and legal rules.

### Consumer and producer surplus

The demand curve shows each buyer's **willingness to pay**. **Consumer surplus** is the difference between what buyers are willing to pay and what they actually pay, summed over all units bought. On a linear demand curve it is the triangle under demand and above the price:

$$CS = \tfrac{1}{2} \times Q \times (P_{\max} - P),$$

where $P_{\max}$ is the demand intercept on the price axis.

The supply curve shows each seller's **marginal cost** (the lowest price at which that unit would be sold). **Producer surplus** is the difference between the price received and that cost, summed over units sold: the triangle above supply and below the price, $PS = \tfrac{1}{2} \times Q \times (P - P_{\min})$, where $P_{\min}$ is the supply intercept on the price axis (zero if supply passes through the origin).

**Total surplus** is $CS + PS$. A competitive market without external effects maximizes total surplus: every unit is traded for which a buyer's value exceeds a seller's cost, and no other unit is.

### Taxes and deadweight loss

A per-unit tax $t$ drives a wedge between the price buyers pay $P_b$ and the price sellers keep $P_s$: $P_b - P_s = t$. It does not matter, for the economics, whether the law collects the tax from buyers or sellers; the **incidence** (who bears the burden) is determined by relative elasticities. The side with the less elastic response bears more of the burden, since it has fewer alternatives.

To find the new equilibrium with a tax on sellers, set $Q_d(P_b) = Q_s(P_b - t)$. The tax reduces quantity from $Q_0$ to $Q_1$, and the government collects revenue $t \times Q_1$. The units between $Q_1$ and $Q_0$ are no longer traded even though buyers valued them more than they cost to make. The lost surplus on those units is the **deadweight loss** (DWL), the triangle with base $t$ and height $Q_0 - Q_1$:

$$DWL = \tfrac{1}{2} \times t \times (Q_0 - Q_1).$$

Tax revenue is a transfer to the government, not a loss, if it is spent on something valued. The DWL is the part of the lost surplus that nobody gets. It grows with the **square** of the tax rate for linear curves, and it is larger when demand and supply are more elastic, because quantity then responds more to the wedge.

A tariff on imports is a tax on a traded good, and the same analysis applies. Whether the executive has statutory authority to impose a given tariff is a separate legal question: in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* (2026) the Supreme Court held, 6 to 3, that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorize tariffs. Economics answers "who bears it and what is lost"; the courts decide who may impose it.

@example
title: Surplus and a per-unit tax
problem: Demand is $Q_d = 100 - 2P$ and supply is $Q_s = 3P$ (price in dollars). (a) Find equilibrium, consumer surplus and producer surplus. (b) A tax of \$5 per unit is imposed on sellers. Find the quantity traded, the price buyers pay, the price sellers keep, tax revenue and the deadweight loss.
step: Setting $100 - 2P = 3P$ gives $P = 20$ and $Q_0 = 60$. Demand's price intercept is $P_{\max} = 50$ (since $Q = 0$ at $P = 50$), and supply passes through the origin.
step: $CS = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 60 \times (50 - 20) = 900$ and $PS = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 60 \times 20 = 600$, so total surplus is 1500.
step: With the tax on sellers, supply as a function of the buyer's price is $Q_s = 3(P_b - 5)$. Set $100 - 2P_b = 3P_b - 15$, so $115 = 5P_b$ and $P_b = 23$. The quantity is $Q_1 = 100 - 46 = 54$, and sellers keep $23 - 5 = 18$. Check with supply: $3 \times 18 = 54$.
step: Buyers pay 3 more than before and sellers keep 2 less, which sums to the tax of 5. Revenue is $5 \times 54 = 270$. The deadweight loss is $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 5 \times (60 - 54) = 15$.

@mistakes
- Using the full quantity $Q_0$ rather than the lost quantity $Q_0 - Q_1$ as the base of the deadweight-loss triangle.
- Counting tax revenue as a deadweight loss; revenue is a transfer, and the loss is the triangle.
- Assuming the party who legally pays the tax bears its burden; incidence depends on elasticities.
- Subtracting the tax from the wrong price, so that the buyer's price and seller's price differ by something other than $t$.

prompt: Demand is $Q_d = 90 - 3P$ and supply is $Q_s = 2P$ (price in dollars). A tax of \$10 per unit is imposed on sellers. Find the deadweight loss in dollars.
answer: 60
check: 0.5 * 10 * (2 * root(lambda p: (90 - 3*p) - 2*p, 0, 100) - (90 - 3 * root(lambda p: (90 - 3*p) - 2*(p - 10), 0, 100)))
unit: dollars
hint: Solve $90 - 3P_b = 2(P_b - 10)$ for the buyer's price.
hint: $P_b = 22$, so the new quantity is $90 - 66 = 24$; the old quantity was 36.
solution: Before the tax, $Q_0 = 36$. After it, $110 = 5P_b$, so $P_b = 22$ and $Q_1 = 24$. $DWL = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 10 \times (36 - 24) = 60$ dollars.

@quiz num
prompt: Demand is $Q_d = 120 - 2P$ and supply is $Q_s = 3P - 30$, with price in dollars. A tax of \$15 per unit is imposed on sellers. Find the deadweight loss in dollars.
answer: 135
check: 0.5 * 15 * (120 - 2 * root(lambda p: (120 - 2*p) - (3*p - 30), 0, 200) - (120 - 2 * root(lambda p: (120 - 2*p) - (3*(p - 15) - 30), 0, 200)))
unit: dollars
hint: Find the quantity with no tax, then with the tax by setting $Q_d(P_b) = Q_s(P_b - 15)$.
hint: Without the tax $P = 30$ and $Q_0 = 60$; with it $195 = 5P_b$.
solution: Before: $150 = 5P$, so $P = 30$, $Q_0 = 60$. After: $120 - 2P_b = 3(P_b - 15) - 30$ gives $P_b = 39$ and $Q_1 = 42$. $DWL = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 15 \times (60 - 42) = 135$ dollars.

@quiz mc
prompt: Why does a per-unit tax on a competitively supplied good create a deadweight loss?
choice: Because the government keeps the revenue and does not spend it.
choice*: Because it stops some trades in which a buyer's value exceeds the seller's cost, and the surplus on those trades is lost to everyone.
choice: Because it raises the price of the good for buyers.
choice: Because it transfers surplus from sellers to buyers.
hint: Deadweight loss is surplus that nobody receives.
hint: Look at the units between the after-tax and before-tax quantities.
solution: Between the new and old quantities, each unit is worth more to a buyer than it costs a seller to make, but the wedge makes the trade unprofitable, so it does not happen. That unrealized surplus is the deadweight loss. A higher price and a transfer to the government are not themselves losses.
