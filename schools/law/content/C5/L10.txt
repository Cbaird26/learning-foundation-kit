@article
The previous lesson fixed externalities with taxes and regulation. The **Coase theorem** shows that sometimes private parties can fix them themselves. It is also the most useful bridge from economics to private law, because the legal system's job is largely to define **entitlements** (who has the right to do or to prevent something) and to enforce them.

### The theorem

**If property rights are clearly defined and enforced, and transaction costs are low enough, the parties will bargain to the efficient outcome whoever initially holds the right.**

"Efficient" here means the action that maximizes the combined value of the parties. The initial assignment of the right affects **who pays whom** (distribution and wealth), but not whether the efficient action is taken.

Consider a factory whose smoke harms a neighboring laundry. Suppose the factory gains \$120 per day from operating and the smoke costs the laundry \$80 per day. Operating is efficient because $120 > 80$.

- **Laundry holds the right to clean air.** The factory needs permission. It will pay up to \$120 and the laundry will accept anything above \$80, so a deal between 80 and 120 is mutually beneficial. The factory operates and compensates the laundry.
- **Factory holds the right to emit.** The laundry would have to pay the factory to stop. It will pay at most \$80, but the factory needs at least \$120, so no deal occurs. The factory operates.

The same action occurs in both cases; the difference is that in the first the factory pays, and in the second the laundry bears the harm.

Now suppose the laundry's harm is \$150 and the factory's gain is \$120. Stopping is efficient. If the laundry has the right, the factory cannot afford to buy it (it would pay at most 120, but 150 is needed), so the factory stops. If the factory has the right, the laundry will pay between \$120 and \$150 to make it stop. Again the efficient action is taken.

### When bargaining fails

The theorem's conditions are strong. Bargaining fails or is inefficient when:

- **Transaction costs are high:** many affected parties (an entire city exposed to smog) make organizing, negotiating and enforcing a deal costly, and **free riding** discourages contributions.
- **Holdouts and strategic behavior:** with a range of acceptable prices, each side may bargain hard for a bigger share, and the deal may collapse. A single holdout can block an assembly of many parcels.
- **Asymmetric information:** each party may not know the other's true gain or harm.
- **Rights are unclear** or hard to enforce, or the parties are not in a continuing relationship.

When the net gain from a deal is smaller than the cost of making it, the deal does not happen. For example, with a gain from trade of \$60 and bargaining costs of \$80, the externality continues, even though a lower-cost method would have fixed it.

### Property rules and liability rules

Law protects entitlements in different ways. A **property rule** protects an entitlement by injunction: the holder can stop the other party and can be bought out only at a price the holder agrees to. A **liability rule** lets the other party take the entitlement, or cause the harm, so long as it pays damages set by a court. In *Boomer v. Atlantic Cement Co.* (1970), the New York Court of Appeals held that a cement plant causing nuisance harm to neighbors would be enjoined unless it paid permanent damages, so that the injunction was to be vacated on payment; in effect the court awarded damages instead of an injunction. Liability rules make sense when transaction costs prevent a market deal, such as when many neighbors are affected, because the court sets the price that bargaining could not. Courts and scholars disagree about when each rule is better, since judges may measure damages imperfectly, and a property rule gives holders the power to extract high payments. Choosing the rule is a matter of weighing transaction costs against information costs.

@example
title: Is a deal feasible?
problem: A bar's live music gives its owner a benefit of \$300 per month. It causes a neighbor harm worth \$220 per month. (a) Which outcome is efficient? (b) If the neighbor holds the right to quiet, what is the range of payments that benefit both, and what is the gain from bargaining? (c) Suppose negotiating costs \$100 in total. Does the deal occur?
step: The bar's gain (300) exceeds the neighbor's harm (220), so allowing the music is efficient, with a net gain of $300 - 220 = 80$ per month.
step: If the neighbor holds the right, the bar must pay to play. The bar will pay up to 300; the neighbor will accept anything above 220. Any payment between 220 and 300 helps both, and the gain from bargaining is $300 - 220 = 80$.
step: If the bar holds the right to play, the neighbor would have to pay it to stop, but the neighbor would pay at most 220, below the bar's 300. No deal, and the music continues. The efficient outcome again, with a different distribution.
step: If bargaining costs 100, the net gain is $80 - 100 = -20 < 0$, so the parties will not bother, and the neighbor holds the right, the music is silenced even though allowing it is efficient. This is an example of transaction costs causing a Coasean failure.

@mistakes
- Reading the theorem as saying the initial assignment of rights never matters; it matters for distribution and for who has to bargain.
- Forgetting the theorem's conditions (low transaction costs and clear, enforced rights) and applying it to cases with many parties.
- Mixing up property rules (injunction, holder's consent) with liability rules (damages set by a court).
- Counting only the gain from the deal and ignoring the cost of making it.
