@article
**Scarcity** means that human wants exceed the resources available to satisfy them. Time, money, land, labor and attention are all limited, so every choice gives something up. Economics is the study of how people, firms and governments choose under scarcity, and of what happens when many such choices interact in markets. For a law student the payoff is practical: much of modern private law, antitrust and regulation is argued in the language of incentives, costs and trade-offs.

### Opportunity cost

The **opportunity cost** of a choice is the value of the best alternative you give up. It is not the sum of all alternatives, only the best one. Opportunity cost includes **explicit costs** (money you actually pay) and **implicit costs** (the value of resources you already own, such as your time). If you spend three hours at a review course and could have earned \$20 an hour, the three hours cost you \$60 of forgone wages in addition to any tuition.

Two rules keep you honest:

- **Sunk costs are irrelevant.** A cost already paid and not recoverable should not affect a forward-looking decision. Only costs and benefits that differ across the remaining options matter.
- **Think at the margin.** Rational choices compare the extra benefit of one more unit with the extra cost of one more unit. You study another hour if the additional benefit exceeds what the hour would otherwise be worth.

### The production possibilities frontier

A **production possibilities frontier** (PPF) shows the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce with given resources and technology. Points on the frontier are efficient, points inside are attainable but waste resources, and points outside are unattainable for now. If the frontier is a straight line, the opportunity cost of one good in terms of the other is constant and equals the ratio of the intercepts. A frontier that bows outward reflects increasing opportunity cost, because resources are not equally suited to both uses.

For a straight-line frontier with intercepts $A$ units of good X (all resources on X) and $B$ units of good Y:

$$\text{opportunity cost of one unit of X} = \frac{B}{A} \text{ units of Y.}$$

### Absolute and comparative advantage

A producer has an **absolute advantage** if it can make more of a good with the same resources. A producer has a **comparative advantage** if its opportunity cost of that good is lower. Absolute advantage compares output; comparative advantage compares opportunity costs, and only the latter determines who should specialize. Even someone better at everything gains by trading, because they cannot be the low-cost producer of everything.

If one producer's opportunity cost of a good is 2 units of the other good and the other producer's is 3, any price between 2 and 3 units makes both better off: the buyer pays less than its own cost of making the good, and the seller receives more than its own cost.

@example
title: Who should specialize in what?
problem: In one day Ana can draft 8 contracts or review 16 files; Ben can draft 6 contracts or review 18 files. (Each person's frontier is a straight line.) Find each person's opportunity cost of a contract, say who has the comparative advantage in contracts, and give the range of trade prices that benefit both.
step: Ana's opportunity cost of one contract is $16/8 = 2$ files. Ben's is $18/6 = 3$ files.
step: Ana's opportunity cost of a contract is lower, so Ana has the comparative advantage in drafting contracts. Equivalently, Ana's cost of a file is $1/2$ contract and Ben's is $1/3$ contract, so Ben has the comparative advantage in reviewing files.
step: Any price between 2 and 3 files per contract helps both. Ana will not sell a contract for fewer than 2 files, and Ben will not pay more than the 3 files it costs him to draft one himself.
step: Check with a price of 2.5 files. Without trade, each splits the day in half: Ana has 4 contracts and 8 files, Ben has 3 contracts and 9 files (7 contracts, 17 files in all). With specialization, Ana makes 8 contracts and Ben 18 files (8 and 18 in all). If Ana sells 4 contracts for 10 files, Ana ends with 4 contracts and 10 files (two more files than before) and Ben ends with 4 contracts and 8 files, one more contract at the price of one file, when drafting that contract himself would have cost him three files.

@mistakes
- Treating the total of all alternatives as the opportunity cost; only the best forgone alternative counts.
- Letting a sunk cost (a nonrefundable deposit, a ticket already bought) drive a forward-looking decision.
- Confusing absolute advantage with comparative advantage and concluding that the more productive person should do everything.
- Forgetting implicit costs such as forgone wages when computing the cost of an activity.

@practice ms
prompt: A country has a straight-line frontier with intercepts of 60 tons of wheat and 30 tons of cloth. Which of the following combinations (wheat, cloth) are attainable with its resources?
choice*: 30 tons of wheat and 15 tons of cloth
choice*: 20 tons of wheat and 10 tons of cloth
choice*: 60 tons of wheat and 0 tons of cloth
choice: 40 tons of wheat and 20 tons of cloth
hint: A combination is attainable if wheat/60 + cloth/30 is at most 1.
hint: Test the combination of 40 and 20: it uses more than all the resources.
solution: The frontier is wheat/60 + cloth/30 = 1. The bundles (30, 15) and (60, 0) lie on it, (20, 10) lies inside it, and (40, 20) gives 2/3 + 2/3 = 4/3 > 1, so it lies outside and is unattainable.
