title: A law-and-economics analysis of a legal rule
overview: Choose a legal rule, such as a negligence standard or a noise ordinance, and analyze the incentives it creates using the tools of this course (supply and demand, surplus, costs, externalities, the Coase theorem or game theory). Write a 1,500-word evaluation that uses numbers to compare the rule with at least one alternative.
goal: Model how a legal rule changes the costs, benefits and choices of the people it governs.
goal: Use numerical examples to compare the efficiency of the rule with an alternative, and say what the analysis leaves out.
goal: Present each side of any policy debate fairly and separate what the model shows from value judgments.
step: Pick one of the two cases in the material below (or your own rule, with data you invent and label as invented). Write the rule in one sentence and name who is affected and what choices they make.
step: Identify the relevant economic tool. For Case A, use marginal cost and marginal benefit of precaution (the logic behind the Hand formula, $B < PL$). For Case B, use externalities and the Coase theorem.
step: Build your numbers. Compute the totals and marginals from the table. Find the efficient outcome, and show what each legal rule (for example negligence with a due-care standard at different levels, versus strict liability, or an injunction versus damages) leads each party to choose.
step: Test the model against its assumptions. What happens if transaction costs are high, if the court sets the standard wrongly, or if one party has information the other lacks (adverse selection or moral hazard)?
step: Write the 1,500-word analysis. Use this order: the rule and its purpose; the model and the numbers; the comparison with an alternative; limits of the analysis and the main arguments on each side of any policy dispute; a short conclusion.
step: Revise. Check that every number in the text can be recomputed from the material, that each claim about incentives is tied to a mechanism from the course, and that the conclusion does not say more than the model supports.
material: **Case A: choosing a negligence standard.** A delivery company chooses a level of precaution (driver training, vehicle maintenance, route planning). The table gives the cost of each level and the expected yearly accident losses to third parties at that level (dollars).

| Precaution level | Cost of precaution | Expected accident loss |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | 1,000 |
| 1 | 100 | 600 |
| 2 | 250 | 350 |
| 3 | 450 | 250 |
| 4 | 700 | 200 |

Compare (i) strict liability (the company pays all accident losses), (ii) negligence with a due-care standard of level 2, and (iii) negligence with a due-care standard of level 4. Assume the court observes the company's precaution level accurately. Then discuss what changes if the court cannot observe the level.
material: **Case B: a noise ordinance.** A bar's live music earns its owner a benefit of \$900 per month. Four neighboring households each suffer harm worth \$275 per month. If the households try to bargain with the bar, organizing, meeting and drafting an agreement costs \$150 per month in total. Compare (i) an ordinance giving households the right to quiet (the bar needs their consent), (ii) no ordinance (the bar may play, and households would have to pay it to stop), and (iii) a liability rule under which the bar may play but must pay the households a court-set amount for the harm. Discuss free riding among households and holdout problems.

deliverable: An appendix listing each calculation with its inputs and result.

rubric: The limits of the model (transaction costs, information, measurement) are discussed, and any policy debate is presented fairly on both sides.

### Week by week

1. **Scarcity and opportunity cost.** Opportunity cost is the value of the best forgone alternative; sunk costs do not count; comparative advantage (lower opportunity cost) drives specialization and trade.
2. **Supply and demand.** Equilibrium sets $Q_d = Q_s$; shifts versus movements; binding ceilings create shortages and binding floors create surpluses.
3. **Elasticity.** Midpoint and point formulas; elastic demand means price and revenue move in opposite directions.
4. **Surplus and taxes.** $CS$ and $PS$ are triangles; a per-unit tax creates a wedge, shifts the burden according to elasticities, and causes deadweight loss $\tfrac{1}{2}t(Q_0 - Q_1)$.
5. **Costs.** $MC$ crosses $ATC$ at minimum $ATC$; fixed costs do not affect marginal cost.
6. **Perfect competition.** $P = MC$; shut down only if $P < AVC$; long-run $P = \min ATC$.
7. **Monopoly.** $MR = MC$, price from demand; deadweight loss from restricted output; price discrimination needs market power, separation and limits on resale.
8. **Oligopoly.** Nash equilibrium, dominant strategies, the prisoner's dilemma, Cournot output $(a - c)/3$ each.
9. **Externalities and public goods.** $MSC = MPC + MEC$; Pigouvian taxes; sum public-good benefits vertically.
10. **Coase.** Efficiency does not depend on who holds the right when transaction costs are low; property versus liability rules.
11. **Information.** Adverse selection (before) versus moral hazard (after); fair premium equals probability times payout.
12. **Labor and inequality.** Hire until $MRP = W$; the competitive and monopsony views of the minimum wage; the Gini coefficient.

### Self-check

- Can you find equilibrium, surplus and deadweight loss from equations without notes?
- Can you set up $MR = MC$ for a monopolist and a Cournot firm, and explain the difference?
- Can you explain, with a numerical example, when bargaining solves an externality and when it fails?
- Can you give one example each of adverse selection and moral hazard and a remedy for each?

### Common errors

- Reading the monopoly price off the marginal revenue curve.
- Confusing a movement along a curve with a shift of the curve.
- Counting a transfer (tax revenue) as a deadweight loss.
- Using average cost instead of marginal cost for the output decision.
- Treating Nash equilibrium as the best joint outcome.
- Applying the Coase theorem when many parties or large bargaining costs are present.
- Treating a policy dispute (minimum wage, price controls) as settled by the model alone.

prompt: In a market, demand is $Q_d = 180 - 3P$ and supply is $Q_s = 2P$ (price in dollars). A tax of \$10 per unit is imposed on sellers. By how many dollars does consumer surplus fall because of the tax?
answer: 264
check: 0.5 * (2 * root(lambda p: (180 - 3*p) - 2*p, 0, 200)) * (60 - root(lambda p: (180 - 3*p) - 2*p, 0, 200)) - 0.5 * (180 - 3 * root(lambda p: (180 - 3*p) - 2*(p - 10), 0, 200)) * (60 - root(lambda p: (180 - 3*p) - 2*(p - 10), 0, 200))
unit: dollars
hint: Find the equilibrium before the tax and the buyer's price after it by setting $Q_d(P_b) = Q_s(P_b - 10)$. The demand price intercept is 60.
hint: Before: $P = 36$, $Q = 72$. After: $P_b = 40$, $Q = 60$.
solution: Before the tax $P = 36$ and $Q = 72$, so $CS = \tfrac{1}{2}(72)(60 - 36) = 864$. After the tax $180 - 3P_b = 2P_b - 20$ gives $P_b = 40$ and $Q = 60$, so $CS = \tfrac{1}{2}(60)(60 - 40) = 600$. Consumer surplus falls by $864 - 600 = 264$ dollars.

prompt: A monopolist faces inverse demand $P = 200 - 2Q$ and has total cost $TC = 500 + 20Q$. Find its maximum profit in dollars.
answer: 3550
check: (200 - 2 * root(lambda q: (200 - 4*q) - 20, 0, 100)) * root(lambda q: (200 - 4*q) - 20, 0, 100) - (500 + 20 * root(lambda q: (200 - 4*q) - 20, 0, 100))
unit: dollars
hint: Marginal revenue is $200 - 4Q$ and marginal cost is the constant 20.
hint: $Q = 45$ and $P = 110$; subtract total cost, including the fixed 500, from revenue.
solution: $200 - 4Q = 20$ gives $Q = 45$ and $P = 200 - 90 = 110$. Revenue is $110 \times 45 = 4950$ and total cost is $500 + 900 = 1400$. Profit is 3550 dollars.

@exam self
prompt: A factory's operations give it a benefit of \$500 per month. Its smoke harms ten households, each to the extent of \$65 per month. (a) Is the factory's operation efficient? (b) Suppose the households hold the right to clean air and bargaining costs total \$200 per month. Would a Coasean deal occur, and what does this imply about the choice between a property rule and a liability rule? (c) Name one other policy that could address the externality and one argument for and against it.
solution: (a) Total harm is $10 \times 65 = 650$, which exceeds the factory's benefit of 500, so operating is inefficient: the net social loss is 150 per month. (b) If the households hold the right, a deal in which they allow the factory to operate would require a payment of at least 650, more than the factory's 500, so no mutually beneficial deal exists, and the factory stops. That is the efficient outcome, and bargaining costs do not matter. If instead the factory holds the right, the households would pay up to 650 to stop it, and the factory will accept at least 500, a gain of 150 from trade, but the net gain after bargaining costs of 200 is $150 - 200 = -50$, and the households must also overcome free riding among ten parties, so the factory may continue and the inefficient outcome persists. Giving the households the entitlement (with an injunction, a property rule) therefore leads to the efficient result here; if the entitlement lay with the factory, a liability rule or regulation would be needed. (c) A Pigouvian tax equal to the external harm (\$65 per household, \$650 a month in all), or a court-set damages award (a liability rule), can internalize the cost: it is flexible and keeps the factory's operating decision in its own hands, but it requires accurate measurement of the harm, and critics note that mistaken measurement can cause too much or too little operation, while supporters of direct regulation point to certainty of outcome.
rubric: Compares total harm (650) with the factory's benefit (500) and concludes the operation is inefficient.
rubric: Analyzes the Coasean bargain under each assignment of rights and uses the bargaining cost of 200 correctly.
rubric: Connects the result to the property-rule versus liability-rule choice and transaction costs.
rubric: Names another policy and fairly gives one argument for and one against.
hint: Add up the harm across all ten households before comparing it with the factory's benefit.
hint: Consider who must pay whom under each assignment of the right, and whether the gain from trade exceeds the bargaining cost.
