@article
A **market** is the set of interactions between buyers and sellers of a good. The **law of demand** says that, other things equal, a higher price lowers the quantity demanded. The **law of supply** says that, other things equal, a higher price raises the quantity supplied. These are statements about **quantity demanded** and **quantity supplied** as functions of price, drawn as curves with price on the vertical axis.

### Movement versus shift

A change in the good's own price moves you **along** a given curve. A change in anything else shifts the whole curve.

- **Demand shifters:** consumer income (for normal goods demand rises with income; for inferior goods it falls), prices of substitutes and complements, tastes, expectations, and the number of buyers.
- **Supply shifters:** input prices, technology, the number of sellers, expectations, and taxes or subsidies on production.

### Equilibrium

The **equilibrium** is the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. At a higher price there is a **surplus** (excess supply) and sellers cut prices; at a lower price there is a **shortage** (excess demand) and buyers bid prices up. To find equilibrium from equations, set

$$Q_d(P) = Q_s(P),$$

solve for $P$, then substitute into either equation for $Q$, and finally check that both equations give the same quantity. For example, with $Q_d = 120 - 2P$ and $Q_s = 20 + 3P$, setting them equal gives $100 = 5P$, so $P^* = 20$ and $Q^* = 80$.

### Comparative statics

To predict the effect of an event, decide which curve shifts and in which direction, then compare the old and new equilibria.

| Event | Curve shifting | Price | Quantity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Demand rises | Demand right | up | up |
| Demand falls | Demand left | down | down |
| Supply rises | Supply right | down | up |
| Supply falls | Supply left | up | down |

When both curves shift, one variable is determinate and the other is ambiguous. If demand rises and supply falls, the price rises for sure, but the change in quantity depends on which shift is larger.

### Price controls

A **price ceiling** is a legal maximum price. It matters only if it is set **below** the equilibrium price, in which case quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied and a shortage results. A **price floor** is a legal minimum price. It matters only if set **above** equilibrium, and then quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded, a surplus. When a price cannot clear the market, the quantity actually traded is the smaller of the two quantities, the "short side", and some other mechanism (queues, rationing, favoritism, quality reduction) allocates the scarce good. Whether controls are good policy is debated: supporters point to affordability or income support for the people who obtain the good at the controlled price, while critics point to shortages, surpluses and reduced quality or investment. The equations tell you the size of the gap; they do not settle the value judgment.

@example
title: Equilibrium, a demand shift and a ceiling
problem: A market has $Q_d = 120 - 2P$ and $Q_s = 20 + 3P$. (a) Find the equilibrium. (b) Demand rises to $Q_d = 150 - 2P$; find the new equilibrium. (c) Return to the original curves and find the shortage if a ceiling of \$15 is imposed.
step: Set $120 - 2P = 20 + 3P$. Then $100 = 5P$, so $P = 20$ and $Q = 20 + 3(20) = 80$. Check demand: $120 - 40 = 80$.
step: With the new demand, $150 - 2P = 20 + 3P$ gives $130 = 5P$, so $P = 26$ and $Q = 20 + 78 = 98$. Both price and quantity rise, as the demand-right row of the table predicts. Check demand: $150 - 52 = 98$.
step: A ceiling of 15 is below the equilibrium of 20, so it binds. At $P = 15$: $Q_d = 120 - 30 = 90$ and $Q_s = 20 + 45 = 65$.
step: The shortage is $90 - 65 = 25$ units, and only 65 units are actually traded.

@mistakes
- Treating a change in the good's own price as a shift of the demand curve; it is a movement along it.
- Shifting both curves when only one is affected by the event (for example, a rise in input prices shifts supply only).
- Applying a ceiling that lies above the equilibrium price and still computing a "shortage"; a nonbinding ceiling has no effect.
- Using the larger of the two quantities as the amount traded under a price control; trade is limited to the short side.

@quiz num
prompt: Demand is $Q_d = 360 - 6P$ and supply is $Q_s = -40 + 4P$. Demand then rises to $Q_d = 460 - 6P$ with supply unchanged. What is the new equilibrium quantity?
answer: 160
check: -40 + 4 * root(lambda p: (460 - 6*p) - (-40 + 4*p), 0, 200)
hint: Use the new demand equation only.
hint: $460 - 6P = -40 + 4P$ gives $500 = 10P$.
solution: $P = 50$, and $Q = -40 + 4(50) = 160$. Check with demand: $460 - 300 = 160$.

@quiz mc

choice: Price is ambiguous and quantity rises.
choice*: Price rises and quantity is ambiguous.

hint: Draw each shift separately and see which variable moves the same way in both.
hint: Supply shifts left, which lowers quantity; demand shifts right, which raises it.
