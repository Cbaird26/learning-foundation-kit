@article
A **labor market** is a market like any other: firms demand labor, workers supply it, and the wage is the price. Applying the tools of the earlier lessons to wages shows what determines pay and how policies and legal rules affect employment.

### Labor demand

A firm hires labor because of what the workers produce. The **marginal product of labor** ($MP_L$) is the extra output of one more worker. The **marginal revenue product** ($MRP_L$) is the extra revenue:

$$MRP_L = MR \times MP_L.$$

For a price-taking seller $MR = P$, so $MRP_L = P \times MP_L$. Because of diminishing marginal returns $MP_L$ falls as workers are added, so $MRP_L$ slopes downward. A firm that takes the wage $W$ as given hires workers until

$$MRP_L = W,$$

that is, it hires every worker whose marginal revenue product is at least the wage. This is the same marginal rule as for output: add a unit while the marginal benefit exceeds the marginal cost. The $MRP_L$ curve is the firm's labor demand curve. It shifts when the output price changes, when technology changes productivity, or when other inputs (such as capital) change.

### Labor supply and equilibrium

Higher wages draw more people into work and make others willing to work more hours, so labor supply slopes upward (though income effects can bend it for some workers). In a **competitive** labor market the wage and employment are set where labor demand equals labor supply, like any equilibrium. Wage differences across jobs are explained by productivity and **human capital** (education, training), by compensating differentials for unpleasant or risky work, and by scarcity of skills, and also, in part, by discrimination, bargaining power and institutions, which are the subject of anti-discrimination and labor law.

### Minimum wage: two views

In the **competitive model**, a minimum wage set above the equilibrium wage is a price floor. Employers demand less labor and more workers seek jobs, so a **surplus of labor** (unemployment) results, and the quantity of employment is the smaller quantity, labor demanded. In a **monopsony** model, where a single employer (or a few) has power over wages in a local market, the employer pays less than the competitive wage and hires fewer workers than a competitive market would; a moderate minimum wage can then raise employment, because the employer no longer needs to raise the wage for all workers to hire one more. Empirical studies of actual minimum-wage changes have produced a range of findings, and economists disagree about how much the competitive or monopsony model describes real labor markets. The models agree that a high enough floor reduces employment; they disagree about where that point lies. Supporters of a higher minimum wage emphasize higher pay for workers who keep their jobs, and opponents emphasize lost hours or jobs for the least experienced.

### Measuring inequality

The **Lorenz curve** plots the cumulative share of income against the cumulative share of households ranked from poorest to richest; a straight 45-degree line means perfect equality. The **Gini coefficient** is the area between the line of equality and the Lorenz curve, divided by the whole area under the line of equality: it is 0 for perfect equality and approaches 1 when one household has everything. For a small group of $n$ incomes with mean $\mu$,

$$G = \frac{\sum_i \sum_j |x_i - x_j|}{2n^2\mu},$$

where the double sum runs over all ordered pairs. Inequality can be measured before or after taxes and transfers. Economists disagree on how much inequality is acceptable and what redistribution costs in efficiency; the descriptive measures above do not settle that normative question.

@example
title: Hiring, a wage floor and a Gini coefficient
problem: (a) A firm sells its output at \$10 per unit. Its workers' marginal products are 8, 6, 4 and 2 units. How many workers does it hire at a wage of \$35? (b) In a competitive labor market $L_d = 100 - 4W$ and $L_s = 20 + 4W$. Find equilibrium, and the unemployment from a minimum wage of \$12. (c) Find the Gini coefficient for incomes of 10, 20, 30 and 40.
step: $MRP$ for each worker is $10 \times$ the marginal product: 80, 60, 40, 20. The firm hires while $MRP \ge 35$: the first three workers qualify (80, 60, 40), but the fourth (20) does not. It hires 3.
step: Equilibrium: $100 - 4W = 20 + 4W$ gives $W = 10$ and $L = 60$. A minimum wage of 12 is above 10, so it binds.
step: At $W = 12$: $L_d = 100 - 48 = 52$ and $L_s = 20 + 48 = 68$. In the competitive model 52 workers are hired and the surplus of labor is $68 - 52 = 16$.
step: The mean income is 25 and there are $n = 4$ people. The unordered pairwise differences are 10, 20, 30, 10, 20, 10, which sum to 100, so the sum over ordered pairs is 200. Then $G = 200/(2 \times 16 \times 25) = 200/800 = 0.25$.

@mistakes
- Hiring until $MRP$ is zero rather than until $MRP$ equals the wage.
- Using marginal product (units) as if it were marginal revenue product (dollars).
- Presenting the competitive prediction for minimum wages as settled fact for every market; the monopsony model gives a different result.
- Dividing by $n$ rather than $2n^2\mu$ when computing the Gini coefficient from pairwise differences.

@practice self
prompt: A company hires a salesperson whose effort and skill it cannot fully observe. Give one example of adverse selection and one example of moral hazard that could arise in this labor market, explain why each is an information problem, and describe one way the employer could respond to each.
solution: Adverse selection (hidden characteristics before the contract): when the employer offers a flat salary regardless of performance, applicants who know they are weak sellers find the job more attractive than strong sellers, who could earn more elsewhere through commissions, so the applicant pool is worse than average. The employer cannot observe ability at hiring. Responses include screening (interviews, trial periods, work samples) or offering a menu, such as a lower salary with commission or a higher flat salary, that lets stronger sellers pick the performance-based plan. Moral hazard (hidden action after the contract): once hired on a flat salary, the employee may put in less effort because the employer cannot observe it directly and the employee does not bear the cost of low sales. Responses include commissions or bonuses tied to results, monitoring, and efficiency wages (paying above the market wage so that losing the job is costly).
rubric: Identifies adverse selection as a hidden characteristic problem before hiring and gives a concrete example.
rubric: Identifies moral hazard as a hidden action problem after hiring and gives a concrete example.
rubric: Explains the information asymmetry in each case.
rubric: Gives a plausible response for each (screening or a menu; incentive pay, monitoring or efficiency wages).
hint: Ask what the employer cannot observe at the time of hiring and what it cannot observe afterward.
hint: Think of screening, incentive pay and monitoring as the tools.

hint: The mean income is 25. List the six unordered pairs and their differences.
