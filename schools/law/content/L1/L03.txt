@article
A promise is enforceable as a contract only if the formation requirements are met and the promise is supported by **consideration** or one of its substitutes. Consideration serves an evidentiary, cautionary and channeling function: it separates serious bargains from casual or gratuitous promises.

### The bargained-for exchange

Under Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 71, a performance or return promise is consideration if it is **bargained for**: it is sought by the promisor in exchange for the promise and given by the promisee in exchange for it. The performance may be an act, a forbearance, or the creation, modification or destruction of a legal relation. Traditionally the test was **legal detriment** to the promisee or benefit to the promisor, and the two ideas are usually met together.

In *Hamer v. Sidway* (1891), an uncle promised his nephew \$5,000 if the nephew refrained from drinking, using tobacco, swearing and gambling until age 21. The nephew's forbearance from acts he had a legal right to do was sufficient consideration, even though the uncle claimed that the nephew was better off. The court does not weigh the **adequacy** of consideration: a peppercorn can support a promise to convey a house. Gross inadequacy may be evidence of fraud, duress or unconscionability. Sham or **nominal consideration** (a recital of "one dollar" never paid, or an exchange that was not actually bargained for) is not enough in most states. A **conditional gift** ("I'll give you a ride if you come to the party") is not an exchange, because the condition is not the price for the promise.

### What is not consideration

- **Past consideration.** A promise made in return for something already done, not requested in advance, lacks the element of exchange. *Mills v. Wyman* (1825) refused to enforce a father's promise to pay for care given to his adult son. Exceptions exist: a promise to pay a debt barred by the statute of limitations or discharged in bankruptcy, and, in some states, the Restatement § 86 rule that a promise to pay for a **material benefit previously received** is binding to the extent necessary to prevent injustice.
- **Illusory promises.** A promise that leaves the promisor free to perform or not is no promise: "I will buy as much as I please." Courts avoid finding a contract illusory by implying an obligation. In *Wood v. Lucy, Lady Duff-Gordon* (1917) an agent with exclusive rights to place a designer's endorsements was held to have impliedly promised reasonable efforts, which made the arrangement a contract. UCC § 2-306 does the same for **output and requirements contracts**: quantity is measured by the actual output or requirements, in good faith, and a demand may not be unreasonably disproportionate to a stated estimate or to prior output or requirements.
- **Pre-existing duty.** Promising to do what one is already legally bound to do is not consideration.

### Modification of contracts

At common law, a modification must be supported by consideration, so a promise to pay extra for the same work is unenforceable (*Alaska Packers' Ass'n v. Domenico*, 1902, where seamen who refused to work unless paid more had no valid claim). There are two exceptions: a new detriment on either side, even a slight one, and the Restatement § 89 rule that a modification is binding if it is **fair and equitable in view of circumstances not anticipated by the parties** when the contract was made.

Under **UCC § 2-209(1)**, an agreement modifying a contract for goods **needs no consideration** to be binding, but the modification must meet the test of **good faith** (§ 1-304); extortion of a modification is not good faith. If the contract has a signed "no oral modification" clause, § 2-209(2) generally enforces it, though a clause on a merchant's form must be separately signed by the other party unless both are merchants, and § 2-209(4) allows a modification that fails to satisfy the clause to operate as a waiver. The statute of frauds still applies to the modified contract (§ 2-209(3)).

### Settlement of claims

A promise to forbear on a claim, or a payment smaller than a claimed debt, is consideration if the claim is **disputed in good faith** (Restatement § 74), because the claimant gives up a right to litigate. Payment of part of an **undisputed, liquidated** debt is not consideration for a release of the rest at common law (the pre-existing duty rule).

### Applying it

Ask: What did the promisor ask for? Did the promisee give it in exchange? If the promise is a modification, is the contract for goods? If so, § 2-209(1) applies and consideration is not required; otherwise find a new duty or an unforeseen change. If the promise looks like a gift or a past favor, look to the substitutes in week 4.

@example
title: A promise for a favor already done
problem: While Owner was on vacation, Neighbor, without being asked, trimmed a fallen tree off Owner's driveway. When Owner returned, Owner said: "That was kind. I'll pay you \$300." Neighbor now sues when Owner refuses to pay. Is the promise supported by consideration?
step: Issue: whether Owner's promise is supported by consideration. Rule: consideration must be bargained for, meaning sought by the promisor in exchange for the promise and given in exchange for it.
step: Application: Neighbor acted before Owner made any promise and without Owner's request. The tree work was not induced by the promise, so it was not given in exchange for it. This is past consideration.
step: Exceptions: no debt barred by limitations or bankruptcy is involved. In a minority of states under Restatement § 86 a promise to pay for a material benefit may be binding, but the majority rule and the question as stated point to the absence of consideration.
step: Conclusion: under the majority rule, the promise is unenforceable for lack of consideration.

@mistakes
- Examining whether the exchange was fair or equal; courts examine whether it was bargained for, not whether it was adequate.
- Enforcing a promise for past benefits that were not requested in advance, other than through the narrow exceptions.
- Applying the common-law pre-existing duty rule to a modification of a contract for goods; under UCC § 2-209(1) no consideration is required, but good faith is.
- Treating a settlement of a disputed claim as a gift of the surrendered amount; the claimant's forbearance is consideration if the claim was asserted in good faith.

prompt: Pat writes to Quinn: "I will buy from you whatever quantity of widgets I decide I want during the next year, at \$5 each, if I decide I want any." Quinn accepts. Pat buys nothing. Write an IRAC analysis of whether Pat's promise is consideration for Quinn's promise.

solution: Issue: whether Pat's statement is a promise that can serve as consideration, or an illusory promise. Rule: consideration requires a bargained-for exchange; a promise that leaves the promisor with unlimited discretion whether to perform is illusory and is not consideration. Courts try to avoid finding illusory promises by implying a good-faith or reasonable-efforts obligation (*Wood v.
