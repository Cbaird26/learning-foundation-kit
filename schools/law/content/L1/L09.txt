@article
### Conditions

A **condition** is an event, not certain to occur, that must occur before a duty to perform arises (**condition precedent**) or whose occurrence ends a duty (**condition subsequent**). A **promise** is a commitment; failure to perform it is a breach that gives damages. Failure of a condition is not a breach (unless a party promised that it would occur); it simply means the duty to perform never matured or was discharged.

Types:

- **Express conditions** are stated by the parties ("only if," "provided that," "on condition that," "subject to"). They must be satisfied **strictly**: substantial compliance is not enough. If doubt exists whether language creates a condition or a promise, courts lean toward finding a promise, to avoid forfeiture (Restatement § 227).
- **Implied-in-fact conditions** arise from the parties' conduct and the nature of the deal.
- **Constructive conditions** (implied in law) are imposed by courts for fairness. Principal examples: in an exchange of promises, each party's duty is conditioned on the other party's **substantial performance**; if one performance takes time and the other is a single act, the one requiring time goes first (Restatement § 234). If performances can be exchanged simultaneously, they are concurrent conditions.

**Satisfaction clauses.** Where a party's duty is conditioned on personal satisfaction with a matter of taste or fancy (a portrait, a gift), the test is **honest dissatisfaction** (subjective good faith). Where the matter is commercial or mechanical (a machine, a building), most courts apply an **objective reasonable-person standard**.

**Excuses of conditions.** A condition is excused by **prevention** (the party who benefits from the condition prevents its occurrence), **waiver** (voluntary relinquishment), **estoppel**, **impossibility** or **futility**, and under Restatement § 229 where strict enforcement would cause a **disproportionate forfeiture** and the condition was not a material part of the agreed exchange.

### Substantial performance and material breach

In an exchange for which performance is a matter of degree (such as construction), a party who has **substantially performed**, with only minor deviations made in good faith, may recover the contract price less damages for the deviations. In *Jacob & Youngs, Inc. v. Kent* (1921), a builder used a different brand of pipe of the same quality, and the court held the owner could not withhold payment, but was entitled only to damages measured by the difference in value, which was nominal, not the cost of replacing the pipe, which would have been wasteful.

A **material breach** goes to the essence of the contract: it gives the injured party the right to **suspend performance and terminate**, and to sue for total breach. A **minor breach** gives only damages. Restatement § 241 lists the factors:

1. the extent to which the injured party is **deprived of the benefit** reasonably expected;
2. the extent to which the injured party can be **adequately compensated** in damages;
3. the extent to which the breaching party will **suffer forfeiture**;
4. the **likelihood of cure**, considering the circumstances and any assurances; and
5. whether the breaching party's conduct comports with **good faith and fair dealing**.

The breaching party may often **cure** a curable breach before the time for performance expires. A minor breach does not excuse the other party's performance, but a material breach does, once the breach is not cured within a reasonable time.

### Anticipatory repudiation

A **repudiation** is an unequivocal statement or voluntary act showing that the party will commit a material breach (*Hochster v. De La Tour*, 1853, allowed suit before performance was due). The aggrieved party may treat it as a total breach and sue immediately, or suspend performance and wait a reasonable time for retraction; the repudiating party may **retract** until the other has materially changed position, cancelled, or said that it considers the repudiation final (UCC § 2-611). Under UCC § 2-610, the aggrieved party may await performance for a commercially reasonable time or resort to remedies, and may suspend its own performance. If a party has **reasonable grounds for insecurity**, UCC § 2-609 allows a written demand for **adequate assurance**; failure to provide it within a reasonable time, not exceeding **30 days**, is a repudiation.

### UCC performance rules

- **Perfect tender rule** (§ 2-601): if goods or tender of delivery fail **in any respect** to conform to the contract, a buyer may reject the whole, accept the whole or accept any commercial units and reject the rest. The rule applies to **single-delivery** contracts.
- **Cure** (§ 2-508): if the time for performance has not expired, the seller may notify the buyer and cure within the contract time; after rejection, if the seller had reasonable grounds to believe the tender would be acceptable, the seller may have a further reasonable time to cure.
- **Installment contracts** (§ 2-612): the buyer may reject an installment only if the nonconformity **substantially impairs** the value of that installment and cannot be cured; the whole contract is breached only if the defect substantially impairs the value of the whole.
- **Acceptance and revocation** (§§ 2-606, 2-608): once goods are accepted, the buyer may revoke acceptance only for a nonconformity that **substantially impairs** value, where acceptance was based on a reasonable assumption it would be cured or on difficulty of discovery.

### Applying it

Identify whether the duty is conditional; classify the condition; check satisfaction or excuse; then evaluate the breach (material or minor, under § 241 or the UCC) and available remedies.

@example
title: The unfinished punch list
problem: Owner agrees to pay Builder \$150,000 to build a home. Builder completes the house, but a bathroom fan of a different brand is installed than the contract names. It works as well and costs \$60 less. Owner refuses to pay the final \$30,000, claiming a breach. What are the parties' rights?
step: Issue: whether Builder's deviation was a material breach that excuses Owner's duty to pay. Rule: a party who substantially performs in good faith may recover the contract price less damages for the deviation; the § 241 factors decide materiality.
step: Application: Owner received the home as bargained for, except for a trivial difference in a fan. Damages can be measured easily (at most the diminution in value, here nothing or \$60). Builder would suffer a large forfeiture (\$30,000) if Owner could withhold. The deviation was not in bad faith.
step: Conclusion: Builder substantially performed. Owner must pay the \$30,000, less damages for the deviation (none or nominal).

@mistakes
- Treating every breach as grounds to stop performing; only a material breach does, and a minor breach gives damages only.
- Applying substantial performance to an express condition; express conditions are strictly enforced unless excused.
- Applying the perfect tender rule to installment contracts or forgetting the seller's right to cure.
- Treating doubt about performance as repudiation without a written demand for assurance under § 2-609 (or an unequivocal statement of refusal).

Write an IRAC analysis of the parties' rights, addressing the express condition and material breach.

solution: Issue: whether Owner may withhold the final payment and terminate. Rule: an express condition must be strictly satisfied before the duty it conditions arises, and failure of the condition means the duty has not matured; a material breach (Restatement § 241 factors: deprivation of expected benefit, adequacy of damages, forfeiture, likelihood of cure, good faith) allows termination, while a minor breach allows only damages.
