@article
The basic aim of contract remedies is to protect a party's **expectation interest**: to put the injured party in as good a position as full performance would have. Two lesser interests are protected in some cases: the **reliance interest** (to restore the party to the position held before the contract was made) and the **restitution interest** (to prevent the defendant's unjust enrichment). Punitive damages are generally not available for breach of contract, and damages for emotional distress are generally not recoverable except in unusual cases (for example, contracts whose very object is emotional well-being in some jurisdictions).

### Expectation damages

Restatement (Second) § 347: damages equal the **loss in value** of the other party's performance caused by the breach, **plus** any other loss, including incidental or consequential loss, **minus** any **cost or other loss avoided** by not having to perform.

$$\text{Expectation} = \text{loss in value} + \text{other loss} - \text{cost avoided} - \text{loss avoided}.$$

For a builder whose owner repudiates, expectation equals the contract price minus the cost the builder saves by not completing. Equivalent: **profit** (price minus total cost) plus **costs already incurred**. **Reliance damages** (§ 349) are the injured party's expenditures in preparing or performing, and they may be recovered instead of expectation when expectation is too uncertain, but may not exceed what the party would have received. **Restitution** (§ 371) returns the benefit conferred.

### Limits on recovery

- **Foreseeability.** Damages are recoverable only for loss that the breaching party had **reason to foresee as a probable result of the breach at the time the contract was made** (§ 351). In *Hadley v. Baxendale* (1854), a miller whose mill was stopped by a broken crankshaft could not recover lost profits from a carrier's delay in delivering the shaft to the repairer, because the carrier did not know that the mill would be idle. **General** (natural) damages are those arising in the ordinary course; **special** (consequential) damages are recoverable if the breaching party knew or had reason to know of the special circumstances. UCC § 2-715(2)(a) uses the same idea: consequential damages include loss resulting from the buyer's requirements and needs of which the seller at the time of contracting had **reason to know** and which could not reasonably be prevented by cover.
- **Certainty.** Damages must be proved with reasonable certainty (§ 352). Lost profits of an established business can usually be shown; those of a new business are treated more cautiously.
- **Mitigation (avoidable consequences)** (§ 350). The injured party cannot recover for loss that could have been avoided without undue risk, burden or humiliation. In *Rockingham County v. Luten Bridge Co.* (1929), a builder who continued building a bridge after the county repudiated could not recover the cost of work done after notice. An employee wrongfully discharged must seek **comparable** employment; earnings from comparable jobs reduce damages, and the employee need not accept a substantially different or inferior job. A **lost-volume seller** who could have made both the sale to the breaching buyer and a resale to another can recover its lost profit, not just the price difference (*Neri v. Retail Marine Corp.*, 1972).
- **Cost of completion versus diminution in value.** When a builder's defect is repairable without economic waste, the measure is the cost to repair; if repair would be wasteful, courts award the difference in value (*Jacob & Youngs v. Kent*). Courts vary on the rule for a defect that does not much affect value (*Peevyhouse v. Garland Coal & Mining Co.*, 1962, awarded diminution in value).

### UCC sales remedies

**Buyer's remedies** for seller's nondelivery or repudiation:

- **Cover** (§ 2-712): buy substitute goods in good faith and without unreasonable delay; damages = **cover price − contract price**, plus incidental and consequential damages, minus expenses saved.
- **Market damages** (§ 2-713): **market price at the time the buyer learned of the breach − contract price**, plus incidental and consequential damages, minus expenses saved.
- **Specific performance** (§ 2-716) where goods are unique or in other proper circumstances.
- **Incidental damages** (§ 2-715(1)): reasonable expenses of inspection, receipt, transportation, care and custody of rejected goods, and of effecting cover.

**Seller's remedies** for buyer's breach:

- **Resale** (§ 2-706): resale price shortfall: contract price − resale price, plus incidental damages, minus expenses saved.
- **Market damages** (§ 2-708(1)): contract price − **market price at the time and place for tender**, plus incidental damages, minus expenses saved.
- **Lost profit** (§ 2-708(2)) if the above is inadequate to put the seller in as good a position, as for a lost-volume seller or a manufacturer.
- **Action for the price** (§ 2-709) for goods accepted, goods lost after risk passed, or goods identified to the contract that the seller cannot resell.

### Equitable relief and liquidated damages

**Specific performance** is available only when damages are **inadequate** (unique goods, land), the terms are certain, and enforcement is feasible. Courts do not order specific performance of personal services contracts, though they may enjoin a performer with unique services from working for a rival (*Lumley v. Wagner*, 1852).

**Liquidated damages** clauses are enforced if the amount is a **reasonable forecast** of the harm in light of the anticipated or actual loss and the difficulty of proof. If the sum is unreasonably large, it is an unenforceable **penalty** (Restatement § 356; UCC § 2-718(1)).

@example
title: The repudiated building contract
problem: Owner contracts to pay Builder \$100,000 for a barn. Builder estimates total cost at \$80,000. After Builder has spent \$12,000 on materials, Owner repudiates. Builder stops work at once. The \$12,000 of materials cannot be returned for a refund. What are Builder's expectation damages?
step: Start with the formula: expectation = loss in value + other loss − cost avoided. The loss in value is the contract price Builder will not receive: \$100,000.
step: Cost avoided: Builder has already spent \$12,000, so the remaining costs of \$80,000 − \$12,000 = \$68,000 are avoided by stopping.
step: Expectation = \$100,000 − \$68,000 = \$32,000.
step: Check using the alternative form: profit (\$100,000 − \$80,000 = \$20,000) plus costs already incurred (\$12,000) = \$32,000.

@mistakes
- Subtracting total cost instead of only the costs avoided (costs already incurred are not avoided).
- Awarding consequential damages without asking whether the breaching party had reason to know of the special circumstances when the contract was made.
- Using the wrong market time under the UCC: § 2-713 uses the time the buyer learned of the breach and § 2-708(1) the time and place for tender.
- Forgetting mitigation, including that an employee need accept only comparable employment.
