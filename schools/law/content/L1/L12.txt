@article
Contracts ordinarily create rights and duties only between the parties (**privity**). Three doctrines let outsiders enter the picture: **third-party beneficiary** rights, **assignment** of rights, and **delegation** of duties.

### Third-party beneficiaries

Under Restatement (Second) § 302, a beneficiary of a promise is an **intended beneficiary** if recognizing a right in the beneficiary is **appropriate to effectuate the intention of the parties**, and either (a) the performance of the promise will **satisfy an obligation of the promisee to pay money to the beneficiary** (the old **creditor beneficiary**), or (b) the circumstances indicate that the promisee **intends to give the beneficiary the benefit of the promised performance** (the old **donee beneficiary**). Any other beneficiary is an **incidental beneficiary**, who has **no rights**.

In *Lawrence v. Fox* (1859), a lender gave money to a man who promised to pay a debt the lender owed to a third person; the third person was allowed to sue the promisor. In *Seaver v. Ransom* (1918), a niece was allowed to enforce a dying woman's husband's promise to leave her a sum in his will. Examples of incidental beneficiaries: a neighbor who would profit from a contractor's construction of a hotel next door; the public, in most contracts with government.

**Vesting.** The rights of an intended beneficiary **vest** when the beneficiary **materially changes position in justifiable reliance** on the promise, **brings suit**, or **manifests assent** to it at the request of the parties (§ 311). Until vesting, the promisor and promisee may modify or rescind the contract and defeat the beneficiary's rights unless the contract provides otherwise. The beneficiary's rights are **subject to the defenses** the promisor has against the promisee (for example, failure of consideration or the promisee's breach). A beneficiary may also be able to sue the promisee in some situations; the promisee may sue for specific performance of the promise to benefit.

### Assignment

An **assignment** is a transfer of a right to receive performance. The **assignor** (obligee) transfers to the **assignee**; the other party is the **obligor**. No special words are needed; a manifestation of intent to transfer the right, present and not future, is enough. Key rules:

- The assignee **stands in the assignor's shoes**: the assignee takes subject to all defenses and setoffs the obligor has against the assignor arising from the contract.
- **Notice** to the obligor is not required for validity but affects the obligor: an obligor who pays the assignor **before notice** of the assignment is discharged.
- A **gratuitous assignment** is revocable, unless the assignee has relied, a writing was delivered, or payment has occurred (§ 332).
- **Non-assignable rights** (§ 317): rights whose assignment would **materially change the obligor's duty or increase burden or risk**, or **impair the obligor's chance of obtaining return performance**; rights in personal-service contracts in some cases; assignments barred by statute or public policy.
- **Anti-assignment clauses.** Under the Restatement (§ 322) and UCC § 2-210(3), a clause prohibiting assignment of "the contract" is construed to bar only **delegation** of performance, not assignment of rights, unless the contract clearly says otherwise (for example, that attempted assignments are void). A violation may give the obligor damages. UCC Article 9 (§ 9-406) overrides anti-assignment terms for accounts, a limit in financing.

### Delegation

A **delegation** is the appointment of another (**delegatee**) to perform the delegator's duty. Most duties can be delegated, but **not** where the obligee has a **substantial interest in having the original promisor perform** (§ 318; UCC § 2-210(1)): personal-skill or trust contracts, and where the contract forbids it. Key points:

- The **delegator remains liable** to the obligee, even if the delegate promises to perform; delegation does not discharge the delegator.
- A delegatee who **assumes** the duty is bound to the delegator, and the obligee is an intended beneficiary of that promise who may sue the delegatee.
- A **novation** (agreement by the obligee to release the original party and accept a substitute) discharges the original party.
- An assignment of "the contract" is generally construed as both an assignment of rights and a delegation of duties (UCC § 2-210(4)).

### Damages with mitigation and foreseeability

Whether the plaintiff is a party or a beneficiary, damages are computed the same way: expectation, minus savings, with consequential loss only if foreseeable and only to the extent not avoidable by reasonable mitigation (week 11).

@example
title: The delegated painting
problem: Owner hires Painter to paint a house for \$4,000. Painter, who becomes too busy, delegates the work to Co-Painter, a competent painter. Co-Painter does shoddy work. Owner sues Painter. What is the likely result?
step: Issue: whether Painter remains liable for Co-Painter's performance. Rule: duties may be delegated unless the obligee has a substantial interest in performance by the original promisor; delegation does not discharge the delegator.
step: Application: ordinary house painting does not call for unique skill or trust, so the delegation was permitted (unless the contract forbade it). But Owner never agreed to release Painter.
step: Conclusion: Painter remains liable to Owner for the shoddy work. Owner may also have a claim against Co-Painter as a beneficiary of Co-Painter's promise to Painter.

@mistakes
- Treating every beneficiary of a contract as having rights; only intended beneficiaries do.
- Forgetting that an assignee takes subject to the obligor's defenses.
- Assuming delegation discharges the delegator; it takes a novation to do that.
- Reading "no assignment of this contract" as barring assignment of the right to payment, rather than only delegation, in the absence of clear language.

answer: 5250
check: (24 - 20) * 1000 + (3000 - 2000) + 250
unit: dollars
hint: Start with the cover difference, then decide which consequential losses are both foreseeable and unavoidable.
