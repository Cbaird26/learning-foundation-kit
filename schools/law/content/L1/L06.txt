@article
A **mistake** is a belief that is not in accord with the facts. This lesson covers mistakes about the facts existing when the contract was made. (A mistake about a future event is a risk allocation or an impracticability question, week 10.) Then it covers bargains the law refuses to enforce for reasons of illegality or public policy.

### Mutual mistake

Under Restatement (Second) § 152, where both parties were mistaken at the time of contracting as to a **basic assumption** on which the contract was made, and the mistake has a **material effect** on the agreed exchange, the contract is **voidable by the adversely affected party**, unless that party **bears the risk** of the mistake.

Elements:

1. a mistake about an existing fact by **both** parties;
2. the mistake concerns a **basic assumption** (usually the identity, existence or nature of the thing, not merely its value or quality, though quality can be basic);
3. a **material effect** on the exchange that is adverse to the party seeking relief; and
4. the party seeking relief **did not bear the risk** of the mistake.

Under § 154, a party bears the risk if the contract allocates it to that party; if the party is aware at the time of having only limited knowledge of the facts but treats that knowledge as sufficient (**conscious ignorance**); or if the court allocates the risk for reasons of reasonableness.

In *Sherwood v. Walker* (1887), a cow sold as barren was found to be with calf and worth many times the price; the court allowed the seller to rescind because the mistake went to the substance of the thing sold, not just its quality. In *Wood v. Boynton* (1885), a seller who sold an unidentified stone for a very small price, knowing she did not know what it was, could not rescind when it turned out to be a valuable diamond. These are best read together: a mistake about a basic assumption supports rescission, but a party who knowingly sells a thing without knowing what it is takes the risk. Mistakes of **value or market conditions**, or about a prediction, are generally not mistakes about basic assumptions.

**Remedy.** Rescission and restitution (each party returns what it received). Where a writing fails to express the actual agreement because of a mistake of expression (a scrivener's error), the remedy is **reformation**.

### Unilateral mistake

Only one party is mistaken. Under § 153, the contract is voidable by the mistaken party if the mistake concerns a basic assumption, has a material effect, the party does not bear the risk, **and** either (a) enforcing the contract would be **unconscionable**, or (b) the other party **had reason to know of the mistake or caused it**. Courts also grant relief for **clerical or computational errors** in a bid when the mistaken party gave prompt notice and the other party has not substantially relied. Carelessness alone does not bar relief unless it amounts to a failure to act in good faith, though a grossly negligent party may bear the risk.

### No meeting of the minds

In *Raffles v. Wichelhaus* (1864), the parties agreed to a cotton sale on a ship named *Peerless* arriving from Bombay, and there were two ships of that name sailing at different times. Each party had a different ship in mind and neither knew of the other's. The court held there was no contract. Under Restatement § 20, there is no contract where the parties attach materially different meanings to a term and neither knows or has reason to know of the other's meaning. If one party knows or has reason to know the other's meaning, that meaning governs.

### Illegality and public policy

A bargain is unenforceable on grounds of public policy if its performance would be criminal, tortious or against public policy (§ 178), weighing the interest in enforcement against the policy. The usual rule: courts leave the parties where they find them, but allow restitution to a party who is not equally at fault, is protected by the statute, or withdraws before performance of the illegal purpose.

Examples:

- **Licensing statutes.** If the statute is **regulatory** (to protect the public from unqualified providers), a contract by an unlicensed party is generally unenforceable by that party; if it is merely **revenue-raising**, the contract is enforceable, though the party may be penalized.
- **Covenants not to compete** are enforced only if reasonable in time, geographic scope and activity, and supported by a legitimate interest (customer relationships, trade secrets). Restraints ancillary to the sale of a business are examined less strictly than those in employment. If a covenant is overbroad, the majority approach lets a court **reform** it or sever the unreasonable part (blue-penciling); a minority refuses enforcement altogether.
- **Exculpatory clauses** are unenforceable against liability for intentional or reckless harm and in transactions affecting the public interest (*Tunkl v. Regents of the University of California*, 1963, in the context of a hospital admission release).
- **Severability.** A court may enforce the remainder of a contract if the illegal term is not essential (§ 184).

### IRAC for a mistake problem

**Issue:** which kind of mistake? **Rule:** the elements and the risk-allocation rule. **Application:** was the matter a basic assumption or only value? who bore the risk? did the other party know? **Conclusion:** voidable or enforceable, and the remedy.

@example
title: The painting that was not a copy
problem: Seller and Buyer, neither an expert, agree that Seller will sell Buyer a framed painting for \$500, both believing it to be a modern copy. Neither has had it appraised, and the contract says nothing on the subject. Before delivery, Seller learns the painting is an original worth \$50,000. May Seller avoid the contract?
step: Issue: whether the parties' shared mistake about the nature of the painting permits Seller to avoid. Rule: a contract is voidable by the adversely affected party if both parties were mistaken about a basic assumption, with material effect, unless that party bears the risk (§§ 152, 154).
step: Application: both believed the painting was a copy. Whether it is an original is the nature of the thing sold, not just its market value, so it is a basic assumption. The effect on Seller is enormous.
step: Risk: the contract does not allocate the risk, and Seller was not consciously ignorant, because Seller believed it knew the painting's nature. A court is unlikely to put the risk on Seller.
step: Conclusion: the contract is voidable by Seller. The remedy is rescission and restitution (Seller returns the \$500, Buyer returns the painting).

@mistakes
- Treating any bad bargain or error in valuation as a mutual mistake; it must concern a basic assumption, and the risk must not be on the party seeking relief.
- Forgetting the risk-allocation step, such as a party who knowingly acts under uncertainty.
- Applying mutual mistake rules to unilateral mistake, where relief usually needs unconscionability or the other party's knowledge.
- Assuming an unlicensed contractor can always recover; the answer depends on whether the statute protects the public.
