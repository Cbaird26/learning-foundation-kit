@article
The **statute of frauds** (SoF) requires certain contracts to be evidenced by a **signed writing** to be enforceable. It originated in the English Statute of Frauds (1677) and is now state law, and for goods, UCC § 2-201. It is a **defense to enforcement**: an oral contract within the statute is not void; it is unenforceable against a party who raises the defense. The statute concerns **evidence**, not whether the contract was formed. For goods, UCC § 2-201(3)(b) removes the defense when the defendant admits in pleading, testimony or otherwise in court that a contract was made, to the quantity admitted.

### The categories ("MY LEGS")

1. **M**arriage: a promise made in consideration of marriage (not a promise to marry).
2. **Y**ear: a contract that **cannot be performed within one year** from the date of making. The test is whether performance is **possible** within a year, not likely. A contract for 18 months is within the statute; a contract "for the rest of Ana's life" is not, because Ana could die within a year; a contract of indefinite duration is not, because it could end within a year. The year runs from the date of the agreement, not the start of performance. Most courts hold that full performance by one party removes the contract from the statute.
3. **L**and: a contract for the sale or transfer of an interest in real property, including mortgages, easements and leases longer than one year.
4. **E**xecutor: a promise by an executor or administrator to pay a debt of the estate from the executor's own funds.
5. **G**oods: a contract for the sale of goods for **\$500 or more** (UCC § 2-201).
6. **S**uretyship: a promise to answer for the debt or default of another, made to the creditor. Under the **main purpose rule**, a promise made primarily for the promisor's own economic benefit is outside the statute.

### What counts as a sufficient writing

The writing must be **signed by the party to be charged** (the defendant), be **adequate to indicate that a contract was made**, and state the **essential terms**: identity of the parties, subject matter, consideration and, for land, the price and a description of the property. A signature can be any symbol adopted with intent to authenticate: initials, letterhead, a typed name in an email. Under federal E-SIGN (15 U.S.C. § 7001) and the Uniform Electronic Transactions Act, electronic records and signatures cannot be denied effect solely because they are electronic. Several writings can be read together. The writing may be informal, such as a memo or check, and may be made after the contract.

For goods, **UCC § 2-201(1)**'s writing need not state all terms, but it **must state a quantity**, and the contract is enforceable only up to the quantity stated. A missing or incorrect price, time or place of delivery does not defeat it.

### Exceptions in a sale of goods (UCC § 2-201)

- **Merchant's confirmation** (§ 2-201(2)): between merchants, if within a reasonable time a signed written confirmation of the contract is sent and the recipient has reason to know of its contents, it satisfies the statute against the recipient unless the recipient gives written notice of objection within **10 days** of receipt. The recipient loses only the statute defense; the sender must still prove that an oral contract was made.
- **Specially manufactured goods** (§ 2-201(3)(a)): goods not suitable for sale to others in the ordinary course of the seller's business, where the seller has made a substantial beginning of their manufacture or commitments for their procurement before notice of repudiation.
- **Admission** (§ 2-201(3)(b)) in pleading, testimony or otherwise in court that a contract was made, but only to the quantity admitted.
- **Payment and acceptance** (§ 2-201(3)(c)): the contract is enforceable for goods **for which payment has been made and accepted** or which have been **received and accepted**, to that extent only.

### Other exceptions

- **Land.** Part performance, usually possession plus either payment or substantial improvements, can allow specific performance of an oral land contract in many states.
- **Promissory estoppel** (Restatement § 139): in many states, reasonable and substantial reliance can overcome the defense when injustice can be avoided only by enforcement; others reject it.
- **Restitution.** A party who has performed under an unenforceable oral contract can recover the reasonable value of the benefit conferred, so the statute does not allow unjust enrichment.

### Modifications

A modification of a contract that is within the statute is itself within the statute if the contract as modified falls within it (UCC § 2-209(3)), though an oral modification can be a waiver where the statute is not violated.

### Applying it

Check whether the contract is within a category; whether a signed writing exists and states essential terms (and quantity, for goods); and whether any exception applies. Then check whether the defendant is the one who signed.

@example
title: Custom banners
problem: Buyer orally orders from Printer, a merchant, 400 banners printed with Buyer's company name and logo, for \$3,000. Printer prepares the printing plates and prints 150 banners. Before delivery, Buyer repudiates, pleading the statute of frauds. Printer has no signed writing. Does the statute bar enforcement?
step: Issue: whether the contract is unenforceable for lack of a writing. Rule: a contract for goods of \$500 or more must be in a writing signed by the party to be charged, unless an exception applies (UCC § 2-201).
step: The price is \$3,000, so the contract is within the statute and Buyer signed nothing.
step: Exception: specially manufactured goods. Banners bearing Buyer's name and logo are not suitable for sale to others in the ordinary course of Printer's business. Printer made a substantial beginning before notice of repudiation (plates made and 150 printed).
step: Conclusion: the statute does not bar enforcement. Printer must still prove that a contract was formed, since the statute of frauds concerns only the writing.

@mistakes
- Treating an oral contract within the statute as void; it is only unenforceable against the party who raises the defense.
- Using the "likely" test for the one-year provision instead of whether performance is possible within a year.
- Forgetting that the writing for goods must state a quantity and that recovery is limited to that quantity.
- Applying the merchant confirmation rule to bar the recipient's defense that no contract was made; it only removes the statute defense.
