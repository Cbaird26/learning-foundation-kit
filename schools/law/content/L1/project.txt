@review
### Week by week

**Weeks 1 to 2: formation.** An offer is a manifestation of willingness to bargain that invites assent; advertisements and quotes are usually invitations. Offers end by revocation, rejection, counteroffer, lapse or death. Option contracts and UCC § 2-205 firm offers are irrevocable; unilateral offers cannot be revoked once performance begins. Acceptance is effective on dispatch under the mailbox rule, except for options (receipt), and revocations and rejections are effective on receipt. UCC § 2-207 turns a definite acceptance with additional terms into a contract; between merchants, additional terms are in unless the offer limits acceptance, they materially alter, or objection is made; conflicting terms usually knock out.

**Weeks 3 to 4: consideration and substitutes.** Consideration is a bargained-for exchange; adequacy is not examined. Past consideration, illusory promises and pre-existing duties fail; modifications of goods contracts need good faith only (UCC § 2-209). Promissory estoppel (§ 90) enforces reasonably relied-on promises; restitution prevents unjust enrichment, with the officious-volunteer limit.

**Weeks 5 to 6: defenses.** Minors, incapacity, duress, undue influence and misrepresentation usually make a contract voidable; unconscionability lets the court refuse to enforce the contract or a term, or limit the term (UCC § 2-302; Restatement § 208). Mistake: mutual (basic assumption, material effect, no risk on the complaining party) and unilateral (unconscionability or reason to know). Illegal and policy-violating bargains are unenforceable, with special rules for licensing statutes and covenants not to compete.

**Weeks 7 to 8: writing and meaning.** The statute of frauds covers marriage, one-year contracts, land, executors, goods of \$500 or more and suretyship, with exceptions such as merchant confirmation and specially manufactured goods. The parol evidence rule bars prior and contemporaneous agreements that contradict a final writing and, for a complete integration, additional terms; it never bars proof of fraud, later modifications or interpretation under the jurisdiction's approach. UCC § 2-202 and § 1-303 govern usage and course of dealing.

**Weeks 9 to 10: performance and excuse.** Express conditions require strict compliance; constructive conditions require substantial performance; material breach is judged by the § 241 factors. Repudiation, adequate assurance, perfect tender and cure. Impracticability, casualty to identified goods, and frustration of purpose require an unforeseen basic-assumption event and do not cover mere cost increases.

**Weeks 11 to 12: remedies and third parties.** Expectation damages equal loss in value plus other loss minus cost and loss avoided, limited by foreseeability, certainty and mitigation; UCC cover and market formulas. Intended beneficiaries may sue; assignees take subject to defenses; delegators remain liable.

### Common errors

- Applying the mirror image rule to a sale of goods.
- Saying a defense makes a contract void when it is voidable.
- Skipping the risk-allocation step in mistake and impracticability problems.
- Awarding consequential damages without a foreseeability check.
- Treating a minor breach as grounds for termination.

prompt: Buyer and Seller are merchants. Buyer emails a signed purchase order for 1,000 units of goods at \$18 per unit. Seller emails back a signed acknowledgment that agrees to the quantity, price and delivery date and adds, "All disputes shall be arbitrated." The acknowledgment is not expressly conditional, and Buyer's order does not limit acceptance to its terms. Buyer says nothing. Later Seller refuses to deliver, and Buyer sues in court. Seller moves to compel arbitration and also argues that the statute of frauds bars the suit. Which statement is most accurate?
choice: A contract exists and the arbitration clause is part of it, because both parties are merchants and Buyer did not object.
choice: No contract exists, because Seller's acknowledgment added a term and so was a counteroffer.
choice: A contract exists, but the statute of frauds bars the suit because Seller did not sign Buyer's purchase order.
choice*: A contract exists under UCC § 2-207(1), the arbitration clause is a proposal that is likely excluded as a material alteration, and Seller's own signed acknowledgment stating the quantity satisfies the statute of frauds against Seller.
hint: Start with § 2-207(1), then sort the additional term under § 2-207(2).
hint: The statute of frauds requires a writing signed by the party to be charged. Who is the defendant here?
solution: The acknowledgment is a definite expression of acceptance and not expressly conditional, so it forms a contract on the offer's terms. Between merchants, an additional arbitration clause is a proposal that is excluded if it materially alters the contract, as most courts hold. Seller is the party to be charged and signed a writing stating the quantity, so § 2-201 is satisfied.

Buyer reasonably covers on April 3 by buying 2,000 pounds at \$11.00 per pound, and incurs \$300 in incidental expenses of cover. Buyer paid Seller nothing and saved no expenses. Seller had no reason to know of any other loss. Under the UCC, what are Buyer's damages, in dollars?
answer: 3300
check: (11.00 - 9.50) * 2000 + 300
unit: dollars
hint: Which section gives damages after a reasonable cover purchase?
hint: Multiply the per-pound difference by 2,000 and add the incidental expenses.
solution: Cover damages are cover price minus contract price: (\$11.00 − \$9.50) × 2,000 = \$3,000. Adding \$300 incidental damages gives \$3,300 (UCC §§ 2-712 and 2-715(1)).

@exam self
prompt: Owner signs a written contract with Builder to build a house for \$300,000. The contract has a merger clause and says nothing about the type of flooring. Before signing, Builder orally promised to install hardwood floors. Halfway through construction, Builder says that unless Owner pays an additional \$30,000, with no change in the work and no unforeseen difficulty, Builder will stop. Owner, having no other builder available, signs a writing promising the extra \$30,000. Builder finishes the house, but installs a different brand of plumbing fixtures of equal quality than the contract specified, and installs laminate floors. Owner refuses to pay the extra \$30,000 and also refuses to pay the last \$20,000 of the original price. Write an IRAC analysis of (a) the \$30,000 modification, (b) the flooring promise, and (c) Owner's right to withhold the final \$20,000.
hint: For (a) consider the pre-existing duty rule, § 89 and duress. For (b) consider integration. For (c) consider substantial performance and damages.
hint: Treat each issue separately, in IRAC form, and finish with a short overall conclusion.
solution: (a) Issue: whether Owner is bound to pay the \$30,000. Rule: at common law a modification needs consideration; a promise to do what one is already bound to do is not consideration, unless the modification is fair and equitable in view of unanticipated circumstances (Restatement § 89). Also, a threat to breach in bad faith leaving no reasonable alternative is an improper threat making the agreement voidable for duress. This is a construction contract, so UCC § 2-209 does not apply. Application: Builder gave nothing new and there was no unforeseen difficulty; Builder's threat to quit was a bad-faith threat, and Owner had no alternative. Conclusion: the modification is unenforceable (and voidable for duress); Owner owes only the original price. (b) Issue: whether Owner may prove the oral promise of hardwood floors. Rule: a completely integrated writing bars evidence of prior agreements that add terms; a merger clause is strong evidence of complete integration (parol evidence rule), subject to exceptions such as fraud. Application: the writing has a merger clause and is detailed; flooring is a term that would naturally be included, so the promise is likely barred, unless the writing is found only partially integrated and the promise is consistent, or Owner proves fraud (Builder had no intent to perform). Conclusion: likely excluded, so no breach for failing to install hardwood. (c) Issue: whether the deviations justify withholding \$20,000. Rule: a good-faith substantial performance entitles the builder to the price less damages for the deviations (*Jacob & Youngs v. Kent*); material breach (§ 241 factors) would excuse payment. Application: the plumbing fixtures are of equal quality, so damages are nominal; the flooring deviation exists only if the contract required hardwood, which (b) suggests it did not. Builder completed the house in good faith, and withholding would cause a forfeiture. Conclusion: Owner must pay the \$20,000, less any proven damages for deviations, and not the \$30,000 modification.
rubric: Applies the pre-existing duty rule or § 89 and duress to the modification and reaches a supported conclusion.
rubric: Applies integration, the merger clause and exceptions to the flooring promise.
rubric: Applies substantial performance and § 241 factors, with the correct measure of damages for deviations.
rubric: Keeps the three issues separate, states each rule accurately and gives a final conclusion on amounts owed.
