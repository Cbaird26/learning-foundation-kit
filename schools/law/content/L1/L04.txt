@article
Weeks 1 and 3 covered promises enforced because they are part of a bargain. Some promises are not bargained for yet the law still protects the person who relied on them. Other times there is no promise at all, but one party was enriched at another's expense. These are **promissory estoppel** and **restitution**.

### Promissory estoppel

Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 90(1): a promise which the promisor should reasonably expect to induce action or forbearance on the part of the promisee or a third person, and which does induce such action or forbearance, is binding if **injustice can be avoided only by enforcement of the promise**. The remedy may be limited as justice requires.

Elements:

1. a **promise** (clear and definite enough to be relied on);
2. **reasonable expectation** by the promisor that it would induce action or forbearance;
3. **actual, reasonable reliance** by the promisee; and
4. **injustice** if the promise is not enforced.

In *Ricketts v. Scothorn* (1898), a grandfather gave his granddaughter a note for \$2,000 so that she need not work, and she quit her job. The court held the note enforceable against his estate because she relied on it. By contrast, in *Kirksey v. Kirksey* (1845), a brother-in-law's promise of a place to live and raise her family to a widow who moved a long distance was treated as a gratuity; modern courts applying § 90 are more willing to find reliance in such cases.

Promissory estoppel is not limited to gifts. *Drennan v. Star Paving Co.* (1958) enforced a subcontractor's bid that the general contractor had relied on by using it in its own bid; Restatement § 87(2). *Hoffman v. Red Owl Stores, Inc.* (1965) allowed recovery by a would-be franchisee who sold his bakery and made other changes in reliance on a series of assurances that a franchise would be granted, even though the final terms had not been agreed. The remedy there was **reliance damages**, not the profits of the franchise.

**Charitable subscriptions** and marriage settlements are binding under § 90(2) without proof that the promise induced action. Many courts also enforce them through consideration (the charity's reliance, or mutual pledges).

**Remedies.** The court may award expectation damages, reliance damages or something between, "as justice requires." Courts often award reliance damages for pre-contract negotiation cases and for gratuitous promises.

**Statute of frauds.** Many courts apply § 139 to enforce an otherwise unenforceable oral promise where reliance was reasonable, enforcement is the only way to avoid injustice, and the factors in § 139 are satisfied; other courts reject the doctrine here.

### Restitution

**Restitution** (quasi-contract, unjust enrichment) is a legal fiction used to prevent unjust enrichment where there is no contract. A plaintiff must show:

1. a **benefit conferred** on the defendant by the plaintiff;
2. the defendant's **appreciation or knowledge** of the benefit, and acceptance or retention of it; and
3. circumstances making it **inequitable** for the defendant to retain it without paying for it.

The measure is usually the **reasonable value** of the services or the amount by which the defendant was enriched, not the price a contract would have specified. A person who confers a benefit **officiously** (a **volunteer** or "officious intermeddler") ordinarily cannot recover, because the recipient had no chance to refuse. An exception is an **emergency** in which the recipient is unable to consent. In *Cotnam v. Wisdom* (1907), a physician who treated an unconscious accident victim could recover the reasonable value of services in quasi-contract.

Restitution is also used where a contract is unenforceable (for example, under the statute of frauds or for lack of capacity), and where a **party in breach** has conferred a benefit: such a party can recover the benefit conferred in excess of the loss caused by the breach (Restatement § 374). *Britton v. Turner* (1834) allowed a farm hand who left before the end of a 12-month term to recover the reasonable value of his labor, less the employer's damages. Many states follow this rule, but a **willful** breach can still be a ground for denying or limiting recovery in some courts.

### Putting it together

When asked whether a promise is enforceable, run the checklist: (1) Is it part of a bargained-for exchange? (2) If not, did the promisee reasonably rely to the promisor's knowledge? (3) If no promise, was a benefit conferred under circumstances that make retention unjust? For each, name the basis: **consideration, promissory estoppel, or restitution**, and the likely remedy.

@example
title: The promised lease
problem: Landlord tells Baker, "The corner shop is yours at \$2,000 a month for five years. Just close your current bakery and be ready by September 1." Baker sells the old bakery equipment at a loss of \$6,000. Landlord then leases the shop to someone else. No lease is signed. What are Baker's best claims?
step: Contract: Landlord's statement was a promise, but Baker gave no promise in return, and a five-year lease would be within the statute of frauds anyway, so a contract claim is weak.
step: Promissory estoppel: Landlord's promise was definite enough, Landlord should reasonably have expected Baker to sell the old equipment, Baker did in fact rely, and injustice could be avoided only by providing some relief.
step: Remedy: because the case is a pre-lease reliance claim, a court is likely to award **reliance damages**, such as the \$6,000 loss, not lost profits from the shop.
step: Conclusion: Baker's best basis is promissory estoppel, and the likely recovery is reliance damages.

@mistakes
- Forgetting that reliance must be reasonable and foreseeable to the promisor; an unrequested expense is not reliance.
- Assuming promissory estoppel always yields expectation damages; courts often limit relief to reliance.
- Awarding restitution to a volunteer who conferred an unrequested benefit with no emergency.
- Measuring restitution by the contract price or the plaintiff's cost instead of reasonable value or the defendant's enrichment.
