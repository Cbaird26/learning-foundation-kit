@article
A **contract** is a promise or set of promises the law will enforce. Formation requires **mutual assent** (an offer and an acceptance) and **consideration** or a substitute for it (weeks 3 and 4). This week is about assent.

### The objective theory

Courts do not ask what each party secretly intended. They ask what a reasonable person in the other party's position would conclude from the words and conduct. In *Lucy v. Zehmer* (1954), a seller who wrote out and signed a sale agreement for his farm was bound even though he claimed to be joking, because the buyer reasonably took him seriously. A secret intention not to be bound does not prevent formation.

### The offer

Under Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 24, an **offer** is a manifestation of willingness to enter a bargain, made so that another person is justified in understanding that their assent is invited and will conclude the bargain. The offeror is the "master of the offer": the offeror chooses who may accept, how, and for how long. An offer creates a **power of acceptance** in the offeree.

Words that look like offers often are not:

- **Preliminary negotiations and price quotes** ("Would you take \$50,000?", "I'm asking about \$300 a unit") invite an offer rather than make one.
- **Advertisements** are usually invitations to make offers, because they lack a commitment to sell to everyone who responds and stock is limited. The exception is an advertisement that is definite and specific about quantity and who may accept, for example a "first come, first served" notice, which was held to be an offer in *Lefkowitz v. Great Minneapolis Surplus Store* (1957).
- **Auctions.** Under UCC § 2-328, a sale is **with reserve** unless the goods are expressly put up without reserve. In a sale with reserve the auctioneer may withdraw goods until the hammer falls. In an auction without reserve, once the auctioneer calls for bids on the goods they may not be withdrawn unless no bid is made within a reasonable time.

### Termination of the power of acceptance

An offer ends by:

1. **Revocation** by the offeror before acceptance. Revocation is effective when received by the offeree. Most states also treat the offer as revoked if the offeree learns from a reliable source that the offeror has taken definite action inconsistent with the offer (for instance, selling the item to someone else).
2. **Rejection** by the offeree, effective when received.
3. **Counteroffer.** A counteroffer is a rejection plus a new offer. A mere inquiry ("Would you take less?") is not a rejection.
4. **Lapse** at the stated time or, if none is stated, after a reasonable time.
5. **Death or incapacity** of either party (before acceptance), in most jurisdictions.

### Irrevocable offers

At common law an offeror may revoke even a promise to "keep the offer open for ten days," unless it is an **option contract** supported by consideration. Three variations matter. A **firm offer** under UCC § 2-205 is a signed writing by a merchant giving assurance that it will be held open; it is irrevocable without consideration for the stated period, or a reasonable time if none, but never more than three months. A subcontractor's bid relied on by a general contractor may be irrevocable by promissory estoppel (*Drennan v. Star Paving Co.*, 1958; Restatement § 87(2)). And in a **unilateral contract**, the offeror asks for performance, not a promise, and once the offeree begins performance the offeror may not revoke (Restatement § 45).

### Acceptance

Acceptance is a manifestation of assent to the terms of the offer, made in the manner invited. Under the common law **mirror image rule**, an acceptance that varies the terms is a counteroffer; UCC § 2-207 relaxes this for goods (next week). Silence is generally not acceptance, unless the parties' dealings or the offeree's conduct make it reasonable to treat it so. An offeree must know of the offer to accept it: a person who performs without knowing of a reward offer has not accepted it.

Unless the offer clearly demands one or the other, it is construed as inviting acceptance either by promise or by performance (Restatement § 32; UCC § 2-206(1)(a)). *Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co.* (1893) held that a company's advertised reward for catching influenza after using its product was an offer of a unilateral contract accepted by performance, and the deposit of money showed seriousness.

### Applying it

For each problem ask: (1) Was there an offer, or only an invitation? (2) Was the power of acceptance still alive when the offeree responded? (3) Was the response an acceptance in the manner invited, and did it match the offer?

@example
title: The truck and the counteroffer
problem: On Monday Seller writes to Buyer: "I will sell you my delivery truck for \$18,000. This offer stays open until Friday." Seller received no payment for holding the offer open. On Tuesday Buyer replies: "I'll pay \$16,500." Seller does not respond. On Thursday Buyer writes: "I accept at \$18,000." Is there a contract?
step: Issue: whether Buyer's Thursday message was an effective acceptance. Seller's Monday letter is an offer: it states the goods, a price and an invitation to assent.
step: Rule: a counteroffer operates as a rejection and terminates the power of acceptance. A promise to hold an offer open is revocable unless supported by consideration or made firm by statute.
step: Application: Buyer's Tuesday reply changed the price, so it was a counteroffer, not an inquiry. That ended Buyer's power to accept the original offer. Seller was not paid for an option, and Seller is not described as a merchant, so the firm-offer rule of UCC § 2-205 does not apply; even a firm offer would not survive the offeree's own rejection.
step: Conclusion: no contract. Buyer's Thursday message is itself a new offer that Seller is free to accept or ignore.

@mistakes
- Treating a newspaper advertisement or price list as an offer that binds the seller to every reader.
- Forgetting that a counteroffer terminates the original offer, even if the offer stated that it would stay open.
- Assuming a promise to keep an offer open is always binding; without consideration, a firm-offer statute or reliance, it is revocable.
- Applying a subjective test ("I didn't mean it") instead of the objective theory of assent.
