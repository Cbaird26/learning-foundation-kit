@article
A contract can satisfy offer, acceptance and consideration and still be unenforceable because of a **defense**. Defenses fall into two groups: those that make the contract **voidable** at the option of the protected party, and those that make it **void**, meaning no contract exists. Most defenses discussed here produce voidable contracts, so the protected party may **affirm** or **avoid** (rescind). A party who affirms, or who delays too long after the defense ends, may lose the power to avoid. Defenses are raised by the party who invokes them, and their burden is on that party.

### Capacity

**Minors.** A contract by a person under the age of majority (18 in nearly all states) is voidable at the minor's option. The minor may **disaffirm** at any time before majority and within a reasonable time after reaching it; failure to disaffirm within a reasonable time, or acts showing ratification after majority, can bar avoidance. Under the majority rule, a minor who disaffirms must return whatever consideration remains in the minor's possession, but need not compensate for depreciation, damage or loss of value, and may recover the price paid. A minority of states apply a "benefit" rule that limits recovery to the price less the use value or deterioration. A minor who disaffirms is nonetheless liable in quasi-contract for the **reasonable value of necessaries** (food, clothing, shelter, medical care), not for the contract price. Many states bar disaffirmance in certain settings by statute, and the adult party cannot avoid.

**Mental incapacity.** Under Restatement (Second) § 15, a person's contract is voidable if, because of a mental illness or defect, the person (a) is unable to understand the nature and consequences of the transaction, or (b) is unable to act in a reasonable manner and the other party has reason to know of the condition. A contract made after a court has adjudicated incompetence and appointed a guardian is void. **Intoxication** is treated similarly: the contract is voidable if the other party had reason to know that the intoxication prevented understanding or reasonable action.

### Duress and undue influence

**Physical duress**, where one is compelled by physical force to appear to assent, makes a contract **void**. **Economic or improper-threat duress** makes it **voidable** (§ 175) if assent was induced by an **improper threat** that left the victim **no reasonable alternative**. Threats of crime or tort, a threat of criminal prosecution, a threat to breach a contract in bad faith, or a threat to use civil process in bad faith are examples. Hard bargaining or a threat to do what one has a legal right to do (for example, to refuse to deal) is not duress.

**Undue influence** (§ 177) is unfair persuasion of a person who is under the domination of the persuader or who, by virtue of a relationship, is justified in assuming that the persuader will not act in a manner inconsistent with the person's welfare. Relationships of trust (parent and child, clergy, lawyer, doctor) raise a presumption.

### Misrepresentation

A **misrepresentation** is an assertion that is not in accord with the facts. A contract is voidable by the recipient if the misrepresentation was **fraudulent or material**, and the recipient **justifiably relied** on it (§ 164). A misrepresentation is fraudulent if the maker knows or believes it is false, or lacks the confidence stated, and intends to induce assent. Statements of **opinion** or puffery are not usually actionable, unless the speaker claims special knowledge or holds a position of trust.

**Nondisclosure** is generally not misrepresentation, but there is a duty to disclose in specific situations: to correct a prior statement that is a **half-truth**, where there is a fiduciary relationship, where facts are known to be basic assumptions of the other, and many states require sellers of homes to disclose known latent defects. **Active concealment** (painting over water stains) is treated like an assertion that the condition does not exist.

**Fraud in the inducement** (deceit about facts that induce assent) makes the contract voidable. **Fraud in the factum** (the victim has no reasonable opportunity to know the character or essential terms of the document being signed) makes it void.

### Unconscionability

Under **UCC § 2-302** and Restatement § 208, a court that finds a contract or term unconscionable at the time it was made may refuse to enforce the contract, enforce the remainder without the unconscionable term, or limit the term's application. Courts generally require both **procedural unconscionability** (unfair process: hidden terms, unequal bargaining power, no meaningful choice, high-pressure tactics) and **substantive unconscionability** (oppressively one-sided terms), often on a sliding scale. In *Williams v. Walker-Thomas Furniture Co.* (D.C. Cir. 1965), a clause keeping every prior purchase as security until all balances were paid, applied against a low-income customer, was held a basis for finding unconscionability. Unconscionability is for the court, not the jury. The Federal Arbitration Act preempts state rules that disfavor arbitration, such as the rule struck down in *AT&T Mobility LLC v. Concepcion* (2011), but generally applicable defenses such as unconscionability still apply to arbitration clauses.

### Applying it

For every defense ask: who may raise it, whether the contract is void or voidable, whether the party has affirmed it, and what the remedy is (rescission and restitution).

@example
title: The supplier's ultimatum
problem: Mfr agrees to deliver 500 specialized parts to Buyer on June 1 at \$40 each. On May 28, Mfr, which has no legal excuse, tells Buyer it will not deliver unless Buyer signs a new agreement at \$52 each. Buyer, who has a customer penalty clause due June 3 and cannot get the parts elsewhere in time, signs under protest. Later Buyer refuses to pay more than \$40. May Buyer avoid the modification?
step: Issue: whether Buyer's assent to the new price was induced by economic duress. Rule: a contract is voidable for duress if assent was induced by an improper threat that left the victim no reasonable alternative (Restatement § 175).
step: Application: Mfr's threat was to breach an existing contract without excuse, a threat that is improper because it violates the duty of good faith. Buyer had no reasonable alternative, since substitute parts could not be had in time and the penalty was imminent.
step: Buyer signed under protest and objected promptly, so Buyer did not affirm the modification.
step: Conclusion: the modification is voidable at Buyer's option. The original \$40 price remains enforceable. Under UCC § 2-209 the modification would also fail the good-faith test.

@mistakes
- Calling every defense "void"; most are voidable and can be ratified.
- Applying the minor's necessaries rule to say the minor owes the contract price; the liability is for reasonable value.
- Treating hard bargaining or refusal to deal as duress; there must be an improper threat and no reasonable alternative.
- Requiring only substantive unfairness for unconscionability; most courts need procedural and substantive elements.

@practice self
prompt: Seller tells Buyer, "The house has never had termite damage," knowing it has had extensive damage that Seller covered with new paneling. Buyer, who does not inspect, buys the house. Buyer later finds the damage. Write an IRAC analysis of whether Buyer may avoid the contract.
hint: Identify whether the statement was an assertion of fact, an opinion, or a nondisclosure.
hint: Walk through falsity, fraud or materiality, inducement and justifiable reliance.
solution: Issue: whether Buyer may avoid the contract for misrepresentation. Rule: a contract is voidable by a party whose assent was induced by a misrepresentation that was fraudulent or material, if the party justifiably relied on it (Restatement § 164). A statement is fraudulent if the maker knows it is false and intends to induce assent. Active concealment is treated as an assertion. Application: "No termite damage" is a statement of existing fact, not opinion. It was false and Seller knew it, so it was fraudulent, and the covering paneling is also active concealment. It was material to a house purchase and likely induced assent. Buyer's reliance is likely justified: a buyer may rely on an assertion of fact even though an inspection would have uncovered the truth, unless the buyer actually knew of the falsity or the falsity was obvious. Conclusion: Buyer may avoid the contract (rescission and restitution of the price) and may also claim damages for deceit.
rubric: States the misrepresentation rule with fraud or materiality, inducement and justifiable reliance.
rubric: Classifies the statement as a fact and notes the concealment.
rubric: Addresses justifiable reliance despite Buyer's failure to inspect.
rubric: States remedy (rescission and restitution, and possibly damages).

@quiz mc
prompt: A furniture retailer sells a low-income customer a \$400 stereo on installment credit. The pre-printed contract, which the customer was told to sign immediately and which the retailer would not negotiate, contains in fine print a clause keeping every item the customer ever bought from the retailer as security until all balances are paid. The customer, who has limited education, does not read it. The customer misses a payment on the stereo. Which is the best analysis of the clause?

choice: Unenforceable only if the retailer's prices were unreasonably high; one-sidedness of other terms is irrelevant.
choice*: A court may refuse to enforce the clause under UCC § 2-302 if it finds both unfair surprise or lack of meaningful choice and oppressively one-sided terms at the time of contracting.
choice: The whole contract is automatically void and the customer may keep the stereo without paying.
hint: Unconscionability has two components. Identify the facts that support each.
hint: What may a court do under UCC § 2-302 when it finds a term unconscionable?
solution: Fine print, no negotiation and a customer with limited education suggest procedural unconscionability; a clause cross-securing all past purchases is oppressively one-sided.
