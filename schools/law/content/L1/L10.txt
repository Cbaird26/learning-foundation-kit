@article
Normally a party who promises performance bears the risk that it will become harder or less valuable. A promisor must pay damages for breach even if performance is unexpectedly costly. This lesson covers the narrow doctrines that **discharge** a duty because of events after the contract was made.

### Impossibility and impracticability

At common law, a destroyed subject matter made performance impossible: in *Taylor v. Caldwell* (1863) a music hall rented for concerts burned before the first concert, and the parties were excused. Modern law extends this to **impracticability**. Restatement (Second) § 261: where, after the contract is made, a party's performance is made impracticable **without his fault** by the occurrence of an event the **non-occurrence of which was a basic assumption** on which the contract was made, the duty is discharged, **unless the language or circumstances indicate the contrary**.

Elements:

1. an **event** occurring after contract formation;
2. **non-occurrence was a basic assumption** of the contract (the risk was not allocated to the promisor, and the event was not reasonably foreseeable in a way that the parties would have addressed it);
3. performance is made **impracticable** (extreme and unreasonable difficulty, expense, injury or loss), not merely more expensive or less profitable; and
4. the party **was not at fault** and did not assume the risk.

Increased cost alone is usually not enough. In *Transatlantic Financing Corp. v. United States* (D.C. Cir. 1966), a carrier whose ship could not use the Suez Canal after its closure had to take the longer route around Africa, and was not excused, because the extra cost was not extreme enough and the risk of closure was foreseeable. In *Mineral Park Land Co. v. Howard* (1916), the cost of performance was ten to twelve times greater than normal, which was held to excuse the party that had promised to take all the gravel it needed from the landowner's land. Personal service contracts are discharged by the **death or incapacity** of the performer (§ 262). **Supervening illegality** discharges contracts (§ 264).

### UCC

- **§ 2-613 (casualty to identified goods).** If the contract requires goods identified when it is made and the goods suffer casualty without fault of either party **before the risk of loss passes to the buyer**, then if the loss is **total** the contract is avoided; if the loss is **partial**, the buyer may inspect and choose to avoid the contract or accept the goods with an allowance.
- **§ 2-615 (excuse by failure of presupposed conditions).** Unless the seller has assumed a greater obligation, delay in delivery or non-delivery is **not a breach** if performance has been made **impracticable** by the occurrence of a contingency whose non-occurrence was a basic assumption, or by good-faith compliance with a **governmental regulation or order** (even if it later proves invalid). If only a **part** of the seller's capacity is affected, the seller must **allocate** production and deliveries among its customers in any manner that is **fair and reasonable**, and must **seasonably notify** the buyer of the delay or non-delivery and of the estimated quota. The buyer may then terminate or modify (§ 2-616).
- A mere rise in market price, or a collapse in market price, does not excuse (Official Comment 4).

### Frustration of purpose

Where performance remains possible but the **principal purpose** of the contract, known to both parties, is **substantially frustrated** by a supervening event whose non-occurrence was a basic assumption, the duty is discharged (§ 265). In *Krell v. Henry* (1903), a room was rented for two days to view the coronation procession; the procession was cancelled because of the king's illness. The renter was excused because viewing the procession was the foundation of the contract known to both parties. A drop in the profitability of a venture is not frustration.

### Limits: foreseeability and risk allocation

The doctrines do not apply where the risk was **foreseeable** and could have been provided for, or where the contract allocates it to a party, as in a **fixed-price** contract or an express **force majeure** clause. A party relying on a force majeure clause must show the event fits the clause as written. Courts differ in how they treat events that are foreseeable in kind.

### Effects of discharge

Both parties are relieved of unperformed duties. A party that has conferred a benefit may recover in **restitution** (§ 272), and a court may grant relief for reliance expenditures as justice requires.

@example
title: The cancelled festival
problem: Business rents a ground-floor shop for three days for \$6,000 to sell souvenirs along a festival route. Both parties know the festival is the reason for the rental. The city cancels the festival because of an unforeseen emergency. The shop is available, and the landlord demands payment. Business has paid a \$2,000 deposit. What are the parties' rights?
step: Issue: whether the cancelled festival discharges Business's duty. Rule: a duty is discharged if its principal purpose, known to both parties, is substantially frustrated by an event whose non-occurrence was a basic assumption (Restatement § 265).
step: Application: the purpose was known to both and was the foundation of the rental. The city's cancellation was not foreseeable and the contract did not allocate that risk. Performance is still possible, but its value to Business is destroyed.
step: Effects: both parties are discharged from unperformed duties. Business may recover the \$2,000 deposit in restitution, though Landlord may offset reasonable expenses incurred in reliance.
step: Conclusion: Business is excused from paying the rest and may recover the deposit, less any proper offset.

@mistakes
- Excusing performance merely because it became more costly or less profitable.
- Confusing impracticability (performance is extremely hard) with frustration (performance is easy, but the purpose is gone).
- Ignoring that foreseeability and express risk allocation limit the defenses.
- Forgetting the seller's duties under UCC § 2-615 to allocate fairly and give notice.
