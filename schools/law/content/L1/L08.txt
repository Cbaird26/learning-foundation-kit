### Interpretation

Courts interpret a contract to give effect to the parties' **objectively manifested** meaning. Two approaches compete. Under the **plain meaning** (four corners) approach associated with Williston, if the words are unambiguous on their face, a court will not consider extrinsic evidence of meaning. Under the **contextual** approach associated with Corbin, a court will consider evidence of the circumstances to decide whether the words are reasonably susceptible to the meaning offered, even if the text looks clear. *Pacific Gas & Electric Co. v. G.W. Thomas Drayage & Rigging Co.* (1968) adopted the contextual view in California, holding that a court may look at extrinsic evidence to determine whether the language of an indemnity clause was reasonably susceptible to the meaning urged. Many states retain a strict plain meaning rule, so this is a point that varies by jurisdiction.

Interpretation is a question of law for the judge when it depends on the words alone, and for the fact-finder when it depends on credibility of extrinsic evidence. In *Frigaliment Importing Co. v. B.N.S. International Sales Group* (1960), a buyer ordered "chicken" and received stewing chickens; the court looked to trade usage, the parties' negotiations and the context, and held the buyer had not proved that "chicken" meant only young chickens, because the buyer bore the burden of proving its meaning.

**Canons of construction** (aids when meaning is uncertain):

- Read the contract as a whole, and give effect to every term.
- **Specific** terms control **general** terms; **handwritten** terms control **typed**, and typed control **printed form** terms.
- **Contra proferentem**: ambiguity is construed against the drafter, usually as a last resort, and especially in standard-form contracts.
- Words are given their ordinary meaning unless a technical meaning is intended; negotiated terms control over standard terms.

**UCC priority** (§ 1-303(e)): when express terms, **course of performance** (the parties' conduct in this contract), **course of dealing** (previous transactions) and **usage of trade** conflict, they prevail in that order: express terms, then course of performance, then course of dealing, then usage of trade. Whenever reasonable, they are construed as consistent.

### The parol evidence rule

The **parol evidence rule** is a rule of substantive law: when parties have reduced their agreement to a **final written expression (an integration)**, evidence of **prior agreements or contemporaneous oral agreements** may not be used to **contradict** its terms; and if the writing is **completely integrated**, such evidence may not be used even to **add consistent terms**.

Steps:

1. **Is there a writing intended as a final expression** of at least some of the terms? If not, the rule does not apply.
2. **Is the writing completely or partially integrated?** A **completely integrated** writing is intended as the full and exclusive statement of the agreement. A **partially integrated** writing is final as to the terms it contains but not exclusive. A **merger clause** ("this is the entire agreement") is strong evidence of complete integration, but courts still examine it, and it does not by itself bar evidence of fraud.
3. **Is the evidence barred?** Evidence of a prior agreement that **contradicts** the writing is barred if the writing is any integration. Evidence of a **consistent additional term** is barred if the writing is completely integrated and allowed if only partially integrated. Courts decide completeness under either the four-corners approach or the Corbin approach (whether the term would naturally have been included in the writing).

**UCC § 2-202** is more liberal: a writing intended as final may be **explained or supplemented** by course of performance, course of dealing and usage of trade (even without a finding of ambiguity), and by evidence of **consistent additional terms** unless the court finds that the writing was intended as a complete and exclusive statement of the terms.

### What the rule does not bar

- Evidence of **fraud, duress, mistake, illegality, lack of consideration** or other defenses to formation.
- Evidence of a **condition precedent to the contract's taking effect**, as opposed to a condition within it.
- Evidence of **ambiguity** and to **interpret** terms (the amount depends on the jurisdiction's approach).
- Evidence of **subsequent** agreements or modifications (the rule bars only prior and contemporaneous).
- Evidence to support **reformation** for mistake.
- Evidence of **collateral agreements** that would not naturally be included in the writing.

@example
title: The merger clause
problem: Seller sells her restaurant to Buyer in a signed 5-page agreement stating the price, the equipment included and "This agreement is the entire agreement between the parties." Buyer later sues, claiming that Seller orally promised before signing to include the recipe book. The written agreement does not mention recipes. Is Buyer's evidence admissible?
step: Issue: whether evidence of a prior oral promise may add a term to the written contract. Rule: if a writing is a complete integration, evidence of prior agreements is not admitted to add or vary terms.
step: Application: the agreement was detailed and signed, listed the equipment included, and had a merger clause. That is strong evidence that it was intended as a complete and exclusive statement.
step: Buyer's evidence would add a term to a writing that is silent on recipes. Buyer might argue that the term was collateral or would naturally be omitted, but a recipe book for a restaurant sold with its equipment would naturally be included in a detailed list of what is sold.
step: Conclusion: the evidence is likely barred. Exceptions such as fraud would need independent proof, since the facts show only an unkept promise.

@mistakes
- Treating the parol evidence rule as a rule against all oral testimony; it applies only to prior and contemporaneous agreements and has many exceptions.
- Applying it to a later oral modification, which it does not bar.
- Treating a merger clause as conclusive or as a bar to proof of fraud.
- Forgetting UCC § 2-202's allowance for course of performance, course of dealing and usage of trade even where the writing appears unambiguous.
