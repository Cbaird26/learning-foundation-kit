### The mailbox rule

Under *Adams v. Lindsell* (1818), an offer by mail was accepted at the moment the acceptance was posted, even though the offeror had not yet received it. Today the **mailbox rule** states that an acceptance is effective on **dispatch** if it is properly addressed and sent by a medium authorized by the offer or reasonable under the circumstances (Restatement (Second) § 63(a)). The offeror is the master of the offer and may avoid the rule by stating that acceptance is effective only on receipt.

Limits on the rule:

- **Option contracts.** Acceptance of an option is effective only on **receipt** (§ 63(b)), because the offeree is protected by the option and should bear the risk of transmission.
- **Revocation and rejection** are effective on receipt, not dispatch. An offeror's revocation mailed before the offeree mails an acceptance does not stop the contract unless it reaches the offeree first.
- **Rejection followed by acceptance.** If the offeree mails a rejection and then an acceptance, the acceptance is effective only if it reaches the offeror before the rejection does (§ 40); otherwise the offeror may rely on the rejection.
- A lost or delayed acceptance still forms the contract if it was properly dispatched (Restatement § 63), unless the offer required receipt; an offeror who wants protection can make acceptance effective only on receipt.

### UCC § 2-206: how to accept in a sale of goods

Unless the offer unambiguously requires otherwise, it invites acceptance in any manner and by any medium reasonable in the circumstances. An order or offer to buy goods for prompt shipment invites acceptance by a **prompt promise to ship or by prompt shipment of conforming or nonconforming goods**. A shipment of nonconforming goods is both an acceptance and a breach, unless the seller seasonably notifies the buyer that the shipment is offered only as an accommodation, in which case it is a counteroffer.

### The battle of the forms: § 2-207

At common law the mirror image rule made every variation a counteroffer, and the "last shot" principle meant that whoever sent the last form, and was followed by the other party's performance, won. Section 2-207 abolishes this for goods.

**§ 2-207(1)** A definite and seasonable expression of acceptance, or a written confirmation, operates as an acceptance even though it states additional or different terms, **unless acceptance is expressly made conditional on the offeror's assent to those terms**. Courts read "expressly conditional" narrowly: it must clearly show that the offeree is unwilling to proceed without the offeror's agreement to the new terms.

**§ 2-207(2)** The additional terms are proposals for addition to the contract. Between **merchants** they become part of the contract unless:

1. the offer expressly limits acceptance to the terms of the offer;
2. they **materially alter** it (a change that would result in surprise or hardship if incorporated without express awareness; examples in the Official Comments include a clause negating standard warranties, and in most courts an arbitration clause or a clause shortening limitations periods unreasonably); or
3. the offeror has already objected, or objects within a reasonable time after notice.

If either party is a non-merchant, additional terms remain proposals that the offeror need not accept.

**Different terms** (conflicting rather than additional) are treated differently by courts. The majority **knockout rule** drops both conflicting terms and fills the gap with UCC default terms. A minority treats different terms like additional terms under (2); another view lets the offeror's term prevail.

**§ 2-207(3)** If the writings do not form a contract (for instance because the acceptance was expressly conditional) but the parties' **conduct** recognizes a contract (shipment and acceptance of goods), the contract consists of the terms on which the writings agree, plus the UCC's supplementary terms (gap fillers). Neither party's disputed terms apply.

### A reliable sequence

1. Is there a **definite expression of acceptance**? If not (for example, the quantity is different or the form just says "we will consider"), there may be no contract by writings.
2. Is it **expressly conditional**? If yes, no contract by writings; look to conduct under (3).
3. If not conditional, a contract exists on the offer's terms. Sort the additional terms under (2): are both parties merchants, is there an express limit, is it a material alteration, was there an objection?
4. For conflicting terms, apply the knockout rule in most courts.

@example
title: The purchase order and the acknowledgment
problem: Buyer, a merchant, sends a purchase order for 1,000 widgets at \$12 each, delivery by June 1. Seller, a merchant, returns an acknowledgment form that agrees to the quantity, price and delivery date and adds: "Seller disclaims all implied warranties." The acknowledgment does not say it is conditional on Buyer's assent. Buyer says nothing and Seller ships. Is the disclaimer part of the contract?
step: Issue: whether the added warranty disclaimer became a term of a contract formed by the exchanged forms. Rule: § 2-207(1) makes a definite expression of acceptance effective despite additional terms unless expressly conditional on assent to them.
step: The acknowledgment matches on subject, quantity, price and delivery and is not expressly conditional, so a contract is formed on the terms of the purchase order.
step: Under § 2-207(2) both parties are merchants, so the disclaimer is a proposal that becomes part of the contract unless an exception applies. A disclaimer of implied warranties is the Official Comments' own example of a term that materially alters the contract, because it would surprise the buyer.
step: Conclusion: a contract exists and the disclaimer is not part of it; Buyer keeps the UCC's implied warranties. Buyer's silence is not assent to the proposal.

@mistakes
- Using the mirror image rule or the last-shot rule for a sale of goods instead of § 2-207.
- Missing that § 2-207(2)(b) screens out material alterations even between merchants; "between merchants" does not mean additional terms automatically win.
- Applying the mailbox rule to option contracts, rejections or revocations.
- Treating different (conflicting) terms as additional terms; most courts knock out both conflicting terms.
