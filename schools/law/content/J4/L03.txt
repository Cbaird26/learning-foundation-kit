@article
Authors do not pile up sentences at random. They arrange ideas in a pattern called **text structure**, and they do it for a reason called the **author's purpose**. Understanding both helps you read faster and think about *why* a text is written the way it is.

### Five common structures

- **Description**: details about a person, place, thing or idea. Signals: for example, such as, looks like, near.
- **Sequence (chronological)**: events or steps in time order. Signals: first, next, then, finally, in 1850.
- **Compare and contrast**: similarities and differences. Signals: both, similarly, however, on the other hand, unlike.
- **Cause and effect**: why something happens and what results. Signals: because, as a result, therefore, consequently.
- **Problem and solution**: a difficulty and ways to fix it. Signals: the problem is, one solution, to solve this, challenge.

Real texts often mix structures. Ask which pattern organizes the *whole* passage and which are used in single paragraphs.

### Author's purpose

Most texts have one main purpose, often remembered as **PIE** (persuade, inform, entertain), with a fourth purpose of **explaining** how something works or how to do it.

- To **persuade**: the author wants you to believe or do something. Look for opinions backed by reasons, strong word choices and calls to action.
- To **inform**: the author gives facts so you can learn. The tone is neutral.
- To **entertain**: the author wants you to enjoy a story, humor or vivid description.
- To **explain**: the author teaches a process or concept step by step.

Many texts have more than one purpose, but one usually dominates.

### How structure serves purpose

Structure is a tool. A writer who wants to persuade may choose **problem and solution**: first make you feel the problem, then offer the fix. A writer teaching a recipe uses **sequence** so you can follow along. A writer warning about a health risk might use **cause and effect** to show consequences. A **compare and contrast** structure helps an author inform about two things or argue that one is better.

When you analyze a text, state the structure, give a signal word or sentence as evidence, and explain how that structure helps the author achieve the purpose.

@example
title: Naming structure and purpose
problem: Read: "Our school's lunch line is a problem. Students wait twenty minutes and then have only ten minutes to eat. One solution is to open a second serving window. If the school does this, students will have time to finish their meals and return to class focused." Identify the structure and the author's purpose.
step: Look for signal words: "problem," "One solution." These are problem-and-solution signals.
step: The text first names a problem with details (twenty-minute wait, ten minutes to eat) and then proposes a fix.
step: The purpose is to persuade: the author wants the school to open a second window and says what will happen "if the school does this."
step: Explanation: the structure supports the purpose because readers first feel the problem and then see the solution as a reasonable answer.
@mistakes
- Judging structure by the topic instead of the pattern of ideas and signal words.
- Calling every text "to inform" when the author is clearly trying to persuade through opinions and calls to action.
- Naming a purpose without explaining how the writing shows it.
