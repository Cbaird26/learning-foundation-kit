@article
Truth tables decide validity, but they grow exponentially and they do not explain *why* a conclusion follows. A **derivation** (or proof) shows the "why": it moves from the premises to the conclusion in small steps, each justified by a rule that cannot lead from truth to falsehood. The system is called **natural deduction** because its rules mirror ordinary reasoning.

### Anatomy of a derivation

Write the premises first, each labelled as a premise. Every later line is a statement plus a **justification**: the name of a rule and the line numbers it uses. The last line must be the conclusion, depending on no assumptions except the premises.

### Basic rules

| Rule | From | Infer |
|---|---|---|
| Modus ponens (MP) | $P \to Q$, $P$ | $Q$ |
| Modus tollens (MT) | $P \to Q$, $\neg Q$ | $\neg P$ |
| Hypothetical syllogism (HS) | $P \to Q$, $Q \to R$ | $P \to R$ |
| Disjunctive syllogism (DS) | $P \lor Q$, $\neg P$ | $Q$ |
| Conjunction (Conj) | $P$, $Q$ | $P \land Q$ |
| Simplification (Simp) | $P \land Q$ | $P$ (or $Q$) |
| Addition (Add) | $P$ | $P \lor Q$ for any $Q$ |

In Fitch-style textbooks these appear under other names ($\to$E, $\land$I, $\land$E, $\lor$I), but each rule is the same idea. Because each rule is a valid form, every line of the derivation is *guaranteed* true whenever the premises are, which means a correct derivation establishes validity.

### Conditional proof

To prove a conditional $P \to Q$, **assume** $P$ temporarily, derive $Q$ inside that assumption, then **discharge** the assumption and conclude $P \to Q$. The assumption is not a premise: once you discharge it, you may not use lines from inside the subproof.

### Proof by contradiction (reductio)

To prove $\neg P$, assume $P$ and derive a **contradiction**, a statement and its negation ($R$ and $\neg R$). Since the assumption leads to absurdity, discharge it and conclude $\neg P$. Reductio is how arguments of the form "if that were true, we would have a contradiction" are made precise. Also, from a contradiction anything follows, which is why one is a fatal flaw in a set of premises.

### Strategy

1. Look at the conclusion. If it is a conditional, try conditional proof; if a negation, try reductio.
2. Work forward from the premises, using MP and MT when a conditional and one of its parts is available.
3. Work backward: ask what line would give the conclusion in one step, then how to get that line.
4. Check each justification: are the cited lines really of the forms the rule requires?

### Derivations and truth tables

A derivation can only show that an argument *is* valid. Failing to find a derivation shows nothing; to show invalidity, use a counterexample row from a truth table. In the propositional system covered here, every valid form has a derivation and every derivable form is valid, but searching for a proof can take insight.

### Where this shows up

Proofs train the habit of making every step explicit. The same habit is what lawyers do in a closely reasoned brief ("because X, Y; because Y, Z") and what LSAT questions reward when you chain conditional rules.

@example
title: A short derivation
problem: Derive $R$ from the premises $P \to Q$, $Q \to R$ and $P$.
step: Line 1: $P \to Q$ (premise). Line 2: $Q \to R$ (premise). Line 3: $P$ (premise).
step: Line 4: $Q$, by modus ponens from lines 1 and 3.
step: Line 5: $R$, by modus ponens from lines 2 and 4. The last line is the conclusion, so the derivation is complete.

@example
title: Conditional proof
problem: Prove $P \to R$ from the premises $P \to Q$ and $Q \to R$ without using hypothetical syllogism.
step: Premises: line 1 is $P \to Q$ and line 2 is $Q \to R$. Assume $P$ as line 3.
step: Line 4: $Q$, by modus ponens from lines 1 and 3. Line 5: $R$, by modus ponens from lines 2 and 4.
step: Discharge the assumption: line 6, $P \to R$, by conditional proof from the subproof running from line 3 to line 5. The assumption $P$ is no longer in force.

@mistakes
- Using a line from inside a closed subproof after the assumption has been discharged.
- Running modus ponens backward: from $P \to Q$ and $Q$ inferring $P$.
- Citing the wrong lines or no lines for a justification.
- Assuming something and forgetting to discharge it, so the conclusion depends on an unproven assumption.

@quiz num
prompt: For the form "$P \lor Q$; $Q \to R$; therefore $R$", how many of the eight rows (values of $P$, $Q$, $R$) make both premises true and the conclusion false?
answer: 1
check: sum(1 for p in (True, False) for q in (True, False) for r in (True, False) if (p or q) and ((not q) or r) and not r)
hint: The conclusion is false when $R$ is false.
hint: With $R$ false, $Q \to R$ requires $Q$ false, and then $P \lor Q$ requires $P$ true.
solution: Set $R$ false. For $Q \to R$ to be true, $Q$ must be false. For $P \lor Q$ to be true, $P$ must be true. The single row $P = T$, $Q = F$, $R = F$ is a counterexample, so the form is invalid and has no derivation.
