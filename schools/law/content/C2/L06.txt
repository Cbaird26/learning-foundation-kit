@article
Once you can translate conditionals, you can transform them. Three relatives of $P \to Q$ appear again and again, and only one of them says the same thing.

### Converse, inverse, contrapositive

Starting from "If it is a rose, then it is a flower":

| Name | Form | Example | Equivalent to the original? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Original | $P \to Q$ | If rose, then flower | |
| **Converse** | $Q \to P$ | If flower, then rose | no |
| **Inverse** | $\neg P \to \neg Q$ | If not rose, then not flower | no |
| **Contrapositive** | $\neg Q \to \neg P$ | If not flower, then not rose | **yes** |

The contrapositive is equivalent to the original: both are false only when $P$ is true and $Q$ is false. The converse and inverse are equivalent to each other, but not to the original. The example shows it: roses are flowers, yet not all flowers are roses.

### Valid and invalid reasoning with conditionals

From $P \to Q$:

- **Modus ponens** (affirm the antecedent): $P$, so $Q$. Valid.
- **Modus tollens** (deny the consequent): $\neg Q$, so $\neg P$. Valid; it is modus ponens applied to the contrapositive.
- **Affirming the consequent**: $Q$, so $P$. **Invalid.** It treats a necessary condition as sufficient.
- **Denying the antecedent**: $\neg P$, so $\neg Q$. **Invalid.** It also treats a sufficient condition as necessary.

In a rule like "If the contract is for land, it must be in writing," finding that a contract is in writing tells you nothing about whether it is for land (affirming the consequent). Finding that a contract is not for land tells you nothing about whether it must be in writing (denying the antecedent).

### Negating a conditional

A conditional is false in exactly one case: the antecedent is true and the consequent is false. So

$$\neg(P \to Q) \equiv P \land \neg Q.$$

The negation of "If you study, you pass" is "You study and you do not pass", not "If you study, you do not pass" and not "If you do not study, you pass". This matters in disputes: to refute a rule, you give a case where the antecedent holds and the consequent fails (a counterexample).

### Chains of conditionals

Conditionals chain: from $P \to Q$ and $Q \to R$ we may conclude $P \to R$ (hypothetical syllogism). Combining chaining with contraposition lets you move in both directions. If $A \to B$ and $B \to C$, then contraposing gives $\neg C \to \neg B$ and $\neg B \to \neg A$, so $\neg C \to \neg A$. Many puzzle-style questions reduce to writing each rule, its contrapositive, and chaining.

### "Only" and "unless" revisited

"Only the owner may sign" means signer $\to$ owner. Its contrapositive: if not the owner, then not a signer. For "$P$ unless $Q$", $\neg Q \to P$, the contrapositive is $\neg P \to Q$: if $P$ fails, then $Q$ must have happened.

### Two warnings

Natural language sometimes invites the biconditional reading ("If you finish your dinner, you get dessert" is usually meant as "if and only if"). In a formal setting, translate what is actually said and flag the ambiguity. And never treat a statistical tendency ("if it is a Tuesday, the line is long") as a strict conditional.

@example
title: Contrapositive and negation of a rule
problem: Rule: "If a vehicle is a motorcycle, then it requires a helmet." Write its contrapositive, converse and negation.
step: Let $M$: motorcycle, $H$: requires a helmet. The rule is $M \to H$.
step: Contrapositive: $\neg H \to \neg M$. If a vehicle does not require a helmet, it is not a motorcycle. This is equivalent to the rule.
step: Converse: $H \to M$. If a vehicle requires a helmet, it is a motorcycle. This is not equivalent, because mopeds or bicycles in some places may require helmets too.
step: Negation: $M \land \neg H$. There is a motorcycle that does not require a helmet.

@example
title: Spotting the fallacy
problem: "If the defendant was at the scene, her car would be on the footage. Her car is on the footage. So she was at the scene." What is wrong with this form?
step: Write it: $S \to F$; $F$; therefore $S$.
step: That is affirming the consequent, which is invalid.
step: The car could be on the footage because someone else drove it. Take $S$ false and $F$ true, which makes both premises true and the conclusion false.

@mistakes
- Treating the converse or the inverse as equivalent to the original.
- Negating "if $P$ then $Q$" as "if $P$ then not $Q$".
- Forgetting to flip and negate both parts when forming the contrapositive.
- Accepting modus tollens in the wrong direction by denying the antecedent.

hint: The new antecedent is the negation of "it must be in writing".

The choice "If a contract must be in writing, then it is for the sale of land" is the converse, and the choice "If a contract is not for the sale of land, then it need not be in writing" is the inverse.

@practice num
prompt: In how many of the four rows (values of $P$ and $Q$) do $P \to Q$ and its converse $Q \to P$ have different truth values?
answer: 2
check: sum(1 for p in (True, False) for q in (True, False) if ((not p) or q) != ((not q) or p))
hint: Compare the two columns row by row.
hint: They differ when exactly one of $P$, $Q$ is true.
solution: In the rows TF and FT the two statements differ (one is true and the other is false). In TT and FF they agree. So they differ in 2 rows.

@quiz mc
prompt: "If the alarm was set, the guard was notified. The guard was not notified." What follows?
choice*: The alarm was not set.
choice: The alarm was set.
choice: Nothing follows, because this is affirming the consequent.
choice: The guard was notified.
hint: Which part of the conditional has been denied?
hint: Take the contrapositive of the rule.
solution: From $A \to G$ and $\neg G$, modus tollens gives $\neg A$. The alarm was not set. It is not an instance of affirming the consequent, since the consequent was denied.
