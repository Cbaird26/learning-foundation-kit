@project
title: Argument audit
overview: You will audit a supplied 1,200-word op-ed from start to finish. You map its premises and conclusion, translate its key conditional claims into logic, test whether those claims support the conclusion, and write a 1,000-word critique of its assumptions and flaws. The result shows the full toolkit of the course applied to one realistic piece of persuasion.
goal: Reconstruct the op-ed's argument in standard form, with unstated premises supplied charitably.
goal: Translate at least four conditional claims into propositional logic and test the resulting argument forms for validity.
goal: Name the flaws and necessary assumptions precisely and evaluate the causal claim and the analogy.
goal: Write a clear, fair, 1,000-word critique that a reader who has not seen the op-ed could follow.
step: Read the op-ed twice. On the first pass, mark the main conclusion and every sentence that seems to be offered as a reason. On the second, note indicator words, the sub-conclusions (claims that are supported and then used as premises) and anything that sounds like rhetoric rather than support.
step: Write the argument in standard form: numbered premises, sub-conclusions marked, and the main conclusion last. List any unstated premises you had to add and say why each is the least controversial link.
step: Find at least four conditional claims (look for "if", "only if", "unless", "whenever", "no ... without"). Translate each with a symbolization key, write its contrapositive, and note any place where the author treats a conditional as if it were its converse.
step: Choose the argument that links the author's conditional claims to the conclusion and test its form: build a truth table or a derivation, and state whether it is valid. Then say whether the premises are actually true or well supported.
step: Evaluate the causal claim and the argument from analogy: list alternative explanations for the cause claim, and similarities and relevant differences for the analogy.
step: List every informal fallacy you can defend, quoting the sentence and explaining the gap between premise and conclusion. Drop any label you cannot defend.
step: State the one or two necessary assumptions the whole argument depends on, with the negation test applied to each.
step: Write the critique (about 1,000 words): a short fair summary, the structural analysis, the strongest flaws, the necessary assumptions, and an overall verdict about how much support the conclusion gets. Say what evidence would make the argument stronger.
material: **Op-ed: "Close Downtown to Cars" (written for this course; all places, people and figures are invented).** The paragraphs are numbered for reference.

[1] Every Saturday morning I walk through the center of Harlow Bay, and every Saturday I see the same thing: a handsome historic downtown choked with idling cars, with pedestrians squeezed onto narrow sidewalks. It does not have to be this way. The Harlow Bay city council should close Main Street and the four blocks around it to private cars, permanently, starting next spring.

[2] The idea is not radical. Cities around the world have done it. Two years ago the city of Ostrand closed its main shopping street to cars, and in the first year retail sales on that street rose 18 percent. Anyone who thinks pedestrianization hurts business should look at Ostrand. If it works there, it will work here.

[3] Let me explain why. If a street is closed to cars, then more people walk along it. If more people walk along it, then shops see more customers. And if shops see more customers, they earn more. So closing the street to cars makes shops earn more. It is as simple as that.

[4] Some merchants object that customers will stop coming if they cannot park outside the door. But merchants have always said this about every change. Last year the Harlow Bay Merchants Association opposed the new bike racks, and their predictions of disaster were laughably wrong. Their opposition to closing Main Street deserves the same respect. Besides, the association's president owns a parking garage on Front Street, so of course she wants more drivers downtown.

[5] A downtown is like a shopping mall, and no one drives a car through a mall. Malls are pleasant, safe and profitable precisely because they are free of traffic. A pedestrian downtown would be a mall with better architecture. If malls can thrive without cars, downtown can too.

[6] Safety matters as well. Three pedestrians were injured downtown last year, and the same year the city repainted the crosswalks. Obviously the new paint did not prevent the injuries, and no one has shown that closing the street would not prevent more of them. Only a car-free downtown can be a safe downtown.

[7] Critics also say that closing Main Street will push traffic onto residential side streets. Perhaps. But the critics never say how many cars they expect, and I have yet to see a study proving that the traffic would reach those streets. Until someone produces such a study, we should assume that the problem does not exist.

[8] The deeper question is what kind of city we want to be. Either we embrace a vibrant, walkable downtown, or we resign ourselves to being a place that people drive through on the way to somewhere else. There is no third option. I know which future I prefer, and I think the residents of Harlow Bay do too.

[9] Consider also what happens if we do nothing. If we leave Main Street open, congestion will worsen. Worse congestion will drive away shoppers. When shoppers leave, shops close, and when shops close, the downtown empties out and the tax base crumbles. Soon enough Harlow Bay will be a ghost town. Today's decision is about whether our city survives.

[10] One more point. Dr. Marcus Ellery, the well-known chef who runs the Seaglass restaurant, tells me that a car-free Main Street would be "the best thing to happen to this city in a generation." A man who has served thousands of diners knows what customers want. His opinion should settle the matter.

[11] A few weeks ago I surveyed forty people at the Saturday farmers market and asked whether they would visit downtown more often if it were closed to cars. Thirty-three said yes. That is more than eighty percent, and it shows what the people of Harlow Bay want. Some will say the farmers market crowd is not typical, but I would remind them that those people are residents and they vote.

[12] Opponents say the plan costs too much. It does not. A street is public property, and the plan needs only planters, bollards, benches and signs. Cities that invest in their streets always get the money back, and no one is entitled to use a public street as a private parking lot. Anyone who calls that spending wasteful has never run a budget.

[13] Nor will the ring road suffer. The ring road was built to carry the downtown's overflow, and it has done so since it opened. If it could carry that load then, it can carry it now. Unless the council widens it, which would be a waste of money, the extra cars will simply spread out, and no neighborhood will notice.

[14] Deliveries are a fair concern, but a solved one. Trucks may enter Main Street only if they arrive before nine in the morning, and no shop can open without a delivery, so a morning window is all they need. Merchants who cannot manage a morning window are, frankly, not cut out for downtown retail, and the city should not rearrange itself around their habits.

[15] Some say that older and disabled residents depend on cars. The plan provides drop-off points at the four corners of the zone. I have never heard a disabled resident say she wants more traffic downtown, and I doubt anyone has. A plan that offers drop-off points cannot fairly be called unfriendly to people with disabilities.

[16] Our own history points the same way. In 1998 the city closed Main Street for a single summer festival, and tourism numbers rose that summer. The festival succeeded because the street was closed. Since the street reopened, tourism has stagnated. The lesson is plain: closed streets bring visitors, and open streets drive them away.

[17] Finally, consider the air. Fewer cars mean cleaner air, and cleaner air means healthier children. A person who opposes the plan is therefore willing to harm children, and I do not believe that is what most of my neighbors want. Every reader should ask which side of that question he or she stands on.

[18] A word about timing. The council has studied downtown traffic for three years, and in that time nothing has improved. The council can claim to be careful only if it is willing to act, and every month of delay is a month in which Harlow Bay loses visitors to neighboring towns that have already made up their minds. Careful people decide; only the timid keep studying. The evidence we already have is more than enough, and a pilot program would merely give the opponents more time to organize.

[19] So the council's choice is clear. Closing Main Street to cars will raise sales, protect pedestrians and secure the city's future, and only people who do not care about Harlow Bay would stand in the way. The council should vote yes, and it should do so before next spring.

@review
### Week by week

**Weeks 1-2: arguments, validity, soundness.** An argument is premises offered for a conclusion. Find the conclusion by asking what the author wants you to accept; use indicator words but check them. Arguments differ from explanations and bare assertions. Valid means it is impossible for the premises to be true and the conclusion false; sound means valid with true premises. Show invalidity with a counterexample of the same form.

**Weeks 3-4: propositional logic and truth tables.** Know the five connectives, inclusive "or", De Morgan's laws, and the translations of "neither", "not both" and "although". A complete table has $2^n$ rows. A form is valid if no row makes all premises true and the conclusion false.

**Weeks 5-6: conditionals.** "If $P$" and "$P$ only if $Q$" differ: "only if" introduces the necessary condition (the consequent). "$P$ unless $Q$" is $\neg Q \to P$. The contrapositive is equivalent; the converse and inverse are not. The negation of $P \to Q$ is $P \land \neg Q$. Modus ponens and modus tollens are valid; affirming the consequent and denying the antecedent are not.

**Weeks 7-8: proofs and quantifiers.** Justify each line with a rule and line numbers; use conditional proof for conditionals and reductio for negations. $\forall$ pairs with $\to$ and $\exists$ with $\land$; negate a quantified sentence by switching the quantifier and negating the matrix; order of unlike quantifiers matters.

**Weeks 9-10: fallacies and assumptions.** Name a fallacy and explain the gap. A necessary assumption passes the negation test; a sufficient assumption guarantees the conclusion. Strengthen by ruling out alternatives, and weaken by supplying them.

**Weeks 11-12: induction, cause and analogy.** Check sample size, representativeness and modesty. A correlation has several explanations: cause, reverse cause, common cause and chance. Controls and random assignment support causation. Distinguish relative from absolute risk and use base rates. For analogies, weigh relevant similarities against relevant differences; one counterexample refutes a universal claim.

### Self-check

- Can you write an argument in standard form and supply a modest missing premise?
- Can you translate "only if", "unless" and "no ... without" without looking?
- Can you build a truth table for three letters and read off a counterexample row?
- Can you write the contrapositive and the negation of any conditional?
- Can you prove a simple result in at least two ways?
- Can you state the flaw in an argument in one precise sentence?

### Most common errors

- Treating "only if" as "if", or reversing the direction of a conditional.
- Calling an argument invalid because a premise is false (or valid because the conclusion is true).
- Negating "if $P$ then $Q$" as "if $P$ then not $Q$".
- Pairing $\forall$ with $\land$ or $\exists$ with $\to$.
- Using a fallacy name without explaining the gap.
- Concluding causation from correlation, or ignoring the base rate.

prompt: Consider this argument: "If the filing was late, the claim is barred. The claim is not barred. Either the filing was late or the clerk made an error. Therefore the clerk made an error." Let $L$: the filing was late, $B$: the claim is barred, $C$: the clerk made an error. Over all eight rows of values of $L$, $B$, $C$, how many rows make all three premises true and the conclusion false?
answer: 0
check: sum(1 for l in (True, False) for b in (True, False) for c in (True, False) if ((not l) or b) and (not b) and (l or c) and not c)
hint: Translate the premises as $L \to B$, $\neg B$ and $L \lor C$ and the conclusion as $C$.
hint: A row with the conclusion false has $C$ false. Check whether the premises can then all be true.
solution: With $C$ false, $L \lor C$ requires $L$ true, then $L \to B$ requires $B$ true, contradicting $\neg B$. So no row has true premises and a false conclusion. The count is 0, and the argument is valid (modus tollens and then disjunctive syllogism).

@exam mc
prompt: An op-ed argues: "Cities that closed their main streets to cars saw retail sales rise. Our city should therefore close its main street, because doing so will raise retail sales here." Which statement is a necessary assumption of the argument?
choice: Every city that closed a street to cars saw sales rise.
choice*: The rise in sales elsewhere was not due entirely to factors that are absent in this city.
choice: Retail sales are the only effect that matters to city councils.
choice: No merchant in the city will object to the closure.
hint: Ask what would have to be true for the evidence from other cities to bear on this city.
hint: Apply the negation test to each choice.
solution: If the sales rise elsewhere were due entirely to factors absent here, the evidence from other cities would not show the closure would raise sales here, so the argument fails. The other choices are too strong or irrelevant.

@exam self
prompt: Write a short audit (about 250 words) of this argument: "No one has proved that the new tax will not hurt small businesses, so it will hurt them. If it hurts small businesses, the city loses jobs. The mayor, who owns a large store, supports the tax, so the tax must be bad for small shops." Do four things: (1) put the argument in standard form, (2) translate the conditional claim and give its contrapositive, (3) name two flaws, and (4) state one necessary assumption.
solution: (1) Premises: (a) No one has proved that the tax will not hurt small businesses. (b) If the tax hurts small businesses, the city loses jobs. (c) The mayor, who owns a large store, supports the tax. Sub-conclusion (from a): the tax will hurt small businesses. From the sub-conclusion and (b): the city will lose jobs. Main conclusion (drawn from c): the tax is bad for small shops. (2) With $H$: the tax hurts small businesses and $J$: the city loses jobs, the conditional is $H \to J$ and its contrapositive is $\neg J \to \neg H$ (if the city does not lose jobs, the tax does not hurt small businesses). (3) Flaws: appeal to ignorance (absence of proof that the tax will not hurt is treated as proof that it will), and a confused ad hominem or conflict-of-interest move (the mayor's ownership of a large store is treated as showing that the tax is bad, which is irrelevant to the tax's effect). (4) A necessary assumption: for the first step, that a harm no one has disproved will in fact occur; or, for the last step, that a tax a large-store owner supports must be bad for small shops. The argument cannot be sound because the key premise is unsupported.
hint: Start by finding the sub-conclusion that the argument uses as a premise for the final claim.
hint: Decide whether the mayor's store is relevant to the effect of the tax.
rubric: The standard form separates the sub-conclusion from the final conclusion.
rubric: The conditional is translated with the antecedent and consequent in the right order and the contrapositive is correct.
rubric: Two flaws are named and explained (for example appeal to ignorance and an irrelevant attack on motive).
rubric: The necessary assumption is stated modestly and links the evidence to the conclusion.
