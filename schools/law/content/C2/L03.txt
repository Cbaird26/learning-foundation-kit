@article
English is flexible and ambiguous, so logic uses a small, precise language. **Propositional logic** (also called sentential logic) treats whole statements as atoms and studies how connectives build bigger statements from them. Its great virtue is that validity can be checked mechanically.

### Sentence letters and connectives

Pick a **symbolization key**, assigning each simple statement a capital letter: $P$: "The contract is signed." $Q$: "The deposit is paid." Then combine them. Each connective is **truth-functional**: the truth value of the whole depends only on the truth values of the parts.

| Symbol | Name | Reads as | True when |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\neg P$ | negation | not $P$ | $P$ is false |
| $P \land Q$ | conjunction | $P$ and $Q$ | both are true |
| $P \lor Q$ | disjunction | $P$ or $Q$ | at least one is true |
| $P \to Q$ | conditional | if $P$ then $Q$ | not ($P$ true and $Q$ false) |
| $P \leftrightarrow Q$ | biconditional | $P$ if and only if $Q$ | $P$ and $Q$ have the same value |

The disjunction is **inclusive**: $P \lor Q$ is true when both are true. English "or" is sometimes exclusive ("soup or salad"). To say "$P$ or $Q$ but not both", write $(P \lor Q) \land \neg(P \land Q)$.

The conditional is true whenever $P$ is false. This "material conditional" is the only truth-functional reading that captures the guarantee "if $P$ then $Q$ is never broken". Week 5 examines it in detail.

### Translation guide

- "$P$ but $Q$", "$P$ although $Q$", "$P$ yet $Q$", "$P$ and also $Q$": $P \land Q$ (the contrast is lost, the truth conditions stay).
- "Not both $P$ and $Q$": $\neg(P \land Q)$. "Both not-$P$ and not-$Q$": $\neg P \land \neg Q$. These differ.
- "Neither $P$ nor $Q$": $\neg(P \lor Q)$, equivalently $\neg P \land \neg Q$.
- "$P$ only if $Q$", "$P$ implies $Q$": $P \to Q$.
- "$P$ just in case $Q$", "$P$ exactly when $Q$": $P \leftrightarrow Q$.

### Scope and parentheses

Parentheses show what each connective governs. $\neg(P \land Q)$ negates the whole conjunction; $\neg P \land Q$ negates only $P$. Conventionally $\neg$ binds tightest, but when in doubt, add parentheses. Mixing $\land$ and $\lor$ without them (as in $P \lor Q \land R$) is ambiguous, so always group.

### De Morgan's laws

Two equivalences are used constantly:

$$\neg(P \land Q) \equiv \neg P \lor \neg Q, \qquad \neg(P \lor Q) \equiv \neg P \land \neg Q.$$

"Not both" means at least one fails; "not either" means both fail.

### What translation loses

Translation keeps truth conditions and discards tone, causation and time order. "She married and had a child" suggests a sequence, but $M \land C$ is symmetric. Good analysts notice what the symbolic form leaves out.

@example
title: Translating a compound sentence
problem: Using $A$: "Ana testifies", $B$: "Ben testifies", $C$: "the case is dismissed", translate: "The case is dismissed unless at least one of Ana and Ben testifies, but they will not both testify."
step: "At least one of Ana and Ben testifies" is the inclusive disjunction $A \lor B$.
step: "They will not both testify" is $\neg(A \land B)$.
step: "The case is dismissed unless $X$" means if $X$ fails, the case is dismissed: $\neg X \to C$. Using $X = A \lor B$, this is $\neg(A \lor B) \to C$.
step: Join the two parts with the "but": $(\neg(A \lor B) \to C) \land \neg(A \land B)$.

@example
title: Evaluating a compound sentence
problem: Let $P$ be true, $Q$ false, $R$ false. Find the truth value of $(P \lor Q) \to (R \land \neg Q)$.
step: $P \lor Q$ is true, since $P$ is true.
step: $\neg Q$ is true, but $R$ is false, so $R \land \neg Q$ is false.
step: A true antecedent with a false consequent makes the conditional false. The sentence is false.

@mistakes
- Translating "not both" as $\neg P \land \neg Q$ (that is "neither").
- Reading "or" as exclusive when the context does not say so.
- Dropping parentheses so that a negation governs only part of what it should.
- Translating "$P$ only if $Q$" as $Q \to P$ (the order is reversed).

@practice ms
prompt: Select every formula equivalent to $\neg(P \land Q)$.
choice*: $\neg P \lor \neg Q$
choice: $\neg P \land \neg Q$
choice*: $P \to \neg Q$
choice: $\neg P \to \neg Q$
hint: Use De Morgan's law, and recall that $P \to R$ is true unless $P$ is true and $R$ is false.
hint: Check each choice at $P$ true, $Q$ true: $\neg(P \land Q)$ is false there.
solution: By De Morgan, $\neg(P \land Q)$ is $\neg P \lor \neg Q$. The conditional $P \to \neg Q$ fails exactly when both are true, as does $\neg(P \land Q)$. The formula $\neg P \land \neg Q$ is too strong (false at $P$ true, $Q$ false), and $\neg P \to \neg Q$ is true when both are true.

rubric: Part (b) has $S$ as the antecedent and $N$ as the consequent.

@quiz mc
prompt: Using $R$: "The tenant paid rent" and $L$: "The lease was renewed", which formula is the best translation of "The lease was renewed although the tenant did not pay rent"?
