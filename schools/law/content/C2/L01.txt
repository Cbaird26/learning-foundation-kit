@article
In logic, an **argument** is not a quarrel. It is a set of statements in which some, the **premises**, are offered as reasons to accept another, the **conclusion**. Everything else in this course, from truth tables to the analysis of causal claims, is a tool for judging whether the reasons really support the conclusion. Before you can judge an argument you have to find it.

### Statements and the "why?" test

Only **statements** (sentences that are true or false) can be premises or conclusions. Questions, commands and exclamations cannot. To find the conclusion, ask of each statement: "Is the author trying to get me to accept this, and are the other statements offered as the reason?" The statement that answers "why should I believe that?" for the rest is the conclusion.

**Indicator words** help. Conclusion indicators include *therefore, thus, hence, so, consequently, it follows that*. Premise indicators include *because, since, for, given that, as, the reason is that*. Indicators are clues, not proof: "since" can also mean "after" ("Since 2020 she has lived abroad"), and many arguments have no indicators at all.

### Standard form

To analyze an argument, strip away rhetoric and list it in **standard form**: numbered premises, a line, then the conclusion.

1. All contracts require agreement.
2. This arrangement had no agreement.
3. Therefore, this arrangement is not a contract.

Note that the conclusion may appear first, last or in the middle of the original passage. Standard form puts it last regardless.

### Arguments versus explanations and assertions

A passage that only states facts is a **description**; one that states a claim with no support is a **bare assertion**. Neither is an argument. An **explanation** is subtler: it gives the cause or reason *why* something that both sides accept happened. "The match went out because the room had no oxygen" is not trying to convince you the match went out; that is taken for granted. In an argument, the conclusion is the point in doubt, and the premises are meant to remove the doubt.

### Unstated premises

Real arguments leave things out. "Ana is a nurse, so she can read an ECG" silently relies on a premise: nurses can read ECGs. An **enthymeme** is an argument with a missing premise. When you reconstruct it, supply the **least controversial** premise that connects the stated premises to the conclusion, and be charitable: do not invent a premise that makes the argument silly. Supplying a premise is not endorsing it; in later weeks, hunting for such hidden premises is the central skill of the LSAT's Logical Reasoning sections (two scored sections since August 2024) and of legal analysis generally.

### Why this matters for pre-law study

Judicial opinions, briefs and exam answers are all arguments. A judge's holding is a conclusion; the stated rule and the facts are premises. Learning to see that structure, quickly and accurately, is the first step in evaluating anything you read.

@example
title: Finding the conclusion in a scrambled passage
problem: Find the conclusion and premises: "The campus shuttle should add a late route. After all, the library now stays open until 2 a.m., and many students have no other way home after midnight."
step: Ask "what is the author trying to get me to accept?" The first sentence is a recommendation, and the rest is offered as support. "After all" signals reasons.
step: Conclusion: The campus shuttle should add a late route.
step: Premise 1: The library now stays open until 2 a.m. Premise 2: Many students have no other way home after midnight.
step: Standard form: (1) The library is open until 2 a.m. (2) Many students have no other way home after midnight. (3) Therefore, the shuttle should add a late route. A hidden premise is that the shuttle would give those students a way home.

@example
title: Argument or explanation?
problem: Is this an argument? "The power went out across the block because a tree fell on the line."
step: Ask whether the outage is in doubt. The speaker assumes the audience already knows power went out.
step: The sentence tells why the outage happened. It is therefore an explanation, not an argument.
step: Contrast: "A tree must have fallen on the line, because the power went out across the block." Now the fallen tree is the point in doubt and the outage is evidence for it. That is an argument.

@mistakes
- Treating the first sentence as the conclusion by default. The conclusion can come first, last or in the middle.
- Treating every "because" as a premise marker without asking whether the passage is explaining a known fact.
- Counting background facts or rhetorical flourishes as premises when they give no support.
- Supplying an unstated premise that is far stronger than the argument needs.
