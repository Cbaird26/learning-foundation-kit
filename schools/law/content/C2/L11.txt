@article
Deductive arguments aim at certainty. Most of what we know, from medicine to evidence in a trial, rests on **inductive** reasoning, where premises make a conclusion probable. Induction is judged by degrees of strength, and the main question is always: "what else could explain this?"

### Generalizing from a sample

An **enumerative induction** moves from a sample to a population: "Of 400 randomly chosen voters, 55% favor the measure, so about 55% of all voters do." Three questions assess it:

1. **Size:** is the sample large enough? Small samples fluctuate a lot.
2. **Representativeness:** was it drawn so as to resemble the population? Random selection is the best defense; **self-selected** samples (people who choose to answer) and **convenience samples** are biased.
3. **Modesty:** does the conclusion claim no more than the evidence supports?

The **margin of error** for a proportion from a random sample is roughly $1.96\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$ at 95% confidence, where $p$ is the sample proportion and $n$ the sample size. For $n = 1{,}000$ and $p = 0.5$ this is about $0.031$, or 3 percentage points. Margin of error quantifies only random sampling error, not bias from bad sampling or bad questions.

### Correlation and causation

Two variables are **correlated** when they tend to move together. A correlation between $A$ and $B$ is consistent with several explanations:

- $A$ causes $B$;
- $B$ causes $A$ (**reverse causation**);
- a **common cause** (confounder) $C$ causes both;
- **chance** (more likely with small samples or many comparisons);
- selection effects in how the data were gathered.

Ice-cream sales and drownings rise together because hot weather raises both. Concluding "ice cream causes drowning" is the fallacy of post hoc reasoning applied to a correlation.

### Experiments and controls

An **experiment** assigns the "treatment" and compares a **treatment group** with a **control group**. **Random assignment** makes the groups alike on average in every other respect, observed or not, so a difference in outcomes is plausibly due to the treatment. **Blinding** keeps participants and evaluators from letting expectations shape results. **Observational studies**, which only record who chose or received what, cannot match this protection against confounders, although careful design can reduce it. Even a good study shows an average effect in a population, not that it works for every individual, and it applies to the kind of people studied.

### Mill's methods (briefly)

The **method of agreement** looks for the one factor common to all cases where an effect occurred. The **method of difference** looks for the one factor that differs between a case with the effect and an otherwise similar case without it. Both are informal tools for narrowing candidate causes.

### Reporting risk

If 5% of a control group and 3% of a treated group get ill, the **absolute** risk difference is 2 percentage points; the **relative risk** is $3/5 = 0.6$, a 40% relative reduction. Headlines often give only the relative figure, which can sound dramatic when the baseline risk is small.

### Base rates

When a rare condition is tested for, most positive results can be false positives. Use Bayes' rule:

$$P(\text{condition} \mid +) = \frac{P(+ \mid \text{condition})\,P(\text{condition})}{P(+ \mid \text{condition})\,P(\text{condition}) + P(+ \mid \text{no condition})\,P(\text{no condition})}.$$

Ignoring the **base rate**, the proportion of the population who have the condition, is the base-rate fallacy. The idea also applies to evidence in court: a match is less telling when the match is common in the population.

@example
title: Evaluating a causal claim
problem: "A survey of volunteers who answered an online poll found that those who meditate daily report lower stress. So meditation reduces stress." Evaluate the claim.
step: The sample is self-selected and the data are self-reported, so representativeness is doubtful.
step: Alternative explanations: reverse causation (less-stressed people find it easier to meditate) and a common cause (people with flexible schedules both have less stress and have time to meditate).
step: There is no control group or random assignment, so the argument shows a correlation. It could be strengthened by a randomized trial with a comparison group.

@example
title: Relative and absolute risk
problem: In a trial, 4% of 1,000 treated patients and 6% of 1,000 control patients had a stroke. Find the absolute risk reduction and the relative risk.
step: Absolute risk reduction: $6\% - 4\% = 2$ percentage points.
step: Relative risk: $4/6 = 2/3 \approx 0.67$. The treated group's risk is about two thirds of the control group's, a relative reduction of about 33%.
step: Both numbers describe the same data; the absolute figure shows how many patients benefit (about 20 per 1,000).

@mistakes
- Concluding causation from a correlation without considering a confounder, reverse causation or chance.
- Treating a large self-selected sample as better than a small random one.
- Quoting a relative risk change without the baseline risk.
- Ignoring base rates when interpreting a positive test or a match.

@practice num
prompt: A disease affects 1% of a population. A test detects it 90% of the time when present and gives a false positive 9% of the time when absent. If a person tests positive, what is the probability she has the disease? Give a decimal rounded to three places.
answer: 0.092
tol: 0.002
check: (0.01 * 0.9) / (0.01 * 0.9 + 0.99 * 0.09)
hint: Compare the true positives with all positives, per 10,000 people.
hint: Per 10,000 people: 100 have the disease, 90 of them test positive; 9,900 do not, and 891 of them test positive.
solution: True positives: $0.01 \times 0.9 = 0.009$. False positives: $0.99 \times 0.09 = 0.0891$. So the probability is $0.009/(0.009 + 0.0891) \approx 0.092$, about 9%.

@practice self
prompt: Study summary: "A university survey of 500 students found that those who study with background music have GPAs averaging 0.2 points higher than those who study in silence. The report concludes that background music improves study performance." Evaluate the causal claim in a short paragraph: name at least two alternative explanations and suggest how to test the claim better.
solution: The data show a correlation between studying with music and GPA, not causation. Students chose their own habits, so a common cause is possible: for example, students who are more organized or have quieter living spaces may both play music and earn higher grades. Reverse causation is also possible (students with higher grades, and more confidence, may feel comfortable studying with music). Self-reported habits and a single university limit the conclusion. A better test would randomly assign students to study with or without music in a controlled setting and compare performance on the same task.
hint: Ask who chose to study with music and what else might differ between them and the other students.
hint: Think of what random assignment would do for this study.
rubric: The answer says that the data show a correlation and that causation is not established.
rubric: It gives at least two alternative explanations (common cause, reverse causation, selection or chance).
rubric: It proposes a design with a control group and random assignment.

@quiz num
prompt: A poll of a random sample of 400 voters finds that 50% favor a measure. Using $1.96\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$, what is the 95% margin of error as a decimal (for example 0.05 means 5 percentage points)? Round to three decimal places.
answer: 0.049
tol: 0.001
check: 1.96 * math.sqrt(0.5 * 0.5 / 400)
hint: Substitute $p = 0.5$ and $n = 400$.
hint: $0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25$, and $0.25/400 = 0.000625$.
solution: $\sqrt{0.000625} = 0.025$, and $1.96 \times 0.025 = 0.049$, about 4.9 percentage points.
