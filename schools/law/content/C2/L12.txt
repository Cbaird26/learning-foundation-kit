@article
Analogy is how we reason about the new by comparison with the familiar. Doctors test drugs on animals, engineers test scale models, and common-law courts decide new disputes by comparing them with decided cases. Analogy is powerful and easily abused, so you need criteria for judging it.

### The structure

An **argument from analogy** has this form:

1. Case $A$ has features $F_1, F_2, \dots, F_n$ and also has feature $G$.
2. Case $B$ has features $F_1, F_2, \dots, F_n$.
3. Therefore, case $B$ probably has feature $G$.

It is an inductive argument: even the best analogy gives probability, not certainty.

### Judging an analogy

- **Relevance of the similarities.** Do the shared features bear on $G$? Two cars sharing a color tells you nothing about reliability; two cars with the same engine and maker does. Many similarities in irrelevant respects add nothing.
- **Number and variety.** More relevant similarities, found in varied respects, strengthen the argument.
- **Disanalogies.** Look for relevant differences. A single decisive difference can sink the analogy: a model plane in a wind tunnel may not capture how a full-size plane behaves, if the scale changes the airflow.
- **Modesty of the conclusion.** The claim should not go beyond what the similarities support.
- **Size of the evidence base.** Several analogous cases are better than one.

A useful discipline is to make two lists, similarities and differences, then ask which of each bear on $G$.

### Analogy and legal reasoning

Common-law courts reason from precedent: like cases should be treated alike, so a party argues that the present case shares the legally relevant facts of an earlier case and should have the same outcome. The opposing party **distinguishes** the earlier case by pointing to a legally relevant difference. The hard question is always what counts as *relevant*, which is answered by the rule or principle the precedent stands for, and the policy behind it. You will use this method throughout law school, and an argument from analogy is a good way to practice it.

### Refutation by logical analogy

To show that a form of reasoning is bad, present a parallel argument with the same form and an obviously false conclusion. "Everyone who commits fraud is dishonest; Dan is dishonest; so Dan commits fraud" has the same form as "All cats are animals; Rex is an animal; so Rex is a cat." Counter-analogy is also used against moral and legal arguments: "if your principle justifies this, it justifies that absurd case."

### Argument by example

Examples play three roles:

- **Illustration:** they make a claim concrete, but do not prove it.
- **Support for a generalization:** many varied, representative examples make a generalization probable (this is enumerative induction, as in Week 11).
- **Refutation:** a single **counterexample** refutes a universal claim. "All birds fly" is refuted by one penguin.

Watch for **cherry-picking** (only favorable cases are cited) and **anecdotal evidence** (a vivid story substitutes for systematic data). A universal claim needs more than examples to be proved but only one counterexample to be refuted; a claim about "most" or "typical" cases needs a representative sample, not a hand-picked one.

### Putting it together

A good evaluation of an analogy names the conclusion, lists the relevant similarities and the strongest disanalogy, judges how much weight each deserves, and states a verdict such as "moderately strong, but the difference in scale limits it."

@example
title: Evaluating an argument from analogy
problem: "Town A banned plastic bags and saw litter drop 30%. Town B is similar in size and also has a river and many grocery stores. So a bag ban in Town B will also cut litter." Evaluate it.
step: Conclusion: a ban in Town B will reduce litter. Shared features: size, river, many groceries. Of these, the number of grocery stores bears on bag use; the river is less clearly relevant.
step: Look for disanalogies: if Town B already has a bag fee and Town A did not, the effect may be much smaller. If most litter in B is not bags, the effect would be small.
step: Verdict: moderate at best. The argument is stronger if the towns share bag use and litter composition, and weaker if there is a decisive disanalogy such as an existing fee.

@example
title: Counterexample to a generalization
problem: "Every successful start-up had a charismatic founder; I can name five." Evaluate.
step: Five examples are a sample, and they may have been selected because they were famous (cherry-picking).
step: A universal claim needs no exceptions. A single successful start-up with a founder who was not charismatic refutes it.
step: The argument might support a weaker claim ("many successful start-ups"), but only if the examples are representative of start-ups generally.

@mistakes
- Counting irrelevant similarities (color, location, name) as support.
- Ignoring a relevant difference between the cases.
- Believing that many examples prove a universal claim.
- Using a vivid anecdote in place of a representative sample.

prompt: Evaluate this argument from analogy in a short paragraph: "Under the school's policy, a student who cheated on a take-home exam was suspended. A student who copied code on a take-home programming assignment is in the same position, so she should be suspended too." List at least two relevant similarities, one possible disanalogy, and your verdict.
solution: Similarities: both involve a take-home assessment, both involve copying someone else's work, and both are governed by the same academic integrity policy. The conclusion is that the same penalty applies. A possible disanalogy: if the programming assignment's rules allowed collaboration or reuse of open-source code, or if the copying was a small part of a low-weight assignment, the act may differ in seriousness or in whether it violated a rule at all. Verdict: fairly strong if the same rule clearly covers both, because the relevant similarity (violating the integrity policy) is shared; weaker if the assignment's instructions permitted reuse, which is the most relevant difference to check.
hint: Ask what the policy protects, and whether the second case shares that feature.
hint: List differences, then keep only those that bear on whether the policy applies.

rubric: It identifies a disanalogy that bears on the conclusion.
