@article
Conditional statements are the backbone of rules, statutes, contracts and the LSAT's logical reasoning questions. A rule of law is nearly always an "if": if the elements are met, then the legal result follows. Mistranslating a conditional is one of the most common logical errors in law-school work, so this week is about getting it right.

### Sufficient and necessary

In $P \to Q$, $P$ is the **antecedent** and $Q$ is the **consequent**.

- $P$ is a **sufficient condition** for $Q$: knowing $P$ is enough to be sure of $Q$.
- $Q$ is a **necessary condition** for $P$: if $Q$ is missing, $P$ cannot hold.

"If it is a poodle, it is a dog": being a poodle is sufficient for being a dog; being a dog is necessary for being a poodle. Necessary does not mean sufficient: being a dog does not make something a poodle.

### Translation patterns

Each of these has the form $P \to Q$:

| English | Antecedent | Consequent |
|---|---|---|
| If $P$, then $Q$ | $P$ | $Q$ |
| $Q$ if $P$; $Q$ provided that $P$; $Q$ whenever $P$ | $P$ | $Q$ |
| $P$ only if $Q$ | $P$ | $Q$ |
| $P$ is sufficient for $Q$ | $P$ | $Q$ |
| $Q$ is necessary for $P$ | $P$ | $Q$ |
| No $P$ without $Q$; $P$ requires $Q$ | $P$ | $Q$ |

The crucial contrast is **"if"** versus **"only if"**. "$Q$ if $P$" introduces the *sufficient* condition ($P \to Q$). "$P$ only if $Q$" introduces the *necessary* condition ($P \to Q$). The word "only" sends the condition after "only if" to the consequent. "You may enter only if you have a ticket" means: entering requires a ticket, so enter $\to$ ticket, not ticket $\to$ enter.

### "If and only if"

"$P$ if and only if $Q$" combines both directions: $(P \to Q) \land (Q \to P)$, written $P \leftrightarrow Q$. Definitions are usually meant as biconditionals ("a triangle is equilateral if and only if all its sides are equal"). Statutes and contracts that say only "if" typically do not give a biconditional, a frequent source of disputes.

### "Unless"

"$P$ unless $Q$" means that if $Q$ does not happen, then $P$: $\neg Q \to P$. This is equivalent to $P \lor Q$. "The meeting proceeds unless the chair is absent" is $\neg A \to M$, or $M \lor A$. A subtle point is that "unless" is usually read as inclusive, so it does not rule out $P$ and $Q$ both being true. If a speaker really means "unless, and in that case not," they mean a biconditional, and context must tell you.

### Why true when the antecedent is false?

A conditional makes one promise: it is never the case that $P$ holds and $Q$ fails. If $P$ is false, nothing was promised about $Q$, so the promise was not broken. Compare "If you park here, you will be ticketed." You did not park, and no one can say the promise was false. In English, conditionals sometimes carry causal or temporal meaning too; the symbol $\to$ keeps only the "never ($P$ and not $Q$)" content.

### Necessary and sufficient in law

Think of the elements of a rule. Each element is **necessary** for liability, and **together** the elements are **sufficient**. Defense arguments often attack one necessary element; plaintiffs must establish all of them. Seeing this structure makes statutes much easier to read.

@example
title: Translating "only if" and "unless"
problem: Translate with $A$: "The license is valid", $R$: "The fee was received", $C$: "The permit is cancelled": (a) "The license is valid only if the fee was received." (b) "The permit is cancelled unless the fee was received."
step: (a) "Only if" introduces the necessary condition: validity requires a fee. So $A \to R$, not $R \to A$.
step: (b) "Unless" means if the fee was not received, the permit is cancelled: $\neg R \to C$.
step: Equivalently, (b) is the disjunction $C \lor R$: either the permit is cancelled or the fee was received.

@example
title: Necessary, sufficient, both or neither
problem: A club rule: "A member can vote only if her dues are paid." Dues are paid. Can we conclude she can vote? Is paid dues necessary, sufficient, both, or neither for voting?
step: The rule is vote $\to$ dues paid, which makes paid dues a necessary condition for voting.
step: Paid dues is not stated to be sufficient, since the rule says only "only if". Other requirements may exist.
step: So we cannot conclude she can vote. Dues paid is necessary but not (known to be) sufficient.

@mistakes
- Treating "$P$ only if $Q$" as $Q \to P$. The "only if" clause is the consequent.
- Reading "if" statements in rules as "if and only if".
- Translating "$P$ unless $Q$" as $P \to Q$ or as $Q \to P$ instead of $\neg Q \to P$.
- Assuming that a necessary condition is also sufficient.
