@article
Almost every real argument has a gap: between what the premises say and what the conclusion claims, something is taken for granted. That something is an **assumption** (also called an unstated premise). Finding it is the central skill in LSAT Logical Reasoning, which has had two scored sections since August 2024, and in everyday criticism of claims.

### Necessary and sufficient assumptions

- A **sufficient assumption**, added to the premises, *guarantees* the conclusion. It makes the argument valid.
- A **necessary assumption** is something the argument *cannot do without*: if it is false, the argument fails.

Take: "Ana trained for months, so she will finish the marathon." A sufficient assumption is "Anyone who trains for months finishes" (too strong to be believable, but it makes the argument valid). A necessary assumption is much weaker: "Ana will not be injured before the race", or "Training for months makes finishing possible". Necessary assumptions are usually modest; sufficient ones are usually strong. The sufficient assumption implies the necessary one, not the other way around.

### The negation test

To check a candidate necessary assumption, **negate it** and ask whether the argument is destroyed. If the negation of the statement leaves the argument unable to work, the statement is necessary. Example: argument, "Free breakfast programs raise test scores because the schools that adopted them saw higher scores." Candidate: "Something other than the breakfast program was not responsible for the whole gain." Negated, "Something else caused the whole gain", the argument collapses. So the candidate is necessary.

### Typical gaps

1. **Correlation to cause:** the argument assumes no other explanation (a third factor, reverse causation).
2. **Sample to population:** the argument assumes the sample is representative.
3. **Evidence to claim:** the argument assumes that the measure used (messages sent, clicks, votes) actually measures what the conclusion is about.
4. **Past to future** or **one case to another:** the argument assumes that the situations are relevantly alike.
5. **Means to end:** the argument assumes the proposed action would work and is feasible.

### Strengthening and weakening

New information **strengthens** an argument if it makes the conclusion more likely given the premises, typically by supporting a necessary assumption or ruling out an alternative. It **weakens** the argument if it undermines an assumption or supports an alternative explanation. Information that merely supports the *conclusion's truth* by another route may not help *this* argument. Information that is irrelevant leaves the argument unchanged.

- To strengthen "A caused B": show that B followed A in a controlled experiment, or rule out a common cause.
- To weaken "A caused B": show that B rose just as much where A was absent, that B occurred first, or that a third factor explains both.
- To weaken a plan: show that it would be ineffective or have a worse side effect. To strengthen: show it worked in a similar case.

### Reading strategy

Mark the conclusion, restate the premises in your own words, and ask: "What would I need to believe for this to work?" Then test your guess with the negation test. Be wary of answer choices that are too strong (all, never, only) when the question asks for a necessary assumption, and of answers that are too weak when it asks for a sufficient one.

### In legal writing

A brief's weakest step is almost always an unstated assumption: that a precedent applies to the facts, that a statute's words have the meaning argued, or that a witness is reliable. Naming the assumption is how you attack your opponent's argument, and how you shore up your own.

@example
title: Finding and testing the necessary assumption
problem: "The town added bike lanes last year. Bike commuting rose 40% this year. So the bike lanes caused the increase." Find a necessary assumption and test it.
step: The premises are the lanes and the increase. The conclusion is a causal claim, so a likely gap is an alternative cause.
step: Candidate: the increase was not due entirely to something else, such as a new employer or a gas-price jump.
step: Negation: the whole increase was due to something else. Then the lanes did nothing, and the argument fails. So the candidate is necessary.

@example
title: Strengthen or weaken?
problem: For the same argument, say whether each new fact strengthens, weakens or does not affect it: (a) Bike commuting also rose 38% in a nearby town with no new lanes. (b) Commuters on streets with the new lanes increased far more than on streets without.
step: (a) The rise happened without lanes, so something else may explain the rise. It weakens the argument.
step: (b) The increase is concentrated where the lanes are. It rules out a general citywide cause, so it strengthens the argument.

@mistakes
- Choosing an assumption that is true but is not needed for the argument.
- Choosing an answer that is too strong ("all", "always") for a necessary assumption.
- Treating information that supports the conclusion by another route as strengthening this argument.
- Skipping the negation test and trusting a feeling that the choice "sounds right".

prompt: Argument: "Students who eat the cafeteria breakfast score higher on morning tests. So the school should provide free breakfast to every student to raise test scores." Which is a necessary assumption?
choice: Students who skip breakfast score lower on every test.
choice*: The higher scores of cafeteria-breakfast eaters are not due entirely to other differences between them and other students.
choice: Free breakfast costs less than any other school program.
choice: Every student will eat the free breakfast.
hint: Use the negation test on each choice.
hint: The argument moves from a correlation to the claim that breakfast raises scores.
solution: If the higher scores were due entirely to other differences, breakfast would not raise scores and the argument fails. The other choices are too strong, irrelevant, or about different tests.

prompt: "After the county installed brighter streetlights, its crime rate fell. So brighter streetlights reduce crime." Which new information most weakens the argument?
choice: Streetlights are expensive to maintain.
choice*: Neighboring counties that installed no new lights saw crime fall by the same amount over the same period.
choice: Many residents say they like the new lights.
choice: Crime in the county had been rising for a few years before the lights.
hint: Look for an alternative explanation of the decline.
solution: If crime fell equally where there were no new lights, something else probably explains the decline. Cost and opinion are irrelevant, and a previous rise does not undermine the effect.

@practice mc
prompt: "People who take a daily walk report better moods than those who do not. So daily walking improves mood." Which new information most strengthens the argument?
choice: Many people who take daily walks also live near parks.
choice*: In an experiment, people randomly assigned to walk daily reported better moods than people randomly assigned not to.
choice: Walking is the most popular form of exercise.
choice: Some people who do not walk report excellent moods.
hint: Which fact rules out an alternative explanation?
solution: Random assignment removes differences between the groups, so it supports causation. The other choices are irrelevant or leave the correlation open.

@practice ms
prompt: Argument: "The company's new office layout improved collaboration, since employees now send more messages to colleagues on other teams." Select every necessary assumption.
choice*: Sending more messages to other teams is a sign of more collaboration.
choice*: The new layout, rather than something else, is responsible for the rise in messages.
choice: Every employee prefers the new layout.
choice: Messages within a single team did not increase.
hint: Ask what links the evidence to the conclusion, and what links the cause to the change.
hint: Negate each choice and see whether the argument still works.
solution: The argument needs the measure (messages) to track collaboration, and needs the layout to explain the rise. It does not need universal approval or any claim about messages within teams.

@practice self
prompt: For each short argument, name the flaw (the gap) and state a necessary assumption. (a) "Our app's users who enable notifications open it more often, so notifications make users open the app more." (b) "Three of my four friends enjoyed the film, so most people will."
solution: (a) The flaw is moving from correlation to cause; users who enable notifications may already be more engaged (reverse causation or a common factor). A necessary assumption is that users who enable notifications are not simply more engaged to begin with. (b) The flaw is an unrepresentative, tiny sample (hasty generalization). A necessary assumption is that the four friends are representative of people in general in their taste for the film.
hint: Ask in each case what the evidence actually measures, and how it might differ from what the conclusion claims.
rubric: Part (a) identifies correlation-to-cause, with an alternative explanation.
rubric: Part (b) identifies the small and unrepresentative sample.
rubric: Each assumption is necessary and modest, and stated as a full sentence.

@quiz mc
prompt: "Ana exceeded her sales target, so she will be promoted." Which statement, added as a premise, would make the argument valid?
choice: Anyone who is promoted exceeded a sales target.
choice*: Anyone who exceeds a sales target is promoted.
choice: Most people who exceed a sales target are promoted.
choice: Ana is hard-working.
hint: A sufficient assumption must guarantee the conclusion.
hint: Be careful about the direction of the conditional.
solution: The needed premise is "exceeds target $\to$ promoted". The choice "Anyone who is promoted exceeded a sales target" is the converse, the "most people" choice gives only probability, and the "hard-working" choice does not link the premise to the conclusion.

@quiz mc
prompt: "A magazine mailed a survey to its readers. Of those who returned it, 80% said they love the magazine. So most readers love the magazine." What is the flaw?
choice: It appeals to an unqualified authority.
choice: It assumes the magazine is popular because it is popular.
choice*: It assumes that readers who returned the survey are representative of all readers.
choice: It infers that each individual reader loves the magazine because the readership as a whole does.
hint: Who is likely to return a survey, and how might they differ from other readers?
solution: People who feel strongly are more likely to respond, so the respondents may not represent all readers. The argument depends on that unsupported assumption (self-selection bias).
