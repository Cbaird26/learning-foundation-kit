@article
Propositional logic cannot see inside "All lawyers are literate; Ana is a lawyer; so Ana is literate". Its three sentences are just $P$, $Q$ and $R$, and that form is invalid. **Predicate logic** (first-order logic) looks inside sentences at their subjects and predicates, which captures this valid argument.

### Vocabulary

- **Names** ($a$, $b$, $c$) pick out individuals: $a$ = Ana.
- **Predicates** ($L$, $F$, $T$) say things about individuals: $Lx$: "$x$ is a lawyer". Relations take several places: $Txy$: "$x$ trusts $y$".
- **Variables** ($x$, $y$) stand for unspecified individuals in the **domain** (the things we are talking about).
- **Quantifiers:** $\forall x$ ("for every $x$") and $\exists x$ ("for at least one $x$").

State the domain when it matters: "everyone" in a domain of people is fine, but in a domain of all things you need a predicate $Px$ for "$x$ is a person".

### The four classic forms

| English | Translation |
|---|---|
| All $S$ are $P$ | $\forall x\,(Sx \to Px)$ |
| Some $S$ are $P$ | $\exists x\,(Sx \land Px)$ |
| No $S$ are $P$ | $\forall x\,(Sx \to \neg Px)$ (or $\neg\exists x\,(Sx \land Px)$) |
| Some $S$ are not $P$ | $\exists x\,(Sx \land \neg Px)$ |

**Pairing rule:** $\forall$ goes with $\to$; $\exists$ goes with $\land$. The sentence $\forall x\,(Sx \land Px)$ says *everything* is an $S$ and a $P$, far too strong. The sentence $\exists x\,(Sx \to Px)$ is true if there is anything that is not an $S$, far too weak.

"Some" means "at least one", and does not imply "not all". "All $S$ are $P$" in logic does not assert that any $S$ exists (an "empty" universal is vacuously true), a point where modern predicate logic differs from older syllogistic reasoning.

### "Only"

"Only citizens may vote": $\forall x\,(Vx \to Cx)$, as with the propositional "only if". Do not reverse it.

### Negation

To deny a universal, find a counterexample; to deny an existential, show all fail:

$$\neg\forall x\,Fx \equiv \exists x\,\neg Fx, \qquad \neg\exists x\,Fx \equiv \forall x\,\neg Fx.$$

The denial of "All lawyers are honest" is "Some lawyer is not honest", not "No lawyer is honest".

### Nested quantifiers

Order matters. Let $Txy$ mean "$x$ trusts $y$" in a domain of people.

- $\forall x\,\exists y\,Txy$: everyone trusts someone (possibly different people).
- $\exists y\,\forall x\,Txy$: there is one person whom everyone trusts.

The second implies the first, but not conversely. Swapping unlike quantifiers is the **quantifier shift fallacy**: "Every nation has a capital, so there is a city that is every nation's capital" is invalid.

### Evaluating over a finite domain

Over a small domain, $\forall x\,Fx$ amounts to a long conjunction and $\exists x\,Fx$ to a long disjunction. In the domain $\{1,2,3\}$, $\forall x\,(x > 0)$ is true, and $\exists x\,(x > 2 \land x < 3)$ is false. This is the easiest way to test a translation: pick a tiny domain and check.

### Validity in predicate logic

The syllogism from the start is formalized as $\forall x\,(Lx \to Fx)$, $La$; therefore $Fa$. It is valid in every domain: if the first premise holds for all individuals, it holds for Ana. Proof rules for quantifiers (universal instantiation and generalization, existential instantiation and generalization) extend natural deduction.

@example
title: Translating classic and nested sentences
problem: With $Cx$: "$x$ is a client", $Lx$: "$x$ is a lawyer", $Txy$: "$x$ trusts $y$", translate: (a) "Every client trusts some lawyer." (b) "No lawyer is trusted by every client."
step: (a) For every client there is a lawyer whom that client trusts: $\forall x\,(Cx \to \exists y\,(Ly \land Txy))$.
step: (b) "Some lawyer is trusted by every client" is $\exists y\,(Ly \land \forall x\,(Cx \to Txy))$. Its denial is (b): $\neg\exists y\,(Ly \land \forall x\,(Cx \to Txy))$.
step: Check the pairing rule: each $\forall$ carries a conditional and each $\exists$ a conjunction.

@example
title: Testing over a small domain
problem: In the domain $\{1,2,3,4\}$, is $\forall x\,(x \text{ is even} \to x > 1)$ true? Is $\exists x\,(x \text{ is even} \land x > 3)$ true?
step: For the first, check the even numbers only (the conditional is vacuous otherwise): 2 and 4 are both greater than 1. True.
step: For the second, look for an even number greater than 3: $x = 4$ works. True.

@mistakes
- Translating "All $S$ are $P$" as $\forall x\,(Sx \land Px)$ or "Some $S$ are $P$" as $\exists x\,(Sx \to Px)$.
- Negating "All are $F$" as "None are $F$" instead of "Some is not $F$".
- Swapping $\forall$ and $\exists$ in nested sentences without checking the meaning.
- Forgetting that "only" reverses the direction of the conditional.

@practice mc
prompt: With $Sx$: "$x$ is a student" and $Fx$: "$x$ failed", which formula best translates "No student failed"?
choice: $\forall x\,(Sx \land \neg Fx)$
choice*: $\forall x\,(Sx \to \neg Fx)$
choice: $\exists x\,(Sx \land \neg Fx)$
choice: $\neg\forall x\,(Sx \to Fx)$
hint: "No $S$ are $F$" says that every student is a non-failer.
hint: A universal with a restriction uses the conditional.
solution: "No student failed" says every student did not fail: $\forall x\,(Sx \to \neg Fx)$. The choice with $\forall x\,(Sx \land \neg Fx)$ says everything is a non-failing student, and the choices beginning with $\exists$ or $\neg\forall$ are existential claims.

@practice self
prompt: Using $Cx$: "$x$ is a client", $Lx$: "$x$ is a lawyer", $Txy$: "$x$ trusts $y$", translate (a) "Some lawyer is trusted by every client" and (b) "Every client trusts some lawyer", and explain in one sentence why (a) implies (b) but not conversely.
solution: (a) $\exists y\,(Ly \land \forall x\,(Cx \to Txy))$. (b) $\forall x\,(Cx \to \exists y\,(Ly \land Txy))$. If a single lawyer is trusted by every client, then each client trusts at least one lawyer, namely that one, so (a) implies (b). But (b) allows different clients to trust different lawyers, so it does not guarantee one lawyer whom all clients trust.
hint: Decide who is doing the trusting: in $Txy$ the first letter trusts the second.
hint: Write the quantifier order first, then restrict each quantifier with its predicate.
rubric: Part (a) has $\exists$ over a conjunction and $\forall$ over a conditional.
rubric: Part (b) has $\forall$ over a conditional containing an $\exists$ with a conjunction.
rubric: The explanation notes that (a) needs one common lawyer while (b) allows different lawyers for different clients.

@quiz num
prompt: In the domain $\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$, for how many values of $x$ is "there is some $y$ in the domain with $y > x$" true?
answer: 4
check: sum(1 for x in range(1, 6) if any(y > x for y in range(1, 6)))
hint: For each $x$, ask whether a larger number exists in the domain.
hint: The only $x$ with no larger number is the largest.
solution: For $x = 1, 2, 3, 4$ a larger number exists. For $x = 5$ none does. So the sentence holds for 4 values.
