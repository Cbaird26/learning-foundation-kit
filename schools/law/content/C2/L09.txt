@article
A **fallacy** is a pattern of reasoning that looks persuasive but fails to support its conclusion. **Formal fallacies**, such as affirming the consequent, are invalid because of their logical form, and a truth table can expose them. **Informal fallacies** go wrong in content, relevance or wording, so you must understand the meaning of the sentences to spot them. Naming a fallacy is useful only if you also explain *why* the premises fail to support the conclusion.

### Fallacies of relevance

- **Ad hominem:** attacking the arguer instead of the argument. "Her proposal on sentencing is worthless; she was once fined for speeding." Careful: questioning a witness's credibility or an expert's conflict of interest is legitimate when it bears on whether to believe what they say. The fallacy is concluding that the *claim* is false because of who made it.
- **Straw man:** refuting a distorted, weaker version of the opponent's position. "She wants to regulate fireworks, so she wants to ban all celebrations."
- **Red herring:** changing the subject to a distracting topic.
- **Appeal to emotion, popularity or tradition:** treating how a claim feels, how many people accept it or how long it has been practiced as proof that it is true.
- **Genetic fallacy:** judging a claim by its origin alone.
- **Appeal to ignorance:** "No one has shown the product is unsafe, so it is safe." Lack of evidence is sometimes informative (after a careful search), but not when no one has looked. In law, the burden of proof gives silence a legal effect, not a logical one.

### Fallacies of weak support

- **Appeal to authority (illegitimate):** citing someone with no expertise in the relevant area, an unnamed source, or experts who disagree. Citing a qualified expert in her field is a legitimate inductive move.
- **Hasty generalization:** drawing a general claim from a sample that is too small or unrepresentative.
- **Post hoc ergo propter hoc:** "after this, therefore because of this." We study cause in Week 11.
- **Slippery slope:** claiming without evidence that one step will lead inevitably to a chain of extreme results. The argument is legitimate when each link is supported by evidence.

### Fallacies of ambiguity and presumption

- **Equivocation:** using a word in two senses. "A feather is light; what is light cannot be dark; so a feather cannot be dark."
- **Composition and division:** inferring that a whole has a property because its parts do (composition: "each player is excellent, so the team is excellent"), or that parts have a property because the whole does (division: "the firm is large, so each lawyer is powerful").
- **Begging the question (circular reasoning):** a premise assumes the conclusion. "The law is fair because it treats everyone justly."
- **False dilemma:** presenting two options as the only ones when there are others. "Either we ban the program or accept widespread abuse."
- **Loaded question:** a question with a presupposition built in: "Have you stopped missing deadlines?"

### How to use the list

1. Identify the conclusion and the premises.
2. Ask what the premises would have to be like to support the conclusion. Name the specific gap.
3. Check context: is a move like "that expert is biased" actually relevant? A fallacy label is a conclusion that needs support, not a trump card.
4. Remember that a fallacious argument can have a true conclusion. Showing a fallacy shows only that *this argument* fails.

### Why this matters

On the LSAT and in practice, flaws are often a subtle version of one of these patterns: a sample that does not represent the population, a part-whole shift or a presumption of only two options. Learning the names gives you a quick way to describe the flaw precisely.

@example
title: Naming and explaining a fallacy
problem: "Of the three students I met from the Law Club, two were rude. So Law Club students are rude." Name the flaw.
step: The conclusion is a general claim about all Law Club students. The only evidence is three people.
step: The sample is tiny and not chosen at random. This is a hasty generalization.
step: Explain: three encounters cannot support a claim about a whole group, and the meeting circumstances may have biased the sample.

@example
title: Fallacy or legitimate move?
problem: A lawyer says: "The expert witness was paid by the plaintiff's insurer, so the jury should give his testimony less weight." Is this an ad hominem fallacy?
step: The lawyer is not claiming the expert's conclusions are false because of who he is. The claim is about reliability.
step: A financial interest is relevant to whether the testimony is trustworthy, and the jury can weigh it.
step: This is a legitimate challenge to credibility, not a fallacy. It would be a fallacy to say "his opinion is wrong because he is dishonest" without examining the evidence.

@mistakes
- Using a fallacy name as a label without explaining the gap between premises and conclusion.
- Calling every attack on an arguer or expert an ad hominem, even when credibility is relevant.
- Assuming a conclusion must be false because the argument for it is fallacious.
- Confusing equivocation (one word with two meanings) with vagueness.

prompt: Select every argument that commits a fallacy of composition or division.
choice*: "Each of the firm's lawyers is brilliant, so the firm is certain to win every case."
choice: "That judge was appointed by the governor, so he must have been picked for his loyalty."
choice*: "The company is extremely wealthy, so each of its employees must be wealthy."
choice: "A survey of 3 shoppers found that all 3 liked the product, so everyone does."
hint: Look for a move between properties of parts and of the whole.
hint: The other two arguments have different problems.
solution: The argument from the brilliant lawyers to the firm infers from parts to whole (composition), and the argument from the wealthy company to its employees goes from whole to parts (division). The judge argument is an origin-based claim (genetic fallacy) and the three-shopper argument is a hasty generalization.

@practice self
prompt: Name the flaw in each, with a one-sentence explanation: (a) "Nobody has proved that the safety rules are unnecessary, so they are necessary." (b) "My opponent wants to limit campaign donations, so he wants to silence all speech."
solution: (a) Appeal to ignorance: absence of proof that the rules are unnecessary does not show that they are necessary. (b) Straw man: a limit on donations is distorted into a ban on all speech, which the opponent never proposed.
hint: For (a), ask what evidence is actually offered. For (b), compare the claim attacked with the claim made.
rubric: Part (a) names appeal to ignorance and notes that lack of evidence is not evidence of the opposite.
rubric: Part (b) names straw man and contrasts the actual position with the exaggerated one.
rubric: Each explanation states the gap between premise and conclusion.
