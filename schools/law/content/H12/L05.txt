@article
By 1900 industrialization had brought wealth and also crowded cities, unsafe workplaces and political machines. **Progressives** were a varied group of reformers, including journalists, social workers, business people, women's clubs, Black reformers, farmers and politicians. They believed that government could and should address social problems using expertise, investigation and regulation.

### Exposing problems

**Muckrakers** were journalists who investigated and published accounts of corruption, unsafe factories and poor living conditions. A 1906 novel about Chicago's meatpacking industry described unsanitary conditions in the plants and shocked many readers. Settlement houses, such as Hull House in Chicago, offered services to immigrant neighborhoods. Reformers also fought child labor and campaigned for shorter hours and safer workplaces.

### Food and drug safety

Before 1906 the federal government had little power over what was sold as food or medicine. Some medicines contained alcohol, opiates or other undisclosed ingredients, and food could be adulterated (mixed with cheaper or harmful material) or mislabeled. In 1906 Congress passed two laws:

- The **Pure Food and Drug Act** banned the interstate sale of adulterated or misbranded food and drugs and required that labels list certain dangerous ingredients.
- The **Meat Inspection Act** required federal inspection of animals before and after slaughter and set sanitary standards for plants.

Supporters argued the laws protected consumers and honest producers. Some manufacturers and members of Congress worried about costs and federal power. The law had limits: it relied on labels rather than requiring proof that drugs worked or were safe before sale. That gap was addressed in 1938 (see week 6). The agency that enforced the 1906 law was later renamed the Food and Drug Administration.

### Political reforms

Progressives sought to make government more democratic and more regulated:

- State reforms included the **initiative**, **referendum** and **recall**, and primaries.
- The **Sixteenth Amendment** (1913) allowed a federal income tax. The **Seventeenth** (1913) provided for direct election of senators. The **Eighteenth** (1919) prohibited the sale of alcohol, a goal of the temperance movement; it was repealed in 1933. The **Nineteenth** (1920) barred denying the vote on account of sex.
- The **Federal Reserve Act** (1913) created the central banking system, and the **Clayton Act** and Federal Trade Commission (1914) targeted unfair business practices.

The **women's suffrage** movement used marches, lobbying and court cases for decades before the Nineteenth Amendment. Many Black women remained blocked from voting in the South, and Native Americans were not granted citizenship nationally until 1924. The NAACP was founded in 1909. Progressive reform often coexisted with segregation and with immigration restriction, and some Progressive ideas, such as eugenics, are now widely rejected.

### World War I

War began in Europe in 1914. The United States declared neutrality but traded heavily with the Allies. German submarine warfare, including the sinking of the *Lusitania* in 1915, and the Zimmermann telegram, a German proposal of an alliance with Mexico, increased tension. Congress declared war on Germany in April 1917, and President Wilson described the aim of making the world safe for democracy.

The **Selective Service Act** (1917) created a draft. Millions of women and men worked in war industries, and the Great Migration of Black Southerners to Northern cities accelerated. About 350,000 Black Americans served in the military, in segregated units. The **Espionage Act** (1917) and **Sedition Act** (1918) criminalized certain speech against the war; in *Schenck v. United States* (1919), the Supreme Court upheld a conviction under the Espionage Act, and the debate over where to draw the line on wartime speech continues.

The **Armistice** on November 11, 1918 ended the fighting. Wilson's proposed League of Nations was part of the Treaty of Versailles, but the Senate did not approve the treaty, and the United States did not join the League.

@example
title: Why a 1906 law counts as regulation
problem: Explain how the Pure Food and Drug Act (1906) changed the federal role in food and drug safety, and name one limit.
step: Identify the problem. Food and medicines were sold across state lines with no federal standard for labeling or purity, so consumers could not know what they were buying.
step: Describe the change. The law banned interstate commerce in adulterated or misbranded food and drugs and required labels to disclose certain ingredients, giving the federal government enforcement power.
step: Link to the constitutional basis. The law relied on Congress's power to regulate commerce among the states.
step: Name a limit. The law focused on honest labeling and did not require manufacturers to prove that a drug was safe or effective before selling it. Later laws, such as the 1938 act, added premarket safety review.

@mistakes
- Assuming the Progressive Era was a single party's program; reformers came from many groups and disagreed with one another.
- Saying the Meat Inspection Act and the Pure Food and Drug Act are the same law; they dealt with meat inspection and with food and drug labeling respectively.
- Forgetting that the Nineteenth Amendment did not remove every barrier to voting, especially for Black women in the South and Native women.
