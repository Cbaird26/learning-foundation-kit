@article
For forty years the most important question in administrative law was how much a court should defer when an agency interprets a statute it administers. That era ended in 2024. To understand current law, you need to know what was overruled and what survives.

### The Chevron framework (1984-2024)

*Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.* (1984) set up two steps. **Step One**: has Congress directly spoken to the precise question? If the intent is clear, the court and agency must give it effect. **Step Two**: if the statute is silent or ambiguous, the court asks whether the agency's answer is a permissible (reasonable) construction. The rationale was an **implicit delegation**: an ambiguity or gap in a statute an agency administers was seen as Congress's choice to let the agency fill it.

The framework had limits. *Christensen v. Harris County* (2000) and *United States v. Mead Corp.* (2001) held that *Chevron* applied only when Congress had delegated authority to make rules with the force of law and the agency used that authority (typically notice-and-comment rulemaking or formal adjudication); other interpretations got only *Skidmore* weight (Lesson 10). *National Cable & Telecommunications Ass'n v. Brand X Internet Services* (2005) held that an agency could adopt a reading different from an earlier judicial construction if the statute was ambiguous and the court had not held that its reading was the only permissible one.

### Loper Bright (2024)

*Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo* (decided together with *Relentless, Inc. v. Department of Commerce*) arose when fishing companies challenged a rule requiring them to pay for at-sea monitors under the Magnuson-Stevens Act. The Court (6-3) **overruled *Chevron***. The majority reasoned that:

1. The APA, 5 U.S.C. § 706, directs the reviewing court to "decide all relevant questions of law" and interpret statutory provisions; it does not tell courts to defer to agencies on law, and it contrasts that with the deferential review of agency fact-finding and policy.
2. Interpreting statutes is the traditional job of courts; the judiciary's independent judgment is the rule from *Marbury v. Madison* and early practice.
3. *Chevron*'s presumption that ambiguity means delegation is a fiction; ambiguities arise for many reasons, and agencies have no special competence in resolving purely legal questions.

The result: courts must **exercise independent judgment to decide the best reading of the statute** using the ordinary tools of interpretation. A court need not defer because a statute is ambiguous. The dissent argued that agencies, not judges, are better placed to resolve technical statutory gaps and that the decision destabilizes settled law.

### What Loper Bright leaves in place

- **Statutory stare decisis**: prior holdings that relied on *Chevron* are not automatically open to relitigation; a party cannot reopen them merely because *Chevron* was overruled.
- **Express delegations**: where a statute expressly delegates authority to an agency to define a term or exercise discretion (using words like "appropriate" or "reasonable"), the court's job is to decide whether the agency has acted within the boundaries of that delegation and has engaged in reasoned decision-making (the arbitrary-and-capricious review of Lesson 8).
- **Skidmore respect**: an agency's interpretation, particularly a contemporaneous and consistent one grounded in expertise, may inform the court's judgment, though it does not control.
- **Deference to fact-finding and policy judgments**, which is reviewed under the deferential standards of § 706.

### Auer and Kisor

Deference to an agency's reading of **its own regulation** (*Auer v. Robbins* (1997)) was narrowed in *Kisor v. Wilkie* (2019): a court first uses all the tools of construction to see whether the regulation is genuinely ambiguous. Only if it is, and the agency's reading is reasonable, authoritative, based on its substantive expertise and a fair and considered judgment (not a new litigating position creating unfair surprise), does the court defer. *Loper Bright* addressed statutes, not regulations, so *Kisor* remains the controlling framework unless the Court revisits it.

### Policy

The case for *Chevron* was accountability and expertise: the politically accountable executive should make policy choices within ambiguous statutes, and agencies understand regulatory context. The case against was separation of powers, judicial duty, and uncertainty when administrations shift interpretations. After *Loper Bright*, interpretations that agencies once secured by *Chevron* deference must be defended as the best reading.

### Applying it (IRAC)

Issue: what does the statute mean? Rule: the court exercises independent judgment (*Loper Bright*), giving the agency's view *Skidmore* respect; where the statute expressly delegates discretion, the court polices its boundaries. Application: interpret the text and context, then ask whether the agency's reading is persuasive. Conclusion: predict the outcome and note the statute's delegation language.

@example
title: Same ambiguity, different law
problem: A statute allows an agency to regulate "manufacturers" of a product. The agency's rule applies to importers as well. A court is asked to decide whether "manufacturer" covers importers. Describe how the analysis differs under Chevron and under current law.
step: Under Chevron, the court would find the term ambiguous at Step One and, at Step Two, ask only whether including importers was a reasonable reading.
step: Under current law, the court decides the best reading itself, using text, structure and canons; the agency's view is considered but does not control.
step: Check for an express delegation: if the statute said the agency may define "manufacturer" by rule, the court would instead ask whether the definition falls within the delegation and is reasonably explained.
step: Conclusion: with no express delegation, the court rules for the agency only if it independently concludes that importers are within "manufacturer."

@mistakes
- Saying that Loper Bright eliminated all respect for agency views; Skidmore weight survives.
- Saying that Loper Bright automatically reopened every earlier decision that relied on Chevron.
- Overlooking express delegations, where a court reviews boundaries and reasoned decision-making rather than deciding the best reading.
- Applying Chevron or Kisor to the wrong object: Chevron concerned statutes; Auer and Kisor concern agency readings of their own regulations.
