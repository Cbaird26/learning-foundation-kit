@article
When text is ambiguous, an interpreter may ask what the enacting Congress was trying to do. **Purposivism** answers that statutes are purposive acts: read the text, in light of the problem it addressed and the evident goal, to reach the meaning a reasonable legislator would have chosen. **Legislative history** (committee reports, hearings, floor debates, and the sequence of drafts and amendments) is the evidence most often used. *Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States* (1892) is the classic purposive opinion: the Court read a ban on contracts for foreign labor not to cover a church's hiring of a pastor, relying on the statute's context and the committee report. A modern purposivist asks what problem the statute was meant to solve and uses that to choose among competing readings of the text, but not to override unambiguous words.

### A hierarchy of reliability

Users of legislative history rank it roughly this way.

1. **Committee reports**: they accompany the bill to the floor and are the main explanation members rely on. They are the most reliable source.
2. **Statements of a bill's sponsor or floor manager** explaining the text: next, though *Garcia v. United States* (1984) called committee reports the authoritative source and said the remarks of a single legislator, even a sponsor, are not controlling.
3. **Other floor statements, and hearing testimony**: weaker; they may reflect advocacy or one member's view.
4. **Drafting history**: dropped or changed language can show what Congress chose not to say, but also may reflect many unrelated reasons.
5. **Post-enactment history**: statements after enactment, and subsequent failures to amend. These are the weakest. *Central Bank of Denver v. First Interstate Bank* (1994) refused to infer meaning from Congress's later failure to enact a bill, because congressional silence is ambiguous.

### The textualist objections

Textualists reject using legislative history to find meaning where the text is clear, for four reasons: only the text was enacted through bicameralism and presentment; history is unreliable and can be manipulated (a member may insert a statement to shape later interpretation); no committee speaks for the full body; and judges can cherry-pick from a large record. *Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Allapattah Services, Inc.* (2005) illustrates this: the majority held that legislative history cannot alter a reading that the text of the jurisdictional statute supports, and explained why it regards such history as unreliable. Textualists nevertheless sometimes consult history for limited purposes, such as to show that a word was a term of art, to document the problem the statute addressed, or to check that a reading is not absurd. Many judges use history only to confirm a reading already supported by text.

### Purpose and other tools

- **Statements of purpose in the statute** (a findings section or "purposes" clause) are part of the enacted text and carry more weight than reports, though they cannot override operative language.
- **Prior-construction canon**: when Congress reenacts language that courts have consistently interpreted, it is presumed to have adopted that interpretation (*Lorillard v. Pons* (1978)).
- *TVA v. Hill* (1978) shows how a court can read both text and purpose together: the Court read the Endangered Species Act to forbid completing a dam that would destroy a snail darter's habitat, relying on the act's language and its evident priority on species protection, notwithstanding later appropriations. Congress later amended the statute to create an exemption process, a reminder that legislative correction is available.

### Why the debate matters

The choice of method affects which arguments a lawyer should make, and to whom. Before a textualist judge, lead with text and structure; before a purposivist, tell the story of the problem and the committee's explanation, but anchor it in the words.

### Applying the sources (IRAC)

Issue: is the text ambiguous? Rule: courts begin with text and canons. Application: if ambiguity persists, consult purpose and history in order of reliability, and note whether the interpreter's method permits them. Conclusion: state the best reading and the strongest counter-argument.

@example
title: Weighing a committee report against a floor remark
problem: A statute exempts "agricultural workers" from overtime rules. Is a cannery employee exempt? The committee report says the exemption covers "those who work in fields and orchards, not in processing plants." One member said during floor debate that "everyone connected with farm products is exempt." The cannery processes produce from many farms. Which source should carry more weight?
step: Identify the ambiguity: "agricultural workers" could cover only farm labor or also processing.
step: Rank the sources: a committee report is the most reliable; a single member's floor remark is weaker and may be advocacy.
step: Note consistency: the report matches a natural reading of "agricultural" as farm work, and it is consistent with the exemption's likely purpose (seasonal farm labor).
step: Conclusion: the report prevails over the floor remark, and the cannery worker is likely not exempt. A textualist would reach the same result only if the text and structure, rather than the report, supported it.

@mistakes
- Treating all legislative history as equal rather than ranking it by reliability.
- Using history to override clear text rather than to resolve real ambiguity.
- Drawing meaning from Congress's failure to pass a later bill.
- Forgetting that a purpose clause in the enacted text is stronger than a committee report.

@practice self
prompt: Apply five canons to this supplied ambiguous provision. "Sec. 10. It is unlawful to operate a motorized vehicle, including a car, truck or motorcycle, in Cedar Park. Sec. 11. This chapter does not apply to emergency vehicles or maintenance vehicles. Sec. 12. A violation of Sec. 10 is a misdemeanor punishable by up to 30 days in jail. Sec. 13. Bicycles may be ridden on the park's paved paths." Ana rides a pedal-assist electric bicycle (a small motor helps while she pedals) on a paved path in Cedar Park and is charged under Section 10. Write a memo section applying ejusdem generis, expressio unius, the whole-text or surplusage canon, the specific-over-general canon and the rule of lenity, and predict the result.
hint: Identify the issue and the governing rule first, then apply it element by element to the facts.
solution: Issue: whether an electric bicycle is a "motorized vehicle" under Section 10. Ejusdem generis: the examples (car, truck, motorcycle) are road vehicles with significant engines; an e-bike with a small assist motor differs in kind, which favors Ana. Counter: "motorized" is the operative adjective, and the examples are introduced by "including," which is nonexclusive. Expressio unius: Section 11 exempts only emergency and maintenance vehicles, which suggests that other motorized vehicles, even benign ones, are not exempt; this favors the prosecution. Whole text: Section 13 permits "bicycles" on paved paths, so reading Section 10 to ban every motorized bicycle would leave Section 13 without effect for e-bikes, and the statute's use of "bicycle" does not exclude motors; this is ambiguous but supports Ana. Specific over general: Section 13 speaks specifically about bicycles on paved paths while Section 10 speaks generally of motorized vehicles; the specific provision controls, favoring Ana. Lenity: Section 12 imposes jail time, so if ambiguity remains after these tools, it is resolved for Ana. Conclusion: Ana likely prevails, though the expressio unius point and the word "motorized" make this a closer case than the other canons suggest; an ordinance amendment defining "bicycle" and "motorized vehicle" would remove the doubt.

rubric: Identifies at least one canon that favors each side.
rubric: Applies lenity last, after the textual canons.

The statute's findings section says that its purpose is "to deter private misconduct." A committee report, by contrast, says in one sentence that "the government is a person." Which source is better evidence of the meaning, and why?
choice: The committee report, because reports always outrank statutory findings.
choice*: The findings section, because it is enacted text and so carries more weight than a report, although neither can override clear operative language.
choice: Neither, because purpose may never be considered.
choice: The report, because it is more specific than the findings.

solution: Enacted purpose clauses are part of the text and carry more weight than legislative history, though they cannot override operative language. The claim that reports always outrank findings is backwards.
