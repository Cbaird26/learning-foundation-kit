@article
A **statute** is a text that satisfies the Constitution's lawmaking procedure. Everything later in this course (reading a text, using canons, deferring or not deferring to an agency) assumes you can tell what counts as the text and how it came to bind. Start with the path.

### The rule: bicameralism and presentment

Article I, Section 7 permits a bill to become federal law only if (1) both the House and the Senate pass **identical text** (**bicameralism**) and (2) the bill is **presented** to the President, who signs it, lets it become law without signature, or is overridden after a veto (**presentment**). *INS v. Chadha* (1983) treats these as the exclusive method for actions that have the force and effect of law.

### The stages

A bill may be introduced in either chamber, except that bills for raising revenue must originate in the House (the **Origination Clause**). Typical steps: introduction, referral to a committee, hearings and **markup**, a committee report, scheduling (in the House, usually through the Rules Committee, which sets the terms of floor debate), floor passage, and then reconciliation of differences. Differences are resolved by one chamber accepting the other's version, by exchanging amendments, or by a conference committee whose report each chamber must then adopt. The final text is **enrolled** and presented. Under the **enrolled bill rule** (*Marshall Field & Co. v. Clark* (1892)), courts generally do not look behind a duly authenticated enrolled bill to ask whether the chambers followed their own internal procedures.

### Thresholds you must know

- A bill passes a chamber by a majority of those voting, assuming a quorum is present.
- In the Senate, ending debate on legislation requires **cloture**: under Senate Rule XXII, three-fifths of the Senators duly chosen and sworn (60 when there are 100). That is a Senate rule, not a constitutional rule.
- After a **veto**, Congress overrides by a two-thirds vote of each chamber (understood as two-thirds of the members present and voting, with a quorum).
- The President has ten days, Sundays excepted, to act once the bill is presented. If the President does nothing and Congress is in session, the bill becomes law. If Congress's adjournment prevents the bill's return, the bill does not become law: the **pocket veto**.

### Why the procedure is strict

*Chadha* held unconstitutional a statute letting one house veto an executive decision to suspend a person's deportation. The one-house veto changed legal rights and so was lawmaking, and lawmaking requires both houses and the President. *Clinton v. City of New York* (1998) held the Line Item Veto Act unconstitutional: it let the President cancel parts of a bill after signing it, but the Presentment Clause gives the President only the choice to approve or reject a bill as a whole. The policy: the Framers deliberately made lawmaking hard, to slow factions and force broad agreement, and they made each step a point at which a different constituency can say no.

### What counts as "the law"

The **Statutes at Large** is the official, chronological record of enacted laws. The **U.S. Code** arranges them by subject. Some titles of the Code have been enacted into **positive law**, so the Code text is itself the law; for the others, the Code is only prima facie evidence (1 U.S.C. § 204), and if the Code and the Statutes at Large conflict, the Statutes at Large control. This is why a careful interpreter checks the enacted text.

### Applying the process (IRAC)

Issue: did a given action satisfy Article I, Section 7? Rule: bicameralism and presentment for any action with the force of law. Application: ask whether the act alters legal rights, duties or relations outside the legislative branch, and whether both houses and the President took part. Conclusion: if it alters rights without the full process, it is invalid.

@example
title: Counting a veto override
problem: The President vetoes a bill. At the override vote 400 members of the House are present and voting, a quorum. What is the smallest number of votes that overrides, and what else must happen before the bill becomes law?
step: The Constitution requires two-thirds of each chamber to override. Two-thirds of the 400 present and voting is $\frac{2}{3}\times 400 = 266.67$.
step: Votes are whole members, and the vote must reach at least two-thirds, so round up: $267$ votes.
step: Check: $266 / 400 = 0.665$ is below two-thirds, while $267 / 400 = 0.6675$ is above it.
step: The override must also succeed in the Senate by two-thirds there. If both chambers override, the bill becomes law without the President's signature.

@mistakes
- Saying a bill needs only majorities in both houses to overcome a veto; the override needs two-thirds of each house.
- Treating the Senate's 60-vote cloture requirement as a constitutional rule rather than a rule the Senate adopted for itself.
- Rounding down when finding two-thirds of a number that does not divide evenly; the threshold must be reached.
- Assuming a committee report or a one-house resolution can have the force of a statute.
