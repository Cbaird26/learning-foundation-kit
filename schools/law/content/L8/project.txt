material: **Sidewalk Delivery Safety Act (invented for this course), selected sections.** Sec. 1 (Findings and purpose). Congress finds that automated delivery of goods to homes and businesses is growing, that automated machines operating on public roads can injure persons, and that safety standards are needed. The purpose of this Act is to protect the public from the dangers of driverless delivery vehicles on public roads. Sec. 2 (Definitions). In this Act: (a) "Autonomous delivery vehicle" means a motor vehicle that performs the entire driving task, without a human driver, in order to carry goods to a place of delivery. (b) "Motor vehicle" means a vehicle that is propelled by an engine or motor and is designed primarily for use on public roads. (c) "Human driver" means a person who is physically present in the vehicle and controls it. Sec. 3 (Standards). (a) The Secretary of Transportation shall issue standards for the safe operation of autonomous delivery vehicles on public roads. (b) In issuing standards, the Secretary shall consider the weight and speed of the vehicles, the density of traffic, and the cost of compliance. Sec. 4 (Exemptions). This Act does not apply to vehicles operated on private campuses or to vehicles operated by the armed forces. Sec. 5 (Effective date). This Act takes effect 180 days after its enactment.

"Autonomous delivery vehicle" means any wheeled machine that carries goods to a place of delivery with no person aboard. Section 3. Standards. ## Week-by-week key ideas

1. **How statutes are made.** Bicameralism and presentment (Art. I, § 7); veto override needs two-thirds of each house; cloture is a Senate rule requiring three-fifths of Senators sworn; *Chadha* and *Clinton v. City of New York* enforce the procedure.
2. **Textualism.** Ordinary meaning in context at enactment; statutory definitions control; absurdity and scrivener's error are narrow exceptions.
3. **Canons.** Semantic: noscitur, ejusdem generis, expressio unius, whole text, surplusage, last antecedent. Substantive: lenity, avoidance, clear statements, against retroactivity and extraterritoriality, against implied repeal.
4. **Legislative history and purpose.** Committee reports rank highest; later inaction ranks lowest; textualists object that history is unenacted; enacted purpose clauses are text.
5. **Agencies and delegation.** Intelligible principle (*Hampton*, *Whitman*, *Consumers' Research*); Appointments Clause (*Lucia*); removal (*Seila Law*, *Collins*, and *Trump v. Slaughter* overruling *Humphrey's Executor*; the Federal Reserve left open in *Trump v. Cook*).
6. **Rulemaking.** Section 553 notice and comment, logical outgrowth, good cause, 30-day effective date, interpretive rules exempt, *Vermont Yankee*.
7. **Adjudication.** Formal versus informal; *Mathews v. Eldridge*; *Chenery*; *Lucia*, *Jarkesy* (jury for SEC civil penalties for fraud) and *Axon*.
8. **Judicial review.** Standing, final agency action, reviewability, § 706(2), *State Farm*, *Corner Post*, *Trump v. CASA*.
9. **Chevron to Loper Bright.** *Chevron* overruled; independent judgment under § 706; prior holdings stand by statutory stare decisis; express delegations; *Kisor* governs regulations.
10. **Skidmore and major questions.** Persuasive weight; clear authorization for questions of vast economic and political significance (*West Virginia v. EPA*, *Learning Resources v. Trump*).
11. **Drafting.** Define terms, vertical lists, "means" versus "includes," mandatory verbs, severability and effective-date clauses.
12. **Comparing methods.** Textualism, purposivism, intentionalism, dynamic interpretation; statutory stare decisis (*Kimble*).

## Common errors

- Teaching Chevron as current law.
- Treating Humphrey's Executor as good law for the FTC.
- Using lenity before exhausting text and canons.
- Applying the arbitrary-and-capricious standard to formal adjudication, which uses substantial evidence.
- Letting legislative history or a title override clear text.
- Counting a statutory period without checking whether the first day is excluded.

@exam mc
prompt: Congress enacts a statute authorizing the Secretary of Commerce to regulate "vessels used in commercial fishing." The Secretary issues a final rule, after notice and comment, applying it to recreational charter boats that carry paying customers to fish. The statute does not define "commercial fishing." A charter company sues, arguing that its boats are outside the statute. The Secretary's explanation is thorough and consistent with 30 years of agency practice, but the statute is ambiguous. Which analysis is most accurate under current law?
choice: The court must uphold the rule if it is a reasonable reading, because the statute is ambiguous and the agency issued the rule through notice and comment.
choice*: The court decides the best reading of "commercial fishing" itself, giving the Secretary's long-standing, reasoned view persuasive Skidmore weight, and sets the rule aside under § 706(2)(C) if the best reading excludes charter boats.
choice: The court may not review the question, because the agency's reading of its own statute is committed to agency discretion by law.
choice: The court must hold the rule invalid, because the major questions doctrine applies to every rule affecting a small industry.
hint: Which standard replaced Chevron, and what role remains for agency views?
hint: Check whether the statute expressly delegates the power to define the term.
solution: After Loper Bright, a court exercises independent judgment under § 706 to decide the best reading; ambiguity alone does not require deference. The agency's thorough, consistent view receives Skidmore weight but does not control. If the best reading excludes charter boats, the rule is in excess of statutory authority under § 706(2)(C). Chevron's reasonableness test no longer applies, review is available, and the major questions doctrine is reserved for extraordinary cases.

@exam ms
prompt: An agency adopts a final legislative rule by notice and comment. The NPRM proposed a fee of 10 units; the final rule adopts a ban. The final rule's preamble does not mention significant comments from regulated firms. The rule takes effect 12 days after publication, and the agency made no good-cause finding. Which grounds are available to a regulated firm that sues promptly?
choice*: The final rule was not a logical outgrowth of a proposal that mentioned only a fee.
choice*: The agency failed to respond to significant comments, so the rule may be arbitrary and capricious.
choice*: The rule's 12-day effective date violates § 553(d), which requires 30 days for a substantive rule absent an exception.
choice: The agency was required to use formal rulemaking with a trial-type hearing, because the rule affects the firm's business.
choice: A court may add cross-examination of agency witnesses as a procedural requirement.
hint: Review § 553(b)-(d) and Vermont Yankee.
hint: Formal rulemaking applies only when a statute requires a hearing on the record.
solution: Logical outgrowth, failure to respond to significant comments, and the 30-day rule are available grounds. Formal rulemaking is not triggered without a statutory on-the-record requirement, and Vermont Yankee bars courts from adding procedures.

@exam self
prompt: Write a 500 to 700 word IRAC memo section on this problem. Statute: "No person shall operate a motorized vehicle, including a car, truck or motorcycle, in Cedar Park." Agency rule (issued by the city's Parks Board after notice and comment under a delegation to define terms for the chapter): "'Motorized vehicle' includes any device with a motor, including pedal-assist electric bicycles." Ana rides a pedal-assist electric bicycle in the park and is charged. Address (a) how a court would read the ordinance itself using text and at least three canons, (b) whether the Board's definition deserves deference under an express delegation, and Skidmore weight if it did not delegate, (c) whether the rule could be challenged for failing to consider less restrictive alternatives, and (d) a one-sentence redraft.
hint: Separate the statutory reading, the standard of review of the agency definition, and the procedural challenge.
solution: Statutory reading: the ordinance prohibits operating a "motorized vehicle," illustrated by a car, truck and motorcycle. Ejusdem generis suggests that the catch-all reaches vehicles similar to the listed road vehicles, which favors Ana, but the word "including" shows the list is illustrative and "motorized" is broad, which favors the prosecution. Under the whole-text canon, a provision that elsewhere permits bicycles would favor Ana; absent one, the question is close. Lenity applies if the ordinance carries criminal penalties and ambiguity remains after the other tools. Standard of review: if the delegation expressly authorizes the Board to define terms, the court asks whether the definition is within the boundaries of the delegation and is the product of reasoned decision-making; if there is no express delegation, the court decides the best reading itself and gives the Board's definition Skidmore weight according to its thoroughness, reasoning and consistency. Challenge to the rule: if the record shows that the Board did not consider alternatives such as a power or speed threshold, or ignored significant comments about e-bikes, the rule may be arbitrary and capricious under State Farm and may be set aside and remanded; the court would not substitute its own policy. Prediction: the rule is likely upheld if the delegation is express and the Board reasoned; otherwise the court decides the best reading and the question is close, with lenity favoring Ana if penalties are criminal. Redraft: "'Motorized vehicle' means a vehicle propelled solely or mainly by an engine or motor, and does not include a bicycle whose motor only assists pedaling and cannot propel it above 20 miles per hour."
rubric: Applies text and at least three canons (such as ejusdem generis, whole text, expressio unius and lenity) to the ordinance.
rubric: Distinguishes express-delegation review from Skidmore weight and states each accurately.
rubric: Identifies the State Farm ground based on alternatives and comments.
rubric: Gives a prediction and a clear one-sentence redraft.
