@article
Rulemaking sets general rules for the future; **adjudication** decides particular cases. The APA defines an **order** as the whole or part of a final disposition of an agency matter other than rulemaking, and **adjudication** as the agency process for formulating an order (5 U.S.C. § 551(6)-(7)). Licensing, benefits decisions, immigration hearings and enforcement actions are adjudications.

### Formal and informal adjudication

**Formal adjudication** is required when a statute requires the matter to be "determined on the record after opportunity for an agency hearing" (§ 554(a)). Then the APA requires notice, an impartial presiding officer (often an **administrative law judge**, or ALJ), a trial-type hearing, evidence and cross-examination (§ 556), and a decision based on the exclusive record (§ 557). Sections 554(d) and 557(d) limit **ex parte contacts** and separate investigative and prosecutorial functions from adjudicative ones. On review, factual findings in formal adjudications are tested under the **substantial evidence** standard (*Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB* (1951)). **Informal adjudication** (everything else) is governed only by the APA's minimal provisions, the agency's own rules and the Constitution.

### Procedural due process

When government deprives a person of life, liberty or property, the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments require adequate procedure. The threshold question is whether the person has a protected interest (*Board of Regents v. Roth* (1972): a legitimate claim of entitlement, such as ongoing benefits or a license). *Goldberg v. Kelly* (1970) held that welfare recipients were entitled to an evidentiary hearing before termination. *Mathews v. Eldridge* (1976) then established the balancing test for how much process is due, weighing:

1. the private interest affected;
2. the risk of erroneous deprivation under current procedures and the probable value of additional safeguards; and
3. the government's interest, including the fiscal and administrative burdens of extra procedures.

Applying it, the Court held in *Mathews* that a Social Security disability recipient was not entitled to an evidentiary hearing before benefits ended, because written medical evidence, notice and a full post-termination hearing gave adequate protection.

### Adjudication versus rulemaking: Londoner and Bi-Metallic

When the government acts on a few individuals based on facts specific to them, due process generally requires an individual hearing (*Londoner v. Denver* (1908)). When it acts on a large group by a general rule, the political process, not individual hearings, is the check (*Bi-Metallic Investment Co. v. State Board of Equalization* (1915)).

### Chenery and policy by adjudication

*SEC v. Chenery Corp.* (1943) (*Chenery I*) holds that a court reviewing agency action may uphold it only on the grounds the agency itself invoked; if the stated ground is wrong, the court must remand rather than supply its own. *Chenery II* (1947) allows an agency to announce new policy through adjudication rather than rulemaking, a choice within the agency's discretion, though reliance and fairness concerns may limit retroactive application (*NLRB v. Bell Aerospace Co.* (1974)). When an agency changes its position, it must acknowledge the change and give a reasoned explanation (*FCC v. Fox Television Stations, Inc.* (2009)).

### Structural challenges

- **Appointments**: *Lucia v. SEC* (2018) treated SEC ALJs as officers; the remedy was a new hearing before a properly appointed, different official.
- **Jury trial**: *SEC v. Jarkesy* (2024) held that when the SEC seeks civil penalties for securities fraud, the Seventh Amendment entitles the defendant to a jury. Fraud is a legal claim akin to common-law fraud, and the "public rights" exception did not permit the agency to adjudicate it internally. The decision concerns this kind of penalty claim; it does not abolish agency adjudication of benefits or licensing matters.
- **Forum for constitutional challenges**: *Axon Enterprise, Inc. v. FTC* (2023) held that a party may bring a structural constitutional challenge to an agency's proceeding (for example, to the agency's structure or its ALJs' removal protection) in district court without first litigating the matter before the agency and its statutory review scheme.

### Policy and application

Adjudication offers flexibility and expertise, and the cost is procedural unfairness risk. In a problem: (1) is the proceeding formal or informal? (2) is there a protected interest? (3) apply *Mathews*; (4) check the agency's stated grounds (*Chenery*); (5) check structure (appointments, jury, forum).

@example
title: Applying Mathews v. Eldridge
problem: A state licensing board suspends a barber's license after receiving a written complaint. The barber receives written notice, an opportunity to submit a response and evidence within 10 days, and a full hearing within 30 days of suspension. The barber demands a full hearing before any suspension. Is the process adequate?
step: Protected interest: a professional license is a property interest, so due process applies.
step: Weigh the three factors: the private interest is significant (livelihood); the risk of error is reduced by the written response opportunity and prompt hearing; the government's interest in protecting the public from alleged hazards is strong.
step: Compare with Mathews: where written submissions and a prompt post-deprivation hearing provide adequate protection, a pre-suspension trial-type hearing is not required.
step: Conclusion: likely adequate. The result might change if the suspension were long, the facts disputed and credibility central, or the hearing delayed.

@mistakes
- Assuming every agency decision needs a trial-type hearing; formal procedures apply only when a statute requires a decision on the record.
- Forgetting the threshold question whether a protected interest exists.
- Having a court affirm on a ground the agency did not rely on, contrary to *Chenery I*.
- Reading *Jarkesy* to forbid all agency adjudication rather than the specific penalty claim.

@practice self
prompt: Explain the Chenery doctrine: state what Chenery I and Chenery II each held, give an example of how a court applies Chenery I when an agency's stated reason is flawed, and explain how Chenery II affects an agency's choice between rulemaking and adjudication.
hint: Chenery I concerns the grounds on which a court may uphold action; Chenery II concerns the choice of procedure.
solution: Chenery I (1943): a reviewing court may affirm an agency action only on the grounds the agency invoked. If an agency relies on a mistaken legal ground, the court may not substitute a ground it finds persuasive; it must remand so the agency can reconsider. For example, if an agency denies a permit on the ground that a statute forbids it, and the court finds the statute does not, the court remands even though the agency might have denied the permit as a matter of discretion. Chenery II (1947): an agency may announce new policy through case-by-case adjudication, and need not use rulemaking, subject to limits where retroactive application would cause serious unfairness (Bell Aerospace). So agencies have discretion to choose between the two procedures.
rubric: States Chenery I accurately, including the remand consequence.
rubric: Gives an example showing a flawed stated ground.
rubric: States Chenery II accurately.
rubric: Notes the limit based on reliance and fairness.

@quiz mc
prompt: An agency denies a company's application for a permit, saying that the statute bars permits for the company's activity. On review, the court concludes the statute does not bar the permit but believes that the agency could properly have denied it on a ground the agency never mentioned. What should the court do?
choice: Affirm the denial on the ground the court thinks is better.
choice: Order the agency to grant the permit immediately.
choice*: Set aside the denial and remand for the agency to reconsider, because the court may uphold agency action only on the grounds the agency invoked.
choice: Dismiss the case because the denial was within the agency's discretion.
hint: Recall Chenery I.
solution: Chenery I forbids a court from affirming on a ground the agency did not invoke. The right course is a remand, and the agency may reach the same outcome on proper grounds.
