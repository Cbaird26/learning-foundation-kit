@article
Canons of construction are conventional presumptions about how texts are read. They are not law passed by Congress, and each can be overcome by contrary text or context. Learn them in two families.

### Semantic (linguistic) canons

These infer meaning from how language ordinarily works.

- **Noscitur a sociis**: a word is known by the company it keeps. In *Gustafson v. Alloyd Co.* (1995) the Court read "prospectus" in the Securities Act in light of the other terms around it, concluding it meant a document soliciting the public to purchase securities from the issuer.
- **Ejusdem generis**: when a list of specific items ends in a general catch-all ("cars, trucks, buses or other vehicles"), the catch-all covers things of the same kind as the list.
- **Expressio unius est exclusio alterius**: expressing one thing excludes others. If a statute exempts churches, schools and hospitals, a reader infers that libraries are not exempt. It works only where the legislature would naturally have listed everything it meant.
- **Whole-text canon**: read each provision in light of the whole statute. Related to it are the **presumption of consistent usage** (the same word usually means the same thing in one statute) and the **presumption against surplusage** (give effect to every word, if possible).
- **Rule of the last antecedent**: a limiting phrase ordinarily modifies only the closest preceding item (*Barnhart v. Thomas* (2003)), but not when context shows it modifies the whole series.

*Yates v. United States* (2015) shows the semantic canons at work. A federal statute on destroying a "tangible object" with intent to impede an investigation followed the words "record" and "document." A plurality held that a fish was not a "tangible object" in that context, relying on the statute's title, its neighboring terms and the canons. The dissent argued the ordinary meaning of "tangible object" plainly included a fish. The case shows that canons assist argument; they do not end it.

### Substantive canons

These are policy-based presumptions that tilt the reading toward a value, even if language alone would not.

- **Rule of lenity**: ambiguity in a criminal statute is resolved in favor of the defendant, but only if, after consulting text, structure and history, a "grievous ambiguity" remains (*Muscarello*). It protects fair notice. It is a last resort, not a first.
- **Constitutional avoidance**: where one fairly possible reading raises serious constitutional doubts and another does not, choose the latter. It does not allow a court to rewrite a clear text.
- **Clear-statement rules**: Congress must speak clearly to alter the federal-state balance (*Gregory v. Ashcroft* (1991)), to abrogate state sovereign immunity, or to authorize extraordinary results. The major questions doctrine, studied in Lesson 10, is a modern example.
- **Presumption against extraterritoriality**: a statute applies only within U.S. territory unless Congress clearly indicates otherwise (*Morrison v. National Australia Bank* (2010)).
- **Presumption against retroactivity**: a statute that would impair rights a party had when it acted, or add new liability for past conduct, does not apply retroactively without clear intent (*Landgraf v. USI Film Products* (1994)).
- **Presumption against implied repeal**, and the related rule that **the specific governs the general**: a later statute is not read to repeal an earlier one unless the two are irreconcilable or the intent is clear (*Morton v. Mancari* (1974)), and a specific provision controls over a conflicting general one (*RadLAX Gateway Hotel, LLC v. Amalgamated Bank* (2012)).

### Order of operations and policy

Start with text, definitions and the semantic canons, since they help find the meaning Congress most likely expressed. Turn to substantive canons only where real ambiguity remains; they are tie-breakers grounded in constitutional values and fair notice. Canons also conflict (expressio unius against ejusdem generis, for instance), so a good argument explains why one fits the facts better.

### Applying them (IRAC)

Issue: what does the ambiguous term mean? Rule: identify the canon and its limit. Application: show how the text, the neighboring words and the structure point one way, and answer the best counter-canon. Conclusion: predict the result and flag the assumption on which it depends.

@example
title: A catch-all at the end of a list
problem: A city ordinance forbids anyone to "bring a car, truck, bus or other vehicle into the Old Town plaza." Ana wheels a baby stroller into the plaza. Does the ordinance reach her?
step: Identify the interpretive question: does "other vehicle" cover a stroller?
step: Apply ejusdem generis: the listed items (car, truck, bus) are motor vehicles, so "other vehicle" is most naturally read to cover similar motorized vehicles.
step: Check the whole-text canon: if the ordinance elsewhere speaks of "engine noise" or "fuel," that supports the motor-vehicle reading; the surplusage canon is satisfied because "other vehicle" still covers, say, motorcycles.
step: Conclusion: the stroller is not an "other vehicle" under the ordinance, and the leading counterargument (the dictionary meaning of "vehicle" is any conveyance) is outweighed by the neighboring terms.

@mistakes
- Using a canon as if it were a binding rule rather than a presumption that context can defeat.
- Invoking the rule of lenity before exhausting text, structure and the other canons.
- Applying expressio unius to a list that was obviously not meant to be exhaustive.
- Using constitutional avoidance to rewrite clear text rather than to choose between fairly possible readings.

prompt: A statute makes it a crime to "sell, trade, give away or otherwise dispose of a controlled substance without a license." Buyer, an unlicensed person, flushes a controlled substance down a toilet to avoid detection. Prosecutors charge Buyer under the statute. Applying ejusdem generis, is the charge likely to succeed?
choice: Yes, because "otherwise dispose of" covers any act that rids the person of the substance.
choice*: No, because the listed verbs all describe transferring the substance to another, and "otherwise dispose of" is read to cover similar transfers.
choice: No, because criminal statutes must always be read narrowly without regard to context.
choice: Yes, because ejusdem generis applies only to lists of nouns, not verbs.
hint: What do sell, trade and give away have in common?
solution: Each listed verb transfers the substance to someone else, so the catch-all is read in kind. Destroying the substance is not a transfer. The canon applies to verbs as well as nouns, and criminal statutes are not read narrowly "always" (lenity is a last resort).

@practice self
prompt: In your own words, distinguish semantic from substantive canons. Name two of each, say when a court turns to substantive canons, and give one reason canons are not binding law.
hint: Identify the issue and the governing rule first, then apply it element by element to the facts.
solution: Semantic canons draw inferences from how language works: for example noscitur a sociis (a word takes meaning from its neighbors) and ejusdem generis (a general catch-all is read like the specific items preceding it). Substantive canons advance a value or protect a background principle: for example the rule of lenity (fair notice in criminal law) and constitutional avoidance (preference for readings that do not raise serious constitutional doubts). A court turns to substantive canons when text, context and semantic canons leave real ambiguity, and as tie-breakers rather than first steps. Canons are not binding law because Congress did not enact them; they are judge-made presumptions that give way to contrary text, context or purpose, and they sometimes point in opposite directions.

@quiz mc
prompt: A statute authorizes a federal agency to regulate "any activity affecting the public health." Under one fairly possible reading the statute would reach the private religious practices of individuals and raise a serious First Amendment question; under another fairly possible reading it reaches only commercial activity. A court prefers the second. The court is most likely applying:
choice: The rule of lenity.
choice*: The canon of constitutional avoidance.
choice: The presumption against retroactivity.
choice: Expressio unius.
hint: Which canon directs a court to choose between two fairly possible readings in light of a constitutional doubt?
solution: Avoidance directs a court to choose a fairly possible reading that avoids serious constitutional doubt. Lenity applies to criminal ambiguity, retroactivity to timing and expressio unius to lists.

@quiz mc
prompt: In 2024 Congress enacts a statute that increases the civil penalty for a type of safety violation. The statute is silent on timing. Company committed the violation in 2022 and is sued in 2025 under the new penalty. Which result is likely?
choice: The higher penalty applies, because courts always apply the law in force when the suit is filed.
choice: The higher penalty applies, because penalties are procedural.
choice*: The old penalty applies, because absent a clear statement a statute is not applied to increase liability for conduct completed before it was enacted.
choice: Neither penalty applies, because the statute is void for retroactivity.
hint: What presumption protects parties who acted under existing law?
solution: Under Landgraf, the presumption against retroactivity means that a statute that would attach new legal consequences to completed conduct does not apply to it unless Congress clearly says so. Retroactive civil statutes are not void; they merely need a clear statement.
