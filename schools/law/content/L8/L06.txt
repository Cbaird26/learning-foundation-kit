@article
A **rule** is the whole or a part of an agency statement "of general or particular applicability and future effect" designed to implement, interpret or prescribe law or policy (5 U.S.C. § 551(4)). The APA gives agencies three main tracks.

### Informal (notice-and-comment) rulemaking, § 553

Most rules are made this way. The steps are:

1. **Notice of proposed rulemaking (NPRM)** published in the Federal Register, stating the time, place and nature of the proceedings, the legal authority, and either the terms or substance of the proposed rule or a description of the subjects and issues.
2. **Opportunity to comment**: interested persons may submit written data, views or arguments. The APA fixes no minimum period; agencies commonly allow 30 to 60 days.
3. **Consideration and a final rule** with "a concise general statement of [its] basis and purpose." Courts require the agency to respond to significant comments.
4. **Publication and effective date**: a substantive rule must be published at least 30 days before it takes effect, § 553(d), unless the rule relieves a restriction, is interpretive or a policy statement, or the agency finds good cause.

The final rule must be a **logical outgrowth** of the proposal: the notice must have fairly alerted the public to the subjects and issues ultimately adopted (*Long Island Care at Home, Ltd. v. Coke* (2007)). The President's regulatory review office (OIRA) reviews significant rules under Executive Order 12866, and Congress may disapprove a final rule through a joint resolution under the Congressional Review Act (5 U.S.C. §§ 801-808), which is subject to veto.

### Exemptions, § 553(a)-(b)

Notice and comment is not required for (a) military or foreign-affairs functions and matters of agency management, personnel, public property, loans, grants, benefits or contracts; (b) **interpretive rules**, **general statements of policy**, and rules of agency organization, procedure or practice; or for **good cause**, when the agency finds notice and comment "impracticable, unnecessary, or contrary to the public interest," and states its finding with the rule. Courts read good cause narrowly: an emergency qualifies; administrative convenience does not.

### Legislative versus interpretive rules

A **legislative rule** is issued under delegated authority and has the force of law (*Chrysler Corp. v. Brown* (1979)); it requires notice and comment. An **interpretive rule** only states the agency's view of what a statute or regulation means, and does not have the force of law. A **policy statement** announces how the agency intends to exercise discretion. An agency may amend or repeal an interpretive rule without notice and comment (*Perez v. Mortgage Bankers Ass'n* (2015)). Where a statute separately requires notice and comment for a new substantive legal standard, as the Medicare Act does, the agency must follow it even when it calls its policy "interpretive" (*Azar v. Allina Health Services* (2019)).

### Formal rulemaking and procedural limits

Formal rulemaking, with trial-type hearings under §§ 556-557, applies only when a statute requires a rule "to be made on the record after opportunity for an agency hearing." Words such as "after hearing" are not enough (*United States v. Florida East Coast Railway Co.* (1973)). Courts may not add procedures beyond the APA's minimum: *Vermont Yankee Nuclear Power Corp. v. NRDC* (1978) rejected a court's order that an agency employ extra procedural devices, though courts may still review the substance of the agency's reasoning. Anyone may petition for a rule under § 553(e) (see *Massachusetts v. EPA* (2007) on review of denials).

### Policy and application

Notice and comment promotes accountability, informed decisions and public participation. The exemptions exist because the procedure is costly. To analyze a problem, ask: is it a rule? is it legislative or exempt? did the agency give adequate notice? did the final rule follow logically from the proposal? did it respond to significant comments? is the effective-date period satisfied?

@example
title: A final rule that surprises the commenters
problem: An agency proposes to limit emissions from Plant Type A to 10 units. The final rule instead bans Plant Type A altogether, and no part of the notice mentioned a ban or any alternative to emission limits. A plant sues. Is the final rule vulnerable?
step: Identify the rule: a legislative rule under delegated authority, so § 553 notice-and-comment applies and no exemption fits.
step: Apply the logical-outgrowth test: did the notice fairly apprise the public of the subjects and issues adopted?
step: Compare: a limit of 10 units and a total ban are very different regulatory approaches, and the notice mentioned only a limit.
step: Conclusion: likely invalid for inadequate notice. The agency should issue a supplemental proposal. A change to 8 units, by contrast, would be a logical outgrowth.

@mistakes
- Treating every agency statement as a rule requiring notice and comment; interpretive rules and policy statements are exempt.
- Using "good cause" for administrative convenience rather than emergency or genuine impracticability.
- Letting a court impose extra procedures beyond the APA, which Vermont Yankee forbids.
- Forgetting the 30-day effective-date period in § 553(d), or its exceptions.
