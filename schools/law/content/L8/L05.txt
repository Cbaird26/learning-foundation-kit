@article
Agencies exist because Congress passes statutes that create them and give them power. Under the APA an **agency** is "each authority of the Government of the United States," with named exceptions such as Congress and the federal courts (5 U.S.C. § 551(1)). The President is not an "agency" for APA purposes (*Franklin v. Massachusetts* (1992)). Three constitutional questions follow: may Congress delegate? who may appoint agency officers? and who may remove them?

### Delegation

Article I vests "all legislative Powers" in Congress. Yet the Court has long held that Congress may delegate rulemaking authority if it supplies an **intelligible principle** to guide the delegate (*J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States* (1928)). Only twice, both in 1935, has the Court struck down a statute on this ground: *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan* and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, where the statute gave the President or industry groups virtually unbounded discretion. Since then the Court has found the test satisfied by broad standards such as "public interest" and, in *Whitman v. American Trucking Ass'ns* (2001), air-quality standards "requisite to protect the public health." *Mistretta v. United States* (1989) upheld the Sentencing Commission. In *Gundy v. United States* (2019) a plurality upheld a broad delegation to the Attorney General, while other Justices signaled interest in reconsidering the test. Most recently, *FCC v. Consumers' Research* (2025) upheld the Universal Service Fund's funding mechanism against a nondelegation challenge, applying the intelligible-principle test: Congress had set a standard, and the agency's role was to fill in details. Delegation doctrine today is therefore largely about statutory interpretation (the canons and the major questions doctrine of Lesson 10) rather than invalidation. A separate line of cases forbids delegation of government power to private parties, such as *Carter v. Carter Coal Co.* (1936).

### Appointment

The **Appointments Clause** requires that **principal officers** be nominated by the President and confirmed by the Senate; **inferior officers** may be appointed by the President, the courts or the heads of departments if Congress so provides. A person who exercises significant authority under federal law is an **officer** (*Buckley v. Valeo* (1976)). *Lucia v. SEC* (2018) held that the SEC's administrative law judges were officers who had not been properly appointed, so the remedy was a new hearing before a different, properly appointed official. Inferior-officer status turns on supervision by a principal officer (*Edmond v. United States* (1997); *United States v. Arthrex, Inc.* (2021)).

### Removal

The President's power to remove executive officers is part of "the executive Power" (*Myers v. United States* (1926)). For nearly ninety years *Humphrey's Executor v. United States* (1935) allowed Congress to protect multimember expert commissions from at-will removal. The Court narrowed it: *Morrison v. Olson* (1988) upheld protection for an inferior officer; *Free Enterprise Fund v. PCAOB* (2010) struck down two layers of for-cause protection; *Seila Law LLC v. CFPB* (2020) struck down for-cause removal for the single director of the CFPB; and *Collins v. Yellen* (2021) did the same for the FHFA director, adding that such a defect does not automatically void the agency's actions unless the challenger shows the removal restriction caused harm. In *Trump v. Slaughter* (June 2026, 6-3) the Court overruled *Humphrey's Executor*: the FTC Act's for-cause protection (inefficiency, neglect of duty or malfeasance in office) is unconstitutional, so FTC commissioners are removable at will. The Court left open the Federal Reserve: in *Trump v. Cook* (2026) it refused to let the removal of a Federal Reserve governor take effect while that case continues. It also left open offices outside the traditional executive branch. Congress itself may not retain removal power over executive officers (*Bowsher v. Synar* (1986)) or control execution through a legislative veto (*INS v. Chadha*).

### Policy and application

The functional argument for delegation is expertise and capacity: Congress cannot write every rule. The structural argument for presidential control is accountability: voters should know whom to blame. In an exam, take each issue in order. Issue one: is the statute's standard adequate under the intelligible-principle test? Issue two: was the actor appointed in compliance with the Appointments Clause? Issue three: is any removal restriction valid after *Slaughter*, and is the agency one the Court has expressly reserved?

@example
title: Testing a delegation
problem: A statute directs the Secretary of Health to set ambient limits for a pollutant "at the level requisite to protect the public health, allowing an adequate margin of safety." A trade group says the delegation is unconstitutional. Is it likely valid?
step: Identify the delegation: Congress has told the Secretary to set a numerical limit, which is a legislative-type power.
step: Find the standard: "requisite to protect the public health" with an "adequate margin of safety" sets the objective, and the agency must find the level that meets it.
step: Compare to precedent: *Whitman* upheld a nearly identical standard; the Court has struck down statutes only where there was no standard at all.
step: Conclusion: valid; the challenger's best argument would be to a future Court reconsidering the test, not to current doctrine.

@mistakes
- Saying the Court has frequently invalidated statutes under the nondelegation doctrine; it has done so only twice, in 1935.
- Treating *Humphrey's Executor* as still good law for the FTC after *Trump v. Slaughter*.
- Confusing the Appointments Clause (who may appoint) with the removal power (who may fire).
- Assuming a removal-restriction defect automatically invalidates every act of the agency (*Collins*).
