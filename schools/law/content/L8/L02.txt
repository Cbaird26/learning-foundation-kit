@article
Every statutory case starts in the same place: the words enacted. **Textualism** holds that the interpreter's task is to find the **ordinary meaning of the text in context**, the meaning a reasonable reader would have taken it to have when it was enacted. The **plain meaning rule** says that when the text is clear, the inquiry ends and the court applies it (*Caminetti v. United States* (1917)). Textualists justify this on grounds of democratic legitimacy (only the enacted text passed both houses and was presented to the President, as Lesson 1 explained), predictability and notice, and judicial restraint.

### The tools of text

1. **Statutory definitions control.** When Congress supplies a definition, the court follows it even if it departs from ordinary usage (*Burgess v. United States* (2008)). Read the definitions section first.
2. **Ordinary meaning.** Absent a definition, give words their ordinary meaning, often shown by dictionaries from the time of enactment (*Wisconsin Central Ltd. v. United States* (2018) asked what "money" meant when the statute was passed). The meaning is fixed at enactment, but it may apply to new facts that fall within it.
3. **Context.** Words are read in the whole sentence, the whole section and the whole statute. *Muscarello v. United States* (1998) read "carries a firearm" to include carrying one in a vehicle's locked glove compartment, relying on ordinary usage; the dissent found the ordinary sense narrower. Textual analysis is not mechanical, and reasonable readers can disagree.
4. **Grammar.** Number, tense, punctuation and the scope of modifiers matter. *Niz-Chavez v. Garland* (2021) held that "a notice" means a single document.
5. **Structure and titles.** Headings and titles may help resolve doubt, but they cannot override a clear text.

*Bostock v. Clayton County* (2020) shows textualism applied to a contested result: the Court read Title VII's "because of ... sex" to require but-for causation and held that firing an employee for being homosexual or transgender discriminates because of sex. The dissents argued that the ordinary meaning of "sex" in 1964 and the ordinary understanding of the statute pointed the other way. Both sides argued from text, which is the point: textualism narrows the sources of argument, not the room for disagreement.

### The exceptions

Courts depart from clear text in two narrow situations. The **absurdity doctrine** applies when applying the text literally would produce a result so absurd that no reasonable legislator could have intended it; disagreement with the policy is not absurdity. *Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States* (1892) refused to apply a statute banning the importation of foreign labor to a church hiring a foreign pastor, reasoning from the statute's evident purpose; later textualists treat that case as a caution, not a model. A **scrivener's error** (an obvious clerical mistake, such as a wrong cross-reference) may be corrected where the error is apparent from the text itself.

### The policy

Textualism protects reliance (citizens can read the law), constrains judges, and respects the bargains struck in enacted text. The main criticisms are that it can produce results Congress did not foresee and that "plain" meaning is often contestable. As you read cases, keep both in view.

### Applying it (IRAC)

Issue: does the statute cover the conduct? Rule: apply the enacted text, using definitions, ordinary meaning and context. Application: parse each element, check for a definition, then read it in context. Conclusion: if the text is clear, apply it even if the result seems unwise.

@example
title: Definition beats ordinary usage
problem: A statute provides: "Section 3. No person shall sell a firearm to a minor. Section 1 defines 'firearm' as 'any weapon that expels a projectile by the action of an explosive.'" Seller sells an air rifle powered by compressed air to a 15-year-old. Has Seller violated Section 3?
step: Identify the operative term: "firearm." Check whether the statute defines it. It does.
step: Apply the definition, not a dictionary: the weapon must expel a projectile by the action of an explosive.
step: Compressed air is not an explosive, so the air rifle falls outside the definition even though many people would call it a gun.
step: Conclusion: no violation of Section 3 on this text. Any argument that Congress meant to cover air rifles must be made to the legislature, because a court following the text applies the definition as written.

@mistakes
- Using a dictionary or common usage when the statute supplies its own definition.
- Reading one phrase alone instead of in the sentence and statute in which it sits.
- Treating a result you dislike as "absurd"; the doctrine requires a result no reasonable legislator could have intended.
- Letting a title or heading override clear operative text.

@practice self
prompt: Read this supplied statute and interpret it by its text alone. "Sec. 4. (a) No person shall operate a commercial drone within 500 feet of a hospital without a permit. (b) In this section, 'commercial drone' means an unmanned aircraft operated for compensation. (c) A permit shall be issued to any applicant who certifies compliance with Section 5." Dana flies, without any payment and without a permit, an unmanned aircraft that carries donated blood samples to a hospital's landing pad. Has Dana violated Section 4(a)? Write an IRAC answer that stays inside the text.
hint: Identify the issue and the governing rule first, then apply it element by element to the facts.
solution: Issue: whether Dana's flight is covered by Section 4(a). Rule: apply the enacted text, and a statutory definition controls over ordinary meaning. Application: Section 4(a) reaches only a "commercial drone," which Section 4(b) defines as an unmanned aircraft operated for compensation. Dana's aircraft is unmanned and flew within 500 feet of a hospital without a permit, but Dana received no compensation, so the aircraft is not a commercial drone under the definition. Dana's purpose (medical, charitable) is not an element and plays no role. A counterargument that Congress meant to cover all drones near hospitals runs against the definition; it would have to be made to the legislature. Conclusion: no violation of Section 4(a) on the text.
rubric: Identifies the operative term and applies the statutory definition rather than ordinary meaning.
rubric: Applies each element to the facts, including the compensation element.
rubric: Does not rely on purpose, policy or legislative history.
rubric: Reaches a clear conclusion and notes the legislative, not judicial, fix.
