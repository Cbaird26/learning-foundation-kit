@article
The APA gives a person "suffering legal wrong because of agency action, or adversely affected or aggrieved by such agency action within the meaning of a relevant statute" a right to judicial review (5 U.S.C. § 702). Review has two stages: can the court hear the case, and if so, what standard does it apply?

### Threshold: can the court hear it?

- **Standing**: injury in fact, caused by the agency's action and redressable by a favorable decision (*Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife* (1992)), and the plaintiff's interest must be within the zone of interests the statute protects. A state or party generally cannot challenge an agency's enforcement priorities for lack of standing (*United States v. Texas* (2023)).
- **Final agency action** (§ 704): the action must mark the consummation of the agency's decision-making process and be one by which rights or obligations are determined or from which legal consequences flow (*Bennett v. Spear* (1997)). A proposed rule is not final.
- **Presumption of reviewability**: courts presume agency action is reviewable unless Congress has indicated otherwise (*Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner* (1967)).
- **Exceptions** (§ 701(a)): review is unavailable where a statute precludes it, or where action is "committed to agency discretion by law," meaning there is no law to apply (*Citizens to Preserve Overton Park v. Volpe* (1971)). An agency's decision not to bring an enforcement action is presumptively unreviewable (*Heckler v. Chaney* (1985)).
- **Timing**: a claim accrues when the plaintiff is injured, so a business formed after a rule issued may challenge it within six years of its own injury (*Corner Post, Inc. v. Board of Governors* (2024), applying 28 U.S.C. § 2401(a)).

### Grounds of review, § 706(2)

A court shall set aside agency action that is: (A) arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law; (B) contrary to constitutional right; (C) in excess of statutory jurisdiction, authority or limitations; (D) without observance of required procedure; (E) unsupported by substantial evidence in a case subject to §§ 556-557 or otherwise reviewed on the record of an agency hearing; or (F) unwarranted by the facts to the extent they are subject to trial de novo. Section 706 also directs the reviewing court to decide all relevant questions of law, a point central to Lesson 9.

### Arbitrary and capricious review

Informal rulemaking is reviewed under § 706(2)(A). *Citizens to Preserve Overton Park v. Volpe* required a "thorough, probing, in-depth review" but said the standard is narrow: the court may not substitute its judgment for the agency's. *Motor Vehicle Manufacturers Ass'n v. State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co.* (1983) states when a rule is arbitrary and capricious: if the agency (1) relied on factors Congress did not intend it to consider, (2) entirely failed to consider an important aspect of the problem, (3) offered an explanation that runs counter to the evidence, or (4) offered an explanation so implausible that it could not be ascribed to a difference in view or agency expertise. The agency must show a rational connection between the facts found and the choice made, and it must consider reasonable alternatives within the scope of its action. Related rules:

- **Change of position**: an agency must acknowledge a change and give good reasons, and consider serious reliance interests (*FCC v. Fox Television*; *Department of Homeland Security v. Regents of the University of California* (2020)).
- **Genuine reasons**: the stated reason must be the real one (*Department of Commerce v. New York* (2019)).
- **Comments**: the agency must respond to significant comments (see *Ohio v. EPA* (2024)).
- **Chenery**: the court judges the agency by its own stated reasoning.

### Remedy

The usual remedy is to **set aside** (vacate) the unlawful action and remand to the agency. *Trump v. CASA, Inc.* (2025) held that universal injunctions likely exceed federal courts' equitable authority, limiting injunctive relief to what is needed to give the plaintiffs complete relief; the Court did not decide whether the APA authorizes vacatur of agency action, a question that remains debated.

### Policy and application

The deferential standard respects agency expertise and the separation of powers, while the requirement of reasoned explanation protects the public from arbitrary government. To analyze a problem: threshold issues first; then identify the § 706 ground; then, for a rule, run through the four *State Farm* categories; finally state the remedy.

@example
title: A rule that ignored its own data
problem: An agency repeals a rule requiring trucks to install a safety device, explaining only that the device is "costly." The agency's own study in the record found that the device cuts fatal crashes by 30 percent, and the agency did not address the study or consider requiring a cheaper version. Is the repeal arbitrary and capricious?
step: Threshold: the repeal is a final rule, and a trucking-safety group injured by it has standing.
step: Apply State Farm: did the agency consider an important aspect of the problem? It ignored the safety benefit in its own study.
step: Did it consider alternatives within its action? It did not examine a cheaper version of the device.
step: Conclusion: likely arbitrary and capricious. The court sets the repeal aside and remands; the agency may repeal again with a reasoned explanation.

@mistakes
- Substituting the court's judgment for the agency's; the standard is narrow and asks only whether the agency's reasoning is reasoned.
- Skipping the threshold questions (standing, final agency action, reviewability).
- Using the arbitrary-and-capricious standard for a formal adjudication, which uses the substantial-evidence standard.
- Treating an agency's decision not to enforce as ordinarily reviewable.
