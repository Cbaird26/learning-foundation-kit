@article
You now have the tools. This lesson compares how judges combine them and ends with the discipline of fixing a text so that the dispute never arises.

### The methods

- **Textualism**: the meaning is the ordinary public meaning of the enacted text in context, at the time of enactment. Legitimate sources: text, definitions, structure, canons, dictionaries and contemporaneous usage. Legislative history is generally rejected for establishing meaning. (*Caminetti v. United States* (1917); *Bostock v. Clayton County* (2020) majority.)
- **Purposivism**: the meaning is what a reasonable legislator would have wanted to achieve, given the problem the statute addressed. Sources: text first, then purposes drawn from findings, structure and legislative history. Where text is clear, modern purposivists generally follow it. (*Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States* (1892).)
- **Intentionalism**: the meaning is the actual intent of the enacting legislators, found in history and drafting. Few judges use it in pure form, because a multimember body has no single intent. A variant, **imaginative reconstruction**, asks how the enacting legislature would have resolved the question had it considered it.
- **Dynamic (evolutive) interpretation**: the meaning of an old statute may evolve to fit present circumstances and values. It is a minority approach in federal courts and more common in discussions of statutes with broad common-law-like language.
- **Pragmatic or consequentialist approaches** weigh the practical consequences of competing readings; most judges use consequences as a check, such as the absurdity doctrine, rather than as a method.

Corpus linguistics (searching large bodies of text for the ordinary usage of a word) has been used in some state courts and in individual opinions as a tool for textualist inquiry.

### Convergence and divergence

In most cases the methods reach the same result because the text is clear, or the structure and canons resolve it. They diverge in hard cases: where the text read literally produces a result inconsistent with the evident purpose, where history suggests a narrower or broader reading than the words, or where the language is old and facts new. *Muscarello* and *Yates* each produced divided Courts in which Justices on both sides argued from text.

### Evaluating methods

Five criteria recur in the debate:

1. **Democratic legitimacy**: does the method respect the enacted text that passed bicameralism and presentment?
2. **Predictability and notice**: can citizens and lawyers know the law in advance?
3. **Institutional competence**: can judges reliably find history, purpose and consequences?
4. **Fidelity to legislative bargains**: does the method preserve compromises struck in the text?
5. **Flexibility**: can the law adjust to circumstances Congress did not foresee?

Textualists emphasize the first three; purposivists emphasize the fourth and fifth and respond that text always has a context. Neutral practitioners argue in the way the judge before them prefers.

### Stare decisis and statutory change

Statutory precedents get **special weight**: Congress can correct a misreading by amending the statute, so courts are slow to overrule their own statutory interpretations (*Kimble v. Marvel Entertainment, LLC* (2015)). Contrast constitutional precedents, which only the Court or an amendment can change. State courts apply a variety of methods and statutes of construction, so check the jurisdiction.

### From interpretation to redrafting

A good lawyer asks of a provision, "Where could two judges using different methods disagree?" and removes those points by drafting. That means defining terms, restating scope with vertical lists, fixing the placement of modifiers, and stating the purpose where the legislature wants it considered. The test of a redraft: it should yield the intended result under every method.

### Applying it (IRAC)

Issue: how do the competing methods resolve the question? Rule: state each method's sources. Application: run the facts through two methods and note whether they converge. Conclusion: state the more persuasive result and, if the question remains close, the drafting fix.

@example
title: Two methods, one provision
problem: A statute lets "any person injured by a violation of this Act" sue for damages. A committee report says the Act was meant to protect consumers. A competing business, injured by a rival's violation, sues. Predict the outcome under textualism and under purposivism.
step: Textualist reading: "any person" is broad, and the Act contains no definition limiting it to consumers; the report is not text. Prediction: the competitor may sue.
step: Purposivist reading: if "any person" is clear in context, a modern purposivist also follows the text; if ambiguity exists, the report and the consumer-protection purpose can narrow it. Prediction: depends on whether the Act's structure shows ambiguity.
step: Compare: the methods converge unless the structure creates ambiguity; the divergence arises in how much weight the report may carry.
step: Drafting fix: either "any consumer injured by ..." or "any person, including a competitor," to make the answer unmistakable.

@mistakes
- Treating purposivists as free to disregard clear text; modern purposivism begins with text.
- Treating textualists as mechanical; they still disagree about ordinary meaning and context.
- Presenting a single method as the law when federal judges use different ones and state courts vary.
- Redrafting without testing the new text against the original facts and against both methods.

@practice self
prompt: Redraft this ambiguous provision to remove the ambiguity. "Sec. 5. A landlord may not retaliate against a tenant who complains of unsafe conditions or reports violations to the city by raising rent, reducing services or beginning eviction proceedings within 90 days." Identify at least two ambiguities, say what you assume the legislature intended, and give a redraft.
hint: Look at what "within 90 days" modifies and at what counts as "retaliate."
solution: Ambiguities: (1) "within 90 days" could modify "complains" and "reports" (the complaint must be recent), only "beginning eviction proceedings," or the retaliatory acts as a group; and from when the 90 days runs is not stated. (2) "retaliate ... by raising rent, reducing services or beginning eviction proceedings" does not say whether the list is exhaustive. (3) It is unclear whether "reports violations to the city" must be made in good faith. Assumed intent: a landlord may not take any listed action because of the complaint, with a presumption of retaliation if the action occurs within 90 days after the complaint. Redraft: "Sec. 5. (a) A landlord must not take any of the following actions because a tenant has made a complaint described in subsection (b): (1) raising the rent; (2) reducing services; or (3) beginning eviction proceedings. (b) A tenant makes a complaint described in this subsection by (1) complaining to the landlord about unsafe conditions; or (2) reporting a violation of a housing law to the city. (c) A landlord who takes an action listed in subsection (a) within 90 days after the date of a complaint is presumed to have acted because of the complaint. The landlord may rebut the presumption by showing a different reason for the action. (d) In this section, 'unsafe conditions' means conditions that threaten the health or safety of occupants."
rubric: Identifies at least two genuine ambiguities, including the scope and trigger of "within 90 days."
rubric: States the intended meaning explicitly.
rubric: Redrafts with separate subsections, vertical lists and a precise time rule.
rubric: Tests the redraft or explains how it removes the ambiguity under both textualist and purposivist reading.
