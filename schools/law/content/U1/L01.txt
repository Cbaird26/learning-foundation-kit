@article
Evidence law starts with a simple question: does this item help the jury decide something it has to decide? The Federal Rules of Evidence (FRE) answer in three layers. Relevant evidence is the starting point (Rules 401 and 402). Some relevant evidence is excluded for a specific reason, such as character, hearsay or privilege (most of this course). And a judge may exclude even clearly relevant evidence when its costs are too high (Rule 403).

### Rule 401: the definition of relevance

Evidence is **relevant** if (a) it has any tendency to make a fact more or less probable than it would be without the evidence, and (b) the fact is of consequence in determining the action. Element (b) is set by the substantive law and the pleadings: in a negligence case, the speed of the car is of consequence; the color of the car usually is not. Element (a) is a low threshold. Evidence need not prove the fact, be conclusive, or even make it more likely than not. A single brick is not a wall, but it is relevant to whether a wall exists. Relevance includes **background** evidence and evidence that bears on a witness's credibility.

### Rule 402: the default

Relevant evidence is admissible unless the Constitution, a federal statute, the Federal Rules of Evidence, or other rules prescribed by the Supreme Court provide otherwise. Irrelevant evidence is not admissible. So relevance is necessary, not sufficient: it gets the evidence to the next question.

### Rule 403: balancing probative value against danger

The court **may** exclude relevant evidence if its probative value is **substantially outweighed** by a danger of one or more of the following: unfair prejudice, confusing the issues, misleading the jury, undue delay, wasting time, or needlessly presenting cumulative evidence. Three points matter on every exam:

- The standard tilts toward admission. Evidence is not excluded merely because the dangers are equal to or somewhat greater than its probative value; they must **substantially** outweigh it.
- **Unfair prejudice** is not the same as damage to the opponent's case. All good evidence hurts someone. Unfair prejudice means an undue tendency to suggest decision on an improper basis, often an emotional one, such as sympathy, horror or hostility.
- Probative value is assessed in context, including how much the proponent needs the evidence. In *Old Chief v. United States* (1997), the Court held that a trial court abused its discretion when it refused a defendant's offer to stipulate to his felon status in a felon-in-possession prosecution and let the prosecution prove the name and nature of his prior offense. The holding is tied to status elements: the stipulation conveyed the same legal fact with less risk of unfair prejudice. Generally the prosecution may still prove its case with evidence of its choosing, because a story has narrative force a stipulation lacks.

### Preliminary questions: Rule 104

Rule 104(a) lets the judge decide preliminary questions, such as a witness's qualification, the existence of a privilege or whether evidence is admissible, and the judge is not bound by the rules of evidence (except privileges) in doing so. Rule 104(b) covers **conditional relevance**: when relevance depends on whether a fact exists (for example, a letter matters only if the defendant received it), the proponent must produce evidence sufficient to support a finding that the fact exists. The judge does not decide the fact; the jury does, and the court admits the item if the jury could reasonably find the condition met by a preponderance of the evidence.

### Preserving error and managing use

Under Rule 103, a party claims error in admitting evidence only by making a timely objection or motion to strike that states the specific ground (unless it is apparent from context). To claim error in excluding evidence, the party must inform the court of the substance through an **offer of proof**, unless the substance was apparent. Once the court rules definitively on the record, including in a ruling on a motion in limine, a party need not renew the objection or offer. Rule 105 requires a limiting instruction on request when evidence is admissible for one purpose but not another. Rule 107, effective December 1, 2024, governs **illustrative aids** (charts, timelines, diagrams) used to help the factfinder understand evidence or argument: the court may allow one if its utility in assisting comprehension is not substantially outweighed by the Rule 403 dangers; an illustrative aid is not evidence and generally does not go to the jury room, unless all parties consent or the court orders it for good cause.

### Applying the rules: IRAC

Issue: is the item relevant, and is it excluded by a specific rule or by Rule 403? Rule: state Rules 401, 402 and 403 in order. Application: identify the fact of consequence, the inference the evidence supports, the dangers, and any less prejudicial alternative. Conclusion: admit, exclude, or admit with a limiting instruction.

@example
title: A gruesome photograph
problem: Ana is charged with aggravated assault for striking Ben with a bat. The prosecution offers a color photograph of Ben's head wound taken at the hospital. Ana's defense is self-defense, and she disputes how many blows she struck. Ana objects under Rule 403. How should the court rule?
step: Relevance (Rule 401): the photograph tends to show the force and number of blows and the severity of injury, which bear on whether Ana used excessive force and on the degree of the offense. Those are facts of consequence, and the low threshold is met.
step: Danger: a graphic photograph can provoke horror and sympathy, which is a risk of unfair prejudice. But there is no claim of delay or confusion, and the photograph is not cumulative if no other evidence shows the wound.
step: Balance: the photograph has real probative value on disputed issues. Rule 403 requires that the dangers substantially outweigh that value, and a photograph of the injury the crime caused is not unfair merely because it is unpleasant.
step: Conclusion: the court should admit the photograph, may consider limiting the number of photographs, and may exclude duplicates as cumulative. Exclusion would be an unusual use of discretion on these facts.

@mistakes
- Treating "relevant" as meaning "proves the point" or "more likely than not": Rule 401 only requires any tendency.
- Confusing harmful evidence with unfairly prejudicial evidence, or applying Rule 403 when the dangers merely equal the probative value.
- Saying the judge decides conditional relevance under Rule 104(b); the judge only screens for sufficient evidence, and the jury decides the fact.
- Forgetting to make a specific objection or an offer of proof, which leaves the issue unpreserved on appeal.

@practice self
prompt: Evan is charged with being a felon in possession of a firearm. The only contested issue is whether he possessed the gun. Evan offers to stipulate that he has a prior felony conviction. The prosecution refuses and wants to read to the jury the judgment showing that Evan was previously convicted of armed robbery. Write a short IRAC analysis of the Rule 403 issue.
solution: Issue: whether the court should require the prosecution to accept Evan's stipulation, or may admit the armed robbery judgment, over a Rule 403 objection. Rule: relevant evidence may be excluded if its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. In Old Chief v. United States (1997) the Court held that a court abuses its discretion by refusing a stipulation to felon status when the only purpose of the prior-conviction evidence is to prove that status element. Application: the prior conviction is relevant because felon status is an element. But Evan offers to concede it, so the name and nature of the prior offense add almost nothing on that element, while an armed robbery conviction risks the improper inference that Evan is dangerous and likely to have possessed the gun. The risk of unfair prejudice substantially outweighs the incremental probative value. Conclusion: the court should accept the stipulation and exclude the judgment's details.
rubric: Identifies felon status as the element and notes the stipulation proves it fully.
rubric: Names unfair prejudice as the improper-propensity inference from the nature of the prior offense.
rubric: Cites Old Chief and applies the substantial-outweighing standard, reaching a clear conclusion.
hint: Ask what the prior conviction proves that the stipulation does not.
