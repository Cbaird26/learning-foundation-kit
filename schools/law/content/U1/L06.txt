@article
The rules on witnesses regulate who may testify and how testimony is elicited. They are mostly short, and bar questions test the exact text.

### Competence, knowledge and oath

**Rule 601.** Every person is competent to be a witness unless the rules provide otherwise. In a civil case, state law governs the witness's competency on a claim or defense for which state law supplies the rule of decision. **Rule 602.** A witness may testify to a matter only if evidence is introduced sufficient to support a finding that the witness has **personal knowledge** of the matter (Rule 104(b) standard); the rule does not apply to expert testimony under Rule 703. **Rule 603.** The witness must give an oath or affirmation to testify truthfully. **Rule 604** requires interpreters to be qualified and to give an oath. **Rule 605.** The presiding judge may not testify as a witness at the trial, and no objection is needed to preserve the point.

### Jurors: Rule 606

A juror may not testify as a witness before the other jurors at the trial in which the juror is sitting (Rule 606(a)). After the verdict, under Rule 606(b), a juror may not testify about any statement made or incident that occurred during the jury's deliberations, the effect of anything on that or another juror's vote, or any juror's mental processes concerning the verdict. The court also may not receive a juror's affidavit or evidence of a statement. The exceptions: a juror may testify about whether (A) **extraneous prejudicial information** was improperly brought to the jury's attention (a newspaper article, an outside investigation), (B) an **outside influence** was improperly brought to bear on a juror (a bribe or threat), or (C) a **mistake** was made in entering the verdict on the verdict form. Intoxication of jurors during deliberations is not an outside influence (*Tanner v. United States* (1987)). In *Pena-Rodriguez v. Colorado* (2017), the Court held that when a juror makes a clear statement showing that the juror relied on racial stereotypes or animus to convict, the Sixth Amendment requires an exception to the no-impeachment rule.

### Mode and order: Rule 611

The court should exercise reasonable control over the mode and order of examining witnesses to make procedures effective for determining the truth, avoid wasting time and protect witnesses from harassment.

**Scope of cross (Rule 611(b)).** Cross-examination should not go beyond the subject matter of the direct examination and matters affecting the witness's credibility, but the court may allow inquiry into additional matters as if on direct examination. This is the federal "restrictive" or scope-of-direct rule; many states follow the "wide-open" rule that allows cross on any relevant matter.

**Leading questions (Rule 611(c)).** Leading questions should not be used on direct examination except as necessary to develop the witness's testimony (preliminary matters, a child or a witness with difficulty communicating, a hostile witness). Ordinarily the court should allow leading questions on cross. When a party calls a hostile witness, an adverse party or a witness identified with an adverse party, the party may use leading questions on direct.

### Refreshing recollection: Rule 612

A witness may use a writing (or any object) to refresh memory. The thing used need not be admissible, because it only jogs the memory; the testimony must come from the refreshed memory. If the witness uses it **while testifying**, the adverse party is entitled to have it produced at the hearing, inspect it, cross-examine the witness on it and introduce into evidence any portion that relates to the witness's testimony. If used **before testifying**, the court has discretion to require the same if justice requires. This is different from **recorded recollection** under Rule 803(5) (week 9), where the witness cannot remember even after refreshing and the writing itself is read to the jury.

### Excluding witnesses: Rule 615

At a party's request, the court must order witnesses excluded so that they cannot hear other witnesses' testimony, and may do so on its own. The rule does not authorize excluding (1) a party who is a natural person, (2) one officer or employee of a party that is not a natural person, designated as the party's representative by its attorney (the 2023 amendment limits the exemption to one designated representative at a time), (3) a person whose presence a party shows to be essential to presenting the party's claim or defense, or (4) a person authorized by statute to be present (Rule 615(a)). Under the amendment effective December 1, 2023 (now Rule 615(b)), the court may also prohibit disclosing trial testimony to witnesses who are excluded, and may prohibit excluded witnesses from accessing it.

### Planning an impeachment

Choose the sequence: first, the facts that help your case (primacy), then credibility attacks. For each tool decide whether it is allowed, how it must be proved and whether extrinsic proof is available.

@example
title: Leading questions and scope
problem: Pam sues Dex Co. for injuries. Pam calls Dex Co.'s warehouse supervisor, Tia, and asks, "You knew the floor was wet that morning, didn't you?" Dex Co. objects that the question is leading. On cross, Dex Co.'s lawyer asks Tia about the company's hiring practices, which Pam's direct never mentioned. Pam objects that it exceeds the scope. Rule on each objection.
step: First objection: leading questions are generally barred on direct but allowed when a party calls an adverse party or a witness identified with an adverse party. A supervisor employed by Dex Co. is identified with the adverse party, so the objection should be overruled.
step: Second objection: under Rule 611(b), federal cross is limited to the subject matter of the direct and credibility, unless the court permits additional matters as if on direct.
step: Hiring practices are beyond the direct and do not affect Tia's credibility, so the court may sustain the objection, but it has discretion to permit the inquiry out of order, in which case Dex Co.'s lawyer must treat it as direct examination.
step: Conclusion: overrule the first objection; sustain the second unless the court exercises its discretion to allow it as if on direct.

@mistakes
- Saying that a writing used to refresh must itself be admissible, or that it automatically becomes an exhibit.
- Applying the wide-open cross rule in federal court without noting that federal courts apply the scope-of-direct rule with discretion.
- Allowing jurors to testify about what happened in deliberations merely because a verdict looks wrong; only the three exceptions apply.
- Excluding a party's designated corporate representative under Rule 615.

prompt: A witness reviews her notes before testifying and brings them to the stand to refresh her memory. Which statements are accurate under Rule 612? Select all that apply.
choice*: The notes may be used to refresh memory even if they are inadmissible hearsay.
choice*: If the witness uses the notes while testifying, the adverse party is entitled to have them produced, inspect them and cross-examine on them.

@practice mc
prompt: After a verdict for the plaintiff, the defendant obtains an affidavit from a juror who says that during deliberations several jurors read a newspaper article about the defendant's previous lawsuits, which the judge had told them not to read. The defendant moves for a new trial. May the court consider the affidavit?
choice: No, because Rule 606(b) bars any juror testimony about deliberations.
choice: No, because the juror's statement is hearsay.

@practice self
prompt: Plan the impeachment of Wes, a prosecution witness (not a defendant) in a robbery trial, using these facts. Convictions: (a) felony forgery, 2008, released 2010; (b) felony drug distribution, 2022, released 2023; (c) misdemeanor disorderly conduct, 2019. Statements: (1) an unsworn statement to police that the robber was "two men"; (2) sworn testimony at the preliminary hearing that the robber's face was "clearly lit." At trial Wes testifies that one man robbed the store and that the street was dark. The trial is in 2026. For each item, say whether you may use it, under what rule and standard, and how it may be used.
solution: (a) Forgery involves a false statement, so Rule 609(a)(2) would require admission, but more than ten years have passed since release (2010 to 2026), so Rule 609(b) applies: admissible only if probative value substantially outweighs prejudicial effect, with written notice; credibility is central here so make the argument. (b) Drug distribution is a felony not involving dishonesty: admissible under Rule 609(a)(1)(A), subject to Rule 403, within ten years. Name, date and sentence only. (c) Disorderly conduct is a misdemeanor not involving dishonesty: not admissible under Rule 609. (1) The unsworn police statement may be used to impeach under Rule 613 (foundation: opportunity to explain or deny); it is not hearsay if offered only for inconsistency, but is not substantive evidence because it was not under penalty of perjury at a proceeding. (2) The preliminary-hearing testimony was under oath at a proceeding, so under Rule 801(d)(1)(A) it is admissible both to impeach and as substantive evidence that the face was clearly lit. Sequence: lead with (2) since it is strongest and substantive, then (1), then convictions (b) and (a).
rubric: Applies Rule 609(b) to the 2008 forgery conviction and Rule 609(a)(1)(A) to the 2022 conviction.
rubric: Excludes the misdemeanor disorderly conduct conviction.
rubric: Distinguishes the unsworn statement (impeachment only) from the preliminary-hearing testimony (substantive under Rule 801(d)(1)(A)).
hint: For each statement, ask whether it was made under oath at a proceeding.
