@article
This lesson covers three topics that decide whether otherwise admissible evidence reaches the jury: privileges (the law will not compel disclosure of certain confidential communications), authentication (proof that an item is what its proponent says), and the best evidence rule (proof of the contents of a writing requires the original, with exceptions).

### Privileges: Rule 501

In federal question and criminal cases, privilege is governed by the **common law**, as interpreted by federal courts in the light of reason and experience, unless the Constitution, a federal statute or Supreme Court rules provide otherwise. In a civil case, **state law** governs privilege regarding a claim or defense for which state law supplies the rule of decision. Privileges exclude relevant, reliable evidence for the sake of a relationship, so they are narrowly construed, and they are held by a person who may assert or waive them.

**Attorney-client privilege.** A confidential communication between client and lawyer made for the purpose of obtaining or giving legal advice. The client holds the privilege. It does not protect underlying facts, only communications; it is lost if confidentiality is destroyed (for example, by presence of an outsider) or waived; it survives the client's death (*Swidler & Berlin v. United States* (1998)); and it does not apply under the **crime-fraud exception** when the client consulted the lawyer to further a continuing or future crime or fraud. In *Upjohn Co. v. United States* (1981), the Court held that the corporation's privilege covers communications between corporate counsel and employees, at the direction of management, made to secure legal advice for the corporation; the corporation holds the privilege, and an employee cannot waive it. Work product (*Hickman v. Taylor* (1947); FRCP 26(b)(3)) is a separate protection of materials prepared in anticipation of litigation.

**Rule 502 (waiver).** For disclosures in a federal proceeding or to a federal office, waiver extends to undisclosed material only if the waiver is intentional, the disclosed and undisclosed material concern the same subject matter, and fairness requires them to be considered together. An **inadvertent disclosure** does not waive the privilege if (1) it was inadvertent, (2) the holder took reasonable steps to prevent disclosure, and (3) the holder promptly took reasonable steps to rectify the error, including by following FRCP 26(b)(5)(B). A court order under Rule 502(d) can provide that disclosure in the litigation is not a waiver, binding in other proceedings.

**Spousal privileges.** (1) Spousal **testimonial** privilege (adverse spousal testimony): in *Trammel v. United States* (1980), the Court held it belongs to the **witness-spouse**, who may choose to testify against the other spouse in a federal criminal case. (2) Confidential **marital communications** privilege: protects confidential communications made during the marriage, held by both spouses.

**Psychotherapist-patient privilege.** Recognized in *Jaffee v. Redmond* (1996), including communications with licensed clinical social workers. **Self-incrimination** is a constitutional privilege that a witness may assert on particular questions.

### Authentication: Rules 901 and 902

To satisfy the requirement of authenticating or identifying an item, the proponent must produce evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what the proponent claims it is (Rule 901(a)). This is a Rule 104(b) standard, a low threshold: the jury decides authenticity. Rule 901(b) lists examples: (1) testimony of a witness with knowledge; (2) nonexpert opinion on handwriting; (3) comparison by an expert or the trier; (4) **distinctive characteristics** and the circumstances (contents, internal patterns, reply-letter doctrine); (5) opinion identifying a voice; (6) telephone conversations; (7) public records; (8) ancient documents; (9) **a process or system** that produces an accurate result (such as a machine log); and others. Text messages and social-media posts are usually authenticated by content known to the purported sender, replies, the sender's number or account and the surrounding circumstances.

**Rule 902 self-authenticating items** need no extrinsic evidence of authenticity: sealed domestic public documents, certified copies of public records, newspapers and periodicals, trade inscriptions, acknowledged documents, commercial paper, certified domestic records of regularly conducted activity (Rule 902(11)), and certified records generated by an electronic process or system or certified data copied from an electronic device (Rules 902(13) and 902(14)). Authentication does not resolve hearsay, privilege or any other objection.

### The best evidence rule: Rules 1001 through 1008

Rule 1002: an **original** writing, recording or photograph is required to **prove its content**, unless the rules or a federal statute provide otherwise. The rule applies only when the **content** is being proved. A witness who saw an event may testify to it even if it was also recorded. Key points:

- **Duplicates (Rule 1003)** are admissible to the same extent as the original unless a genuine question is raised about the original's authenticity or the circumstances make it unfair to admit the duplicate. For electronically stored information, an "original" includes any printout or output readable by sight that accurately reflects the data (Rule 1001(d)).
- **Other evidence of content (Rule 1004)** is admissible if the originals are lost or destroyed (and not by the proponent in bad faith), cannot be obtained by any available judicial process, are in the opponent's possession and the opponent was put on notice and did not produce them, or relate to a collateral matter.
- **Public records (Rule 1005)**, **summaries of voluminous writings (Rule 1006)** (the underlying material must be made available), and **testimony or a written admission of a party (Rule 1007)** also permit proof without the original. Rule 1008 allocates questions between court and jury.

### Rule 702 and Daubert in practice

For a proposed expert, ask: (1) qualified? (2) will it help the trier (fit)? (3) sufficient facts or data? (4) reliable principles and methods (Daubert factors: testing, peer review, error rate and standards, general acceptance)? (5) reliable application? The proponent must show each is more likely than not met. Look for an analytical gap (*Joiner*).

@example
title: Authenticating a text message
problem: Plaintiff Ana sues Ben for breach of a delivery contract. Ana offers a printout of a text message that reads, "I'll deliver Friday at the dock, same as last time," and testifies that she received it from the number she had always used to reach Ben, that the text referred to their prior dealing at the dock, and that Ben called her an hour later and confirmed it. Ben objects that the text is not authenticated. How should the court rule?
step: The standard is whether there is evidence sufficient to support a finding that Ben sent the message (Rule 901(a)). The jury decides authenticity if the threshold is met.
step: Ana's evidence includes the sender's number, content referring to facts likely known only to Ben (distinctive characteristics under Rule 901(b)(4)), and a follow-up call from Ben confirming it. Her testimony as a person with knowledge is also relevant under Rule 901(b)(1).
step: That is enough to support a finding of authenticity; Ben may argue to the jury that someone else sent it.
step: Conclusion: overrule the authentication objection. Hearsay must be separately considered (Ben's own message is an opposing-party statement under Rule 801(d)(2)(A)).

@mistakes
- Treating authentication as proof of authenticity beyond doubt, rather than as sufficient evidence for a jury finding.
- Applying the best evidence rule whenever a document exists, instead of only when its content is being proved.
- Saying a privilege protects underlying facts, or that an employee can waive a corporation's privilege.
- Confusing the spousal testimonial privilege (held by the witness-spouse) with the marital communications privilege.

@practice self
prompt: Apply Rule 702 and Daubert to this proposed expert. Plaintiff sues a chemical plant alleging that exposure to its emissions caused her cancer. Plaintiff's expert, Dr. Ray, a toxicologist, would testify that the emissions caused the cancer. Her opinion rests on (1) one unpublished study in which mice were given doses about a thousand times higher than the plaintiff's likely exposure; (2) a "temporal correlation method" she developed for this case, never used outside litigation and never tested; and (3) an assumption that the plaintiff's exposure was high, for which she reviewed no exposure data. She cannot state an error rate. Write a short memo section deciding whether the opinion should be admitted.
solution: Issue: whether Dr. Ray's causation opinion is admissible under Rule 702. Rule: the proponent must show to the court that it is more likely than not that the expert's knowledge will help the trier of fact, that the testimony rests on sufficient facts or data, that it is the product of reliable principles and methods, and that the opinion reflects a reliable application of the methods to the facts. Under Daubert, the court is the gatekeeper and considers whether the method can be and has been tested, peer review, error rate and standards, and general acceptance; under Joiner, the court may exclude an opinion when there is too great an analytical gap between the data and the opinion; Kumho Tire applies the same gatekeeping to all expert testimony. Application: Dr. Ray is probably qualified as a toxicologist. But (a) the sufficiency of facts or data fails: the only study used doses far above plaintiff's exposure, and she assumed exposure without reviewing data. (b) Reliable method fails: the temporal correlation method was developed for litigation, never tested, unpublished and has no known error rate or standards. (c) Reliable application fails: extrapolating from the high-dose mouse study to plaintiff's exposure leaves an analytical gap bridged only by the expert's say-so. Conclusion: the plaintiff has not shown by a preponderance that Rule 702(b) through (d) are met, so the court should exclude the causation opinion (and may permit a properly supported alternative opinion).
rubric: States the four Rule 702 requirements and the proponent's burden by a preponderance.
rubric: Applies at least three Daubert factors and the Joiner analytical-gap concept to the facts.
rubric: Reaches a clear conclusion that the opinion is excluded, with reasons tied to sufficiency, method and application.
hint: Test each fact about Dr. Ray against one requirement of Rule 702, then check the Daubert factors.
