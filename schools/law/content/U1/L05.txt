@article
**Impeachment** is the process of attacking (or supporting) a witness's credibility. Credibility is always relevant, so the rules concern which methods are allowed and how far a lawyer may go. Four classic methods are: bad character for truthfulness, prior convictions, prior inconsistent statements and bias. Learn, for each, whether **extrinsic evidence** (proof from another witness or document, not the witness's own mouth) is allowed.

### Who may impeach: Rule 607

Any party, including the party that called the witness, may attack the witness's credibility. The old "voucher rule" is gone. A party may not, however, call a witness mainly to put an otherwise inadmissible prior statement before the jury under the guise of impeachment.

### Character for truthfulness: Rule 608

**Rule 608(a).** A witness's credibility may be attacked or supported by testimony about the witness's reputation for having a character for truthfulness or untruthfulness, or by opinion testimony. Evidence of truthful character is admissible only after the witness's character for truthfulness has been attacked.

**Rule 608(b).** Except for a criminal conviction under Rule 609, extrinsic evidence is **not admissible** to prove specific instances of a witness's conduct in order to attack or support the witness's character for truthfulness. But the court may allow them to be inquired into on **cross-examination** if they are probative of the character for truthfulness or untruthfulness of the witness being cross-examined or of another witness whose character the witness being cross-examined has testified about. The examiner is bound by the answer, and the rule does not allow acts that do not bear on truthfulness (such as a barroom fight). By testifying on a matter, a witness does not waive the privilege against self-incrimination.

### Prior convictions: Rule 609

- **Rule 609(a)(2): crimes of dishonesty or false statement.** A conviction for **any** crime, whatever the punishment, must be admitted if the elements of the crime required proving, or the witness's admitting, a dishonest act or false statement (perjury, fraud, forgery, false statement). There is no balancing, and the court has no discretion.
- **Rule 609(a)(1): crimes punishable by death or imprisonment for more than one year.** In a civil case, or a criminal case where the witness is **not a defendant**, the conviction must be admitted subject to Rule 403. If the witness is a **criminal defendant**, the conviction is admitted only if its probative value **outweighs** its prejudicial effect **to that defendant** (the burden is on the prosecution, and the standard is more protective than Rule 403).
- **Rule 609(b): the ten-year limit.** If more than ten years have passed since the witness's conviction or release from confinement for it, whichever is later, the conviction is admissible only if its probative value, supported by specific facts and circumstances, **substantially outweighs** its prejudicial effect, and the proponent gives reasonable written notice. Courts measure the ten years to the start of trial or to the witness's testimony; they differ on which.
- **Rule 609(c)–(e).** Convictions are not usable after a pardon or similar relief based on innocence, or (with limits) after rehabilitation certificates; juvenile adjudications are generally inadmissible (with a narrow exception for non-defendant witnesses in criminal cases); and a pending appeal does not make the conviction inadmissible.
- Ordinarily the **name, date and sentence** of the conviction, not the details, may be elicited. In *Luce v. United States* (1984) the Court held that a defendant must testify to preserve a claim that the trial court erred in ruling that a prior conviction could be used to impeach. In *Ohler v. United States* (2000), a defendant who introduces her own conviction on direct examination to soften its effect cannot appeal the ruling that allowed it.

### Prior inconsistent statements: Rule 613

A party may cross-examine a witness about a prior statement without first showing it to the witness, but on request the statement must be shown or its contents disclosed to the adverse party's attorney. Under Rule 613(b), **extrinsic evidence** of a prior inconsistent statement is admissible only if the witness is given an opportunity to explain or deny it and an adverse party is given an opportunity to examine the witness about it, or if justice requires otherwise. Under the amendment effective December 1, 2024, Rule 613(b) does not apply to an opposing party's statement under Rule 801(d)(2). The matter must not be **collateral**: you may prove by extrinsic evidence only points that matter to the case or that show bias. A prior inconsistent statement used only to impeach is not hearsay because it is not offered for its truth, and the jury should be so instructed. It is admissible as substantive evidence only if it fits Rule 801(d)(1)(A) (week 8) or another hearsay rule.

### Bias

Showing that a witness is biased (by friendship, hostility, payment, a deal with the prosecution or a financial interest) is always allowed and may be proved by extrinsic evidence. Rule 608(b)'s extrinsic-evidence bar does not apply to bias, and *United States v. Abel* (1984) held that evidence of a witness's shared membership in a secret prison gang with the defendant was admissible to show bias.

### Applying the rules

Issue: which impeachment method is being used? Rule: the specific rule and whether extrinsic evidence is allowed. Application: check the type of crime, the time, who the witness is, and the foundation required. Conclusion: ask, bar, or allow extrinsic proof, and ask for a limiting instruction.

@example
title: Ten years and a prior fraud conviction
problem: Ruth is a witness (not a party) in a civil contract suit. She was convicted of felony wire fraud in 2012 and released from prison in 2014. The trial is in 2026. The opposing party wants to impeach Ruth with the conviction. How should the court rule?
step: Identify the crime: wire fraud has as an element a dishonest act or false statement, so Rule 609(a)(2) applies, not just Rule 609(a)(1).
step: Check the ten-year limit: the later of conviction (2012) and release (2014) is 2014. The trial in 2026 is 12 years later, which is more than ten, so Rule 609(b) applies. The automatic admissibility of Rule 609(a)(2) is lost.
step: Apply the standard: the proponent must give written notice and show that probative value, supported by specific facts and circumstances, substantially outweighs prejudicial effect. A fraud conviction bears directly on truthfulness, but 12 years is old and the proponent must show more, for example that credibility is central.
step: Conclusion: the court has discretion; absent a strong showing that credibility is central, the better ruling is to exclude, and in any case the conviction is inadmissible unless notice was given.

@mistakes
- Applying Rule 609(a)(2) automatically to a conviction more than ten years old; Rule 609(b) controls.
- Using the Rule 403 standard for a testifying criminal defendant's prior conviction instead of the more protective standard in Rule 609(a)(1)(B).
- Offering extrinsic evidence of a specific act of untruthfulness under Rule 608(b); the questioner is bound by the answer.
- Treating a prior inconsistent statement as substantive evidence when it was unsworn and made outside a proceeding.

@practice self
prompt: A witness testified at trial that the light was red. Last month, in an unsworn conversation with an investigator, she said the light was green. Explain how to impeach her with the earlier statement, what foundation Rule 613 requires, and whether the jury may consider the earlier statement as proof the light was green.
solution: Ask the witness on cross whether she spoke with the investigator on the stated date and said the light was green, ideally committing her first to her trial testimony. Under Rule 613(a), the examiner need not show her the statement first, but on request must show it or disclose its contents to opposing counsel. If she denies the statement, extrinsic evidence of it (the investigator's testimony or a recording) is admissible under Rule 613(b) only if she has had a chance to explain or deny it and the other side has a chance to examine her about it, and only if the point is not collateral; the color of the light is central, so it is not. Because the statement was unsworn and not made at a proceeding, it does not qualify under Rule 801(d)(1)(A), so it may be used only to impeach and not for its truth; on request the court should give a limiting instruction.
rubric: Describes the foundation: opportunity to explain or deny, and disclosure on request.
rubric: Notes that extrinsic evidence is allowed because the matter is not collateral.
rubric: States that the unsworn statement is impeachment only, not substantive evidence, with a limiting instruction.
hint: Compare the circumstances of the statement with the requirements of Rule 801(d)(1)(A).
