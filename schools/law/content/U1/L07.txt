@article
Hearsay is the most tested subject in evidence, and the key is a disciplined definition. If you apply the definition step by step, most problems resolve before you reach an exception.

### The definition

Under Rule 801(c), **hearsay** is a statement that (1) the declarant does not make while testifying at the current trial or hearing, and (2) a party offers in evidence to prove the truth of the matter asserted in the statement. Rule 802 provides that hearsay is not admissible unless a federal statute, the Federal Rules of Evidence, or other rules prescribed by the Supreme Court provide otherwise.

A **statement** (Rule 801(a)) is a person's oral assertion, written assertion, or nonverbal conduct, if the person intended it as an assertion. The **declarant** (Rule 801(b)) is the person who made the statement. So both pieces of the definition matter: the statement must be made out of court, and it must be offered for the **truth** of what it asserts.

### Why the rule exists

The rule responds to the lack of an oath, observation of demeanor and cross-examination at the time the statement was made. A statement carries four risks, sometimes called the **hearsay dangers**: the declarant's perception may have been flawed, memory may have failed, the narration may have been ambiguous, and the declarant may have been insincere. Cross-examination tests each; hearsay avoids it.

### Four-step analysis

1. **Identify the statement and who made it** (the declarant; any chain of speakers).
2. **Identify the purpose** for which it is offered. What fact does the proponent want the jury to find?
3. Ask whether the statement's **usefulness depends on the declarant being right**. If the relevance of the statement does not depend on its truth, it is not hearsay.
4. If it is hearsay, look for a Rule 801(d) exclusion (week 8), an exception (week 9), and the Confrontation Clause (week 10).

### Statements not offered for their truth

- **Effect on the listener.** The statement is offered to show what the hearer knew, believed or feared, so it explains conduct, shows **notice** or **knowledge**, or supports the reasonableness of an action. (A warning, "The stairs are broken," offered to show the landlord had notice.)
- **Verbal acts (operative facts).** Words that have legal significance themselves: a contract offer or acceptance, a defamatory statement offered to show it was made, a threat in an assault or extortion case, a will or deed.
- **Circumstantial evidence of the declarant's state of mind.** Rambling statements, such as "I am the Emperor," offered to show the declarant's mental condition, rely on the fact that the words were said, not on their truth.
- **Impeachment by prior inconsistent statement**, offered only to show that the witness said something different, not for its truth.
- **Falsity.** A statement offered to show it is false, or to show that the declarant said it as a pretext.

### Conduct, machines and implied assertions

Nonverbal conduct is a statement only if the person intended it as an assertion. Nodding yes to a question or pointing at a suspect in a line-up is assertive conduct, and is hearsay if offered for its truth. Conduct that is not intended as an assertion (a driver pulling over for a siren, a person carrying an umbrella) is not a statement, even if the proponent wants an inference about what the person believed. The Advisory Committee's note says the drafters intended to exclude "implied assertions" from hearsay when the declarant did not intend to assert the implied fact; courts apply this to conduct readily and differ somewhat on verbal statements. **Machine output** (a radar reading, an automatically generated timestamp or phone log) is not a statement because no person is the declarant; the question is authentication and reliability. A person's recording of a machine's reading may still raise hearsay.

### Hearsay within hearsay: Rule 805

Hearsay within hearsay is admissible only if each part of the combined statements conforms with an exception to the rule. Draw the chain: for example, a nurse's chart (level 1, the nurse's entry) recording what the patient's neighbor said (level 2). Each level needs its own exclusion or exception, and a level that fails excludes the evidence unless that part is not hearsay.

### Applying the rule: IRAC

Issue: is the proffered statement hearsay? Rule: Rule 801(a) through (c) and Rule 802. Application: statement, declarant, out of court, purpose, whether truth matters. Conclusion: not hearsay (state why), or hearsay and then analyze exceptions and exclusions.

@example
title: Three statements in a slip-and-fall case
problem: Nora sues Store Co. after falling on a wet floor. Three pieces of testimony are offered: (1) Nora testifies that two days earlier she told the manager, "The floor by the door is always wet," offered to show Store Co. had notice. (2) A witness testifies that a customer said at the scene, "That puddle has been there all day," offered to prove it had been there all day. (3) The manager testifies that the store's floor-mat sensor logged "Mat removed 9:02" offered to show the time the mat was removed. Classify each.
step: Statement (1): Nora spoke in court as a witness, but the earlier statement to the manager is out of court. Is it offered for its truth? No. It is offered to show the manager heard a warning, which proves notice regardless of whether the floor really was always wet. Not hearsay.
step: Statement (2): the customer's statement is out of court and offered to prove the puddle had been there all day, which is the truth of the matter asserted. It is hearsay and needs an exception; Rule 803(1) (present sense impression) or Rule 803(2) (excited utterance) might apply, but the facts do not yet supply them.
step: Statement (3): the log is machine output with no human declarant, so it is not a statement under Rule 801(a). The proponent must authenticate it (Rule 901(b)(9)), and the manager's testimony about the log is not hearsay.
step: Conclusion: (1) and (3) are not hearsay; (2) is hearsay unless an exception is shown.

@mistakes
- Treating every out-of-court statement as hearsay without asking what it is offered to prove.
- Calling a statement non-hearsay because the declarant is "a party" or "available as a witness"; availability is irrelevant to the definition, and party status only matters under Rule 801(d)(2).
- Forgetting that a witness repeating her own earlier out-of-court statement is still offering hearsay if offered for its truth.
- Stopping at the first level of a multi-level statement instead of analyzing each level under Rule 805.

@practice self
prompt: At a malpractice trial, a nurse testifies for the plaintiff, "Patient's neighbor called the hospital and said the doctor told her that the doctor was not sure the surgery was needed." The plaintiff offers this testimony to prove that the doctor doubted the surgery was needed. Identify each level of hearsay, say whether each level is hearsay, and say whether the evidence is admissible on these facts.
solution: There are three speakers. The doctor made the original statement to the neighbor (level 1: doubt about whether surgery was needed). The neighbor repeated it to the nurse in a phone call (level 2). The nurse repeats it in court (testifying to what she heard). The nurse's present testimony is made in court, but she is reporting the neighbor's out-of-court statement, and the neighbor's statement in turn reports the doctor's out-of-court statement. Each out-of-court statement is offered for its truth. Under Rule 805 each level must fall within an exclusion or exception. The doctor's statement may be excluded from hearsay under Rule 801(d)(2) if the doctor is the opposing party, or a party's agent speaking within the scope of the relationship (the hospital's employee) under Rule 801(d)(2)(D). The neighbor's statement has no exclusion or exception on these facts: she is a bystander reporting what she was told, and a phone call to the hospital is not within an exception. Because one level fails, the testimony is inadmissible unless the plaintiff can supply an exception for the neighbor's statement, or call the neighbor.
rubric: Separates the statements into levels and treats each as an out-of-court statement offered for its truth.
rubric: Applies Rule 801(d)(2) or another basis to the doctor's statement.
rubric: Concludes that every level must satisfy a rule and the neighbor's level fails, so the evidence is inadmissible as presented.
hint: Draw the chain from the original declarant to the courtroom witness.
