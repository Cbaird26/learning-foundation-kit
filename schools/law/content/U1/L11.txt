@article
Witnesses ordinarily testify to facts they perceived. Opinion testimony is the exception, and the rules sort it into two bins: opinions any witness may give from everyday perception (Rule 701), and opinions that depend on specialized knowledge, which only a qualified expert may give (Rule 702).

### Lay opinion: Rule 701

If a witness is not testifying as an expert, the testimony in the form of an opinion is limited to one that is (a) **rationally based on the witness's perception**; (b) **helpful** to clearly understanding the witness's testimony or to determining a fact in issue; and (c) **not based on scientific, technical or other specialized knowledge** within the scope of Rule 702. Typical lay opinions: a person appeared drunk or angry, a car was going fast, a voice or handwriting was familiar (with proper foundation), a smell was gasoline. The 2000 addition of (c) prevents a party from evading expert-disclosure and reliability requirements by calling an expert as a lay witness. Opinions of a business owner about the business's own affairs, drawn from particularized knowledge gained in that role, are generally treated as lay opinion; testimony based on training in a technical field is not.

### Expert testimony: Rule 702

A witness who is qualified as an expert by **knowledge, skill, experience, training or education** may testify in the form of an opinion or otherwise if the **proponent demonstrates to the court that it is more likely than not that**:

- (a) the expert's scientific, technical or other specialized knowledge will **help the trier of fact** to understand the evidence or determine a fact in issue;
- (b) the testimony is based on **sufficient facts or data**;
- (c) the testimony is the product of **reliable principles and methods**; and
- (d) the expert's **opinion reflects a reliable application** of the principles and methods to the facts of the case.

The December 1, 2023 amendment to Rule 702 did two things: it made explicit that the admissibility requirements must be shown by the proponent by a preponderance of the evidence (a Rule 104(a) question for the judge), and it clarified (d) to say that the opinion must reflect a reliable application. It was a response to opinions admitted on the view that the sufficiency of an expert's basis and application "goes to weight, not admissibility." After the amendment, those are questions for the judge, though disputes about the strength of a sound basis still go to weight.

### The gatekeeping cases

- *Frye v. United States* (1923) required that a scientific technique be **generally accepted** in its field. A number of states still use *Frye* or a modified version.
- *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals* (1993): the Federal Rules replaced *Frye* in federal court. The trial judge is a **gatekeeper** who ensures that expert testimony rests on a reliable foundation and is relevant ("fit"). Non-exclusive factors: whether the theory or technique **can be and has been tested**; whether it has been subjected to **peer review and publication**; its known or potential **error rate** and the existence of standards controlling its operation; and **general acceptance**.
- *General Electric Co. v. Joiner* (1997): appellate review of the decision to admit or exclude is for **abuse of discretion**, and a court may exclude an opinion if there is too great an **analytical gap** between the data and the opinion; the court need not admit an opinion connected to the data only by the expert's say-so (*ipse dixit*).
- *Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael* (1999): the gatekeeping obligation applies to **all** expert testimony, including technical and experience-based expertise, and the *Daubert* factors are flexible.

### Bases, ultimate issue and form: Rules 703 to 705

- **Rule 703.** An expert may base an opinion on facts or data the expert has been made aware of or personally observed. If experts in the field would reasonably rely on those kinds of facts or data, they need not be admissible for the opinion to be admitted. But if the facts or data would otherwise be inadmissible, the proponent may disclose them to the jury only if their probative value in helping the jury evaluate the opinion **substantially outweighs** their prejudicial effect. The jury does not receive them for their truth. (Constitutional limits remain: *Smith v. Arizona* (2024), week 10.)
- **Rule 704(a).** An opinion is not objectionable just because it embraces an ultimate issue. But an expert may not simply tell the jury what legal conclusion to reach or how to apply the law. **Rule 704(b).** In a **criminal case**, an expert must not state an opinion about whether the defendant did or did not have a mental state or condition that is an element of the crime or a defense; those matters are for the trier of fact.
- **Rule 705.** The expert may state an opinion and give reasons without first testifying to the underlying facts or data, unless the court orders otherwise, but may be required to disclose them on cross-examination.
- **Rule 706** lets the court appoint its own expert.

### Applying the rule

Issue: is the opinion lay or expert? Rule: Rule 701 or Rule 702. Application: qualifications, helpfulness, data, method, application, fit; then bases and form. Conclusion: admit, exclude or limit.

@example
title: A lay witness who sounds like an expert
problem: Rita is charged with arson. A fire investigator, Zed, who has not been disclosed or qualified as an expert, testifies for the prosecution, "From my training in fire-pattern analysis, the burn pattern on the floor shows an accelerant was poured." Rita objects. A neighbor, Nan, testifies that the fire "looked like it started fast" and "smelled like gasoline." Rule on each.
step: Zed's opinion rests on training in fire-pattern analysis, which is specialized knowledge. It falls within Rule 702 and so is barred as lay opinion by Rule 701(c).
step: The prosecution may call Zed as an expert, but must then show that he is qualified and that the requirements of Rule 702 are more likely than not met, and satisfy any notice or disclosure rules.
step: Nan's observations are rationally based on her own perception (what she saw and smelled) and helpful, and require no specialized knowledge, so they are proper lay opinion under Rule 701.
step: Conclusion: sustain the objection to Zed as presently offered; admit Nan's testimony.

@mistakes
- Allowing opinion based on specialized training as lay opinion because the witness was "experienced."
- Stating that the sufficiency of an expert's basis and methods "goes only to weight" after the 2023 amendment to Rule 702.
- Believing that an expert's bases must be admissible; Rule 703 allows reliance on reasonably relied-upon inadmissible facts, with limits on disclosure to the jury.
- Applying a ban on ultimate-issue opinions generally; Rule 704(a) allows them, and only Rule 704(b) bars opinions about a criminal defendant's mental state.
