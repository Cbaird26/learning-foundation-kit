@article
Rules 407 through 411 are policy rules. The evidence they cover is often logically relevant, but the law excludes it for a stated purpose because admitting it would discourage socially valuable behavior: fixing hazards, settling disputes, helping an injured person, buying insurance, or pleading guilty. Each rule bars the evidence only for certain purposes and permits it for others. On an exam, the first question is always: what is the evidence offered to prove?

### Rule 407: subsequent remedial measures

When measures are taken that would have made an earlier injury or harm less likely to occur, evidence of the subsequent measures is not admissible to prove negligence, culpable conduct, a defect in a product or its design, or a need for a warning or instruction. The court may admit the evidence for another purpose, such as impeachment or, **if disputed**, proving ownership, control or the feasibility of precautionary measures.

The policy is to avoid discouraging repairs. Key limits:

- The measure must come **after** the injury or harm. A repair made before the accident is not covered.
- Most courts hold the rule does not reach remedial measures taken by non-parties, because the policy of encouraging the defendant's repairs is not at stake.
- The rule covers strict liability and product-design claims, as the text says.
- Feasibility is "disputed" only if the defendant contests it. If the defendant concedes feasibility, the exception does not apply, and a defendant who claims the old design was the safest possible may open the door.

### Rule 408: compromise offers and negotiations

Evidence of furnishing, promising or offering (or accepting) valuable consideration in compromising a claim, and conduct or statements made during compromise negotiations about the claim, is not admissible on behalf of any party to prove or disprove the **validity or amount of a disputed claim**, or to impeach by a prior inconsistent statement or contradiction. In a criminal case, the bar does not apply to statements made during compromise negotiations about a claim by a public office in the exercise of its regulatory, investigative or enforcement authority. The court may admit the evidence for another purpose, such as proving a witness's bias or prejudice, negating a contention of undue delay, or proving an effort to obstruct a criminal investigation or prosecution. There must be an actual dispute about the claim, and a pre-existing document does not become inadmissible just because it is shown during negotiations.

### Rule 409: payment of medical expenses

Furnishing, promising to pay or offering to pay medical, hospital or similar expenses resulting from an injury is not admissible to prove liability for the injury. Rule 409 protects the payment or offer only. A statement of fault made along with the offer ("I'm sorry, I drove too fast, I'll pay your bills") is not covered by Rule 409, though it may be protected by Rule 408 if made in compromise negotiations, and it is otherwise a party-opponent statement.

### Rule 410: pleas and plea discussions

In a civil or criminal case, the following are not admissible against the defendant who made the plea or participated in the plea discussions: (1) a guilty plea that was later withdrawn; (2) a plea of nolo contendere; (3) a statement made during a Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 11 plea proceeding (or a comparable state proceeding) about either plea; and (4) a statement made during plea discussions with an **attorney for the prosecuting authority** if the discussions did not result in a guilty plea or resulted in a guilty plea later withdrawn. A statement to a police officer is not within paragraph (4) unless the officer was acting for the prosecutor. A guilty plea that stands is admissible as a party admission. Two exceptions: another statement from the same plea or discussion has been introduced and fairness requires admitting the rest; and a criminal prosecution for perjury or false statement when the defendant made the statement under oath, on the record, and with counsel present. In *United States v. Mezzanatto* (1995), the Court held that a defendant may waive Rule 410's protections as a condition of plea negotiations.

### Rule 411: liability insurance

Evidence that a person was or was not insured against liability is not admissible to prove whether the person acted negligently or otherwise wrongfully. It may be admitted for another purpose, such as proving a witness's bias or prejudice, or proving agency, ownership or control. Insurance is of doubtful relevance to fault, and the jury may inflate damages if told someone else will pay.

### The method

For each rule: (1) identify the proposition the evidence is offered to prove; (2) check whether it falls within the barred purpose; (3) check for a permitted other purpose that is actually in dispute; (4) apply Rule 403 even if the evidence is admissible, and request a limiting instruction (Rule 105).

@example
title: A repair after the accident
problem: Pia is injured when a warehouse shelf collapses. She sues Store Co. for negligence. After the accident, Store Co. replaced all shelf brackets with heavier ones. Pia offers evidence of the replacement (1) to show Store Co. was negligent, and (2) to show that Store Co. owns the warehouse, which Store Co. denies. Rule on each purpose.
step: Identify the rule: the replacement is a measure taken after the injury that would have made the harm less likely. Rule 407 applies.
step: Purpose (1): offering the replacement to prove negligence is exactly the barred use. Sustain the objection.
step: Purpose (2): ownership is a permitted other purpose, and Store Co. disputes it, so the exception is available. Admit the evidence for that purpose only.
step: Evaluate the merits: the replacement is weak proof of ownership if others, such as tenants, could have made repairs, but that goes to weight and Rule 403. The court should give a limiting instruction under Rule 105.

@mistakes
- Applying Rule 407 to a repair made before the injury, or to repairs by someone who is not a party.
- Using Rule 408 for an undisputed debt or when no claim is in dispute, or forgetting that bias is a permitted other purpose.
- Excluding the statement of fault under Rule 409 because it accompanied a payment; the rule protects the payment, not the accompanying statement.
- Treating a statement to a police officer as a plea-discussion statement under Rule 410.
