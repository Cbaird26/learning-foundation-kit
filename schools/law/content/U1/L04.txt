@article
Rule 404(a) bars propensity use of a person's **character**. Rule 404(b) applies the same ban to a person's **other crimes, wrongs or acts**. But other acts are often highly probative for reasons that have nothing to do with propensity, and Rule 404(b)(2) lets them in for those purposes. The skill is separating the inference you may draw ("he had a motive") from the one you may not ("he is the type").

### The rule

Rule 404(b)(1): evidence of any other crime, wrong or act is not admissible to prove a person's character in order to show that on a particular occasion the person acted in accordance with the character. Rule 404(b)(2): the evidence may be admissible for **another purpose**, such as proving motive, opportunity, intent, preparation, plan, knowledge, identity, absence of mistake or lack of accident. The list is illustrative, not exclusive. The rule applies in both civil and criminal cases and to acts of anyone, not only the defendant. It does not require that the act be criminal, and it does not apply to acts that are part of the charged event itself (intrinsic evidence).

### Common non-propensity theories

- **Motive:** prior threats or a prior dispute explain why the defendant acted.
- **Intent, knowledge, absence of mistake or accident:** prior similar incidents make an innocent explanation less plausible. This uses the **doctrine of chances**: one fire may be an accident; several fires at businesses insured by the same owner are unlikely to be.
- **Identity:** requires a **signature**, a distinctive method so unusual that the same person likely committed both crimes. Generic similarity (a robbery with a gun) is not enough.
- **Plan:** the other act and the charged act are parts of a single scheme.
- **Preparation or opportunity:** acquiring tools, or access to the scene.

### Procedure and standards

1. **Notice.** In a criminal case the prosecutor must provide reasonable notice of the evidence so the defendant has a fair opportunity to meet it, articulate in the notice the permitted purpose and the reasoning that supports it, and give the notice in writing before trial (or in any form during trial if the court, for good cause, excuses lack of pretrial notice). The 2020 amendment removed the former requirement that the defendant first request notice.
2. **Proof that the act occurred.** Under *Huddleston v. United States* (1988), the question is one of conditional relevance under Rule 104(b): the court need not find by a preponderance that the defendant committed the act. It is enough that the jury could reasonably find that the act occurred and that the defendant did it. Even evidence of an act for which the defendant was **acquitted** may be offered, because an acquittal means only that the jury had a reasonable doubt, not that the act did not occur (*Dowling v. United States* (1990)).
3. **Relevance to a non-propensity issue.** The proponent must identify a genuinely disputed issue. If the defendant concedes intent, prior acts to prove intent have little probative value.
4. **Rule 403.** The court must balance probative value and unfair prejudice. Other-acts evidence is especially prone to propensity use, so courts scrutinize it closely.
5. **Limiting instruction** on request, under Rule 105.

### Rule 406: habit and routine practice

Evidence of a person's **habit** or an organization's **routine practice** may be admitted to prove that on a particular occasion the person or organization acted in accordance with the habit or routine practice. It may be admitted whether or not it is corroborated and regardless of whether there was an eyewitness. A habit is a regular response to a repeated **specific** situation, such as always buckling a seatbelt on entering the car, or an office that always mails invoices on the day they are generated. It is more specific and automatic than character. "He is careful" is character, and "he always checks the stove before leaving the house" is habit. The greater the specificity and regularity, and the more instances, the stronger the claim to habit. One or two incidents do not make a habit.

### The five uses to know

For the project and exam, classify each use of "character-type" evidence: (1) the defendant offering his own good trait (Rule 404(a)(2)(A)); (2) the defendant offering the victim's trait (Rule 404(a)(2)(B)); (3) a prior act for a non-propensity purpose (Rule 404(b)(2)); (4) habit or routine practice (Rule 406); and (5) sexual-assault or child-molestation propensity (Rules 413 through 415).

### Applying the rule: IRAC

Issue: is the other act offered for a purpose other than propensity? Rule: Rule 404(b), Huddleston, Rule 403. Application: name the specific inference the evidence supports, show that the inference does not require the intermediate step "he is a bad person," identify whether the issue is actually disputed, and weigh prejudice. Conclusion: admit for the stated purpose with a limiting instruction, or exclude.

@example
title: Prior fires and the doctrine of chances
problem: Hal is charged with arson for burning his restaurant. Hal says the fire was an electrical accident. The prosecution gives pretrial written notice and offers evidence that two earlier businesses Hal owned burned, and that Hal collected insurance each time. Hal objects under Rule 404(b). How should the court rule?
step: Identify the inference: the prosecution offers the earlier fires to show absence of accident, a permitted purpose, by the unlikelihood that three businesses of one owner burn accidentally. That chain does not depend on Hal being a bad person.
step: The issue is disputed because Hal claims accident, so the evidence has real probative value. The prosecution also articulated its purpose and reasoning in its notice.
step: Proof of the act: under Huddleston the jury need only be able to find that the earlier fires were set by Hal. If the fire marshal found the earlier fires accidental, there may be insufficient evidence of an act at all.
step: Rule 403: the court weighs the similarity of the fires and their closeness in time against the prejudice of suggesting that Hal is an arsonist. If the evidence of the earlier fires being set by Hal is substantial, the court should admit it for absence of accident only, with a limiting instruction.

@mistakes
- Excluding other-acts evidence merely because it is damaging; the question is whether the inference requires propensity.
- Requiring the court to find by a preponderance that the act occurred; Huddleston applies a Rule 104(b) sufficiency standard.
- Treating one or two prior incidents as "habit"; habit requires regular, specific, repeated conduct.
- Listing a purpose (like "intent") without showing the issue is genuinely disputed.

@practice self
prompt: Decide the admissibility of each of the following five uses of character or other-acts evidence and give a one-sentence reason. (1) In a civil negligence suit, the plaintiff offers evidence that the defendant driver has two prior speeding tickets, to show he was speeding. (2) In an assault trial, the defendant offers a witness to testify that she knows the defendant to be peaceable. (3) In a theft trial, the prosecution offers evidence that the defendant stole a similar item from the same employer a month earlier, to show he knew how to bypass the lock. (4) In a suit over an unpaid invoice, the defendant says he never received it and the plaintiff offers evidence that his office mails all invoices the day they are issued. solution: (1) Inadmissible: offered to show propensity to speed, barred by Rules 404(a)(1) and 404(b)(1); there is no civil exception. (2) Admissible: a defendant may offer a pertinent trait of his own character under Rule 404(a)(2)(A), by reputation or opinion; it opens the door to rebuttal. (3) Admissible for knowledge, preparation or plan, if the jury could find that the defendant committed the earlier theft and Rule 403 does not bar it, with a limiting instruction. (5) Admissible under Rule 413 for any relevant matter including propensity, subject to Rule 403 and the 15-day disclosure requirement.
