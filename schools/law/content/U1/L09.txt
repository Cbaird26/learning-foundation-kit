@article
If a statement is hearsay and no Rule 801(d) exclusion applies, look for an exception. The exceptions rest on circumstances that make a statement unusually reliable or that make live testimony unnecessary. There are two lists: Rule 803, where the declarant's availability is **immaterial**, and Rule 804, where the declarant must be **unavailable**. A statement that meets an exception still must pass Rule 403 and, in a criminal case, the Confrontation Clause.

### Rule 803: availability immaterial

- **(1) Present sense impression.** A statement describing or explaining an event or condition, made while or immediately after the declarant perceived it. The reliability comes from the lack of time to fabricate.
- **(2) Excited utterance.** A statement relating to a startling event or condition, made while the declarant was under the stress of excitement that it caused. The time gap is measured by how long the stress lasted, not by seconds.
- **(3) Then-existing mental, emotional or physical condition.** A statement of the declarant's then-existing state of mind (such as motive, intent or plan) or emotional, sensory or physical condition (such as mental feeling, pain or bodily health), but **not** a statement of memory or belief to prove the fact remembered or believed, unless it relates to the validity or terms of the declarant's will. A statement of intent ("I'm going to Denver tomorrow") may prove the declarant later acted on the intent (*Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Hillmon* (1892)); a statement "I was angry because Tom cheated me" is a statement of memory and is excluded.
- **(4) Statement made for medical diagnosis or treatment.** A statement made for, and reasonably pertinent to, medical diagnosis or treatment, describing medical history, past or present symptoms, or their inception or general cause. It covers statements to nonphysicians, and to a physician consulted only for testimony; fault ("the driver ran the light") is generally not pertinent.
- **(5) Recorded recollection.** A record on a matter the witness once knew but now cannot recall well enough to testify fully, made or adopted when the matter was fresh in the witness's memory and accurately reflecting the witness's knowledge. It may be read into evidence but received as an exhibit only if offered by an adverse party.
- **(6) Records of a regularly conducted activity.** A record of an act, event, condition, opinion or diagnosis if (A) it was made at or near the time by, or from information transmitted by, someone with knowledge; (B) it was kept in the course of a regularly conducted activity of a business, organization, occupation or calling, whether or not for profit; (C) making the record was a regular practice of that activity; (D) these conditions are shown by the testimony of the custodian or another qualified witness, or by a certification under Rule 902(11) or (12); and (E) the opponent does not show that the source of information or the method or circumstances of preparation indicate a lack of trustworthiness. A report prepared for litigation fails (E). A person outside the business who supplies information must also be covered by an exception, a Rule 805 issue.
- **(8) Public records.** Records of a public office that set out (i) the office's activities, (ii) a matter observed under a legal duty to report, but in a criminal case not matters observed by law-enforcement personnel, or (iii) in a civil case or against the government in a criminal case, factual findings from a legally authorized investigation, unless the opponent shows a lack of trustworthiness.
- Others to recognize: (7) absence of a record, (18) learned treatises (read into evidence, not received as exhibits), and (22) judgment of a previous felony conviction offered to prove any fact essential to the judgment.

### Rule 804: declarant unavailable

**Unavailability** (Rule 804(a)): the declarant is exempted by a privilege ruling; refuses to testify despite a court order; testifies to not remembering the subject; cannot be present because of death or a then-existing infirmity, physical illness or mental illness; or is absent and the proponent has not been able, by process or other reasonable means, to procure the declarant's attendance (or testimony for exceptions (2), (3), (4)). A proponent who procures the declarant's absence to use the exception cannot rely on it. The exceptions:

- **(b)(1) Former testimony.** Testimony given as a witness at a trial, hearing or lawful deposition, offered against a party who had (or in a civil case, whose predecessor in interest had) an opportunity and similar motive to develop it by direct, cross or redirect examination.
- **(b)(2) Statement made under the belief of imminent death** (dying declaration). Only in a homicide prosecution or a civil case; the declarant must believe death is imminent and speak about its cause or circumstances.
- **(b)(3) Statement against interest.** A statement that a reasonable person in the declarant's position would have made only if believing it true because it was so contrary to the declarant's proprietary or pecuniary interest, or had so great a tendency to invalidate the declarant's claim against someone else or to expose the declarant to civil or criminal liability; and that is supported by corroborating circumstances that clearly indicate its trustworthiness, if offered in a criminal case as one that tends to expose the declarant to criminal liability. Under the amendment effective December 1, 2024, the court must consider the totality of circumstances under which the statement was made and any evidence corroborating it. In *Williamson v. United States* (1994), the Court held that only the specifically self-inculpatory parts of a narrative are within the exception; statements that shift blame to others are not.
- **(b)(6) Forfeiture by wrongdoing.** A statement offered against a party that wrongfully caused, or acquiesced in wrongfully causing, the declarant's unavailability as a witness, and did so intending that result. The exception applies only if the wrongdoing was intended to prevent the declarant from testifying (*Giles v. California* (2008)).

### Rule 807: the residual exception

A hearsay statement that falls within no other exception may be admitted if it is supported by sufficient guarantees of trustworthiness, after considering the totality of circumstances under which it was made and evidence, if any, corroborating the statement, and it is more probative on the point than any other evidence the proponent can obtain through reasonable efforts. The proponent must give reasonable written notice before trial. Courts use it sparingly.

@example
title: A statement at the scene and a doctor's visit
problem: Pia is hit by a truck. A bystander, Gus, shouts moments later, still shaking, "That truck ran the red light!" An hour later Pia tells an emergency doctor, "A truck hit me while I was crossing, and the driver ran the red light. My left leg hurts." In Pia's suit against the truck's owner, Pia offers evidence of both statements to prove the truck ran the red light. Gus will not testify. Analyze.
step: Gus's statement is hearsay offered for truth. It relates to a startling event and was made under the stress of the event, so it qualifies as an excited utterance under Rule 803(2). Gus's availability is immaterial.
step: Pia's statement to the doctor: "a truck hit me" and "my left leg hurts" describe the cause and symptoms and are pertinent to diagnosis and treatment, so they fall within Rule 803(4).
step: "The driver ran the red light" assigns fault, which is not reasonably pertinent to treating her leg, so that portion does not fall within Rule 803(4).
step: Conclusion: Gus's statement comes in on liability under Rule 803(2). The part of Pia's statement about the red light is excluded under Rule 803(4), though it may be admissible on another basis, for example if made under stress, and she is available to testify to it herself.

@mistakes
- Saying a statement is an excited utterance merely because it was made soon after an event; the declarant must still be under the stress of the event.
- Applying Rule 803(3) to a statement of memory, such as "I was angry because Tom cheated me," to prove what happened.
- Admitting an entire medical history statement, including fault, under Rule 803(4).
- Treating a declarant as unavailable merely because the proponent did not try to secure attendance, or admitting dying declarations in non-homicide criminal cases.

@practice self
prompt: Sam told his coworker Lee on Friday, (a) "I'm driving to Denver tomorrow," and (b) "I'm upset because Tom cheated me last month." Sam is missing and has not been found, and he is not a witness. In a civil suit, the plaintiff offers Lee's testimony about (a) to prove that Sam drove to Denver, and about (b) to prove that Tom cheated Sam. Analyze each under Rule 803(3).
solution: Both statements are hearsay offered for their truth, so an exception is needed. (a) is a statement of Sam's then-existing intent or plan. Under Rule 803(3) and Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Hillmon (1892), a declarant's statement of intent may be used to prove that the declarant later acted on that intent, so it is admissible to show that Sam drove to Denver. Availability is immaterial. (b) states that Sam is upset, which is a then-existing emotional condition and could be admitted for that purpose, but it also asserts that Tom cheated Sam, which is a statement of memory or belief offered to prove the fact remembered. Rule 803(3) excludes statements of memory or belief to prove the fact remembered or believed, unless they relate to the validity or terms of a will. So the portion offered to prove that Tom cheated Sam is inadmissible under Rule 803(3).
rubric: Identifies statement (a) as a statement of intent or plan and admits it to prove Sam's own later conduct.
rubric: Identifies statement (b) as a statement of memory and excludes it to prove the past fact.
rubric: Cites Rule 803(3) and the will exception or Hillmon.
hint: Separate the statement of feeling from the statement about what happened in the past.
