@article
**Character evidence** is evidence of a person's disposition or trait, such as honesty, violence or carelessness. The question in evidence law is not whether a person's character is relevant, since it often has some probative value, but whether the law lets a party use it to prove that the person acted **in conformity** with that character on a particular occasion. The answer, in general, is no.

### The rule: Rule 404(a)(1)

Evidence of a person's character or character trait is not admissible to prove that on a particular occasion the person acted in accordance with the character or trait. The policy is that propensity reasoning is overvalued by juries and invites punishing a person for who they are rather than what they did. The rule applies in civil and criminal cases, and it has no civil exceptions: in a civil assault case, you cannot show the defendant has a violent reputation to prove he struck first.

### Exceptions in criminal cases: Rule 404(a)(2)

- **The defendant's own character.** A defendant may offer evidence of a **pertinent** trait of the defendant, such as law-abidingness in a theft case. If the defendant does, the prosecution may offer evidence to rebut it. This is "opening the door."
- **The alleged victim's character.** A defendant may offer evidence of a pertinent trait of the alleged victim, such as violence in a self-defense case. If the defendant does, the prosecution may rebut it and may also offer evidence of the **defendant's** same trait.
- **Homicide, peacefulness.** In a homicide case, the prosecution may offer evidence of the victim's trait of peacefulness to rebut evidence that the victim was the first aggressor.
- **Witness credibility.** Rule 404(a)(3) refers to Rules 607, 608 and 609 (week 5).

The trait must be **pertinent** to the charge: honesty in a fraud case, peacefulness in an assault case; "good character" in general is too broad in most courts.

### How to prove character: Rule 405

When character evidence is admissible, it may be proved by testimony about **reputation** or in the form of an **opinion**. On **cross-examination of the character witness**, the court may allow inquiry into relevant specific instances of the person's conduct. This is how the prosecution tests the witness's knowledge: "Have you heard that the defendant was arrested for fraud?" or "Did you know…?" (*Michelson v. United States* (1948) upheld such questions, subject to the court's discretion, with the examiner needing a good-faith basis for the incident and the court guarding against unfair prejudice.) The questioner is bound by the answer: extrinsic proof of the incident is not allowed, because it is collateral. Specific instances on **direct** are permitted only when character is an **essential element** of a charge, claim or defense (Rule 405(b)): examples are the truth defense in some defamation claims or fitness in a custody dispute. Essential-element cases are rare, so do not confuse them with ordinary uses of character as circumstantial evidence.

### Rule 412: the rape-shield rule

In a civil or criminal proceeding involving alleged sexual misconduct, evidence offered to prove that a victim engaged in **other sexual behavior** or to prove a victim's **sexual predisposition** is not admissible. Exceptions in a criminal case: (A) specific instances of sexual behavior offered to prove that someone other than the defendant was the source of semen, injury or other physical evidence; (B) specific instances of sexual behavior with the accused offered to prove consent, or offered by the prosecutor; and (C) evidence whose exclusion would violate the defendant's constitutional rights. In a civil case, the evidence is admissible only if its probative value **substantially outweighs** the danger of harm to any victim and of unfair prejudice to any party, and reputation evidence is admissible only if the victim has placed it in controversy. A party who intends to offer evidence under an exception must file a motion that specifically describes it, at least 14 days before trial unless the court sets a different time for good cause. The court must hold an in camera hearing.

### Rules 413 through 415: propensity allowed

In a criminal case in which the defendant is accused of sexual assault (Rule 413) or child molestation (Rule 414), the court may admit evidence that the defendant committed any other sexual assault or child molestation, and it may be considered on **any matter to which it is relevant**, including propensity. Rule 415 does the same in civil cases based on a party's alleged sexual assault or child molestation. The prosecution must disclose the evidence to the defendant at least 15 days before trial. Rule 403 still applies.

### Applying the rules

Issue: is the evidence character evidence offered to show conduct in conformity? Rule: Rule 404(a)(1), then check whether a criminal exception applies and whether the method complies with Rule 405. Application: identify who opened the door, whether the trait is pertinent, and whether the form is reputation, opinion or specific instances. Conclusion: admit, exclude, or admit for a limited purpose.

@example
title: Opening the door in a self-defense case
problem: Kay is charged with assault for stabbing Lou. Kay claims self-defense. Kay's lawyer calls a witness to testify that Lou has a reputation in the community for violence. On cross-examination, the prosecutor wants to ask Kay's own witness whether she has heard that Kay beat up a neighbor last year. Later the prosecutor wants to call a witness to testify that Kay has a reputation for violence. Rule on each.
step: Kay offered a pertinent trait of the alleged victim under Rule 404(a)(2)(B). Reputation is a permitted method under Rule 405(a), so the testimony about Lou is admissible.
step: Kay opened the door. The prosecution may rebut Lou's character evidence and may also offer evidence of Kay's same trait, violence. That includes the prosecution's own reputation witness about Kay.
step: The "have you heard" question is a specific-instance question on cross, allowed under Rule 405(a) to test a character witness's knowledge, but only about the person whose character the witness described. This witness described Lou, not Kay, so the question about Kay's assault on a neighbor is improper and the objection should be sustained; the prosecutor must use her own witness on Kay's character instead.
step: Conclusion: admit the reputation testimony from both sides; refuse the specific-instance question to the witness who testified only about Lou. When a character witness has testified about the defendant, the prosecutor may ask such questions with a good-faith basis and is bound by the answers.

@mistakes
- Using specific instances on direct examination to prove character when character is not an essential element; direct proof is limited to reputation or opinion.
- Allowing the prosecution to offer the defendant's bad character first; the door must be opened by the defendant (or by the first-aggressor evidence in a homicide case).
- Applying a criminal exception to a civil case, where Rule 404(a)(1) has no exception.
- Treating Rules 413 through 415 as ordinary Rule 404(b) evidence; they authorize propensity use, subject to Rule 403.

@practice self
prompt: Vera is charged with sexual assault. She is not the accuser; the accuser, Ward, says Vera assaulted him. Vera claims consent. Vera wants to offer evidence that Ward had consensual sexual encounters with two other people last year, to show that Ward is sexually promiscuous and so likely consented. solution: Issue: whether evidence of Ward's other sexual behavior is admissible to prove consent. Conclusion: the evidence is inadmissible, and Vera would need a pretrial motion with a specific showing under Rule 412(c) to offer any evidence under an exception.
