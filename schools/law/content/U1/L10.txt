@article
The Sixth Amendment says that in all criminal prosecutions the accused shall enjoy the right "to be confronted with the witnesses against him." A statement can satisfy the hearsay rules and still be barred by the Confrontation Clause. The two inquiries are separate, and in a criminal case you must run both.

### The Crawford rule

*Crawford v. Washington* (2004) held that **testimonial** out-of-court statements by a witness who does not appear at trial are inadmissible against a criminal defendant unless the declarant is **unavailable** and the defendant had a **prior opportunity to cross-examine**. The Court rejected the earlier reliability test of *Ohio v. Roberts*: a judge's finding that a statement is reliable does not substitute for the constitutionally required test of cross-examination. Statements that are **nontestimonial** are not subject to the Confrontation Clause at all and are governed only by the hearsay rules and due process. The Clause also does not bar statements offered for purposes **other than their truth** (*Crawford* n.9). The Clause applies only in criminal prosecutions, and it is the defendant's right.

### What is testimonial?

*Crawford* gave a core class: prior testimony at a preliminary hearing, before a grand jury or at a former trial, and statements made in police interrogations. Later cases adopted a **primary purpose** test, objectively judged from the circumstances:

- *Davis v. Washington* (2006) (with *Hammon v. Indiana*): statements made to police in the course of interrogation are **nontestimonial** when the circumstances objectively indicate the primary purpose is to enable police to meet an **ongoing emergency** (a 911 call as the attack is under way), and **testimonial** when the primary purpose is to establish or prove past events potentially relevant to later prosecution (a victim's account to officers after the danger has passed).
- *Michigan v. Bryant* (2011): whether there is an ongoing emergency is a broad, objective inquiry, including the threat to the public and the weapon used; the informality of the setting also matters.
- *Ohio v. Clark* (2015): statements by a young child to a teacher about abuse were nontestimonial; statements to persons other than law enforcement are much less likely to be testimonial, though the Court did not hold such statements are categorically outside the Clause.
- Statements between private persons in casual settings (a remark to a friend, a boast to a cellmate) and ordinary business records are generally nontestimonial.

### Forensic reports and experts

In *Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts* (2009), sworn certificates of drug analysis offered to prove the substance's identity were testimonial; the analyst had to testify. In *Bullcoming v. New Mexico* (2011), a surrogate analyst who did not perform or observe the test could not testify about another analyst's testimonial report. In *Smith v. Arizona* (2024), the Court held that when an expert conveys an absent analyst's statements in support of the expert's opinion, and the statements support the opinion only if true, they are offered for their truth, so the Confrontation Clause applies if the statements are testimonial; the Court remanded for the testimonial question. The expert cannot serve as a conduit for an absent analyst's testimonial statements.

### Codefendant confessions: Bruton

In a joint trial, a nontestifying codefendant's confession that **directly implicates** the defendant cannot be admitted against the defendant even with a limiting instruction (*Bruton v. United States* (1968)). *Richardson v. Marsh* (1987) allowed a confession redacted to remove all reference to the defendant, with a limiting instruction. *Gray v. Maryland* (1998) held that obvious redactions, such as a blank or the word "deleted," are not enough. In *Samia v. United States* (2023), the Court held there was no violation when the confession was redacted to refer to "the other person" and a limiting instruction was given. The remedy for a *Bruton* problem is redaction, severance or exclusion.

### Forfeiture and other points

A defendant forfeits confrontation rights by wrongdoing intended to prevent the witness from testifying (*Giles v. California* (2008)): a defendant who kills a victim for reasons unrelated to preventing testimony does not forfeit. Opening the door to a statement does not alone forfeit the right (*Hemphill v. New York* (2022)). A defendant may be able to waive the right, and notice-and-demand statutes for lab reports are acceptable if the defendant can insist on live testimony. The Court has left open whether dying declarations form a historical exception.

### Framework

1. Is the case a criminal prosecution and is the statement offered against the defendant for its truth?
2. Is the statement testimonial (primary purpose)?
3. If testimonial and the declarant is not testifying: was the declarant unavailable and was there a prior opportunity for cross-examination, or has the defendant forfeited the right?
4. If nontestimonial, apply the hearsay rules only.

@example
title: A 911 call and a later interview
problem: Vic calls 911 while Ned is still in her house: "Ned is breaking in and has a knife!" Later, after Ned has left, Vic gives a recorded interview at the police station describing the break-in. Vic does not testify at Ned's trial and Ned never cross-examined her. The prosecution offers both. Assume the hearsay rules are satisfied. Analyze under the Confrontation Clause.
step: The case is a criminal prosecution and the statements are offered for their truth against Ned, so the Clause applies.
step: The 911 call: Vic was reporting an emergency in progress to get police help. The primary purpose was to enable police to meet an ongoing emergency (Davis), so it is nontestimonial and the Clause does not bar it.
step: The station interview: the emergency was over and the primary purpose was to record past events for prosecution, which is a police interrogation within the core of testimonial statements (Hammon).
step: Because Vic is not testifying and Ned had no prior chance to cross-examine her, the interview is barred, regardless of its reliability. The 911 call comes in if it satisfies a hearsay exception such as excited utterance.

@mistakes
- Treating a statement as admissible under the Confrontation Clause because it fits a hearsay exception, or deciding admissibility by whether the statement is "reliable."
- Applying the Clause to statements offered for a non-truth purpose, or to nontestimonial statements.
- Assuming the Clause protects only against police interrogation; the primary-purpose test also reaches forensic reports and prior testimony.
- Overlooking that Bruton problems arise only when the confession of a nontestifying codefendant is used in a joint trial against the defendant.
