@article
Rule 801(d) lists statements that are **defined as not hearsay**, even though they are out-of-court statements offered for their truth. The label is a policy judgment: these statements are considered reliable enough or fair enough to admit without an exception. Because they are not hearsay, you reach them before the exceptions of Rules 803 and 804.

### Rule 801(d)(1): prior statements of a testifying witness

The declarant must testify and be **subject to cross-examination about the prior statement**. Then the statement is not hearsay if it is:

- **(A) Inconsistent** with the declarant's testimony and **given under penalty of perjury** at a trial, hearing or other proceeding, or in a deposition. Grand-jury testimony qualifies; a sworn affidavit given to police or an unsworn statement does not. If the statement is inconsistent but not given this way, it can be used only to impeach.
- **(B) Consistent** with the declarant's testimony and offered (i) to rebut an express or implied charge that the declarant recently fabricated it or acted from a recent improper influence or motive in testifying, or (ii) to rehabilitate the declarant's credibility as a witness when attacked on another ground (for example, to explain an apparent inconsistency); since the 2014 amendment, consistent statements in both situations are admissible for their truth, not just to rehabilitate. Under *Tome v. United States* (1995), a consistent statement offered to rebut a charge of recent fabrication or improper motive must have been made **before** the alleged motive or influence arose (the premotive requirement).
- **(C) Identification** of a person the declarant perceived earlier (a lineup, photo array or showup identification), made after perceiving the person.

### Rule 801(d)(2): an opposing party's statement

A statement is not hearsay if it is offered **against an opposing party** and:

- **(A)** was made by the party in an individual or representative capacity;
- **(B)** is one the party **manifested that it adopted or believed to be true**, including by silence in circumstances where a reasonable person would have denied it, but not silence after receiving a *Miranda* warning;
- **(C)** was made by a person whom the party **authorized** to make a statement on the subject;
- **(D)** was made by the party's **agent or employee** on a matter within the scope of that relationship **and while it existed**; or
- **(E)** was made by the party's **coconspirator during and in furtherance of the conspiracy**.

Points that recur: no personal-knowledge requirement and no requirement that the statement was against the party's interest when made; a party's own statement is admissible only when offered **against** that party, not by that party; the statement must be considered but does not by itself establish the declarant's authority (C), the existence or scope of the agency or employment (D), or the existence of the conspiracy and the participants' membership (E). Under *Bourjaily v. United States* (1987), the court decides these preliminary facts under Rule 104(a) by a preponderance of the evidence, and may consider the statement itself, though the rule now says the statement alone does not establish these facts. Once the conspiracy has ended, or a member has been arrested and is cooperating or confessing, later statements such as a confession to police are not made during and in furtherance of the conspiracy. The 2024 amendment adds that if a party's claim, defense or potential liability is directly derived from a declarant or the declarant's principal, a statement admissible against the declarant or principal under Rule 801(d)(2) is also admissible against the party.

### Other cautions

Rule 801(d)(2) deals only with hearsay. A confession by a co-defendant that implicates the defendant is not an opposing-party statement as to the defendant, and in a joint trial the Confrontation Clause may bar it (*Bruton*, week 10). Statements made during and in furtherance of a conspiracy are ordinarily nontestimonial, so the Confrontation Clause rarely bars them. Offering a statement against the declarant under Rule 801(d)(2)(A) does not make it admissible against a codefendant.

### Classifying statements

The deliverable asks you to classify statements. For each: (1) identify the declarant and the purpose of the offer; (2) if offered for its truth, check Rule 801(d)(1) and (d)(2); (3) if no exclusion applies, label it hearsay and move to the exceptions. Label as **not hearsay** a statement that is not offered for its truth or is defined as not hearsay by Rule 801(d).

@example
title: Grand-jury testimony and a deal
problem: Al is charged with fraud. Witness Ruth testifies at trial that she did not see Al sign the contract. The prosecution offers Ruth's grand-jury testimony that she saw Al sign, to prove he signed. Later another prosecution witness, Sam, testifies that he saw Al sign, and the defense suggests Sam is testifying only to win leniency on his own charges, filed in May. The prosecution then offers a statement Sam made to an agent in March that he saw Al sign. How should the court rule on each?
step: Grand-jury testimony: Ruth testifies at trial and is subject to cross-examination. Her grand-jury testimony is inconsistent with her trial testimony and was given under penalty of perjury at a proceeding, so it is not hearsay under Rule 801(d)(1)(A) and may be used for its truth.
step: The March statement is consistent with Sam's testimony, Sam is subject to cross-examination, and the defense has charged improper motive. Under Rule 801(d)(1)(B)(i), it is admissible substantively if it was made before the motive arose.
step: The alleged motive (leniency) arose when charges were filed in May. The March statement predates that, which is what Tome requires, so it is admissible for its truth.
step: Conclusion: both statements are admissible as substantive evidence. If the March statement had been made in June, after the alleged motive arose, it would not qualify under Rule 801(d)(1)(B)(i).

@mistakes
- Using an unsworn inconsistent statement, or a statement to police, as substantive evidence under Rule 801(d)(1)(A).
- Admitting a prior consistent statement made after the alleged motive to fabricate arose.
- Requiring a party's own statement to be against interest, or based on personal knowledge, before admitting it under Rule 801(d)(2).
- Applying Rule 801(d)(2)(D) to statements made after the employment ended or outside its scope, or admitting a statement by a coconspirator made after the conspiracy ended.
