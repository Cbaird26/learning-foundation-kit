@article
Friction and circular motion both apply Newton's second law to situations where forces point in awkward directions.

### Friction

**Friction** is a contact force parallel to the surfaces, opposing relative motion or its tendency. It depends on how hard the surfaces are pressed together, which is the normal force $N$:

- **Static friction** acts when there is no sliding. It adjusts to match the applied force up to a maximum: $f_s \le \mu_s N$.
- **Kinetic friction** acts when surfaces slide: $f_k = \mu_k N$, with $\mu_k$ usually smaller than $\mu_s$.

The coefficients $\mu$ are unitless. Friction does not depend much on contact area. On a flat surface with only gravity vertical, $N = mg$, but always find $N$ from the vertical force balance rather than assuming it.

### Inclined planes

On a slope of angle $\theta$, choose axes **along** and **perpendicular** to the surface. Weight $mg$ points straight down and splits into

$$mg\sin\theta \ \text{(down the slope)}, \qquad mg\cos\theta \ \text{(into the surface)}.$$

There is no acceleration perpendicular to the surface, so $N = mg\cos\theta$. Along the slope, for a block sliding down with kinetic friction:

$$ma = mg\sin\theta - \mu_k mg\cos\theta \quad\Rightarrow\quad a = g(\sin\theta - \mu_k\cos\theta).$$

Without friction, $a = g\sin\theta$. A block at rest on the verge of slipping satisfies $\tan\theta = \mu_s$.

### Uniform circular motion

An object moving at constant speed $v$ on a circle of radius $r$ is still **accelerating**, because the direction of its velocity changes. The acceleration points toward the center:

$$a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{4\pi^2 r}{T^2},$$

where $T$ is the period. By the second law there must be a net inward force, the **centripetal force**:

$$F_c = \frac{mv^2}{r}.$$

"Centripetal" is not a new kind of force; it is the name for whatever real forces add up to point inward. For a car on a flat curve it is static friction; for a ball on a string, the tension; for a satellite, gravity; at the top of a vertical loop, a combination of weight and normal force.

If the inward force disappears, the object moves off in a straight line along the tangent, not outward along a radius. The "outward push" you feel in a turning car is your body's inertia.

### Method

Draw the FBD, choose one axis pointing toward the center (or along the slope), write $\sum F = ma$ for that axis, and keep the perpendicular axis for finding $N$.

@example
title: Friction on a flat curve
problem: An $800\ \text{kg}$ car goes around a flat circular curve of radius $50\ \text{m}$ at $15\ \text{m/s}$. What friction force is needed, and what is the minimum coefficient of static friction? Use $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.
step: The centripetal force is supplied by static friction: $f_s = mv^2/r$.
step: Compute: $f_s = 800(15)^2/50 = 800(225)/50 = 3600\ \text{N}$.
step: The normal force on level ground is $N = mg = 800(9.8) = 7840\ \text{N}$.
step: Static friction must satisfy $f_s \le \mu_s N$, so $\mu_s \ge 3600/7840 \approx 0.46$.

@mistakes
- Using $f = \mu N$ for static friction in every case; $\mu_s N$ is only the maximum.
- Assuming $N = mg$ on an incline instead of $N = mg\cos\theta$.
- Drawing a "centrifugal force" outward on the object in an inertial-frame free-body diagram, or adding a separate centripetal arrow in addition to the real forces.
- Using the diameter instead of the radius in $v^2/r$.

@practice num
prompt: A $20\ \text{kg}$ box slides across a horizontal floor with a coefficient of kinetic friction $\mu_k = 0.25$. What is the kinetic friction force in newtons? Use $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.
answer: 49
check: 0.25 * 20 * 9.8
tol: 0.01
hint: On a horizontal floor with no vertical acceleration, $N = mg$.
hint: $f_k = \mu_k N$.
solution: $N = (20)(9.8) = 196\ \text{N}$, so $f_k = 0.25(196) = 49\ \text{N}$.
unit: N

@practice num
prompt: A horizontal $39.2\ \text{N}$ force pushes a $10\ \text{kg}$ crate across a floor with $\mu_k = 0.20$. What is the crate's acceleration in $\text{m/s}^2$? Use $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.
answer: 1.96
check: (39.2 - 0.20 * 10 * 9.8) / 10
tol: 0.01
hint: Find the kinetic friction first, from $N = mg$.
hint: $f_k = 19.6\ \text{N}$ opposes the push; then $a = (F - f_k)/m$.
solution: $f_k = 0.20(10)(9.8) = 19.6\ \text{N}$. $a = (39.2 - 19.6)/10 = 1.96\ \text{m/s}^2$.
unit: m/s^2

@practice num
prompt: A cyclist rides at $10\ \text{m/s}$ around a circular track of radius $25\ \text{m}$. What is her centripetal acceleration in $\text{m/s}^2$?
answer: 4
check: 10**2 / 25
hint: Use $a_c = v^2/r$.
hint: $v^2 = 100$.
solution: $a_c = 10^2/25 = 4\ \text{m/s}^2$, directed toward the center of the track.
unit: m/s^2

@practice mc
prompt: A car rounds a flat, level curve at constant speed. Which force provides the centripetal force that keeps the car on the curve?
choice: The engine's forward driving force
choice: A centrifugal force pushing the car outward
choice*: Static friction between the tires and the road, directed toward the center
choice: The car's weight
hint: The net force must point toward the center of the curve and be horizontal.
solution: Weight and the normal force are vertical and cancel. The only horizontal force pointing toward the center is static friction between the tires and road.

@practice num
prompt: A block slides down a frictionless ramp inclined at $30^\circ$ above the horizontal. What is its acceleration down the ramp in $\text{m/s}^2$? Use $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.
answer: 4.9
check: 9.8 * sin(pi / 6)
tol: 0.01
hint: Only the component of weight along the ramp accelerates the block.
hint: $a = g\sin\theta$.
solution: $a = 9.8\sin 30^\circ = 9.8(0.5) = 4.9\ \text{m/s}^2$.
unit: m/s^2

@quiz num
prompt: A block slides down a $30^\circ$ incline with a coefficient of kinetic friction $\mu_k = 0.10$. Using $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$, what is its acceleration down the incline in $\text{m/s}^2$, to the nearest $0.01$?
answer: 4.05
check: 9.8 * (sin(pi / 6) - 0.10 * cos(pi / 6))
tol: 1%
hint: Along the slope: $ma = mg\sin\theta - f_k$, and $N = mg\cos\theta$.
hint: $a = g(\sin\theta - \mu_k\cos\theta)$ with $\sin 30^\circ = 0.5$ and $\cos 30^\circ \approx 0.866$.
solution: $a = 9.8(0.5 - 0.10 \times 0.866) = 9.8(0.4134) \approx 4.05\ \text{m/s}^2$.
unit: m/s^2

@quiz num
prompt: A car rounds a flat circular curve of radius $40\ \text{m}$. The coefficient of static friction between tires and road is $0.50$. What is the maximum speed at which the car can take the curve without slipping, in m/s? Use $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.
answer: 14
check: sqrt(0.50 * 9.8 * 40)
tol: 0.01
hint: At the limit, static friction is at its maximum and supplies all the centripetal force.
hint: $\mu_s mg = mv^2/r$, so the mass cancels.
solution: $v = \sqrt{\mu_s g r} = \sqrt{0.50(9.8)(40)} = \sqrt{196} = 14\ \text{m/s}$.
unit: m/s
