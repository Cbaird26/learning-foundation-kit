@article
Oral argument is a conversation with judges who have usually read the briefs. It is not a second reading of your brief. The judges use it to test your position, find the limits of your rule, and decide what to write. Your task is to make your best points clearly and to be most useful when the judges ask questions.

### The rules (FRAP 34)

A party may file a statement explaining why oral argument should be allowed (Rule 34(a)(1)). Oral argument must be allowed in every case unless a panel of three judges unanimously agrees that it is unnecessary because (A) the appeal is frivolous, (B) the dispositive issue or issues have been authoritatively decided, or (C) the facts and legal arguments are adequately presented in the briefs and record, and the decisional process would not be significantly aided by argument (Rule 34(a)(2)). Many appeals are decided without argument. The clerk must advise the parties whether argument will be scheduled and how much time each side is allowed (Rule 34(b)); local rules and circuit practice usually fix the time, often 10 to 15 minutes a side. The appellant opens and concludes unless the court orders otherwise (Rule 34(c)). That is why the appellant may reserve part of the time for rebuttal.

### Structure

**Opening (about one minute).** Greet the court ("May it please the court"), identify yourself and your client, state whether you reserve time for rebuttal, and give a **roadmap**: the relief you seek and the two or three reasons the court should grant it. A good opening can be said in one breath: "This is a slip-and-fall case in which the district court granted summary judgment for lack of notice. We ask the court to reverse for two reasons: Acme's own employee created the hazard, and notice therefore was not required."

**Body.** Argue your strongest point first, unless the court's questions or the logic of the case require otherwise. For each issue state the rule, the key facts, and why the rule applied to the facts requires your result. Use record cites sparingly and precisely. Expect to cover fewer points than you outlined, because questions take time. The appellee should respond to what is most important to the panel rather than repeat the brief, and may concede a point to gain credibility on the rest.

**Conclusion.** State the precise relief ("reverse and remand for trial") and sit down, or stop when time expires. If the red light is on, ask permission to finish a sentence.

**Rebuttal.** The appellant's rebuttal is limited to answering the appellee's argument. Do not introduce new issues, and remember that arguments not made in the brief are generally treated as forfeited. Choose one or two points where the appellee's argument is weakest.

### Preparing

1. Master the record: know the key dates, the page cites and the facts in both directions.
2. Boil the case down to a theme and two or three reasons.
3. Write a **one-page outline** with headings and key cites, not a script. A script produces reading, and judges want eye contact and responsiveness.
4. Budget the time. With a 10-minute argument, a typical plan is: one minute for the opening, six or seven for the body, and two reserved for rebuttal, with room for questions throughout.
5. Practice aloud with someone who interrupts.

### Delivery

Speak conversationally, at a measured pace, and address the bench. Use plain words. Never interrupt a judge; when a judge starts a question, stop. Answer the question that was asked, then return to your outline. Be candid about weaknesses, and avoid personal attacks on the court below or opposing counsel.

@example
title: Budgeting a ten-minute argument
problem: Appellant has 10 minutes and wants 2 minutes for rebuttal. The argument has a one-minute opening, a 30-second conclusion, and two issues of equal length. How many minutes should each issue be given in the plan, leaving no slack for questions? Then say what to do about questions.
step: Subtract rebuttal: 10 - 2 = 8 minutes of main argument.
step: Subtract the opening and conclusion: 8 - 1 - 0.5 = 6.5 minutes for the issues.
step: Divide between the two issues: 6.5 / 2 = 3.25 minutes each.
step: Recognize that questions will take much of this time, so build each issue so it can be delivered in under two minutes and mark which parts can be cut if the bench asks many questions.

@mistakes
- Reading a script or restating the brief instead of conversing with the judges.
- Failing to reserve rebuttal time at the start, or raising new issues on rebuttal.
- Spending all the time on weak or secondary points rather than the strongest argument.
- Treating questions as interruptions to get through rather than as the best indication of what concerns the court.
