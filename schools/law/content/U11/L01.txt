@article
In Legal Writing I you wrote **objective** documents: an office memo that predicts how a court will rule and tells a supervising lawyer the truth, including the bad news. In this course you write **persuasive** documents: motions, memoranda and briefs that ask a court to rule for your client. The law and facts are the same. What changes is the reader, the purpose and the way you arrange the material.

### Two documents, two jobs

| | Objective memo | Persuasive brief |
|---|---|---|
| Reader | A lawyer who must decide what to do | A judge or clerk who must decide the case |
| Purpose | Predict and advise | Persuade and give the court a path to your result |
| Tone | Balanced, hedged where uncertain | Confident, measured, never shrill |
| Structure | Both sides given equal weight | Your best arguments first, the other side answered |
| Candor | Complete | Accurate and complete on law, selective on emphasis |

### The limits: persuasion is not distortion

An advocate chooses what to emphasize but may not misstate. Under ABA Model Rule 3.3(a)(1), a lawyer may not knowingly make a false statement of fact or law to a tribunal. Under Rule 3.3(a)(2), a lawyer must disclose legal authority in the controlling jurisdiction that the lawyer knows to be directly adverse to the client's position and that opposing counsel has not disclosed. The duty reaches controlling authority only; adverse persuasive authority need not be volunteered, though a strong brief often addresses it anyway. FRCP 11(b) adds that legal contentions must be warranted by existing law or a nonfrivolous argument to change it, and factual contentions must have evidentiary support. A court that catches one misstatement stops trusting the rest of your brief, so **credibility** is your most valuable asset.

### The persuasive toolkit

1. **Order and position.** Readers remember what comes first and last. Lead with your strongest argument and end paragraphs and sections on your best point.
2. **Framing.** State the issue so that your answer feels natural. A question presented that recites the favorable facts and the governing rule frames the case before the court reads a word of argument.
3. **Emphasis through structure.** Put favorable facts in short main clauses with concrete subjects ("Acme's employee mopped the aisle"). Put unfavorable facts in subordinate clauses, with their context, in the middle of a paragraph. Do not omit a fact the court needs to decide the issue fairly.
4. **Affirmative rule statements.** State the rule in the form that supports you, using the precedent's own language, not a paraphrase that quietly changes it.
5. **Word choice.** Use the client's name rather than "the plaintiff," active verbs rather than nominalizations, and accurate nouns. Avoid adjectives and intensifiers ("clearly," "outrageous"); they signal weakness and invite a skeptical reading.
6. **Strategic concession.** Concede what you cannot win and say why it does not matter. A concession buys credibility for the points you contest.

### Dealing with bad facts and bad law

Do not hide them. Meet them early enough to control the framing, in a sentence or two, and then explain why they do not control. Distinguish adverse authority honestly (Week 5), or argue that it was wrongly decided or should not be extended.

@example
title: Turning an objective sentence into advocacy
problem: Hypothetical facts: Ana slipped on an aisle at Acme Market. The record shows an Acme employee mopped the aisle at 2:10 p.m. and Ana fell at 2:14 p.m. No warning sign had been placed. A "wet floor" sign was stored in a back room. Objective memo sentence: "Ana may be able to show that Acme created the hazard, but Acme will argue that a short interval is not unreasonable." Rewrite it persuasively for Ana.
step: Identify the strongest accurate facts: Acme's own employee created the hazard, there was no sign, and the fall followed within four minutes.
step: Make Acme the subject and use active verbs: "An Acme employee mopped the aisle and left without posting a warning."
step: Add the time sequence in its own short sentence, so the brevity of the interval works for Ana: "Four minutes later, Ana slipped on the wet floor."
step: Deal with the adverse point by reframing it: the sign was in the store, which shows Acme knew how to warn and chose not to. Result: "An Acme employee mopped the aisle at 2:10 p.m. and left without posting a warning, though a sign sat in the back room. At 2:14 p.m., Ana slipped on the wet floor."

@mistakes
- Hiding or misstating a material adverse fact or controlling adverse authority, which violates Model Rule 3.3 and destroys credibility.
- Using inflammatory adjectives and intensifiers ("clearly," "shocking") instead of concrete facts.
- Keeping the objective memo's hedges ("may," "arguably") in the brief's statement of your own position.
- Treating persuasion as one-sided exaggeration rather than careful selection, order and framing.

@practice ms
prompt: Tenant's brief argues that Landlord breached the implied warranty of habitability. The record shows Tenant gave written notice of no heat on January 4, Landlord repaired the heat on January 25, and Tenant stopped paying rent on January 5. Which of the following techniques for the brief are consistent with persuasive writing within ethical limits? Select all that apply.
choice*: Open the statement of facts with the notice and the three weeks without heat, and mention the rent stoppage later in a subordinate clause with its context.
choice*: Use Landlord's name as the subject of active sentences ("Landlord left the apartment without heat").
choice: State that Tenant never stopped paying rent, because the court will not check the record closely.
choice: Describe Landlord's conduct as "outrageous and heartless" in every paragraph so the court feels the equities.
hint: Persuasion works through selection, order and framing, not through false statements.
hint: Ask of each choice whether it is accurate and whether it relies on a fact or on an intensifier.
solution: Leading with favorable facts and placing the adverse fact in context is ordinary emphasis, and active sentences with Landlord as the subject are concrete and accurate. Saying Tenant never stopped paying rent is false and violates Model Rule 3.3(a)(1) and FRCP 11(b)(3). Repeated inflammatory adjectives replace facts with adjectives and cost credibility.

@practice self
prompt: Hypothetical facts: Ben bought a used boat from Seller. The record shows Seller told Ben the engine had "no known problems," and Seller's own mechanic had written two weeks earlier that the engine "needs a rebuild soon." Objective memo sentence: "Ben may be able to prove misrepresentation, although Seller will argue that the statement was only an opinion." Rewrite the sentence persuasively for Ben in two or three sentences, using only facts stated here.
hint: Plan your answer around this goal before you write: Uses only facts supplied and quotes them accurately.
solution: Two weeks before the sale, Seller's own mechanic wrote that the engine "needs a rebuild soon." Seller nonetheless told Ben the engine had "no known problems." A seller who has a written warning in hand cannot call the opposite statement mere opinion. This version leads with the strongest fact, makes Seller the subject, uses the exact quotations, and turns the adverse "opinion" point into an argument.
rubric: Uses only facts supplied and quotes them accurately.
rubric: Leads with the strongest fact and makes Seller the active subject.
rubric: Confronts the "opinion" counterargument instead of ignoring it.
rubric: Avoids intensifiers and hedges such as "arguably" and "may."

@quiz mc
prompt: Hypothetical facts: The record shows Tenant gave written notice of no heat on January 6 and Landlord repaired the heat on January 20. Tenant stopped paying rent on January 7. Which opening sentence best serves Tenant's brief while remaining accurate?
choice*: "After receiving Tenant's written notice on January 6, Landlord left the apartment without heat for two weeks and repaired it only on January 20." It leads with concrete favorable facts, in active voice.
choice: "Landlord's outrageous and heartless neglect left Tenant freezing for a month." It uses strong language that conveys the equities.
choice: "The record may show that, at some point, heat was allegedly unavailable for a period." It is safe because it hedges.
choice: "Tenant stopped paying rent on January 7, and Landlord later repaired the heat." It candidly puts every fact in chronological order.
hint: Fourteen days run from January 6 to January 20.
solution: The sentence beginning "After receiving Tenant's written notice" is accurate, concrete, active and leads with the favorable facts. "A month" overstates two weeks, and the adjectives are inflammatory. The hedged sentence is objective-memo style and states nothing firmly. The sentence that begins with the rent stoppage gives up the favorable frame by leading with Tenant's rent stoppage and omitting the notice.

@quiz self
prompt: In a brief you are writing, you find a decision of the controlling court of appeals that is directly adverse to your client and not cited by the other side. Explain in four or five sentences what Model Rule 3.3(a)(2) requires and how you would handle it in the brief.
hint: Plan your answer around this goal before you write: States the duty accurately (controlling jurisdiction, directly adverse, not disclosed by opposing counsel).
solution: Rule 3.3(a)(2) requires me to disclose legal authority in the controlling jurisdiction that I know to be directly adverse and that opposing counsel has not disclosed. I would cite the decision myself, early in the relevant argument, and state its holding accurately. I would then distinguish it on the facts and rationale that matter, or argue that it should be limited, overruled by a higher court, or reconsidered. Disclosing it myself lets me control the framing and builds credibility, while hiding it risks sanctions and loss of the court's trust.
rubric: States the duty accurately (controlling jurisdiction, directly adverse, not disclosed by opposing counsel).
rubric: Chooses disclosure with an accurate statement of the holding.
rubric: Proposes a legitimate strategy to distinguish or limit the case.
