@article
In a brief the **point headings** are the most-read words you write. Under FRAP 28(a)(2) the brief must include a table of contents, and when the headings are written as full sentences that table becomes a one-page version of your argument. A judge who reads only the headings should know what you want, why you should win, and what the law and facts are.

### What a good point heading does

A strong heading is a **full-sentence conclusion** that combines three things:

1. **The ruling you want** (for example, that summary judgment should be reversed).
2. **The rule or standard** that supports it.
3. **The key facts** that bring the case within the rule.

Compare a label ("Notice") with a heading: "Because Acme's own employee created the wet floor, the district court erred in requiring proof that Acme had notice of it." The second tells the court the law and the facts. It is argumentative but accurate.

Guidelines:

- Use a complete sentence with a subject and verb. Avoid labels, questions and fragments.
- Be concrete: name the parties and the facts, not "the defendant" and "the circumstances."
- Keep it short enough to read at a glance, generally no more than two to three lines. Long headings are skipped.
- Use the same terms the court will use in the rule, so the heading can be quoted back.
- Do not overstate the law. A heading that misstates the standard loses credibility at the first glance.

### Organizing the argument

**Order.** Decide the order of arguments by two rules. First, address **threshold** questions first. A court must satisfy itself that it has jurisdiction before it reaches the merits (*Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment* (1998)), so an argument that the court lacks power, or that a claim is moot or unripe, comes before the merits. Second, among merits arguments, lead with your **strongest**, because readers remember first and last impressions, unless a weaker argument is logically needed to understand a stronger one.

**Hierarchy.** Use main headings (I., II., III.) for independent grounds and subheadings (A., B.) for steps within a ground. Outline logic applies: if you have an A you need a B, and the subheadings together should prove the main heading. Keep subheadings parallel in grammar.

**Theme.** Every heading should advance the theory of the case (Week 2). Read the headings in sequence; if the story stops making sense, rewrite them.

### Inside a section: CREAC

Beneath each heading, use a predictable structure:

- **C**onclusion: restate the heading's point in a sentence.
- **R**ule: state the governing rule, with authority.
- **E**xplanation: show how courts have applied the rule, using cases (Week 5).
- **A**pplication: apply the rule to your facts and answer the opponent.
- **C**onclusion: close with the result and the relief requested.

CREAC is the persuasive form of the IRAC you used in objective writing. It begins with the conclusion because the judge should never have to guess where an argument is going.

### Checking your headings

After drafting, test each heading: Does it say what the court should do? Does it include the governing rule? Does it include a fact that matters? Does it read as a sentence a judge could adopt? Then read all headings together as an outline.

@example
title: Turning a topic label into a point heading
problem: Hypothetical appeal: Ana lost on summary judgment in her slip-and-fall case against Acme Market. The record shows an Acme employee mopped aisle 7 at 2:10 p.m., left no warning sign, and Ana fell at 2:14 p.m. The district court held that Ana had no evidence that Acme knew of the wet floor. Draft a point heading for Ana's first argument.
step: Identify the ruling sought: reverse the grant of summary judgment.
step: Identify the rule that carries the argument: where the defendant's own employee created the dangerous condition, the plaintiff need not separately prove notice (the general rule in most jurisdictions).
step: Choose the facts that bring the case within the rule: Acme's employee mopped the aisle, no warning was posted, and the fall came four minutes later.
step: Combine them into one sentence: "Because Acme's own employee mopped the aisle and left no warning, Acme created the hazard and Ana did not need to prove notice, so the grant of summary judgment should be reversed."
step: Test it: it is a complete sentence, states the ruling, rule and facts, and uses the court's own vocabulary.

@mistakes
- Using topic labels ("Negligence," "The standard of review") or questions instead of full-sentence conclusions.
- Writing headings so long that the court skips them, or so general that they could fit any case.
- Putting a weak argument first, or placing a merits argument ahead of a threshold jurisdictional challenge.
- Writing subheadings that do not prove the main heading or are not parallel.
