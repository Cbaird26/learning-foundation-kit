@article
The **standard of review** tells you how much deference the appellate court gives the ruling below. It often decides the appeal before the merits are argued. FRAP 28(a)(8)(B) requires the appellant's argument to include, for each issue, a concise statement of the applicable standard of review, which may appear in the discussion of the issue or under a separate heading. Pick the right standard and frame your argument to meet it.

### The three main standards

| Standard | Applies to | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **De novo** | Questions of law: legal rulings, statutory and contract interpretation, dismissal under FRCP 12(b)(6), grant of summary judgment, judgment as a matter of law | The appellate court decides the question afresh, giving no deference |
| **Clear error** | Findings of fact after a bench trial (FRCP 52(a)(6)) | Reversal only if the court is left with a firm conviction of a mistake; a plausible view of the whole record stands even if the appellate court would have weighed the evidence differently (*Anderson v. City of Bessemer City* (1985)); due regard is given to the trial court's credibility choices |
| **Abuse of discretion** | Discretionary rulings: case management, many evidentiary rulings, denial of a continuance, scope of discovery, sanctions, denial of a new trial | Reversal only if the ruling rests on an error of law, a clearly erroneous finding or a decision outside the range of reasonable choices; a court abuses discretion when it makes an error of law (*Koon v. United States* (1996)) |

For a ruling that applies law to facts, courts look at whether the question is primarily legal, which is reviewed de novo, or primarily factual, which gets deference (*U.S. Bank N.A. v. Village at Lakeridge, LLC* (2018)). On summary judgment the appellate court views the record in the light most favorable to the non-movant. On a jury verdict it views the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict winner.

### Agencies and statutes after *Loper Bright*

When the issue is what a federal statute means, courts decide the best reading themselves. *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo* (2024) overruled *Chevron*, so an agency's interpretation gets at most *Skidmore* respect, depending on its thoroughness and persuasiveness. Prior holdings that relied on *Chevron* remain precedents, but new arguments should not cite *Chevron* deference as current law. For an appellant challenging an agency reading, de novo review is the friendly standard.

### Use the standard strategically

- **Appellant:** recast the issue as legal if possible ("the court applied the wrong test") because legal questions are reviewed de novo. A discretionary ruling is reversible if it rests on a legal error.
- **Appellee:** emphasize deference ("the finding is plausible in light of the entire record") and that the ruling falls in the range of reasonable choices.
- **Preservation:** issues not raised below are generally forfeited. Unpreserved errors in criminal cases may be reviewed for **plain error** (Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(b); *United States v. Olano* (1993)). Errors that did not affect substantial rights are **harmless** (FRCP 61; Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(a)).

### The record

The **record on appeal** consists of the original papers and exhibits filed in the district court, the transcript of proceedings, and a certified copy of the docket entries (FRAP 10(a)). An appellate court decides on that record and ignores anything outside it. FRAP 28(e) requires references to the record, and FRAP 30 governs the appendix. The practice of citing the record is part of the brief: every factual statement in the argument and facts should carry a pinpoint cite in the form the court's rules require (for example, "R. 45" or "Tr. 112:3-9"). Never cite a fact you cannot find in the record.

### Point headings that reflect the standard

A point heading can build the standard in. If the issue is de novo, say "as a matter of law." If the issue is discretionary, argue that the discretion rested on an error of law or no reasonable basis. If the standard is clear error, argue that the finding lacks support in the record.

@example
title: Matching the standard to each issue in a three-issue brief
problem: Hypothetical appeal: Ana sued Acme Market and lost. The district court (1) granted summary judgment on her negligence claim for lack of evidence of notice, (2) excluded the testimony of her floor-safety expert under FRE 702, and (3) denied her motion to amend the complaint to add a claim. Identify the standard of review for each and draft a heading for the first.
step: Summary judgment is reviewed de novo, with the record viewed in the light most favorable to Ana as the non-movant.
step: A decision to exclude expert testimony is reviewed for abuse of discretion. Ana should argue the court applied the wrong legal standard, since an error of law is an abuse of discretion; FRE 702 as amended December 1, 2023 requires the proponent to show the court that it is more likely than not that each requirement is met.
step: Denial of leave to amend is reviewed for abuse of discretion, though a denial based on legal futility is often reviewed de novo.
step: Heading for issue 1: "Because Acme's employee created the hazard, there was a genuine dispute of material fact as to Acme's liability, and the grant of summary judgment, reviewed de novo, should be reversed."

@mistakes
- Citing the wrong standard of review, or ignoring the standard entirely.
- Treating a mixed question as purely factual when it turns on the legal test applied.
- Citing facts not in the record, or omitting pinpoint record cites.
- Forgetting that unpreserved issues are generally forfeited, so an appellate theory must have been raised below.

@quiz num
prompt: A district court's judgment was entered on October 15. Under FRAP 4(a)(1)(A), a notice of appeal in a civil case in which the United States is not a party must be filed within 30 days after entry of judgment. Assume the last day is not a weekend or legal holiday. On what day of November is the deadline? Enter the day of the month. (October has 31 days.)
answer: 14
check: 15 + 30 - 31
hint: Count 30 days beginning the day after October 15; do not count the day of entry.
hint: There are 16 days left in October after October 15.
solution: Excluding October 15, the 16 remaining days of October bring you to October 31, and 14 more days reach November 14. The deadline is November 14. FRAP 26(a)(1) says to exclude the day of the event, count every day, and include the last day.
