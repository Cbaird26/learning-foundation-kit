@article
Appellate judges ask questions because they want to decide the case correctly and have unresolved concerns. A question is not an attack; it is a signal of what matters to the panel, and it is the best chance you have to address it. Most arguments are won or lost in the answers.

### Kinds of questions

- **Clarifying questions** ask what you are arguing or what a record fact is.
- **Record questions** ask where something is in the record, or whether a fact was disputed or preserved.
- **Testing questions** and **hypotheticals** change the facts to see where your rule stops ("Suppose the floor had been mopped thirty minutes earlier?").
- **Limiting-principle questions** ask what stops your rule from sweeping too broadly.
- **Remedy questions** ask what exactly you want the court to do and what happens next.
- **Concession probes** ask whether you agree that a point cuts against you.
- **Friendly questions** invite you to make a point you want to make; use them.

### The method

1. **Listen and pause.** Let the judge finish. Take a moment to think. A brief pause looks confident.
2. **Answer first.** If the question can be answered "yes" or "no," begin with that word, then explain: "No, Your Honor, because . . . ." Judges notice evasion and will repeat the question.
3. **Explain briefly,** then tie the answer to your theme and the rule. Do not give a speech.
4. **Do not defer.** "I'll get to that" is almost always a mistake. Answer now.
5. **Bridge back** to your outline when the answer is done.

### Hypotheticals and limiting principles

A hypothetical tests whether your rule is principled. Accept it, answer it, and then connect your answer to the rule: "If the employee had mopped thirty minutes earlier and a sign had been posted, the answer would be different, because the store would have warned. That is the point of our rule: it applies when the defendant created the danger and did not warn." For a limiting-principle question, offer a concrete line grounded in the rule and the record, such as "our rule applies only where the defendant's own conduct created the hazard," rather than saying there is no limit.

### Concessions and fallback positions

A good advocate concedes what is plainly true, because it builds credibility and keeps the court's focus on the real dispute. Decide in advance which points you will concede and which fallback position you will offer ("if the court disagrees on notice, it should at least remand for the jury to decide"). When a remedy question comes, state the precise relief and the alternative.

### Candor

You must not make a false statement of fact or law to the tribunal (Model Rule 3.3(a)(1)). If you do not know a fact in the record, say so, give your best understanding if you have one, and ask whether you may supplement by letter, following the court's procedures. If you realize you misspoke, correct it promptly, in the argument or by letter. Do not guess.

### Building the ten-minute outline

Prepare an outline that fits one page: (1) the opening and roadmap; (2) the two or three reasons in order, each with the rule, key facts and cites; (3) anticipated questions with answers in a line or two each; (4) concessions and fallbacks; (5) the conclusion and relief; and (6) rebuttal points reserved. Mark the points that can be cut when questions take time. Practice aloud with a partner who interrupts and who asks the hardest question you fear.

@example
title: Answering a limiting-principle question
problem: Hypothetical: Ana appeals summary judgment against her in a slip-and-fall case. The judge asks Ana's counsel: "If you win, what stops every shopper who slips from suing the store without proof that it knew about the hazard?" Prepare an answer.
step: Start with a direct answer, not a deferral: "The rule has a clear limit, Your Honor."
step: State the limit tied to the rule and facts: "It applies only where the defendant's own employee created the dangerous condition. Where a customer or a third party created it, the plaintiff must still prove notice."
step: Anchor to the record: "Here, Acme's incident log shows its own employee mopped aisle 7 at 2:10 p.m. and left without posting a warning."
step: Bridge back: "So reversing here would not remove the notice requirement in other cases; it would simply apply the long-standing rule for hazards the store itself creates."

@mistakes
- Dodging, deferring ("I'll get to that") or answering a different question from the one asked.
- Refusing to concede an obvious point, or conceding a point that undermines the whole position without a fallback.
- Guessing about a record fact rather than saying that you do not recall and offering to supplement.
- Interrupting the judge or turning an answer into a speech.

@practice self
prompt: Hypothetical: A judge asks Acme's counsel: "Ana testified she was looking at the shelf. Isn't she comparatively at fault as a matter of law?" Write a direct, candid answer in three or four sentences, assuming the jurisdiction follows comparative fault.
hint: Start with "No" or "Yes" and tie it to the standard of review.
hint: Under comparative fault, a plaintiff's fault generally reduces recovery rather than barring it, and it is usually a jury question.
solution: No, Your Honor. Under comparative fault, a plaintiff's own negligence ordinarily reduces her recovery rather than barring it, and the allocation of fault is generally a question for the jury. Looking at a shelf in a grocery aisle is what shoppers do, and on summary judgment the record is viewed in Ana's favor, so a jury could reasonably assign her little or no fault. The proper course is to reverse and let a jury decide.
rubric: Begins with a direct answer.
rubric: States the comparative fault rule accurately and notes that it can vary by jurisdiction.
rubric: Connects the answer to the summary judgment standard and the relief sought.

@quiz self
prompt: Prepare a one-page, ten-minute oral argument outline for the appellee, Acme Market, in Ana's appeal from summary judgment on lack of notice (hypothetical record: an Acme employee mopped aisle 7 at 2:10 p.m. and posted no warning, though a wet-floor sign was kept in the back room; Ana fell at 2:14 p.m.; Ana was looking at the shelf; review is de novo). Include an opening, two or three reasons, three anticipated questions with answer plans, one concession and the requested relief.
hint: Appellee's strongest ground may be narrower than the district court's: consider what the record shows about notice and what Acme must answer.
hint: Be candid that the employee mopped the floor and plan for the "created the hazard" question.
solution: Opening: Counsel for Acme; affirm the grant of summary judgment. Reason 1: no genuine dispute that Ana cannot show a breach, because a short interval after cleaning is reasonable and Acme was cleaning the floor, which is what a store should do. Reason 2: the record shows no evidence that the mopped floor was unreasonably left unmarked beyond a few minutes. Reason 3 (fallback): if the court disagrees, the proper course is a remand for a jury determination, not a ruling for Ana as a matter of law. Anticipated questions: (1) "Didn't your employee create the hazard?" Answer: yes, and that is why the question is reasonableness of the response in four minutes, not notice. (2) "Why was no sign posted?" Answer: the record shows a sign was stored in the back room; the dispute is whether a four-minute interval was unreasonable. (3) "What is your limiting principle?" Answer: stores are liable for unreasonable conduct, not for every fall. Concession: Acme's employee mopped the aisle and no sign was posted. Relief: affirm; alternatively, remand.
