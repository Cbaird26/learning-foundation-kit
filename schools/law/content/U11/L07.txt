@article
Most disputes are decided on motions, not at trial. A **motion** is a request for a court order. A good motion is brief, precise about what it wants and why, and supported by a memorandum that persuades. The same skills used in an appellate brief apply: theory, headings, record cites and candor.

### What the rule requires

FRCP 7(b)(1) says that a request for a court order must be made by written motion (unless made during a hearing or trial), must state with particularity the grounds for seeking the order, and must state the relief sought. Rule 7(b)(2) applies the form rules of Rule 10 to motions. The Federal Rules do not themselves require a separate memorandum, but **local rules** in most districts require a supporting memorandum or brief, set page limits, require a proposed order, and may require the movant to confer with the opposing party first. Always read the local rules and the judge's standing order.

A typical motion package has: (1) the motion (short: the relief and the grounds), (2) a **memorandum** (introduction, statement of facts with record cites, legal standard, argument with point headings, conclusion), (3) supporting **declarations or exhibits** (an affidavit or an unsworn declaration under 28 U.S.C. § 1746), and (4) a proposed order.

### The standards

- **Motion to dismiss for failure to state a claim, FRCP 12(b)(6).** The court takes well-pleaded facts as true, but a complaint needs enough facts to state a claim that is plausible on its face (*Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly* (2007); *Ashcroft v. Iqbal* (2009)). Labels and conclusions do not suffice. The court generally looks at the pleading and documents it incorporates.
- **Summary judgment, FRCP 56(a).** The movant must show that there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and that it is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. A movant without the burden at trial may point to the absence of evidence (*Celotex Corp. v. Catrett* (1986)). The non-movant must then cite specific record materials showing a genuine dispute (Rule 56(c)(1)); a dispute is genuine if a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the non-movant (*Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.* (1986)). Rule 56(d) allows a non-movant to ask for time for discovery by showing, by affidavit or declaration, why it cannot present facts.
- **Preliminary injunction.** The movant must show likely success on the merits, likely irreparable harm without relief, that the balance of equities tips in its favor, and that an injunction is in the public interest (*Winter v. NRDC* (2008)).

### Recent change: scope of injunctions

*Trump v. CASA, Inc.* (2025) held that universal injunctions likely exceed the equitable authority Congress gave federal courts. Relief should be limited to what is needed to give the plaintiffs complete relief. A motion for preliminary relief must therefore ask for relief tailored to the parties before the court, and a response may argue that a broader request is overbroad.

### Timing

Computing time (FRCP 6(a)): exclude the day of the event, count every day including weekends and holidays, and include the last day; if the last day is a Saturday, Sunday or legal holiday, the period runs until the next day that is not. Key periods: a defendant must answer within 21 days after being served with the summons and complaint (Rule 12(a)(1)(A)(i)), and a Rule 12 motion made in that time postpones the answer (Rule 12(a)(4)). A written motion and notice of hearing must be served at least 14 days before the hearing, and opposing affidavits at least 7 days before it, unless the rules or court say otherwise (Rule 6(c)). A Rule 59(e) motion to alter or amend a judgment must be filed no later than 28 days after entry of judgment. Under Rule 11(c)(2), a party served with a sanctions motion has 21 days to withdraw the challenged paper (the safe harbor).

### Recent change: scheduling and privilege

Effective December 1, 2025, amended FRCP 16(b) and 26(f) require the parties to address, in their discovery plan and the scheduling order, how they will describe withheld privileged material (for example, the timing and method of a privilege log). Raise it early to avoid a later motion to compel.

@example
title: Structuring a summary judgment memorandum
problem: Hypothetical facts: Contractor sues Owner for the \$12,000 final installment on a kitchen remodel finished six days after the contract date. The contract did not make time of the essence. Owner admits the work is complete and that Owner lost no money from the delay. Plan Contractor's memorandum supporting partial summary judgment.
step: Open with a short introduction stating the relief and the theory: Owner has admitted every fact that matters, so the only question is legal and the six-day delay does not excuse payment.
step: Write the statement of undisputed facts, each fact cited to the record under Rule 56(c)(1), such as Owner's admission of completion and of no loss.
step: State the Rule 56(a) standard in one short paragraph, and state the governing contract rule.
step: Use argumentative headings: "I. Because time was not of the essence and Owner lost nothing, the six-day delay did not excuse payment." and "II. Owner's admissions leave no genuine dispute of material fact."
step: Conclude with the exact relief: judgment for the \$12,000 installment, and attach a proposed order.

@mistakes
- Skipping the local rules, with the result that the motion is struck for form, length or failure to confer.
- Stating grounds only in general terms, in violation of FRCP 7(b)(1)'s particularity requirement, or failing to say what relief is sought.
- Responding to summary judgment with pleadings or argument instead of specific record evidence.
- Miscounting deadlines, including by excluding weekends or forgetting that the day of the event is not counted.
