@article
A clause is **ambiguous** if it is reasonably susceptible of more than one meaning. Ambiguity creates disputes, and a court will resolve them using rules that may not favor your client. Drafting for clarity is the cheapest form of risk management.

### Kinds of ambiguity

- **Lexical:** a word with two meanings. In *Frigaliment Importing Co. v. B.N.S. International Sales Corp.* (S.D.N.Y. 1960), a contract for "chicken" led to a dispute over whether it meant young chickens only; the buyer failed to prove the narrow trade meaning.
- **Latent:** an ambiguity that appears only when the contract is applied. In *Raffles v. Wichelhaus* (1864), the parties each meant a different ship named Peerless, and there was no contract because there was no meeting of the minds on the term.
- **Syntactic:** structure leaves doubt about what a modifier or list refers to. "Consultant shall indemnify Client for losses caused by Consultant and its subcontractors' negligence" is unclear about whether "negligence" modifies both. A missing serial comma created a famous dispute over a statute's overtime exemption in *O'Connor v. Oakhurst Dairy* (1st Cir. 2017), which the court resolved in favor of the drivers.
- **Time and number:** "within 30 days of the invoice" (invoice date, or receipt?), "monthly" (in advance, or in arrears?), "and/or."

Fix ambiguity by naming the thing precisely, restructuring the sentence, using a list or a table, and defining terms.

### How courts interpret

Under the traditional plain-meaning approach, courts read the text as a whole and consider extrinsic evidence only if the language is ambiguous; under the Restatement (Second) and Corbin approach, followed in many states, courts may consider context and extrinsic evidence in deciding whether it is ambiguous at all. Such evidence includes course of performance, course of dealing and usage of trade (UCC § 1-303 for sales of goods). Common preferences include giving effect to all terms, preferring specific terms over general ones (Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 203), and interpreting against the drafter, *contra proferentem* (§ 206), when other aids fail. You may be the drafter, so ambiguity can cost your client.

### Parol evidence and the merger clause

The **parol evidence rule** says that when parties have adopted a writing as the final expression of their agreement, evidence of prior or contemporaneous agreements cannot contradict it (Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 213 to 215; for sales of goods, UCC § 2-202). If the writing is **completely integrated**, the rule also bars evidence of consistent additional terms. A **merger (integration) clause** states that the writing is the entire agreement and is strong evidence of complete integration. The rule does not bar evidence offered to interpret ambiguous terms, or to show fraud, mistake or illegality.

### Core provisions of a services agreement

A services agreement commonly includes: (1) **scope of services and deliverables**, with specifications and a schedule; (2) **acceptance** procedure (time to review, how to reject); (3) **fees and payment** (amount, milestones, invoices, due dates, late charges); (4) **term and termination** (term, termination for cause with cure period, termination for convenience on notice, effect of termination); (5) **independent contractor** status; (6) **intellectual property:** under the Copyright Act, work by an independent contractor is a work made for hire only if it falls into one of nine statutory categories and the parties so agree in a signed writing (17 U.S.C. § 101; *Community for Creative Non-Violence v. Reid* (1989)), so the agreement should include an express assignment of rights to the client; (7) **confidentiality**; (8) **warranties** and a limit on **liability**; and (9) **indemnification.**

### Boilerplate

- **Entire agreement (merger):** the writing is the whole deal.
- **Amendment and waiver:** changes must be in a signed writing; failing to enforce a right once does not waive it.
- **Severability:** if a provision is invalid, the rest survives.
- **Governing law:** courts generally honor the parties' choice if the chosen state has a substantial relationship or other reasonable basis and the choice does not offend a fundamental policy of the state whose law would otherwise apply (Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws § 187).
- **Forum selection:** a valid forum-selection clause is enforced; for a clause naming a federal forum, it is enforced through a motion to transfer under 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a), and the private interests are treated as favoring the chosen forum (*Atlantic Marine Construction Co. v. U.S. District Court* (2013)).
- **Notices, assignment, counterparts, force majeure, no third-party beneficiaries.**
- **Liquidated damages** are enforceable only if reasonable in light of the anticipated or actual loss; a penalty is unenforceable (Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 356).
- **Attorney's fees:** under the American rule each side pays its own unless a contract or statute shifts fees.

Boilerplate is not decoration: each clause allocates risk and should be reviewed against the deal.

@example
title: Fixing an ambiguous payment clause
problem: Hypothetical clause: "Client shall pay Consultant \$30,000 in installments of 30%, 40% and 30% within 30 days of milestones." Identify the ambiguities and redraft.
step: Identify the ambiguities: which milestones; whether "within 30 days of" counts from the milestone's occurrence, acceptance or invoice; and whether the percentages apply to the total fee.
step: Name the milestones in a table: Milestone 1, signed design approval; Milestone 2, delivery of the beta site; Milestone 3, final acceptance.
step: Fix the trigger and the counting: "within 30 days after Client receives Consultant's invoice for that Milestone, which Consultant may submit after the Milestone is accepted in writing."
step: State the amounts: "Milestone 1: \$9,000 (30% of the Fee); Milestone 2: \$12,000 (40%); Milestone 3: \$9,000 (30%)."
step: Check: \$9,000 + \$12,000 + \$9,000 = \$30,000, and every term is defined and used consistently.

@mistakes
- Leaving time periods or triggers unclear ("within 30 days of").
- Assuming a merger clause bars all extrinsic evidence, including evidence of fraud or evidence to interpret an ambiguity.
- Relying on work-made-for-hire language for an independent contractor instead of including an express assignment.
- Copying boilerplate without checking that it fits the deal and the governing law.

prompt: A services agreement provides liquidated damages of \$500 for each day of delay in delivery, capped at 10 percent of the \$40,000 fee. Consultant delivers 12 days late. What amount of liquidated damages is payable, in dollars?
answer: 4000
check: min(500 * 12, 0.10 * 40000)
unit: dollars
hint: Compute the uncapped amount and the cap and take the smaller.
solution: Twelve days at \$500 is \$6,000, but the cap is 10 percent of \$40,000, or \$4,000. The payable amount is \$4,000, assuming the clause is enforceable as a reasonable estimate of loss rather than a penalty.
