@article
The statement of the case is often the most persuasive part of a brief, because judges believe facts more readily than argument. Done well, it makes the court want to rule for you before the argument begins. Done badly, it reads as spin and costs you credibility.

### What the rule requires

FRAP 28(a)(6) requires the statement of the case to set out the facts relevant to the issues submitted for review, describe the relevant procedural history, and identify the rulings presented for review, with appropriate references to the record. Rule 28(e) governs how the record is cited. In practice, the statement includes: (1) the nature of the case, (2) the course of proceedings and the ruling below, and (3) the facts relevant to the issues. Many courts' local rules and practice add other requirements. In the district court a similar section appears in the memorandum supporting a motion.

### Rules of the road

1. **Cite the record for every factual assertion.** A statement without citations is not credible, and the court will not hunt for support. Cite the specific page or paragraph ("R. 12," "Tr. 45:3-9").
2. **Be accurate and complete on material facts.** You may emphasize and arrange, but you may not misstate, and you may not leave out a fact the court needs to decide fairly. Include the bad facts, briefly and in context. If the other side reveals a fact you omitted, you lose the court.
3. **Do not argue.** The statement should tell a story that supports your theory without legal conclusions or adjectives. Let the facts lead the court to the conclusion.
4. **Include only relevant facts.** A fact that does not bear on the issues, or the theory, is clutter.

### Techniques of persuasive storytelling

- **Start with your theme.** Open with a sentence or two that frames the story in the client's favor, grounded in the record.
- **Tell it in a clear order.** Chronological order is the default because it is easy to follow. Use topical order or headings when there are distinct stories (for example, one section for liability and another for injuries). A short, accurate introduction often helps.
- **Humanize your client.** Use names rather than party labels. Introduce who the person is, what she was doing, and what happened to her.
- **Emphasize through placement and syntax.** Put favorable facts in short, strong sentences with a concrete subject, early in paragraphs. Put unfavorable facts in subordinate clauses and mid-paragraph, with context.
- **Use active voice for the other side's conduct and precise detail.** "An Acme employee mopped aisle 7 at 2:10 p.m." is stronger than "the floor was mopped."
- **Use the record's own words.** Short, accurate quotations from key documents and testimony are persuasive because they are the record speaking.
- **Describe procedural history neutrally,** and identify the ruling under review precisely.

### Record citations and caution

Cite to the record as the court requires (the district court docket entry, page or paragraph, or the appendix page). Check each cite against the record before filing. A misquotation, or a fact not found where cited, damages credibility out of all proportion to its importance. On appeal, a fact not in the record cannot be relied on at all.

@example
title: Building a persuasive statement from record excerpts
problem: Hypothetical record: R. 12 (Acme incident log): an employee mopped aisle 7 at 2:10 p.m. R. 15 (video): Ana slips at 2:14 p.m. R. 18 (Acme policy): employees must post a wet-floor sign "whenever a floor is wet." R. 20 (manager testimony): no sign was posted in aisle 7, and a sign was stored in the back room. R. 31 (Ana's testimony): she was looking at items on the shelf. R. 40 (medical record): a fractured wrist requiring surgery. Draft the opening paragraph of Ana's statement of facts.
step: Choose the theme from the record: Acme mopped the aisle and left it unmarked, in violation of its own policy.
step: Lead with the strongest facts in concrete active sentences, each cited: "At 2:10 p.m., an Acme employee mopped aisle 7. (R. 12.) Acme's policy required a wet-floor sign 'whenever a floor is wet.' (R. 18.) The employee posted none; a sign sat in the back room. (R. 20.)"
step: Add the fall and the injury: "Four minutes later, Ana slipped. (R. 15.) She fractured her wrist and needed surgery. (R. 40.)"
step: Handle the adverse fact briefly and in context: "Ana was looking at items on the shelf, as customers do. (R. 31.)" The statement does not argue; it places the fact where it does the least harm and with an accurate description.

@mistakes
- Omitting record citations, or citing facts that are not in the record.
- Arguing in the facts ("Acme was clearly negligent") instead of letting the facts persuade.
- Hiding a material bad fact, so that the opponent discloses it and the court loses trust.
- Telling the story in a confusing order or cluttering it with irrelevant details.
