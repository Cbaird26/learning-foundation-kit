@article
Persuasive writing seeks a ruling from a neutral reader. **Contract drafting** has different goals: the reader is a future judge, arbitrator or business person who may disagree with you, and the document must allocate risk and say clearly who must do what. A good drafter writes so that the document works without the drafter present, and so that a dispute, if it comes, can be decided from the text.

### The parts of a contract

1. **Title** that identifies the type of agreement (Services Agreement, Asset Purchase Agreement).
2. **Preamble:** date (or effective date) and the parties, with their legal names and entity types.
3. **Recitals** ("Background"): the context and purposes. Recitals are not operative promises, but they can help interpret ambiguous terms. Do not hide obligations in them.
4. **Definitions:** the meanings of capitalized terms.
5. **Operative provisions:** the exchange: what each party must do, when, and for what price (scope, deliverables, payment, term).
6. **Representations and warranties:** statements of present fact on which a party relies (authority, ownership, compliance with law). A false representation can support remedies such as damages or rescission.
7. **Covenants** (promises about future conduct), **conditions**, and **remedies**.
8. **Term and termination.**
9. **General provisions** ("boilerplate," Week 12).
10. **Signature blocks** and **exhibits or schedules.**

Order the document so a reader can find things: put the main business terms first, and define terms in a definitions section or where first used, but not both.

### Obligation words

Use each word for one function:

- **shall** imposes a duty ("Contractor shall deliver the Report by June 1"). **shall not** prohibits.
- **may** grants discretion or a right ("Client may terminate on 30 days' notice").
- **must** can express a condition or requirement on a person ("To obtain a refund, Client must submit a request in writing").
- **will** states a future event or a promise; avoid it where you want a clear duty.
- **is / are** states a fact or a representation ("Contractor is a corporation duly organized under the laws of Delaware").

Avoid "should," "intends to," "endeavors to," unless you mean a soft undertaking. If a standard such as "reasonable efforts" is meant, say so and consider defining it.

### Conditions versus promises

A **condition** is an event, not certain to occur, that must occur, unless excused, before performance under a contract becomes due (Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 224). Courts prefer to read a term as a promise rather than a condition when the language is doubtful (§ 227), because a failed condition can forfeit a party's rights. If you want a condition, say so expressly: "Client's obligation to pay any invoice is conditioned on Client's written acceptance of the Deliverable."

### Definitions

- **Define once and use consistently.** Choose one term for one concept and use it everywhere. A new word suggests a new meaning.
- **Capitalize defined terms** so a reader can see them, and do not capitalize them when not using the defined meaning.
- **Avoid circular definitions** ("Affiliate means an affiliate of Client") and **hidden obligations** (put duties in operative sections, not in definitions).
- **Use "includes" carefully.** "Includes" may be read as non-exhaustive, but to avoid doubt say "including without limitation," or say "means" for an exhaustive list.
- **Define numbers and times:** say what a "Business Day" is, from when time runs, and in which time zone.
- Avoid "and/or"; say "A or B or both."
- When numbers appear in words and in figures, state which controls; courts commonly treat the words as controlling if they conflict, but do not rely on the rule.

### Know the client's goals

Before drafting, find out what the deal is, who bears which risk, what could go wrong, and what the client wants if it does. Draft for the client but do not create unreadable or unfair terms that could harm the relationship or be struck down.

@example
title: Drafting an operative clause and a definition
problem: Hypothetical: Consultant will deliver a market report to Client within 20 Business Days after the Effective Date. Client will pay \$8,000 within 30 days after receiving the invoice. Draft the delivery clause, the payment clause and a definition of Business Day.
step: Identify the parties and use defined terms: "Consultant" and "Client," "Report," "Effective Date."
step: Use shall for the duties: "Consultant shall deliver the Report to Client within 20 Business Days after the Effective Date."
step: Fix the trigger and the amount: "Client shall pay Consultant \$8,000 within 30 days after Client receives Consultant's invoice for the Report."
step: Define the time term: "'Business Day' means a day other than a Saturday, a Sunday or a day on which commercial banks in New York, New York are authorized or required by law to close."
step: Check the consistency: each defined term is used the same way, and no duty is hidden in the definition.

@mistakes
- Using different words for the same thing, or the same word for different things.
- Using "will," "should" or "may" when a duty is meant, or creating a condition by accident.
- Burying obligations in the recitals or in definitions, or writing definitions that are circular.
- Leaving time periods without a starting point or counting rule.

prompt: Hypothetical: Client wants a services agreement under which Consultant has a firm duty to deliver a report by June 1. Which clause best accomplishes this?
choice*: "Consultant shall deliver the Report to Client by June 1," because "shall" imposes a duty.
choice: "Consultant may deliver the Report to Client by June 1," because "may" imposes a duty.
choice: "Consultant should deliver the Report to Client by June 1," because "should" is the standard word for contractual duties.
choice: "Consultant intends to deliver the Report to Client by June 1," because a statement of intention binds a party as a promise.
hint: Match the word to its function: duty, discretion, aspiration or statement of fact.
solution: "Shall" creates a duty. "May" grants discretion, "should" is hortatory, and a statement of intention is not a promise.

@quiz mc
prompt: Hypothetical: Client wants to make clear that it need not pay Consultant's invoice unless Client has accepted the Deliverable in writing. Which clause most clearly makes acceptance a condition of payment?
choice*: "Client's obligation to pay any invoice for a Deliverable is expressly conditioned on Client's written acceptance of that Deliverable."
choice: "Client shall pay each invoice after Consultant delivers the Deliverable."
choice: "Client expects to accept the Deliverable before paying."
choice: "Payment is due when the parties are satisfied."
hint: Courts prefer promises over conditions unless the language is clear.
solution: Express conditional language is the clearest way to create a condition (Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 224, 227). The other clauses either create a promise to pay or state expectations and are vague.

prompt: Which of the following definitions have a drafting problem? Select all that apply.
choice*: "Affiliate means an affiliate of Client." (circular)
