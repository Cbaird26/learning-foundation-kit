@article
Legal argument is mostly the use of precedent. A persuasive brief picks the right authorities, explains them accurately, and shows why they lead to your result. This lesson covers weight, explanation, analogy, distinction and the checks that keep you from citing bad law.

### Weight of authority

**Mandatory (binding) authority** is authority a court must follow: statutes and constitutional provisions that apply, U.S. Supreme Court decisions on federal questions, and decisions of higher courts in the same jurisdiction. A federal district court is bound by its own court of appeals on federal law; a three-judge panel is generally bound by earlier panel decisions until the court sits en banc or the Supreme Court intervenes. A state's highest court binds federal courts on that state's law. **Persuasive authority** includes decisions of other circuits, lower courts, other states, Restatements and treatises. Persuasive authority can matter a great deal when binding authority is silent. Unpublished opinions may be cited: under FRAP 32.1, a court may not prohibit citation of federal judicial opinions designated unpublished or non-precedential if issued on or after January 1, 2007, though their weight is for the court.

Only the **holding**, the rule necessary to the result on the material facts, binds. **Dictum** is language not necessary to the result. It may persuade but does not bind, and distinguishing a case as dictum is a legitimate move only if you are accurate.

### Explaining a case

When you rely on a case, give the reader what it needs to see the analogy: the key facts, the question, the holding and the reasoning. Then **synthesize** several cases into a rule if they point the same way. Use a **parenthetical** to give a short, accurate description. Signals show how authority supports: no signal when it directly states the proposition; *see* when it supports it; *see also* for additional support; *cf.* for support by analogy; *but see* when it clearly supports a contrary proposition; *contra* when it directly states the contrary.

### Analogizing and distinguishing

- **Analogize** to favorable cases by pointing to facts that matter to the court's reasoning, and then to the policy that makes the result fair here.
- **Distinguish** adverse cases honestly: (1) a material fact differs and the court's reasoning depended on it; (2) the rationale or policy does not apply; (3) the language is dictum; or (4) the case was decided under a different rule. Do not distinguish on a fact the court did not find important, and do not narrow the holding beyond what the opinion supports.
- If you cannot distinguish a controlling case, say that it was wrongly decided, preserve the point for further review, and argue the best available alternative.

### The duty of candor

Under Model Rule 3.3(a)(2) you must disclose directly adverse controlling authority the other side has not cited. After filing, if significant new authority emerges before decision, FRAP 28(j) lets a party send a letter to the clerk; the body of the letter must not exceed 350 words, and it states the authority and the page or section to which it relates without argument.

### Check subsequent history and recent changes

Before you cite, confirm that authority is still good law. Recent changes you must know:

- *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo* (2024) overruled *Chevron*. Courts decide the best reading of a statute and give an agency's view *Skidmore* respect. Prior holdings that relied on *Chevron* stay in force under statutory stare decisis, so they remain binding as holdings, but the deference they applied is not available for new questions.
- *Trump v. Slaughter* (2026) overruled *Humphrey's Executor*: the FTC Act's for-cause removal protection is unconstitutional.
- *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization* (2022) overruled *Roe* and *Casey*.
- *Kennedy v. Bremerton School District* (2022) abandoned the *Lemon* test.

Citing overruled law as good law is an error of law and a candor problem.

@example
title: Distinguishing an adverse case
problem: Hypothetical authority: *Pruitt v. Lakeshore Racquet Club* (Hypothetical Circuit 2014) enforced a signed waiver against a member who was hurt when she overexerted herself on a gym machine; the court wrote that "exculpatory agreements are enforceable." Hypothetical facts: Mara was hurt when a treadmill belt snapped after the club ignored written repair warnings. Draft the argument distinguishing *Pruitt* for Mara.
step: State the holding accurately: the waiver barred a claim for injury caused by the member's own use of the equipment.
step: Identify material differences: in *Pruitt* the injury came from the member's own exertion; here a defect the club knew about caused it.
step: Tie the difference to the rationale: waivers allocate the risks of ordinary participation, not the risk of a known, unrepaired defect.
step: Treat the broad sentence as dictum or as limited to the facts: a statement about enforceability generally does not decide whether the clause covers the club's own known maintenance failure.
step: Draft: "In *Pruitt*, the member was hurt by her own overexertion. Mara was hurt when a treadmill belt the club knew was failing snapped. *Pruitt*'s general statement about exculpatory agreements did not decide whether such a clause covers a known, unrepaired defect."

@mistakes
- Treating dictum as holding, or distinguishing a case on facts the court did not consider important.
- Citing a case for a proposition it does not support, or quoting a favorable sentence while omitting a limiting one.
- Failing to check subsequent history, and so citing overruled authority, such as Chevron, Humphrey's Executor or Roe, as current law.
- Failing to disclose directly adverse controlling authority.

The 2015 decision has not been overruled by the circuit sitting en banc. Is the district court still bound by the 2015 holding?

choice: No, because every decision that applied Chevron was automatically vacated when Loper Bright was decided.
choice: No, because Loper Bright requires every district court to reconsider each earlier Chevron decision on the merits.

@practice self
prompt: Hypothetical authority: In *Hale v. Pine Ridge Gym* (Hypothetical Circuit 2018), the court enforced a waiver and held that a plaintiff who was injured when she dropped a weight on her foot could not recover on her negligence claim, the only claim she pleaded. The court noted in passing that "waivers also bar claims of gross negligence." Hypothetical facts: Mara's treadmill belt snapped after the club ignored three written repair warnings. Write a short paragraph (three to five sentences) that explains *Hale* accurately and distinguishes it.
hint: Start with the key facts and the actual holding before you distinguish.
hint: The remark about gross negligence was said "in passing"; ask whether it was necessary to the result.
solution: In *Hale*, a gym member dropped a weight on her foot and the court enforced the waiver against her negligence claim. The holding covers an injury caused by the member's own handling of equipment. The court's remark that waivers "also bar claims of gross negligence" was not necessary to that result, because no gross-negligence claim was before it, and it is dictum. Mara's injury came from a belt the club had been warned, in writing, was failing. Whether a waiver reaches that kind of conduct is a question *Hale* did not decide.
rubric: States the holding and key facts of the adverse case accurately.
rubric: Identifies the gross-negligence remark as dictum for a stated reason.
rubric: Contrasts the material facts without overstating the holding.

@practice self
prompt: Explain in four or five sentences how a lawyer should use signals and a parenthetical to cite three cases: one that directly states the proposition, one that supports it by an analogous rule, and one from another circuit that reaches the opposite result.
hint: Match each case to a signal: no signal, cf., and but see or contra.
hint: The parenthetical should describe the case's holding briefly and accurately.
solution: The case that directly states the proposition is cited with no signal, followed by a parenthetical giving its holding. The case that supports the proposition only by analogy takes the signal *cf.* with an explanatory parenthetical. The other circuit's decision takes *but see* if it clearly supports a contrary proposition, or *contra* if it directly states the contrary, and the lawyer should say why it is not persuasive. Each parenthetical should be accurate, brief and fair, so the court can trust the citation.
rubric: Matches each case to a correct signal.
rubric: Uses parentheticals briefly and accurately.
rubric: Acknowledges the contrary authority rather than hiding it.

@quiz mc
prompt: In July 2026, a lawyer files a brief arguing that FTC commissioners cannot be removed by the President except for cause, and cites Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935) as controlling and unquestioned. What is the central problem?
choice*: Humphrey's Executor was overruled by Trump v. Slaughter (2026), which held the FTC Act's for-cause removal protection unconstitutional, so citing it as controlling misstates current law.
choice: There is no problem, because Humphrey's Executor remains controlling for independent agencies.
choice: The brief must cite Chevron instead, because removal questions turn on agency deference.
choice: The problem is only that the case is old; age alone makes a precedent unusable.
hint: Check the subsequent history of the case.
solution: Trump v. Slaughter (June 2026, 6-3) overruled Humphrey's Executor and held the FTC Act's for-cause removal protection unconstitutional, so FTC commissioners are removable at will. Citing the overruled case as controlling is an error of law and a candor problem. Old cases are not unusable as such, and Chevron concerns agency deference, not removal.
