material: **The case (hypothetical).** Mara Ellison sued Cobalt Fitness Center in a federal district court in the Hypothetical Circuit, in diversity (28 U.S.C. § 1332), for negligence and gross negligence after a treadmill belt broke. The district court (1) excluded the testimony of her expert, Dr. Lena Ortiz, under FRE 702, and (2) granted summary judgment to Cobalt on the ground that a signed release barred the negligence claim and, with Dr. Ortiz excluded, there was no evidence of gross negligence. Ellison appeals from the final judgment under 28 U.S.C. § 1291. Assume the Hypothetical Circuit applies the law of the State of Hypothetical, which is described only by the invented cases below and the real rules and cases named.
material: **Record excerpts (hypothetical).** R. 8: Membership Agreement, section 9, signed by Ellison on March 2: "Member assumes all risks of injury arising from use of Cobalt's facilities and equipment and releases Cobalt from any and all claims, including claims arising from Cobalt's negligence." The section does not mention gross negligence or willful misconduct. R. 12: On September 9, while walking at 3.0 miles per hour on Treadmill 4, Ellison fell when the belt snapped; she fractured her wrist and tore a ligament. R. 15: Cobalt's service log shows the last documented inspection of Treadmill 4 on the preceding February 2, with no entries afterward. R. 17: An email from a member to the front desk dated August 12: "Treadmill 4's belt is slipping and making a grinding noise. Please look at it." R. 18 (deposition of Cobalt's manager): An "Out of Order" tag was placed on Treadmill 4 on August 14; the tag was removed on August 16 without any repair; he does not know who removed it; he did not answer the August 12 email. R. 22 (report of Dr. Ortiz, a mechanical engineer with 15 years of experience in commercial fitness equipment maintenance): she reviewed Cobalt's service log, the August 12 email, the manager's deposition, and 14 photographs of the broken belt and drive; she applied the manufacturer's inspection intervals and a standard failure-analysis method; she concludes that the belt failed from wear and fraying that routine inspection would have detected, and that a grinding noise and slipping are known warning signs. She did not physically test the belt, which Cobalt kept and which was available to her. R. 30 (order excluding Dr. Ortiz): the court excluded her testimony because she "did not test the belt herself" and "has not published peer-reviewed work on treadmill failure," and concluded that the testimony was therefore not reliable. R. 33 (order granting summary judgment, entered January 12): the release is enforceable against negligence claims; without Dr. Ortiz, no evidence supports gross negligence. R. 35: Notice of appeal filed February 3.
material: **Hypothetical authority: Pruitt v. Lakeshore Racquet Club (Hypothetical Circuit 2014).** A member hurt when she overexerted herself on a weight machine sued for negligence. The court enforced a release that clearly covered "negligence," holding that a clear and unambiguous release of ordinary negligence is enforceable. The court added that "exculpatory agreements are generally enforceable." No gross-negligence claim was before the court.
material: **Hypothetical authority: Dunmore v. Pine Ridge Gym (Hypothetical Circuit 2018).** A member hurt by a defective cable machine that the gym had been told, in writing, was frayed sued for gross negligence. The court held that, as a matter of public policy, a release cannot bar a claim for gross negligence, which it defined as conduct showing reckless disregard of a known risk of serious harm. It held that whether the gym's conduct reached that level was a question for the jury where the evidence showed prior written warnings and no repair.
material: **Hypothetical authority: Kessler v. Brightfield Athletic Club (Hypothetical Circuit 2020).** The court held that exculpatory clauses are strictly construed against the party that drafted them and enforced a release against an ordinary-negligence claim where the clause expressly listed "equipment malfunction." The court held that a plaintiff who contends the defendant was grossly negligent must come forward with specific evidence of the defendant's knowledge of the risk.
material: **Hypothetical authority: Alder v. Westmark Industries (Hypothetical Circuit 2021).** In reviewing the exclusion of an engineering expert, the court held that a district court abuses its discretion when it excludes an experience-based expert solely because the expert did not publish or did not test the item personally, if the expert explained a reliable methodology and relied on adequate data such as maintenance records and photographs. The court stated that the reliability inquiry looks to methodology and its application, not to whether the court agrees with the conclusion.
material: **Hypothetical authority: Brandt v. Corvane Tools (Hypothetical Circuit 2016).** The court affirmed the exclusion of an expert who offered a conclusion based only on "my experience" without explaining how the experience led to the opinion and without examining any data about the product in question.
material: **Real law you may cite.** FRCP 56(a) (summary judgment: no genuine dispute as to any material fact and entitlement to judgment as a matter of law) and Celotex Corp. v. Catrett (1986) and Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc. (1986). FRE 702, as amended December 1, 2023: the proponent must show the court that it is more likely than not that the expert's qualifications, the helpfulness of the testimony, the sufficiency of facts or data, the reliability of the methods, and the reliable application of the methods to the case are met. ### Week by week

**Weeks 1-2: persuasion and theory.** An objective memo predicts; a persuasive brief advocates through order, emphasis, framing and word choice, never through misstatement (Model Rule 3.3; FRCP 11(b)). Build a theory of the case (law, record and fairness in one or two sentences) and a short theme.

**Weeks 3-4: headings and review.** Point headings are full-sentence conclusions that state the ruling sought, the rule and key facts. Put threshold questions first, then your strongest argument. Use CREAC. Know the standards: de novo for law (including summary judgment and 12(b)(6) dismissals), clear error for bench-trial findings (FRCP 52(a)(6)), abuse of discretion for discretionary rulings (an error of law is an abuse). After *Loper Bright* courts decide statutory meaning themselves and give agencies *Skidmore* respect. The record is defined by FRAP 10(a) and cited under 28(e).

**Weeks 5-6: authority and facts.** Distinguish holding from dictum, mandatory from persuasive authority. Explain a case, then analogize or distinguish on material facts and rationale. Check subsequent history: *Chevron* (overruled by *Loper Bright*), *Humphrey's Executor* (overruled by *Trump v. Slaughter*), *Roe* and *Casey* (overruled by *Dobbs*), *Lemon* (abandoned in *Kennedy*). FRAP 28(j) letters are limited to 350 words. The statement of the case (FRAP 28(a)(6)) is cited to the record, accurate, complete on material facts and non-argumentative.

**Weeks 7-8: motions and briefs.** FRCP 7(b)(1) (written motion, grounds with particularity, relief sought); 12(b)(6) plausibility; 56(a); *Winter* factors; *Trump v. CASA* limits universal injunctions; timing under FRCP 6 and 12 and FRAP 4(a)(1)(A) (30 days), 31(a)(1) (40, 30, 14 days; reply 7 days before argument) and 32(a)(7) (13,000 words; reply half). Appellee need not repeat the jurisdictional statement, issues, case or standard of review (28(b)).

**Weeks 9-10: oral argument.** FRAP 34: argument may be dispensed with only by unanimous panel on stated grounds. Open with a roadmap, argue the strongest points, reserve rebuttal, use a one-page outline. Answer first, explain briefly, concede what is true, and be candid about the record.

**Weeks 11-12: drafting.** Parts of a contract; shall, may, must; conditions versus promises (Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 224, 227); consistent definitions. Ambiguity (*Frigaliment*, *Raffles*), *contra proferentem* (§ 206), the parol evidence rule and merger clauses, work made for hire versus assignment, governing law and forum-selection (*Atlantic Marine*), liquidated damages versus penalties (§ 356).

### Common errors

Overstating the holding of a case; citing overruled law as current; labeling instead of arguing in headings; missing the standard of review; counting deadlines wrongly; using "will" or "may" where a duty is meant; relying on boilerplate without checking it against the deal.

prompt: Hypothetical: In a gym-injury appeal, the appellant argues that the district court excluded her engineering expert, Dr. Ortiz, only because the expert did not publish peer-reviewed work and did not test the broken belt personally, although the expert used maintenance logs, photographs and a standard failure-analysis method. Which point heading best serves the appellant and reflects the correct standard of review?
choice*: Because the district court excluded Dr. Ortiz on grounds that do not reflect Rule 702's reliability inquiry (no publication and no personal testing), it committed a legal error and thus abused its discretion.
choice: The district court's evidentiary ruling was wrong and unfair, and this Court should review it de novo as a pure question of law.
choice: Expert testimony.
choice: Did the district court err in excluding the expert?
hint: An error of law is an abuse of discretion, and a good heading states the ruling sought, a rule and key facts.
solution: Expert-exclusion rulings are reviewed for abuse of discretion, but a court abuses its discretion when it errs on the law. The heading that calls the ruling a legal error and thus an abuse of discretion states that route, names the key facts and the Rule 702 inquiry in a full sentence. The heading urging de novo review misstates the standard, "Expert testimony" is a label and the heading phrased as a question does not assert a conclusion.

@exam self
prompt: You represent the appellee, Cobalt, in Ellison v. Cobalt Fitness Center (hypothetical). Ellison's brief relies on Dunmore v. Pine Ridge Gym (Hypothetical Circuit 2018: a release cannot bar a gross-negligence claim; whether a gym that ignored prior written warnings and did not repair a machine was grossly negligent was a jury question). Write the part of your argument (200 to 300 words) that deals with Dunmore. Use these facts: a member emailed on August 12 that Treadmill 4's belt was slipping; an Out of Order tag was placed on August 14 and removed on August 16 without repair; the last logged inspection was the preceding February 2. State the controlling authority accurately, then distinguish or limit it, and say what standard governs summary judgment. Also use Kessler v. Brightfield Athletic Club (Hypothetical Circuit 2020), which held that a plaintiff who contends the defendant was grossly negligent must come forward with specific evidence of the defendant's knowledge of the risk.
hint: Disclose Dunmore and state its holding accurately before distinguishing it.
hint: Remember that summary judgment is reviewed de novo with the record viewed in Ellison's favor, so be careful about which facts you say are undisputed.
solution: Under de novo review, this Court asks whether, viewing the record in Ellison's favor, there is a genuine dispute of material fact on gross negligence (FRCP 56(a)). Ellison is right that, under Dunmore v. Pine Ridge Gym, a release cannot bar a claim for gross negligence, which is conduct showing reckless disregard of a known risk of serious harm. Cobalt does not ask this Court to hold otherwise. Cobalt's position is that the record does not show that kind of conduct. In Dunmore, the gym had been told in writing that a cable was frayed and did nothing. Here, one member's email was followed within two days by an Out of Order tag, which tends to show that someone at Cobalt responded to the complaint, although the record does not show who placed or removed the tag. Kessler v. Brightfield Athletic Club requires a plaintiff who asserts gross negligence to come forward with specific evidence of the defendant's knowledge of the risk. Cobalt acknowledges the record shows the tag was removed on August 16 without repair and that the last logged inspection was in February; it contends that these facts show, at most, carelessness in follow-through and not reckless disregard, and that the release therefore bars the negligence claim. If the Court finds a dispute, the proper remedy is remand for trial on gross negligence alone.

rubric: States the de novo summary judgment standard with the record viewed in Ellison's favor.
rubric: Offers a fallback remedy and keeps within 200 to 300 words.
