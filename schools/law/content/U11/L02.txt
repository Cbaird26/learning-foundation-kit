@article
A good brief is not a pile of arguments. It tells one coherent story about why your client should win. That story is your **theory of the case**: a short account that joins the facts, the governing law and the equities into a single reason for a ruling. A **theme** is the phrase or image that makes the theory memorable, one that the court can repeat in its own opinion.

### What a theory must do

A usable theory passes three tests.

1. **Legal soundness.** It must lead to the result through the governing rule. If your theory is "the defendant was careless" but the claim requires a duty you cannot establish, the theory fails no matter how appealing it is.
2. **Factual support.** Every fact the theory depends on must be in the record. On appeal you cannot add facts, and in the trial court a theory built on unproved facts collapses at summary judgment (FRCP 56(a)).
3. **Fairness.** Judges want to rule in a way that feels just. The theory should answer the question: why is it right, and not only permissible, for my client to win?

If the theory works only by ignoring the best facts of the other side, it is a slogan, not a theory. Test it by stating the opponent's theory in one sentence and checking that yours still answers it.

### From theory to theme

A theory is usually one or two sentences. A theme is shorter. Compare two sides in the hypothetical slip-and-fall case (Ana v. Acme Market):

- Ana's theory: A store that mops an aisle and walks away without a warning created the hazard and is responsible when a customer slips. Theme: *Acme made the hazard and left it unmarked.*
- Acme's theory: A fall on a wet floor that was visibly mopped, in a store with posted policies, is the customer's own failure to watch where she walked. Theme: *a wet floor anyone could see.*

Each theme is built from record facts, states the equities, and can be woven through the headings, the facts and the conclusion. A theme should be a short phrase, not a speech, and it should not be a label such as "justice" that fits every case.

### How the theory organizes the document

Once chosen, the theory controls every drafting decision:

- **Facts.** Include and emphasize facts that serve the theory; state the others accurately and briefly (Week 6).
- **Headings.** Each point heading should advance the theory in sentence form (Week 3).
- **Authority.** Choose cases whose reasoning supports the theory and explain how they do.
- **Rule framing.** Describe the rule in the form that makes your result the natural one. Where a rule has two readings, argue for the one that fits the theory.
- **Candor.** The theory must still deal with adverse facts and law honestly. A theory that cannot survive honesty is the wrong theory.

### Rewriting an objective section persuasively

An objective memo section is organized by element, balancing both sides. To rewrite it persuasively: (1) choose the theory and theme; (2) restate the conclusion first, in affirmative form; (3) reorder the analysis so that strongest points lead; (4) recast hedged statements into confident but accurate ones; (5) confront the adverse point in the middle, with context; (6) end on the strongest point and the relief requested.

@example
title: Choosing a theory and theme
problem: Hypothetical facts: Contractor finished Owner's kitchen remodel six days after the completion date in the contract. The contract did not make time of the essence. Owner withheld the final installment, saying the work was late, though Owner lost no money and had no event planned. Contractor sues for the final installment. Write a theory and theme for Contractor.
step: Identify the governing question: whether a short delay excused payment when time was not of the essence. Owner's strongest argument is that the contract set a date, so Contractor's theory must answer it.
step: List supportive record facts: only six days late, time not made of the essence, Owner lost no money, the work was complete.
step: Write the theory: Where time is not of the essence, a short delay is at most a minor breach that supports damages for actual loss, and Owner, who lost nothing, cannot use it to avoid paying for completed work.
step: Write the theme: *a delay that cost Owner nothing.* Check it: it rests on the record, answers Owner's theory and gives the court a fair reason to rule for Contractor.

@mistakes
- Choosing a theory that the record does not support or that ignores the governing elements.
- Using an abstract theme ("fairness," "justice") that fits any case and persuades no one.
- Choosing a theory but then drafting headings and facts that do not follow it.
- Writing a theory so long that it cannot be repeated by the court.

prompt: Rewrite this objective memo paragraph persuasively for Ben. Use only the facts given. Seller's own mechanic had written that the engine "needs a rebuild soon," and Seller told Ben the engine had "no known problems." "Ben bought a used boat from Seller. The mechanic's report is relevant but its meaning is unclear. It may be that Seller knew the engine needed repair, but Seller says the statement 'no known problems' was true to Seller's knowledge. hint: Plan your answer around this goal before you write: Leads with the theory and conclusion rather than a neutral summary.
solution: Seller told Ben the engine had "no known problems" after Seller's own mechanic had written that it "needs a rebuild soon." Seller cannot claim ignorance of a warning in Seller's own file. The statement was not a hope or an opinion; it was a flat assertion of the engine's condition. The court should hold Seller liable for misrepresentation. This version states the conclusion first, removes hedges, reorders around the theory, and meets the adverse "true to my knowledge" point with the mechanic's report.
rubric: Leads with the theory and conclusion rather than a neutral summary.
rubric: Removes hedges but invents no facts (the prompt gives no timing for the mechanic's report).
rubric: Addresses Seller's defense directly.
rubric: Ends on the relief requested or strongest point.

@quiz mc
prompt: Hypothetical facts: Acme Market's own incident log shows that an Acme employee mopped the aisle at 3:20 p.m., Ana fell at 3:23 p.m., and the aisle had no warning sign. Acme's counsel proposes the theme: "Acme could not have known about the spill." What is the best assessment?
choice*: The theme is weak because the record shows Acme's own employee created the condition, and where the defendant created a hazard notice is generally not an issue.
choice: The theme is strong because lack of notice is always a complete defense for a store.
choice: The theme is strong because a theme need not match the record if it is memorable.
choice: The theme is weak only because it is too short.
hint: Ask who created the condition and whether notice matters when the defendant did.
solution: A theme must fit the record. Where the defendant created the dangerous condition, most jurisdictions do not require separate proof of notice. Lack of notice is a defense mainly when a third party or an unknown cause created the hazard.
