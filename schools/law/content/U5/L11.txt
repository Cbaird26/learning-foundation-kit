@article
A trustee holds title to property for others and so is a **fiduciary**, held to standards higher than those of the marketplace. UTC § 801 requires the trustee to administer the trust in good faith, according to its terms and purposes and in the interests of the beneficiaries.

### The core duties

- **Loyalty** (UTC § 802). The trustee must administer the trust **solely in the interests of the beneficiaries**. A transaction involving trust property that the trustee enters for the trustee's own account, or that is affected by a conflict between fiduciary and personal interests, is **voidable by a beneficiary** unless (1) the trust terms authorized it, (2) a court approved it, (3) the beneficiary did not sue within the limitation period, (4) the beneficiary consented or ratified, or (5) it was a claim or contract the trustee acquired before becoming trustee. A sale or other transaction with the trustee's spouse, descendants, siblings, parents or their spouses, or an agent or attorney, is **presumed** to be affected by a conflict. Under the strict **no-further-inquiry rule**, followed by the UTC and *Matter of Rothko* (N.Y. 1977) (executors who dealt with a gallery connected to one of them), the beneficiary need not show that the deal was unfair; a minority of states and the Restatement (Third) of Trusts allow a *fairness* defense in limited cases. The policy is that undetectable self-dealing is deterred better by a bright-line rule than by case-by-case review.
- **Prudence** (UTC § 804; UTC § 901, which adopts the Uniform Prudent Investor Act). The trustee must administer the trust as a prudent person would, with reasonable **care, skill and caution** (a trustee with special skills must use them, § 806). The old *prudent man* rule of *Harvard College v. Amory* (Mass. 1830) judged each investment; the modern prudent investor rule judges the **portfolio as a whole**, requires **diversification** unless special circumstances make it unwise, and permits delegation with reasonable care in selecting and supervising agents (§ 807).
- **Impartiality** (§ 803): the trustee must treat income and remainder beneficiaries fairly given the purposes of the trust.
- **Informing and reporting** (§ 813): the trustee must keep qualified beneficiaries reasonably informed, give notice of the trust's existence and of the right to request a copy of the instrument within 60 days after accepting trusteeship or after the trust becomes irrevocable, and provide an annual report (later amendments to the UTC reorganize these provisions).

### Remedies and exculpation

For breach, a court may compel performance, enjoin, order an accounting, remove the trustee, void the act, impose a lien or **constructive trust**, and trace (UTC § 1001). **Damages** are the greater of (1) the amount required to restore the trust property and distributions to what they would have been had there been no breach, or (2) the **profit** the trustee made from the breach (§ 1002). A term that excuses the trustee from liability is unenforceable to the extent it relieves liability for **bad faith or reckless indifference**, or was inserted as a result of an abuse of a fiduciary relationship (§ 1008). A beneficiary generally has one year to sue after receiving a report that discloses the facts (§ 1005).

### Modification and termination

A trust is irrevocable if its terms say so (or, in a state that keeps the common-law default, unless the settlor reserved a power to revoke). A court can still modify an irrevocable trust in several ways:

- **Consent of the settlor and all beneficiaries** may modify or terminate a noncharitable trust even if it frustrates a material purpose (UTC § 411(a)).
- **Consent of all beneficiaries alone** may do so if the court concludes that continuing the trust is not needed to achieve **any material purpose** (§ 411(b)). This is the **Claflin doctrine** (*Claflin v. Claflin* (Mass. 1889)): a trust that postpones a beneficiary's enjoyment until a stated age has a material purpose, so the beneficiary cannot terminate it early. A spendthrift clause is **not presumed** to be a material purpose (§ 411(c)).
- **Unanticipated circumstances** (§ 412), **uneconomically small trusts** (§ 414), **mistake** (§ 415, reformation by clear and convincing evidence), **tax objectives** (§ 416), and **cy pres** for charitable trusts (§ 413).
- Many states also authorize **decanting** (distributing to a new trust under the trustee's discretion), and a **trust protector** may be given limited powers by the terms.

### Applying the rules

For breach: (1) identify the duty; (2) test the transaction against § 802's exceptions (authorization, consent, court approval); (3) apply the presumption for relatives; (4) choose a remedy, comparing restoration to profit. For modification: who consents; is a material purpose at stake; is there a statute for the circumstance?

@example
title: Trustee's loan to her own business
problem: Trustee lends \$100,000 of trust money to her own company at 3% interest for one year. A prudent investment would have earned 7%. The company earned 12% on the money. The trust terms do not authorize the loan. What are the damages under UTC § 1002?
step: Duty and breach: the loan is a transaction for the trustee's own account, so it violates the duty of loyalty (UTC § 802) and is voidable by a beneficiary.
step: Measure (1), restoration: the trust would have earned 7% = \$7,000 but received 3% = \$3,000, a shortfall of \$4,000.
step: Measure (2), profit: the company earned \$12,000 and paid \$3,000 interest, so the trustee's profit is \$9,000.
step: Damages are the greater of the two: \$9,000. The beneficiary could also seek to void the loan, remove the trustee and recover costs.

@mistakes
- Requiring the beneficiary to prove that a self-dealing transaction was unfair; under the no-further-inquiry rule it is voidable regardless of fairness.
- Holding a trustee to the lower marketplace standard; a trustee owes the duty of loyalty, not merely good faith.
- Applying the prudent investor rule investment by investment instead of to the whole portfolio.
- Letting beneficiaries terminate a trust by consent when a material purpose, such as delaying distribution to an age, remains.
