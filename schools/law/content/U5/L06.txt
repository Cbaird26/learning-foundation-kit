@article
After execution, validity questions give way to **construction**: what does the will mean, and what happens to gifts that cannot take effect as written? This lesson covers three recurring problems: ambiguous language, devisees who die first, and property that is no longer in the estate.

### Interpretation

The goal is the testator's intent as expressed in the will. The traditional **plain-meaning rule** reads clear words as written and excludes extrinsic evidence that would contradict them. If the will contains a **latent ambiguity** (clear on its face but unclear when applied: a gift "to my cousin Rob" when the testator had two cousins named Rob), courts admit extrinsic evidence of intent. A **patent ambiguity** (apparent on the face of the will) historically allowed less evidence, but most courts now admit it for both. Traditionally a court could not reform a will to correct a mistake. Modern law, reflected in Restatement (Third) of Property § 12.1 and UPC § 2-805, permits a court to **reform** the terms of a will to conform to the testator's intent if it is proved by **clear and convincing evidence** that both the terms and the testator's intent were affected by a mistake of fact or law. A will may also incorporate an existing writing by reference (UPC § 2-510) and may dispose of tangible personal property by a later separate writing (§ 2-513).

### Lapse and anti-lapse

If a devisee predeceases the testator, the gift **lapses** (fails) at common law, and a lapsed gift passes to the **residue**. If the lapsed gift is itself a gift of the residue, the common-law **no-residue-of-the-residue** rule sends it by intestacy rather than to the other residuary devisees. A **class gift** to "my children" survives for the members who live; the share of a predeceased member goes to the survivors.

Nearly every state has an **anti-lapse** statute that substitutes the predeceased devisee's descendants for certain relatives. Statutes differ in coverage: some limit it to the testator's descendants, some extend it to all relatives. Under **UPC § 2-603(b)**, if the devisee is a **grandparent, a descendant of a grandparent** (child, sibling, nephew, aunt, cousin) **or a stepchild** of the testator, and the devisee fails to survive the testator by 120 hours but leaves descendants who survive by 120 hours, the descendants take the devise by representation. Words of survivorship ("to A if she survives me" or "to my surviving children") alone do not defeat anti-lapse under the UPC, but the will can say expressly that the gift is conditioned on survival. Friends, spouses, charities and in-laws are not within the UPC's list, so their gifts lapse. Under **§ 2-604** a lapsed or failed devise falls into the residue, and if one of two or more residuary devisees fails, the other residuary devisees take the failed share in proportion to their interests, which abolishes the no-residue-of-the-residue rule.

### Ademption and satisfaction

A **specific devise** (a particular house, car or account) is **adeemed by extinction** if the property is not in the estate at death. Under the traditional **identity theory**, the gift fails even if the testator did not intend this result, and the beneficiary gets nothing; some courts follow an **intent theory**. The UPC (§ 2-606) protects the specific devisee by giving the devisee, among other things, any unpaid purchase price owed on a sale of the property, an unpaid condemnation award, unpaid casualty insurance proceeds, property received through foreclosure of a specifically devised obligation, replacement property, and, if the property was sold by a conservator or agent under a power of attorney, a general pecuniary devise equal to the net sale price. A general devise (a sum of money) cannot be adeemed.

**Ademption by satisfaction** arises when the testator gives the devisee the gift during life: under UPC § 2-609, the gift is treated as satisfied in whole or in part only if the will provides for it, the testator declares in a contemporaneous writing, or the devisee acknowledges in writing.

### Abatement

If the estate cannot pay all gifts after debts, gifts **abate** in this order (UPC § 3-902): intestate property first, then the residuary gifts, then general gifts, and finally specific gifts. Within a class, gifts abate ratably (pro rata).

### Applying the rule

For each gift in turn: (1) is the devisee alive; (2) if not, does anti-lapse apply (is the devisee a grandparent, descendant of a grandparent, or stepchild, and are there surviving descendants), or does the gift fall to the residue; (3) if the gift is specific, is the property in the estate or covered by § 2-606; (4) is the estate large enough, or must gifts abate? Always add the distributions and check that they equal the estate.

@example
title: Three gifts, three results
problem: Ray's net probate estate is \$300,000. His will says: (1) \$90,000 to my brother Rob; (2) \$30,000 to my friend Fay; (3) the residue to my daughter Dee and my friend Gus in equal shares. Rob, Fay and Gus died before Ray. Rob left two children, Ann and Ben; Dee survives. Distribute the estate under UPC §§ 2-603 and 2-604, then say what the result would be under the no-residue-of-the-residue rule.
step: Gift (1): Rob is a descendant of Ray's grandparents (Ray's brother), so § 2-603(b) substitutes his surviving descendants by representation: Ann and Ben take \$45,000 each.
step: Gift (2): Fay is a friend, so no anti-lapse applies and the gift lapses into the residue (§ 2-604(a)). Residue = \$300,000 - \$90,000 = \$210,000, which includes Fay's \$30,000.
step: Gift (3): Gus's half of the residue fails and, under § 2-604(b), passes to the other residuary devisee, Dee. Dee takes the entire \$210,000.
step: Check: \$45,000 + \$45,000 + \$210,000 = \$300,000. Under the common-law no-residue-of-the-residue rule, Gus's half (\$105,000) would instead pass by intestacy to Ray's heirs, so under the will Dee would take only her own \$105,000 half.

@mistakes
- Applying anti-lapse to a friend, spouse or charity under the UPC list; those gifts lapse into the residue.
- Giving a lapsed share of a residuary gift by intestacy in a UPC state; § 2-604(b) gives it to the other residuary devisees.
- Treating a sum of money as specific and letting it adeem; only specific gifts adeem.
- Abating specific gifts before the residue; the order is residue, then general, then specific.

@practice self

His will gives (1) \$30,000 to his stepdaughter Sasha, (2) \$20,000 to his friend Bo, and (3) the residue to his niece Nan and his friend Cal in equal shares. Sasha, Bo and Nan all died before Vic. Sasha left two children, Bo left one child, and Nan left two children, all of whom survive Vic. Cal survives Vic. Apply UPC §§ 2-603 and 2-604 to the three gifts and state each person's share.
solution: Gift (1): a stepchild is within § 2-603(b), so Sasha's two children take \$15,000 each. Gift (2): Bo is a friend, so his gift lapses into the residue (§ 2-604(a)); his child takes nothing. Nan, a niece (a descendant of Vic's grandparents), is within § 2-603(b), so her half, \$85,000, passes to her two children, \$42,500 each. Cal takes \$85,000. Check: \$15,000 + \$15,000 + \$42,500 + \$42,500 + \$85,000 = \$200,000.
rubric: Correctly applies anti-lapse to the stepchild and the niece (Sasha's and Nan's descendants take by representation) and denies it to the friend.
rubric: Puts Bo's lapsed gift into the residue before dividing the residue.
rubric: States each share (\$15,000, \$15,000, \$42,500, \$42,500, \$85,000) and checks that the total is \$200,000.
hint: Take the gifts in order and ask of each whether the devisee is in the § 2-603 list.
