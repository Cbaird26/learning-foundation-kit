@article
A will that satisfies the formalities can still fail because it does not express the testator's own free and informed intent. The grounds for **contest** are lack of capacity, insane delusion, undue influence, duress, fraud and mistake. A contest is brought by an interested person (an heir or a beneficiary under an earlier will) who would take more if the will fails. Under UPC § 3-407 the contestant has the burden of proving lack of intent or capacity, undue influence, fraud, duress, mistake or revocation, while the proponent has the burden of establishing prima facie proof of due execution.

### Testamentary capacity

The standard is lower than the standard for contracts. A testator has **capacity** if, at the time of execution, she (1) understands the **nature of the act** she is performing (making a will), (2) knows the **nature and extent of her property**, (3) knows the **natural objects of her bounty** (the people, usually family, who would ordinarily be expected to benefit), and (4) understands the **relationship of these elements**, that is, the plan of disposition. Capacity is judged at the moment of execution, so a testator who is often confused may have capacity during a **lucid interval**. Old age, eccentricity, illness, even a diagnosis of dementia, does not itself establish incapacity. Courts presume capacity if the will was duly executed, which is one reason that a self-proving affidavit and a contemporaneous lawyer's note are valuable.

### Insane delusion

An **insane delusion** is a false belief that the testator holds without any evidentiary basis and that she clings to despite all evidence to the contrary. If the delusion materially affected the will, then the will, or the part produced by the delusion, fails, even though the testator has capacity in all other respects. *In re Strittmater's Estate* (N.J. 1946) is the classic case: a testator with a morbid aversion to men left her estate to a women's organization. A belief with some factual basis, even a weak or mistaken one, is not a delusion in this sense.

### Undue influence

**Undue influence** is influence that overpowers the testator's free will and substitutes the influencer's wishes for her own. Mere affection, persuasion, flattery or a request does not suffice. A contestant shows (1) a **susceptible** testator, (2) an **opportunity** to exert influence, (3) a **disposition** to do so for an improper purpose, and (4) a result that is the **product of the influence** (a common formulation; *Lipper v. Weslow* (Tex. Civ. App. 1963) is a well-known undue-influence case). Because direct proof is rare, most states shift the burden once the contestant shows a **presumption**: (a) a **confidential or fiduciary relationship** (attorney-in-fact, caretaker, lawyer, trustee) between the testator and the beneficiary, (b) the beneficiary's **active participation in procuring** the will (choosing the lawyer, arranging the signing, being present, supplying the provisions), and (c) an **unnatural or unduly large benefit** to that beneficiary. The presumption shifts the burden of production (and in some states of persuasion) to the proponent to show that the gift reflects the testator's free will. The Restatement (Third) § 8.3 reaches a similar result. In many jurisdictions the presumption does not invalidate the whole will: only the provisions procured by undue influence fall.

### Fraud, duress and mistake

- **Fraud in the execution**: the testator is deceived about the character or contents of the document she signs (for example, she is told it is a power of attorney). The will, or the affected part, is invalid because the testator did not intend to make a will.
- **Fraud in the inducement**: someone makes a false statement of fact intended to cause the testator to make or change a will, and it does. Only the provisions that result from the fraud fail; courts often impose a **constructive trust** on the wrongdoer rather than invalidating the will (*Latham v. Father Divine* (N.Y. 1949) allowed such a claim to proceed). A false statement that the testator's child had died is an example; honest misunderstanding is not fraud.
- **Tortious interference with an expected inheritance** (Restatement (Second) of Torts § 774B) is available in many states when probate relief is not adequate.
- **Duress**: a threat that overcomes the testator's will, treated like undue influence.
- **Mistake** is generally not a ground for invalidation, but courts may reform the will under the modern rule (UPC § 2-805) or reject a will that is the product of a mistake in the identity of the document.

### No-contest clauses

An **in terrorem** (no-contest) clause provides that a beneficiary who contests forfeits her gift. The UPC (§ 2-517) and most states enforce the clause only if the contest was brought without **probable cause**; a contest brought in good faith on reasonable grounds will not trigger forfeiture. A few states, such as Florida, do not enforce them at all. The clause is meaningless against a person who takes nothing under the will.

### Applying the rule

Ask: (1) which ground is raised; (2) who bears the burden; (3) at what time is the testator's state judged (execution); (4) if a presumption of undue influence is raised, do all three elements appear; (5) what remedy fits (invalidate the will, strike a provision, impose a constructive trust).

@example
title: Presumption of undue influence
problem: Sam, 84, is frail and relies on his nephew Nick, who is his attorney-in-fact and lives with him. Nick chooses a lawyer, drives Sam to the lawyer's office, waits in the room during the signing, and handed the lawyer a list of what Sam "wants." The new will leaves Nick 90% of the estate instead of Sam's three children, who had been the beneficiaries under his earlier will. Analyze whether the children can invoke the presumption of undue influence.
step: Confidential relationship: Nick is Sam's agent under a power of attorney and his caretaker, a fiduciary relationship. The first element is met.
step: Active procurement: Nick chose the lawyer, arranged the signing, attended it and supplied the instructions. The second element is met.
step: Unnatural or undue benefit: Nick, a nephew, takes 90% in place of Sam's children. The third element is met.
step: Result: the presumption arises and shifts to Nick the burden of producing evidence (or in some states persuading) that the will reflects Sam's free will; if Nick fails, the will, or the gift to him, is invalid. Sam's age and frailty support susceptibility, but they would not be enough without the other elements.

@mistakes
- Treating lack of capacity as a matter of general mental health, rather than the four elements judged at the moment of execution.
- Calling any persuasion or family pressure undue influence; the influence must overcome the testator's free will, and the presumption needs all three elements.
- Voiding the entire will for a fraud or influence that affected only one gift.
- Treating a mistaken but factually grounded belief as an insane delusion.

@practice self
prompt: Dale's will leaves \$200,000 to his daughter Dee and provides: "Any beneficiary who contests this will or any provision in it forfeits all benefits under it." Dee believes, based on a doctor's written statement that Dale was heavily medicated when he signed, that he lacked capacity and files a contest. The court finds that she had probable cause but that she loses on the merits. Does she forfeit the \$200,000 under UPC § 2-517? Explain the rule, the policy and the result.
hint: Ask whether the contest had probable cause, and what the UPC does with a clause when it did.
solution: Under UPC § 2-517, a provision that penalizes an interested person for contesting a will is unenforceable if probable cause exists for instituting proceedings. Most states follow this rule; a few enforce clauses strictly, and a few (Florida) enforce them not at all. The policy is balance: enforcing the clause deters frivolous contests and protects the testator's plan, but enforcing it against good-faith challenges would shield invalid wills from review. Application: Dee acted on a doctor's written statement about Dale's medication at execution, which is probable cause, so the forfeiture clause cannot be enforced against her even though she lost. Result: she keeps her \$200,000.
rubric: States the probable-cause exception of UPC § 2-517 and notes that jurisdictions vary.
rubric: Explains the policy tension between deterring contests and permitting review of invalid wills.
rubric: Applies the exception to Dee's facts and concludes that she does not forfeit.
