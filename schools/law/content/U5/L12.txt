@article
### Powers of appointment

A **power of appointment** is the authority, created by one person (the **donor**) in favor of another (the **donee**), to designate who will receive property that the donor owns or controls. The persons to whom the donee may appoint are the **objects**; if the power is not exercised, the property goes to the **takers in default** named by the donor. The donee never owns the property; the donor's instrument controls, and the appointment is read as if written into the donor's instrument.

- **General power**: the donee may appoint to herself, her estate, her creditors, or the creditors of her estate (the federal tax definition, IRC § 2041(b)(1), also treats these four as the tests). A power limited by an **ascertainable standard** relating to health, education, support or maintenance is not general for tax purposes.
- **Special (limited) power**: the donee may appoint only to a group that excludes herself, her estate and her creditors, such as "among my descendants."
- **Presently exercisable**: exercisable now (during life); **testamentary**: exercisable only by will.

**Exercise.** A donor may require that the donee refer to the power specifically (*specific reference*). Where the donor does not, UPC § 2-608 provides that a general residuary clause or a will disposing of all of the donee's property exercises a power of appointment only if (1) the power is **general** and the donor's instrument has **no gift in default**, or (2) the donee's will shows an intent to include the appointive property. Some states apply a blanket exercise more broadly. If the donee does not exercise a power, the property passes to the takers in default; if none, a special power's appointive property passes to the objects equally (or to the donor's successors), and a general power's property usually to the donee's estate or the donor's estate. If a donee of a general power attempts an appointment that fails, many courts apply the **capture** doctrine, treating the property as part of the donee's estate if the donee intended to take it out of the default clause. For a general power, creditors of the donee may be able to reach the appointive property (the rule varies by state and by whether the power is presently exercisable).

**Tax.** Property subject to a **general** power is included in the donee's gross estate (IRC § 2041), and a lifetime exercise or release can be a gift (§ 2514); special powers are not included. Planners use special powers to give flexibility without adding the property to the donee's taxable estate.

### Estate-planning basics

A **basic plan** usually includes: a **will** (to name an executor, a guardian for minor children, and a pour-over gift); a **revocable trust** (to avoid probate and give continuity if the client becomes incapacitated); a **durable power of attorney** (financial agent); an **advance health care directive**; and **beneficiary designations** that are coordinated with the plan. The planner also asks about the client's assets, their titles, family circumstances, taxes and goals, since the property that passes by designation or title is not controlled by the will (Lesson 7).

**Federal transfer taxes.** The federal estate tax applies to the taxable estate over the **applicable exclusion amount** (adjusted for inflation; the amount changes, so every problem here gives it as an assumption). The principal deductions are the **unlimited marital deduction** for property passing outright to a U.S.-citizen spouse (IRC § 2056, and *QTIP* trusts qualify under § 2056(b)(7)) and the **charitable deduction** (§ 2055). A surviving spouse may use the **deceased spousal unused exclusion** (DSUE) if the executor makes a **portability** election on a timely estate tax return (IRC § 2010(c)). Lifetime gifts above the **annual exclusion** (§ 2503(b)) use up the exclusion. A **credit shelter** (bypass) trust uses the first spouse's exclusion to shelter property for the family. Many states also have their own estate or inheritance taxes.

**Income-tax basis.** Property acquired from a decedent generally takes a basis equal to its fair market value at death (a **stepped-up basis**, IRC § 1014), while property received by lifetime gift generally takes the donor's basis (a **carryover basis**, § 1015). This is a reason to hold appreciated assets until death.

**Ethics.** A lawyer who drafts for a couple must address the conflict of interest in joint representation and what will happen if one spouse's confidences matter to the other (ABA Model Rule 1.7), must assess the client's capacity and ask whether the client is acting freely (Rule 1.14), and generally owes duties to the client, not to the intended beneficiaries.

### Applying the rules

For a power of appointment: (1) classify it; (2) look for a specific-reference requirement; (3) test the donee's will under § 2-608; (4) go to the default takers. For a plan: list goals, match each to a document or title, and run the tax computation from the stated assumptions.

@example
title: A residuary clause and a general power
problem: Donor's trust gives Dara a testamentary power to appoint the trust property to anyone, including her estate. If she does not exercise it, the property goes to Donor's nephew. Dara's will leaves "all the residue of my estate" to her friend Fay and does not mention the power. Dara dies. Who takes the trust property under UPC § 2-608?
step: Classify: Dara may appoint to her estate and to anyone else, so the power is general (and testamentary).
step: Rule: a general residuary clause exercises a general power only if the donor's instrument has no gift in default, or the will manifests an intent to include the appointive property.
step: Application: the trust has a gift in default (to the nephew), and Dara's will does not mention the power or the property. Neither condition is met, so the power was not exercised.
step: Conclusion: the nephew, the taker in default, takes the property. Fay takes only Dara's own property. A blanket-exercise state that treats any residuary clause as an exercise would reach the opposite result.

@mistakes
- Treating the donee as the owner of the appointive property, or letting the donee's creditors reach a special power's property.
- Assuming a general residuary clause always exercises every power the donee holds.
- Treating all powers of appointment as taxable to the donee; only general powers (without an ascertainable standard limit) are.
- Stating an estate-tax exclusion amount from memory; the amount is indexed and changes.

@practice self
prompt: Trustee Mo manages a trust for Ana and Ben. Mo causes the trust to buy \$200,000 of shares in a company that Mo owns, at a price above market value, and the company pays Mo a bonus for the sale. The trust terms do not authorize the purchase, and Mo did not tell the beneficiaries. Analyze Mo's breach of the duty of loyalty and the remedies available to Ana and Ben.
solution: Issue: whether Mo breached the duty of loyalty and what remedies exist. Rule: UTC § 802(a) requires the trustee to administer the trust solely in the beneficiaries' interests; § 802(b) makes a transaction involving trust property entered into for the trustee's own account, or affected by a conflict between personal and fiduciary interests, voidable by an affected beneficiary unless authorized by the terms, approved by a court, consented to or ratified by the beneficiary, or time-barred. The no-further-inquiry rule means fairness is not a defense. Application: Mo bought stock in a company Mo owns, with trust money, at an inflated price, and received a bonus, so Mo had a personal interest in the transaction; no authorization, court approval or consent exists, and nondisclosure defeats ratification. Conclusion: the purchase is voidable. Under UTC §§ 1001 and 1002, Ana and Ben may void the purchase, recover the overpayment, or recover damages equal to the greater of the amount that restores the trust to where it would have been or Mo's profit (including the bonus), remove Mo, compel an accounting, and impose a constructive trust on proceeds Mo received. Exculpatory terms would not protect bad-faith self-dealing (§ 1008).
rubric: States the duty of loyalty and the voidability rule of UTC § 802 with its exceptions.
rubric: Applies the rule to the facts (self-interest, overpayment, bonus, no authorization or disclosure).
rubric: Identifies remedies under UTC §§ 1001 and 1002 and concludes that the sale is voidable.
hint: Identify what personal interest Mo had, then check each exception in § 802(b).
