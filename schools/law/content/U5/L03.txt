@article
A will is a revocable, ambulatory instrument that takes effect at death. Because the testator is gone when it is offered for probate, the law requires **formalities** that serve four functions: the *evidentiary* function (proof that the document is genuine), the *ritual* function (the testator understands the seriousness of the act), the *protective* function (guarding against pressure), and the *channeling* function (a uniform form so courts can process wills efficiently).

### The attested will

Under the traditional Wills Act approach, a will is valid if it is (1) **in writing**, (2) **signed by the testator** (or by another person in the testator's presence and at the testator's direction), and (3) **attested by two witnesses**. Most states then require that the testator sign or acknowledge the signature in the witnesses' presence, and that the witnesses sign in the testator's **presence** (a few say also in each other's presence). Courts split on "presence": the *line-of-sight* test asks whether the testator could have seen the witness sign from where the testator was; the *conscious-presence* test asks whether the testator was aware of the signing through sight, hearing or other senses. Some states also require **publication** (telling the witnesses it is a will), though most do not. Testators must be 18 or older and of sound mind (UPC § 2-501).

**UPC § 2-502(a)** relaxes the rule. The will must be in writing, signed by the testator (or by another in the testator's conscious presence and at the testator's direction), and signed by at least two individuals, each of whom signed within a **reasonable time** after witnessing either the testator's signing or the testator's acknowledgment of the signature or of the will. Alternatively, the testator may acknowledge the signature before a notary public. Witnesses need not be present at the same time, need not sign in the testator's presence, and publication is not required. A signature on a self-proving affidavit that is attached to the will counts as a signature on the will if needed.

### Holographic wills

A **holographic will** is a will in the testator's own handwriting, usually unwitnessed. About half the states, and the UPC (§ 2-502(b)), recognize one if the **signature and the material portions** are in the testator's handwriting; the UPC does not require a date, and extrinsic evidence may show testamentary intent (§ 2-502(c)). Where the state requires the entire will to be handwritten, a preprinted form with handwritten blanks can fail. Oral (nuncupative) wills are valid in only a handful of states and only in narrow circumstances.

### Interested witnesses and self-proving affidavits

At early common law a beneficiary could not be a competent witness, so the will failed. Today, UPC § 2-505(b) provides that the signing of a will by an interested witness does not invalidate the will or any provision of it. Some states have **purging statutes**: an interested witness takes the lesser of the gift or the intestate share unless a sufficient number of other disinterested witnesses signed. A **self-proving affidavit** (UPC § 2-504), sworn by the testator and the witnesses before a notary, lets the court admit the will without calling the witnesses; it proves due execution but, under the traditional rule, is not a substitute for the testator's signature on the will; UPC § 2-504(c) instead lets a signature on the affidavit count as a signature on the will if necessary to prove execution.

### Harmless error and electronic wills

Under the traditional rule of **strict compliance**, a will with any formal defect fails, even when intent is clear. A growing minority of states have adopted a **harmless-error** or *dispensing power* rule modeled on **UPC § 2-503**: a document that was not executed in compliance with § 2-502 is treated as if it had been, if the proponent establishes by **clear and convincing evidence** that the decedent intended it to be the decedent's will. The doctrine corrects failed formalities (a missing witness, an unsigned will); it does not excuse a document that was never intended to be a will. A few states, and the Uniform Electronic Wills Act (2019), permit electronic wills with e-signatures and remote witnessing; adoption varies.

### Applying the rule

Ask: (1) Which law governs (UPC, strict Wills Act, or a statute described in the problem)? (2) Is there a writing, a testator's signature, and enough witnesses? (3) Did the witnesses see the signing or the acknowledgment, and did they sign in time and place the law requires? (4) If there is a defect, does the state apply harmless error and is the evidence clear and convincing?

@example
title: An acknowledgment to witnesses in a UPC state
problem: Rosa, domiciled in a UPC state, signs her typed will alone on Monday. On Wednesday she shows the signed document to her neighbors Ned and Ona and says, "This is my will and that is my signature." Ned signs as witness that day, and Ona signs the next day. Neither is a beneficiary. Is the will validly executed?
step: Under § 2-502(a), the will is in writing and signed by Rosa. The only question is whether two persons witnessed and signed.
step: Witnesses may witness either the testator's signing or her acknowledgment of her signature or of the will. Rosa acknowledged both in Ned's and Ona's presence.
step: Each witness signed within a reasonable time after witnessing the acknowledgment. The UPC does not require the witnesses to be present together or to sign in Rosa's presence, so signing one day apart is acceptable.
step: Conclusion: the will is valid. Under a strict traditional statute, Rosa would have had to sign or acknowledge in the witnesses' presence and the witnesses would have had to sign in her presence.

@mistakes
- Applying the traditional "witnesses must sign in the testator's presence" rule in a UPC state, or assuming the UPC drops the requirement of two witnesses.
- Treating an interested witness as voiding the will; under the UPC and in most states the will stands, and a purging statute at most limits the witness's gift.
- Using harmless error as an excuse for a missing intent: the proponent must prove by clear and convincing evidence that the decedent intended the document to be a will.
- Assuming that a notarized or self-proved will is automatically valid even if the testator never signed the will itself.
