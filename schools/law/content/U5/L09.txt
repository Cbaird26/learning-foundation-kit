@article
A **trust** is a fiduciary relationship in which one person, the **trustee**, holds legal title to property for the benefit of one or more **beneficiaries**. The person who creates it is the **settlor** (or trustor). Legal title and management rest with the trustee; the beneficial (equitable) interest rests with the beneficiaries, who can enforce the trustee's duties. The **Uniform Trust Code (UTC)** has been enacted in a majority of states; the common law and the Restatements (Third) of Trusts fill the gaps.

### Methods of creation

Under UTC § 401, a trust is created by: (1) a **transfer of property** to another person as trustee, during the settlor's life or by will or other disposition taking effect at death; (2) a **declaration** by the owner of property that the owner holds identifiable property as trustee (no transfer of title is needed, because the owner changes capacity); or (3) the **exercise of a power of appointment** in favor of a trustee. A trust created by will is a **testamentary trust** and must meet the Wills Act; a trust created during life is an **inter vivos** (living) trust.

### Requirements for a valid express private trust

Under UTC § 402(a) a trust is created only if:

1. the settlor has **capacity** to create a trust (the capacity needed for a will if the trust is revocable);
2. the settlor indicates **intent** to create a trust (no particular words are needed, such as "trust" or "trustee"; but **precatory** words, such as "I hope" or "I request", usually show only a wish and not a duty);
3. the trust has a **definite beneficiary** or is a charitable trust, a trust for the care of an animal (§ 408), or a noncharitable trust without an ascertainable beneficiary (§ 409, limited to 21 years);
4. the trustee has **duties to perform**; and
5. the **same person is not the sole trustee and sole beneficiary** (the interests *merge* and no trust results).

Two additional ideas matter in practice. First, there must be **identifiable trust property** (the res): a trust of a mere expectancy, such as hoped-for future inheritance, fails. Second, a trust requires a *present* transfer or declaration; a **gratuitous promise** to create a trust in the future is unenforceable for lack of consideration. A trust does not fail for want of a trustee: the court appoints one (UTC § 704(b)). A trust with no active duties (a **passive** or *dry* trust) is disfavored, and some states treat it as executed so that the beneficiary holds legal title.

**Formalities.** Under UTC § 407, a trust need not be evidenced by a writing, but the creation and terms of an **oral trust** can be established only by **clear and convincing evidence**. The **statute of frauds** in many states requires a writing for a trust of land, and a will must be executed with the Wills Act formalities if it creates a testamentary trust.

### Kinds of trusts

- **Express trust**: created by the settlor's intent, as above.
- **Resulting trust**: implied by law to return property to the settlor (or the settlor's successors) when an express trust fails or is exhausted, or when one person pays the purchase price and title is taken in another's name (a *purchase-money resulting trust*).
- **Constructive trust**: an *equitable remedy* that makes a wrongdoer or unjustly enriched person hold property for the person entitled to it. It is not a true trust and requires no intent.
- **Revocable vs irrevocable.** The common-law default was irrevocability; **UTC § 602** reverses it: unless the terms expressly make the trust irrevocable, the settlor may revoke or amend the trust (a minority of states keep the common-law default). While the trust is revocable, the trustee owes duties only to the settlor (UTC § 603).
- **Special trusts**: a **pet (animal) trust** is valid for the animal's lifetime (UTC § 408), and a **noncharitable purpose trust** (UTC § 409) may last up to 21 years. Charitable trusts are covered in Lesson 10.

### Policy and application

The requirements ensure the settlor intended a legal obligation, that someone can enforce it, and that the property is identifiable so a court can supervise. Apply them in order: (1) capacity; (2) intent (not merely precatory or a promise); (3) a present method of creation (transfer, declaration, appointment) of identifiable property; (4) a definite beneficiary or permitted purpose; (5) active duties and no merger; (6) any formality (statute of frauds or Wills Act). If the express trust fails, consider a resulting or constructive trust.

@example
title: Declaration of trust
problem: Owner signs a document that says, "I declare that I hold my 1,000 shares of Acme Corp. in trust for my niece, Nia, to pay her the dividends each year and to deliver the shares to her at age 30." Owner does not change the share registration. Owner is the sole trustee. Is a valid trust created?
step: Method: UTC § 401(2) allows the owner to create a trust by declaring that the owner holds identifiable property as trustee. No transfer to another person is required, and the 1,000 Acme shares are identifiable property.
step: Intent: the words "I declare that I hold ... in trust" show intent to create a trust, not a hope or a promise of a future trust.
step: Beneficiary and duties: Nia is a definite beneficiary, and Owner has duties (pay dividends, deliver shares at 30). Owner is trustee but not the sole beneficiary, so there is no merger.
step: Conclusion: the trust is valid. Failing to retitle the shares does not matter in a declaration of trust, though it creates proof and administration problems (the shares should be identified as trust property).

@mistakes
- Requiring a transfer of title to a third-party trustee even though the owner has validly declared a trust.
- Treating precatory language ("I hope you will use it for ...") as creating a trust.
- Treating a gratuitous promise to create a trust in the future as an enforceable trust.
- Forgetting that the same person cannot be sole trustee and sole beneficiary; the interests merge.
