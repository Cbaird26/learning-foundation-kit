@article
Most wealth in the United States no longer passes under wills. A **will substitute** (or **nonprobate transfer**) is a transfer of property at death by contract, title or trust that does not require probate. The principal substitutes are **life insurance**, **retirement accounts** (IRAs, 401(k) plans), **payable-on-death (POD)** bank accounts and **transfer-on-death (TOD)** securities registrations, **joint tenancy with right of survivorship (JTWROS)** and tenancy by the entirety, **transfer-on-death deeds** for real property (authorized in a majority of states, many under the Uniform Real Property Transfer on Death Act of 2009), and the **revocable trust**.

### The rule: validity without Wills Act formalities

Under the traditional view, any transfer that takes effect only at death is testamentary and must satisfy the Wills Act. Courts and legislatures have rejected that view for will substitutes. UPC § 6-101 provides that a provision for a nonprobate transfer on death is *nontestamentary* and need not meet the formalities of a will, and a revocable trust is valid if the settlor satisfies the trust's creation requirements (*Farkas v. Williams* (Ill. 1955) upheld a revocable trust against a Wills Act attack). Policy: the substitutes are cheap, private, and avoid probate delay; the beneficiary's rights arise by contract or title at death, so the property is not part of the **probate estate** and is not controlled by the will. A will cannot override a valid beneficiary designation, and an obsolete designation (an ex-spouse named years ago) is a classic malpractice trap.

### How each substitute works

- **Life insurance** and **retirement accounts** go to the named beneficiary; if none survives, to the contingent beneficiary or the policy's default (often the estate).
- **JTWROS**: on a joint tenant's death, the survivor takes the property, free of the will. A unilateral conveyance or sale by one joint tenant severs the joint tenancy. A **joint bank account** is different: under UPC § 6-211, during the parties' lifetimes the account belongs to the parties in proportion to their net contributions, unless there is clear and convincing evidence of a different intent, and at death a survivorship account belongs to the survivor.
- **POD and TOD** designations pass to the named person at death and may be changed at will.
- **Revocable trust**: the settlor transfers assets to a trustee, keeps the power to revoke and the right to income, and names remainder beneficiaries. At death the trust becomes irrevocable and the trustee distributes by the terms. A **pour-over will** leaves the residue to the trustee; under UPC § 2-511 (the Uniform Testamentary Additions to Trusts Act) the devise is valid if the trust is identified in the will and its terms are in a writing, even if the trust is unfunded or amended after the will was signed.

### Limits: creditors, family, divorce and federal law

- **Creditors.** During life, the property of a revocable trust is subject to the claims of the settlor's creditors (UTC § 505(a)(1)). After death, trust property that was revocable at death is subject to claims of the settlor's creditors, costs of administration, funeral expenses and statutory allowances to the surviving spouse and children, to the extent the probate estate is inadequate (UTC § 505(a)(3)). State law on nonprobate assets generally gives the surviving spouse's elective share a reach into them (Lesson 8; *Sullivan v. Burkin* (Mass. 1984) reached revocable-trust assets under the spouse's elective share in a state that lacked an augmented estate).
- **Divorce.** UPC § 2-804 revokes revocable beneficiary designations of a former spouse in life insurance, trusts and POD accounts, and treats the ex-spouse as having predeceased.
- **ERISA preemption.** Federal law governs employer-sponsored retirement plans. *Egelhoff v. Egelhoff* (2001) held that ERISA preempts a state statute that revoked a divorced participant's beneficiary designation, and *Kennedy v. Plan Administrator for DuPont Savings and Investment Plan* (2009) held that the plan administrator must pay the beneficiary named in the plan documents (the *plan documents rule*), even if a divorce decree purported to waive the ex-spouse's rights. *Hillman v. Maretta* (2013) reached the same preemption result under the federal employees' life insurance statute.
- **Slayer, undue influence and capacity** rules apply to will substitutes too (UPC § 2-803 and § 6-101).

### Applying the rule

Sort every asset by how title passes: in the decedent's sole name with no designation (probate); or by contract, survivorship or trust (nonprobate). Then ask: (1) who is the named beneficiary; (2) has the designation been revoked by divorce or by the owner; (3) is a federal statute controlling; (4) do creditors or the spouse reach the asset; (5) does the will matter at all for this asset?

@example
title: Probate or nonprobate?
problem: At death, Quin owned: a house in her sole name (\$320,000, subject to a \$100,000 mortgage); a checking account in her sole name (\$30,000); a brokerage account held in the name of her revocable trust (\$250,000); a life insurance policy naming her niece as beneficiary (\$200,000); and a bank account held jointly with her brother with right of survivorship (\$40,000). Her will leaves everything to her friend. Compute the net probate estate and say who gets the other assets.
step: Probate property is what Quin owned in her sole name without a designation: the house (\$320,000) and the checking account (\$30,000), a total of \$350,000.
step: Subtract the mortgage, which is a debt secured by the house: \$350,000 - \$100,000 = \$250,000 net. The friend takes this under the will (subject to other debts and expenses).
step: The brokerage account passes to the trustee under the trust; the insurance goes to the niece; the joint account goes to the brother by survivorship. The will does not control any of them.
step: Result: the friend takes \$250,000 net; the trust beneficiaries take \$250,000, the niece \$200,000 and the brother \$40,000, whatever the will says.

@mistakes
- Letting the will dispose of assets that pass by beneficiary designation, survivorship or trust.
- Treating a joint bank account as owned equally during life; under UPC § 6-211 ownership follows net contributions unless a different intent is proved.
- Assuming a divorce revokes an ex-spouse's designation under an ERISA plan; federal law and the plan documents control.
- Forgetting that revocable-trust assets remain subject to the settlor's creditors and may be reached for the spouse's elective share.

@practice self
prompt: Explain, in a short memo section, why the following four assets are not controlled by a will: (1) a life insurance policy naming a niece; (2) a house held in joint tenancy with right of survivorship with a brother; (3) a POD bank account; (4) a revocable trust. Then name one limit that applies to each of the four, either a creditor, spouse or federal-law limit.
solution: Each asset passes by contract, title or trust, which operate at the owner's death and are nontestamentary under UPC § 6-101, so the Wills Act does not apply and the will does not govern. (1) Life insurance passes by the beneficiary designation contract; the limit is divorce revocation under UPC § 2-804 for ordinary policies (federal law may preempt for plans such as ERISA plans or the federal employees' program). (2) The joint-tenancy house passes to the survivor by operation of law; the limit is that the survivor's right can be defeated by severance during life, by the slayer rule, or by the spouse's elective share where the augmented estate includes it. (3) A POD account passes to the named payee at death; the limit is the elective share's reach into POD accounts and creditors' claims to the extent the probate estate is inadequate. (4) The revocable trust passes by its terms; the limit is that its assets remain subject to the settlor's creditors, costs of administration and statutory allowances if the probate estate is inadequate (UTC § 505(a)), and to the spouse's elective share.

rubric: Names an appropriate limit for each of the four assets.
