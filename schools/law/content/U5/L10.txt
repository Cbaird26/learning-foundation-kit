@article
Two special features of trust law shape planning: the **charitable trust**, which has relaxed requirements and special privileges, and the **spendthrift trust**, which shields a beneficiary's interest from creditors.

### Charitable trusts

A trust is **charitable** if its purpose is the relief of poverty, the advancement of education or religion, the promotion of health, governmental or municipal purposes, or another purpose beneficial to the community (UTC § 405(a)). The following rules distinguish charitable from private trusts:

- No **definite beneficiaries** are required; the community or an indefinite class benefits.
- The **Rule Against Perpetuities** does not apply, so a charitable trust may last forever. (A gift from one charity to another is also exempt.)
- The trust is enforced by the **state attorney general**, by the settlor (UTC § 405(c)), and, in many states, by a person with a **special interest** in the trust (an identifiable class of beneficiaries); an ordinary taxpayer or member of the public does not have standing.
- **Cy pres.** If a particular charitable purpose becomes unlawful, impracticable, impossible to achieve or wasteful, the trust does not fail and the property does not revert to the settlor; the court may apply **cy pres** and direct the property to a purpose that is as near as possible to the settlor's (UTC § 413(a)). Under the common law the court first needed to find a **general charitable intent**; the UTC largely presumes it unless the terms provide for a gift over to a noncharitable beneficiary if the purpose fails.

A **noncharitable purpose trust** (a trust for an object that has no human beneficiary) is generally invalid at common law as a trust, though some states allow **honorary trusts** under which the trustee may carry out the purpose if willing. UTC § 408 validates a **pet trust** for the animal's lifetime, and UTC § 409 permits other noncharitable purposes for up to 21 years.

### Spendthrift trusts

A **spendthrift provision** restrains both voluntary and involuntary transfer of a beneficiary's interest (UTC § 502). It is valid only if it restrains both; a clause restraining only voluntary transfer is not effective. *Nichols v. Eaton* (1875) upheld spendthrift trusts in federal law and *Broadway National Bank v. Adams* (Mass. 1882) in state law; critics argue that a person should not enjoy wealth free of the debts that the wealth could pay. A valid clause bars a creditor from reaching the beneficiary's interest **while it is in the trustee's hands**; once the trustee distributes the money to the beneficiary, creditors may reach it.

**Exceptions** (UTC § 503): a spendthrift provision is unenforceable against (1) a beneficiary's **child, spouse or former spouse** who has a judgment or court order for **support or maintenance** (*Shelley v. Shelley* (Or. 1960) is a classic child-support case); (2) a judgment creditor who provided **services for protecting the beneficiary's interest** in the trust (a lawyer, for example); and (3) a claim of a **state or the United States** to the extent a statute provides (such as the federal tax lien). Many states add tort creditors, though the UTC does not. A **discretionary trust** (the trustee may distribute in the trustee's discretion) also generally prevents the creditor from compelling a distribution (UTC § 504), though a child-support claimant may reach it.

**Self-settled trusts.** A spendthrift provision does not protect a trust that the **settlor created for the settlor's own benefit**. During the settlor's lifetime a creditor of the settlor may reach the property of a **revocable** trust (UTC § 505(a)(1)), and in an irrevocable trust the creditor may reach the **maximum amount that can be distributed to or for the settlor's benefit** (UTC § 505(a)(2)). A minority of states (more than a dozen, beginning with Alaska and Delaware) have enacted **domestic asset protection trust** statutes that allow limited protection of a self-settled irrevocable trust, subject to the rules on fraudulent transfers.

### Applying the rules and testing for validity

To decide whether a valid trust was created, run the Lesson 9 checklist (capacity, intent, definite beneficiary or permitted purpose, duties, no merger, formalities) and then check whether the purpose is charitable, a pet trust or a noncharitable purpose trust and its duration limits. For a creditor problem: (1) is there a valid spendthrift clause; (2) is the creditor in an exception class; (3) is the trust self-settled; (4) is the property still in the trustee's hands or has it been distributed?

@example
title: A creditor against a spendthrift beneficiary
problem: A trust pays all income to Ben for life, with a valid spendthrift clause. Ben owes \$20,000 on a credit card judgment and \$12,000 in unpaid child support under a court order. The trust is not self-settled. Which creditor can reach Ben's interest?
step: The spendthrift clause is valid because it restrains voluntary and involuntary transfers (UTC § 502).
step: The credit card creditor is an ordinary judgment creditor and is barred from reaching Ben's interest while the trust holds the funds.
step: The child-support claimant is within the UTC § 503 exception, so the clause does not defeat the claim; the court may order the trustee to pay the claimant out of present or future distributions.
step: Conclusion: only the child-support claimant (\$12,000) reaches the trust. After distributions reach Ben's hands, the credit card creditor can pursue the money Ben has received.

@mistakes
- Assuming that a spendthrift clause protects against child-support or alimony claimants, or against creditors of a self-settled trust.
- Applying the Rule Against Perpetuities to a charitable trust.
- Letting a charitable trust fail whenever its purpose cannot be performed, instead of applying cy pres.
- Giving every member of the public standing to sue to enforce a charitable trust.

Rule: UTC § 402 requires capacity, intent, a definite beneficiary or a permitted purpose, duties, and no merger; UTC § 408 allows a trust to provide for the care of an animal alive during the settlor's lifetime, valid for the life of the animal.
