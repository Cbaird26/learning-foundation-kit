@article
Last week's rules assume that the survivors are known and that each heir is entitled to a full intestate share. Three doctrines change that picture: **advancements** (an heir already received part of the share), **survival** (who outlived whom), and **disqualification** (an heir who must not take).

### Advancements and hotchpot

An **advancement** is a lifetime gift to a would-be heir that the decedent intended as an early payment of the heir's intestate share. At common law, a lifetime gift from a parent to a child was *presumed* to be an advancement unless the evidence showed an ordinary gift. The UPC reverses the presumption. Under § 2-109, a lifetime gift reduces the donee's intestate share only if (1) the decedent declared in a **contemporaneous writing**, or the heir acknowledged in writing, that the gift is an advancement, and (2) the donee survives the decedent; if the donee predeceases, the gift is ignored unless the writing says otherwise. The property is valued as of the time the heir came into possession or enjoyment, or the decedent's death, whichever is earlier.

The method is **hotchpot**: add the advancement to the estate to create a notional total, divide that total among the heirs who share, and subtract each advancement from the donee's share. For an estate $E$ divided among $n$ equal heirs with an advancement $A$ to one of them, that heir takes $\frac{E + A}{n} - A$ and each other heir takes $\frac{E + A}{n}$. If the advancement exceeds the donee's notional share, the donee takes nothing but does not repay the excess; the rest is then recalculated without that heir. The policy is equality among heirs without forcing repayment of gifts.

### Survival and simultaneous death

An heir must survive the decedent. The UPC adds a **120-hour rule**: under § 2-104 an individual who does not survive the decedent by 120 hours (established by clear and convincing evidence) is treated as having **predeceased**. The rule avoids pointless double administration when spouses die in the same accident, and it mirrors the Uniform Simultaneous Death Act (the 1993 text revised in 2006), now in most states. If the order of death cannot be established, each person's property passes as if that person had survived the other. For property held by joint tenants or spouses with survivorship, each person is treated as surviving as to one-half. For life insurance, the insured is treated as the survivor, so the proceeds pass as if the beneficiary had died first. UPC § 2-702 applies the same 120-hour requirement to wills and other transfers at death. Under the older common-law and original 1940 act approaches, any survival, however brief, was enough, so property could move through a second estate.

### Disqualification

- **Slayer rule (UPC § 2-803).** An individual who feloniously and intentionally kills the decedent forfeits benefits from the decedent's death: the killer is treated as having predeceased, revocable beneficiary designations naming the killer are revoked, and survivorship interests are severed so the killer cannot take the decedent's share. A final conviction is conclusive; without one, the probate court decides by a preponderance of the evidence. Accidental killings and killings in self-defense do not trigger the rule, and states differ about voluntary manslaughter. Because the slayer is treated as predeceased, the slayer's descendants ordinarily take by representation, although a few states bar them.
- **Abuse, neglect or abandonment.** Many states bar an abuser of an elderly or dependent decedent, and UPC § 2-114 bars a parent whose parental rights could have been terminated for nonsupport, abandonment or abuse from inheriting from a child who died before 18.
- **Disclaimer.** An heir may refuse property by a written, irrevocable disclaimer; the heir is then treated as having predeceased, so the share passes to the heir's descendants if any. For federal tax purposes a qualified disclaimer must be made within nine months, before the heir accepts benefits (Internal Revenue Code § 2518).

### Applying the rules

Sequence matters: (1) fix who survived by 120 hours; (2) remove disqualified or disclaiming heirs, treating each as predeceased; (3) find the spouse's share and the next class; (4) apply hotchpot to any recorded advancement; (5) divide by representation and confirm that the shares sum to the estate.

@example
title: Hotchpot with a documented advancement
problem: Pat dies intestate with a probate estate of \$270,000. Pat's three children, Ana, Ben and Cal, survive, and there is no spouse. Pat's signed note, written when the gift was made, says that the \$30,000 given to Ana last year was an advancement. Find each child's share under UPC § 2-109.
step: The note is a contemporaneous writing by the decedent and Ana survives, so the gift is an advancement. Add it to the estate: \$270,000 + \$30,000 = \$300,000.
step: Divide among the three children: \$300,000 / 3 = \$100,000 each.
step: Charge Ana with the advancement: \$100,000 - \$30,000 = \$70,000. Ben and Cal each take \$100,000.
step: Check: \$70,000 + \$100,000 + \$100,000 = \$270,000, the actual estate.

@mistakes
- Treating every lifetime gift to a child as an advancement; under the UPC a writing is required, while the common-law presumption is the opposite.
- Subtracting the advancement from the estate instead of adding it to the pot first and then charging it to the donee.
- Forgetting the 120-hour rule: an heir who dies 30 hours after the decedent is treated as having predeceased.
- Treating a slayer as taking and then forfeiting, instead of as having predeceased, so the slayer's descendants may take.
