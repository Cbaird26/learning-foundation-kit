@review
### Key ideas by week

1. **Intestacy.** Order: spouse, descendants, parents, descendants of parents, grandparents (half to each side), then the state. UPC § 2-102 gives the spouse a share that depends on who else survives; descendants take by representation, and the UPC divides **per capita at each generation**.
2. **Advancements, survival, disqualification.** An advancement counts only with a contemporaneous writing and if the donee survives (§ 2-109); use hotchpot. The 120-hour rule (§ 2-104) treats someone who survives by less than 120 hours as having predeceased. The slayer (§ 2-803) is treated as predeceased.
3. **Execution.** Writing, signature, two witnesses; the UPC allows acknowledgment and signing within a reasonable time; holographs need signature and material portions in handwriting; harmless error (§ 2-503) requires clear and convincing evidence of intent.
4. **Revocation and revival.** Later writing or physical act with intent; divorce revokes gifts to the ex-spouse (§ 2-804); DRR for mistaken revocation; a revoked will is not revived without evidence of intent (§ 2-509).
5. **Capacity, influence, fraud.** Four capacity elements at execution; insane delusion; undue influence presumption (confidential relationship, active procurement, undue benefit); fraud in execution versus inducement; no-contest clauses and probable cause.
6. **Interpretation, lapse, ademption.** Latent ambiguity and reformation (§ 2-805); anti-lapse for grandparents, descendants of grandparents and stepchildren (§ 2-603); lapsed gifts fall to the residue (§ 2-604); specific gifts adeem; abatement order.
7. **Will substitutes.** Insurance, retirement accounts, POD, joint tenancy and revocable trusts pass outside the will; pour-over (§ 2-511); creditors, divorce and ERISA preemption.
8. **Spouse and omitted heirs.** UPC augmented estate: 50% times the marriage-length percentage times the augmented estate, less what the spouse already has (transfers from the decedent at full value; the spouse's own property only at the marriage-length percentage, § 2-209); omitted spouse (§ 2-301) and omitted child (§ 2-302).
9. **Creating trusts.** Capacity, intent, definite beneficiary, duties, no merger (UTC § 402); declaration, transfer or appointment (§ 401).
10. **Charitable and spendthrift.** Cy pres; no Rule Against Perpetuities; spendthrift limits and exceptions; self-settled trusts.
11. **Trustee duties.** Loyalty (no-further-inquiry), prudence, damages as the greater of restoration or profit; Claflin and UTC § 411.
12. **Powers and planning.** General versus special powers; § 2-608; marital deduction, portability and stepped-up basis (with stated assumptions).

### Self-check

- Can you distribute an estate and show that the shares add up?
- Can you state the UPC rule, then the common-law or minority variation?

- Can you explain, without notes, one major idea aloud (for example, why a will substitute needs no Wills Act formalities)?

### Common errors

- Giving the spouse everything (or too little) without checking whether all descendants are the spouse's.
- Applying strict per stirpes where the UPC uses per capita at each generation.
- Treating a lifetime gift as an advancement without a writing, or ignoring the 120-hour rule.
- Letting the will control nonprobate assets, or letting anti-lapse protect friends.
- Forgetting to credit transfers to the spouse and the spouse's own property (at the marriage-length percentage) in the elective-share computation, or crediting the own property at full value.
- Stating a current tax figure from memory instead of using the problem's assumptions.

prompt: Under the UPC, Ora dies intestate and unmarried with an estate of \$450,000. Her children are Abe and Bea, who survive her, and Cy, who died earlier leaving two children. Ora signed a note when she gave Abe \$30,000 stating it was an advancement. Using hotchpot (UPC § 2-109) and per capita at each generation (UPC § 2-106), how much does each of Cy's children take?
answer: 80000
check: (450000 + 30000) / 3 / 2
hint: Add the advancement to the estate to find the notional total.
hint: The children's generation has three shares (Abe, Bea, and Cy's branch). Divide Cy's share between his two children.
solution: Notional total = \$450,000 + \$30,000 = \$480,000; each of three shares is \$160,000. Abe takes \$160,000 - \$30,000 = \$130,000, Bea takes \$160,000, and Cy's \$160,000 is divided between his two children, \$80,000 each. Check: \$130,000 + \$160,000 + \$80,000 + \$80,000 = \$450,000.

@exam mc
prompt: Tom's will leaves \$50,000 to his niece Nia and the residue to his son. Nia survives Tom by 48 hours and then dies, leaving a daughter who survives Tom by more than 120 hours. Tom's will is silent on survival. Under the UPC, who takes the \$50,000?
choice*: Nia's daughter, because Nia is treated as having predeceased Tom (120-hour rule) and anti-lapse substitutes the descendants of a niece.
choice: Nia's estate, because she survived Tom and the gift vested.
choice: Tom's son, because a gift to a niece lapses into the residue when she dies soon after the testator.
choice: Tom's heirs at law, because a lapsed specific gift passes by intestacy.
hint: Combine the survival rule with the anti-lapse class.
solution: Under the UPC's 120-hour requirement, Nia, who did not survive Tom by 120 hours, is treated as having predeceased him. A niece is a descendant of a grandparent, so UPC § 2-603 substitutes her surviving descendants, and her daughter takes by representation.

She wants the children to share equally, a gift to the friend, privacy and incapacity planning. In one paragraph, recommend a plan that uses a revocable trust, a pour-over will and updated designations, and identify two points to review (for example the obsolete IRA designation and risks of undue influence).
solution: I recommend a revocable trust funded with the house and brokerage account, naming the client as trustee and a child as successor trustee, with terms giving a specific gift to the friend and the rest equally to the two children; this avoids probate, keeps the plan private and allows the successor trustee to manage assets if she becomes incapacitated (UTC § 602 makes the trust revocable unless it says otherwise). A pour-over will under UPC § 2-511 catches any asset left outside the trust, and a durable power of attorney and health care directive complete the plan. The IRA names a deceased husband, so a new designation (children equally, perhaps through a trust) is needed because the will does not control it. Two review points: the obsolete IRA designation, and the risk that a child or the friend who assists her could be accused of undue influence, so she should use her own lawyer, meet alone with the lawyer, and keep a record of capacity.
