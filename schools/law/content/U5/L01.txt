@article
When a person dies owning property that no valid will or will substitute controls, a state **intestate succession** statute decides who takes. Every statute is a legislative guess about what the typical decedent would have wanted. This course uses the **Uniform Probate Code (UPC)**, which about eighteen states have adopted in large part; other states differ, especially in dollar amounts and fractions, and each lesson flags the common-law or majority variations. Intestacy reaches only the **intestate estate**: probate property not disposed of by a valid will. Nonprobate assets such as life insurance, survivorship accounts and trusts pass by their own terms (Week 7).

### The order of takers

Heirs are fixed at the moment of death; a living person has no heirs, only hopeful expectants. The UPC works down a list, and the first class with a survivor takes everything not given to the spouse:

1. The **surviving spouse** (§ 2-102), who takes a share that depends on who else survives.
2. **Descendants**, by representation (§ 2-103(a)(1)).
3. **Parents**, equally or the survivor.
4. **Descendants of the parents** (siblings, then nieces and nephews), by representation.
5. **Grandparents and their descendants**: half to the paternal side and half to the maternal side, each half going to the grandparents on that side or, if none survive, to their descendants (aunts, uncles, cousins).
6. If no one qualifies, the property **escheats** to the state (§ 2-105). The 2008 text first lets descendants of a deceased spouse take.

### The spouse's share

The 2008 version of § 2-102 uses these figures (earlier versions and many states use smaller dollar amounts, and many states simply give the spouse a fraction such as one-half or one-third):

| Who survives | Spouse takes |
|---|---|
| No descendant and no parent; or every descendant of the decedent is also the spouse's, and the spouse has no other descendant | the entire estate |
| No descendant, but a parent | first \$300,000 plus three-fourths of the balance |
| Every descendant of the decedent is also the spouse's, but the spouse has another descendant | first \$225,000 plus one-half of the balance |
| At least one descendant of the decedent is not the spouse's | first \$150,000 plus one-half of the balance |

The balance passes to the descendants or parents under § 2-103. The blended-family rows exist because a spouse who keeps everything might not share with the decedent's children from another relationship. *Obergefell v. Hodges* (2015) means a same-sex spouse is a spouse for intestacy. Community property states (such as California and Texas) treat each spouse as already owning half of the community property, so intestacy covers only the decedent's half.

### Division among descendants

Suppose three children, A, B and C, were the decedent's children; A and B are dead, A left two children, B left one, and C survives. There are three systems.

- **Strict per stirpes** (the English, traditional rule): divide into shares at the children's generation, whether or not those children are alive. C takes 1/3, A's children 1/6 each, B's child 1/3.
- **Modified per stirpes** (per capita with representation, the 1969 UPC): divide at the first generation that has a living member. Here it produces the same result as strict per stirpes.
- **Per capita at each generation** (UPC § 2-106 in the 1990 and 2008 texts): each survivor in the closest generation with a survivor takes one share, and the shares of the dead in that generation are pooled and divided equally among their descendants. C takes 1/3; the remaining 2/3 is pooled and divided among the three grandchildren, 2/9 each. Check: $\frac{1}{3} + 3 \times \frac{2}{9} = 1$.

The UPC's aim is equal treatment of equally related heirs: the grandchildren stand at the same distance from the decedent, so each gets the same amount.

### Who counts as a relative

- A child adopted by the decedent is a child, and the adopted child inherits from the adoptive family (§ 2-118); a stepchild never adopted is not an heir.
- Parentage does not depend on marriage (§ 2-117), but a father's relationship to a nonmarital child must be established as the Code provides. *Trimble v. Gordon* (1977) struck down a rule that barred nonmarital children from inheriting from their fathers.
- Half-blood relatives take the same as whole-blood relatives (§ 2-107).
- A child in gestation at death counts if the child lives 120 hours after birth.
- A parent may be barred from inheriting from a child whose parental rights could have been terminated (§ 2-114).

### Applying the rule

Work in order: (1) who survives, and by 120 hours (Lesson 2); (2) which spouse row applies; (3) which class takes the rest; (4) divide by representation; (5) add the shares and confirm they equal the estate.

@example
title: A blended family under UPC § 2-102
problem: Dee dies intestate with an estate of \$600,000. Her husband Hal, her daughter Ana (from an earlier marriage) and her son Ben (the child of Dee and Hal) survive her. Use the 2008 UPC figures. Find each person's share.
step: Which spouse row? Ana is a descendant of Dee who is not Hal's descendant, so § 2-102(4) applies: Hal takes the first \$150,000 plus one-half of the balance.
step: Compute Hal's share: balance = \$600,000 - \$150,000 = \$450,000; half is \$225,000; Hal takes \$150,000 + \$225,000 = \$375,000.
step: The rest, \$600,000 - \$375,000 = \$225,000, passes to Dee's descendants under § 2-103. Ana and Ben are in the same generation, so each takes \$112,500.
step: Check: \$375,000 + \$112,500 + \$112,500 = \$600,000.

@mistakes
- Giving the spouse everything whenever the spouse survives, or only a fixed fraction, without checking whether every descendant is also the spouse's child.
- Treating a stepchild or foster child as an heir, or letting siblings take while a parent survives.
- Applying strict per stirpes when the jurisdiction follows the UPC's per capita at each generation (the shares of grandchildren come out equal, not by branch).
- Forgetting the last step: the shares must add up to the whole estate.
