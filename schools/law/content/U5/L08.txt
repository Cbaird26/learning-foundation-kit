@article
A testator has broad freedom to disinherit, with two main limits. A **surviving spouse** is protected by an **elective share** (or community property), and a child or spouse **omitted by oversight** may take an intestate-type share. (Louisiana's forced heirship for young or disabled children is the exception on children.)

### Systems of spousal protection

- **Common-law (separate property) states** give a surviving spouse a right of election: instead of taking under the will, the spouse may take a statutory fraction (traditionally **one-third** of the probate estate; many states use one-half of the probate estate or a sliding fraction). The traditional probate-only base lets a spouse be defeated by lifetime transfers to a revocable trust; courts adopted tests (*illusory transfer*, *intent to defraud*, and in *Sullivan v. Burkin* (Mass. 1984) the revocable trust reached by the elective share) to prevent this. The **UPC** and several states close the gap with an **augmented estate**.
- **Community property states** (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington and Wisconsin) treat property earned during marriage as owned half by each spouse. Each spouse can dispose by will only of his or her half; a few states also have *quasi-community property* rules for property acquired elsewhere. Dower and curtesy have been abolished in most states.
- **UPC (2008) elective share**: a surviving spouse (domiciled in the state) may elect to take **50 percent of the marital-property portion of the augmented estate** (§ 2-202), with a **supplement** to a minimum amount if the spouse is poor (\$75,000 in the 2008 text).

### The UPC augmented estate

The **augmented estate** (§ 2-203) is the sum of four components:

1. the decedent's **net probate estate**;
2. the decedent's **nonprobate transfers to others** (revocable trusts, POD accounts, joint accounts, certain life insurance and retirement benefits, and property transferred within two years before death beyond the annual gift tax exclusion);
3. the decedent's **nonprobate transfers to the spouse**; and
4. the **surviving spouse's own property** and the spouse's nonprobate transfers to others.

The **marital-property portion** is a percentage of the augmented estate that rises with the **length of the marriage**, from 3 percent for a marriage under one year, by steps, to 100 percent at fifteen years or more. The schedule in the 2008 text is: under 1 year, 3%; 1 year, 6%; 2 years, 12%; 3 years, 18%; 4 years, 24%; 5 years, 30%; 6 years, 36%; 7 years, 42%; 8 years, 48%; 9 years, 54%; 10 years, 60%; 11 years, 68%; 12 years, 76%; 13 years, 84%; 14 years, 92%; 15 years or more, 100%. The idea is a partnership theory: the longer the marriage, the more of the couple's property is treated as marital property.

$$\text{Elective share} = 0.50 \times (\text{percentage for the marriage}) \times (\text{augmented estate}).$$

The amount the spouse must receive from the decedent's side is the elective share **minus** what the spouse already has: what the spouse received from the decedent by will, intestacy or nonprobate transfer (at full value), and the spouse's own property counted only at the marital-property percentage for the length of the marriage (§ 2-209(a)). Those amounts are applied first, so they reduce or eliminate the contribution due from the estate and from nonprobate recipients. The remaining shortfall is satisfied from the probate estate and from nonprobate transferees proportionately.

### Omitted spouse and omitted children

**A spouse married after the will** (UPC § 2-301) takes the intestate share unless (1) the will shows it was made in contemplation of the marriage, (2) the will expresses an intent to be effective despite a later marriage, or (3) the testator provided for the spouse by transfer outside the will, with intent that the transfer be in lieu of a testamentary provision. The spouse's share comes only from property not devised to the testator's children from an earlier relationship (children who are not also the spouse's children), which is protected from the claim.

**A child born or adopted after the will** (UPC § 2-302) takes as follows. If the testator had **no** child living when the will was executed, the omitted child takes an intestate share, unless the will devised substantially all of the estate to the child's other parent, the testator provided for the child outside the will, or the omission was intentional. If the testator had **one or more children** living at execution and the will devised property to one or more of them, the omitted child takes an **equal share of the portion of the estate devised to the then-living children**, as if all had been given equal shares; the share abates ratably from those children's devises. A child whom the testator believed dead, or did not know about, takes an intestate share. Several states use broader or narrower statutes. Intentional omission is never remedied.

### Applying the rule

For the elective share: (1) check domicile and the election; (2) determine the length of marriage and the percentage; (3) total the four components; (4) compute 50% of the percentage times the augmented estate; (5) subtract any transfers from the decedent to the spouse and the spouse's own property times the marital-property percentage to see the shortfall. Always test the answer: the spouse's total after election should be at least the elective share.

@example
title: Computing an elective share
problem: Walt dies after a marriage of exactly 10 years. His net probate estate is \$600,000 and his nonprobate transfers to others are \$200,000. He transferred nothing to his spouse Ivy. Ivy owns \$100,000 in her own right. Use the 2008 UPC schedule (a 10-year marriage gives 60%), a \$75,000 supplemental minimum, and the § 2-209(a)(3) rule that the spouse's own property is credited only at the marital-property percentage. How much must Ivy receive from Walt's estate and nonprobate recipients to receive her elective share?
step: Augmented estate = \$600,000 + \$200,000 + \$0 + \$100,000 = \$900,000.
step: Marital-property portion = 60% x \$900,000 = \$540,000.
step: Elective-share amount = 50% x \$540,000 = \$270,000.
step: Ivy's own property is credited at the marital-property percentage (§ 2-209(a)(3)): 60% x \$100,000 = \$60,000. She is entitled to \$270,000 - \$60,000 = \$210,000 from the decedent's side; the supplemental minimum of \$75,000 does not matter because her total (\$100,000 + \$210,000) is far above it.

@mistakes
- Applying the percentage to the probate estate only and forgetting nonprobate transfers and the spouse's own property.
- Failing to credit the spouse's own property (at the marital-property percentage) and transfers from the decedent against the elective-share amount, or crediting the spouse's own property at full value.
- Giving an omitted child an intestate share in all cases; when the testator had children at execution, the UPC limits the share to devises made to those children.
- Allowing the omitted-child statute to override an intentional omission.
