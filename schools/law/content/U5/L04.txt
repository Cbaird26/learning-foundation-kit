@article
A will is **ambulatory**: it has no effect until death, and the testator may revoke it at any time while competent. The law polices revocation as it polices execution, because the same risks of fraud and mistake appear. Revocation can occur in three ways: by a later will or codicil, by a physical act on the will, or by operation of law.

### Revocation by a writing

A will can be revoked by a later will or codicil that (1) **expressly** revokes the earlier will, or (2) is **inconsistent** with it. Under UPC § 2-507, a later will that does not expressly revoke the earlier one revokes it only to the extent of the inconsistency, unless it completely disposes of the estate, in which case it is presumed to replace the earlier will. The later instrument must itself be validly executed; an unwitnessed note saying "I revoke my will" is not a revocatory writing unless it qualifies as a holograph or is saved by harmless error. A will that revokes by inconsistency gives rise to a **partial revocation**; the earlier will's consistent provisions remain, and the two are read together.

### Revocation by physical act

The testator revokes by burning, tearing, canceling, obliterating or destroying the will or any part of it, if the testator does so with **intent to revoke**, or if another person does it in the testator's conscious presence and at the testator's direction (UPC § 2-507(a)(2)). Under the UPC the burn, tear or cancellation need not touch the words of the will. Some states require that the act touch the words, and some bar **partial revocation** by physical act, because deleting a gift could increase another beneficiary's share without the formalities of a will. The UPC permits partial revocation by physical act, and the deleted gift then falls to the residue or by intestacy.

If a will was last in the testator's possession and cannot be found at death, a rebuttable **presumption of revocation** arises. The proponent may rebut it with evidence, for instance that a person with an interest in destroying the will had access to it. If the will is revoked, a copy cannot be probated unless the presumption is rebutted.

### Revocation by operation of law

Under UPC § 2-804, a **divorce or annulment** revokes any provision in the will in favor of the former spouse, and also provisions in favor of the former spouse's relatives who are not the testator's relatives; those provisions are read as though the former spouse and the relatives had disclaimed or predeceased. Remarriage to the same spouse revives the provisions. Section 2-804 also revokes the former spouse's designation as beneficiary of a revocable trust, life insurance policy or similar nonprobate transfer, but federal law can preempt state revocation-on-divorce for ERISA plans (Lesson 7). A few states revoke a will by later marriage or birth of a child; most protect the new spouse or child in other ways (Lesson 8).

### Dependent relative revocation

**Dependent relative revocation (DRR)** is a doctrine that disregards a revocation that was based on a mistake. If the testator destroys a will in the mistaken belief that a new will (or a different disposition) is valid, and the court concludes that the testator would not have revoked had she known the truth, then the revocation fails and the earlier will is probated, provided that the earlier will comes closer to the testator's presumed intent than intestacy would. The mistake must be the reason for revoking, and many courts apply DRR when the mistake is one of law or fact that appears from the circumstances. It is not available when the testator would have revoked regardless.

### Revival

If a later will revoked an earlier will and the testator then revokes the later will, is the earlier will back? Approaches differ. At common law in England and in some states, the earlier will is **not revived** unless it is re-executed or republished by codicil. Under UPC § 2-509, if the later will *wholly* revoked the earlier will and is then itself revoked by a physical act, the earlier will remains revoked unless it appears from the circumstances of the revocation, or from the testator's declarations, that the testator intended the earlier will to take effect. If the later will only *partly* revoked the earlier will and is revoked, the earlier will is revived unless there is evidence of contrary intent. A test for revival always asks about the testator's intent.

### Policy and application

The rules aim to ensure that revocation reflects an intentional act, not accident, and that the testator's plan is respected. Apply: (1) was there a valid earlier will; (2) was there a revoking writing, act or operation of law; (3) did the testator have the required intent; (4) was the revoking act itself defective or based on a mistake (DRR); (5) if the revoking instrument was revoked, is the earlier will revived?

@example
title: A revoking will that is later destroyed
problem: In 2019 Tess validly executes Will 1, leaving her estate to her niece. In 2022 she validly executes Will 2, which states "I revoke all prior wills" and leaves her estate to a charity. In 2024 she tears Will 2 in half, intending to revoke it. Will 1 is intact in her desk. She dies without any other declaration. Who takes under the UPC?
step: Will 2 expressly revoked Will 1 (§ 2-507(a)(1)), so Will 1 was revoked in 2022.
step: Tearing Will 2 with intent to revoke is a valid revocation by physical act (§ 2-507(a)(2)), so Will 2 is also revoked.
step: Revival: Will 2 wholly revoked Will 1, so under § 2-509(a) Will 1 remains revoked unless the circumstances or Tess's declarations show she intended it to take effect. There is no such evidence.
step: Conclusion: Neither will operates, and Tess's property passes by intestacy. The niece takes only if she is an heir. A re-execution of Will 1 would have been needed.

@mistakes
- Assuming that destroying a revoking will automatically brings the earlier will back to life.
- Applying DRR when the testator would have revoked anyway, or applying it so that the result is further from the testator's plan than intestacy.
- Requiring witnesses for revocation by physical act; the act itself needs no witnesses, only intent.
- Forgetting that divorce revokes provisions for the ex-spouse, and, under the UPC, for the ex-spouse's relatives.
