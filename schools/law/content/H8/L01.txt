@article
Chemistry is a quantitative science, so every result you report is a **measurement**: a number, an uncertainty and a unit. A mass of "5" means nothing; "5.02 g" tells a reader what was measured, in what unit and how finely the balance could read.

### SI units and prefixes

The metric system used in science is the **SI**. The base units you will use most are the meter (m) for length, the kilogram (kg) for mass, the second (s) for time, the kelvin (K) for temperature and the mole (mol) for amount of substance. Prefixes scale a unit by a power of ten: kilo (k) $=10^3$, centi (c) $=10^{-2}$, milli (m) $=10^{-3}$, micro ($\mu$) $=10^{-6}$, nano (n) $=10^{-9}$ and pico (p) $=10^{-12}$. A liter is $1\ \text{dm}^3$, so $1\ \text{L}=1000\ \text{mL}$ and $1\ \text{mL}=1\ \text{cm}^3$.

### Significant figures

The **significant figures** of a measurement are all the digits you are sure of plus one estimated digit. Rules for counting:

- Nonzero digits always count.
- Zeros between nonzero digits count ($1005$ has 4).
- Leading zeros never count ($0.0042$ has 2).
- Trailing zeros count if there is a decimal point ($2.50$ has 3), and are ambiguous without one ($1500$; scientific notation such as $1.50\times10^{3}$ removes the doubt).
- Exact numbers, such as $12$ eggs in a dozen or the $1000$ in $1\ \text{km}=1000\ \text{m}$ (a definition), have unlimited significant figures.

### Calculating with significant figures

**Multiplication and division:** the answer has as many significant figures as the measurement with the fewest.

**Addition and subtraction:** the answer has as many *decimal places* as the measurement with the fewest. Round only at the end, keeping one or two extra digits in intermediate steps.

### Accuracy and precision

**Accuracy** is closeness to the true value; **precision** is the agreement among repeated measurements. A scale that always reads $0.5$ g too high is precise but not accurate.

### Dimensional analysis

Any equality between units gives a **conversion factor** equal to $1$, for example $\dfrac{1000\ \text{m}}{1\ \text{km}}$. Multiply the given quantity by factors arranged so that the unwanted unit cancels:

$$\text{given} \times \frac{\text{wanted}}{\text{given unit}} = \text{wanted}.$$

If the units do not cancel to the unit you want, the setup is wrong, and you find that out before doing any arithmetic. For a rate such as miles per hour, convert the numerator and the denominator in separate factors.

@example
title: Converting a speed
problem: Convert $88.0\ \text{km/h}$ to $\text{m/s}$ and report the answer with the correct number of significant figures.
step: Write the given quantity as a fraction and plan the factors: kilometers must become meters, and hours must become seconds. $\dfrac{88.0\ \text{km}}{1\ \text{h}}$.
step: Multiply by $\dfrac{1000\ \text{m}}{1\ \text{km}}$ so km cancels, and by $\dfrac{1\ \text{h}}{3600\ \text{s}}$ so h cancels: $\dfrac{88.0\ \text{km}}{1\ \text{h}}\times\dfrac{1000\ \text{m}}{1\ \text{km}}\times\dfrac{1\ \text{h}}{3600\ \text{s}}$.
step: Only m and s remain. Compute $\dfrac{88.0\times1000}{3600}=24.44\ldots\ \text{m/s}$.
step: The factors $1000$ and $3600$ are exact, so only $88.0$ limits the result: $3$ significant figures, $24.4\ \text{m/s}$.

@example
title: Adding measurements
problem: Add $12.11\ \text{g}$, $0.023\ \text{g}$ and $3.1\ \text{g}$ and report the sum.
step: Add the numbers: $12.11 + 0.023 + 3.1 = 15.233$.
step: Count decimal places: $12.11$ has two, $0.023$ has three, $3.1$ has one. The least precise value has one decimal place.
step: Round the sum to one decimal place: $15.2\ \text{g}$.

@mistakes
- Treating trailing zeros as insignificant even when a decimal point is present ($2.50$ has three significant figures, not two).
- Rounding at every intermediate step, which piles up rounding error; round once at the end.
- Using the multiplication rule (fewest significant figures) for a sum, or the addition rule (fewest decimal places) for a product.
- Inverting a conversion factor, so the unwanted unit does not cancel.

prompt: How many significant figures are in the measurement $0.004050\ \text{g}$?
answer: 4
check: len('4050')
hint: Leading zeros never count.
hint: After the leading zeros, the digits are $4$, $0$, $5$, $0$. The final zero follows a decimal point.

answer: 2500

tol: 1%
unit: mL

@practice num
prompt: A rectangular card measures $12.5\ \text{cm}$ by $3.2\ \text{cm}$. Report its area in $\text{cm}^2$ with the correct number of significant figures.
answer: 40
check: round(12.5 * 3.2, -1)
tol: 1%
unit: cm^2
hint: The product of the two numbers is $40.0$ before rounding.
hint: $3.2$ has two significant figures and $12.5$ has three. Which limits the answer?
solution: $12.5\times3.2=40.0$. The factor $3.2$ has only two significant figures, so the area is $40\ \text{cm}^2$ (best written $4.0\times10^{1}\ \text{cm}^2$).

@practice num
prompt: A sign reads $65\ \text{mi/h}$. Using $1\ \text{mi}=1609.34\ \text{m}$, express the speed in $\text{m/s}$. Give two significant figures.
answer: 29
check: 65 * 1609.344 / 3600
tol: 1%
unit: m/s
hint: Convert miles to meters in the numerator and hours to seconds in the denominator.
hint: $65\times1609.34\ \text{m}$ per $3600\ \text{s}$.
solution: $65\ \dfrac{\text{mi}}{\text{h}}\times\dfrac{1609.34\ \text{m}}{1\ \text{mi}}\times\dfrac{1\ \text{h}}{3600\ \text{s}}=29.06\ \text{m/s}$, which is $29\ \text{m/s}$ to two significant figures.

@practice mc
prompt: Four students each measure the same $10.00\ \text{g}$ standard mass three times. Student 1 reads $10.01, 10.00, 9.99\ \text{g}$. Student 2 reads $10.52, 10.51, 10.53\ \text{g}$. Student 3 reads $9.60, 10.40, 10.00\ \text{g}$. Student 4 reads $11.0, 9.0, 10.0\ \text{g}$. Which student's data are both accurate and precise?
choice*: Student 1: the readings cluster tightly around the true value.
choice: Student 2: the readings cluster tightly, so they must be accurate.
choice: Student 3: the average equals $10.00\ \text{g}$, so the data are precise.
choice: Student 4: the average equals $10.00\ \text{g}$, so the data are accurate and precise.
hint: Precision is about agreement among repeated readings; accuracy is about closeness to the true value.
hint: A good average from widely scattered readings is accurate on average but not precise.
solution: Only the set that clusters tightly and sits near $10.00\ \text{g}$ is both. The tightly clustered set near $10.5\ \text{g}$ is precise but not accurate, and the two sets with the correct average are scattered, so they are not precise.

@quiz num
prompt: A metal sample has a mass of $24.3\ \text{g}$ and a volume of $9.0\ \text{mL}$. Calculate its density in $\text{g/mL}$ with the correct number of significant figures.
answer: 2.7
check: 24.3 / 9.0
tol: 0.05
unit: g/mL
hint: Density is mass divided by volume.
hint: The volume has two significant figures; use that to round.
solution: $\dfrac{24.3\ \text{g}}{9.0\ \text{mL}}=2.70\ \text{g/mL}$, rounded to two significant figures: $2.7\ \text{g/mL}$.

@quiz num
prompt: A student measures $15.2\ \text{g}$ of one solid and $3.446\ \text{g}$ of another and combines them. Report the total mass in grams with the correct number of decimal places.
answer: 18.6
check: 15.2 + 3.446
tol: 0.05
unit: g
hint: This is an addition, so decimal places, not significant figures, set the precision.
hint: The first mass is known only to tenths of a gram.
solution: $15.2+3.446=18.646$. The least precise value ($15.2$) has one decimal place, so the sum is $18.6\ \text{g}$.
