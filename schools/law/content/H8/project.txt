@project
title: Kitchen titration: how much acetic acid is in vinegar?
overview: You will estimate the acetic acid content of white vinegar, either by a safe kitchen titration with baking soda solution or by analyzing the supplied titration data below, and report the concentration and mass percent with an uncertainty. The project uses measurement and significant figures, the mole, stoichiometry, solution concentration, acid–base chemistry and equilibrium.
goal: Apply a balanced equation and mole ratios to turn a measured volume into a concentration.
goal: Carry significant figures through a multi-step calculation and estimate the uncertainty from repeated trials.
goal: Explain sources of error, including the role of equilibrium in the endpoint.
step: Write the balanced reaction for acetic acid with a base. With baking soda: $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}+\text{NaHCO}_3\rightarrow\text{CH}_3\text{COONa}+\text{H}_2\text{O}+\text{CO}_2$. With sodium hydroxide (the supplied data): $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}+\text{NaOH}\rightarrow\text{CH}_3\text{COONa}+\text{H}_2\text{O}$. Both are $1:1$ in the acid.
step: Kitchen option: make a baking soda solution of known molarity by dissolving $4.20\ \text{g}$ of baking soda ($M=84.01\ \text{g/mol}$) in water and making the volume up to $500\ \text{mL}$ ($0.100\ \text{M}$). Use a kitchen scale that reads to $0.1\ \text{g}$ or better, a measuring syringe or cup marked in mL, and a clear container.
step: Kitchen option: measure $5.0\ \text{mL}$ of white vinegar into a clear cup, add about $45\ \text{mL}$ of water and a few drops of red-cabbage juice indicator (purple-red in acid). Add the baking soda solution a few mL at a time, swirling, until the color turns blue-green, which marks the endpoint. The solution fizzes at first as carbon dioxide forms. Record the total volume added. Repeat for at least three trials.
step: Data option: if you cannot do the experiment, use these supplied data. A $10.00\ \text{mL}$ sample of vinegar was titrated with $0.500\ \text{M}$ NaOH using phenolphthalein; three trials needed $16.9\ \text{mL}$, $16.7\ \text{mL}$ and $16.8\ \text{mL}$. Take the density of vinegar as $1.01\ \text{g/mL}$ and the molar mass of acetic acid as $60.05\ \text{g/mol}$.
step: Calculate the mean titrant volume, the moles of base added (molarity times liters), the moles of acetic acid by the mole ratio, and the vinegar molarity (moles divided by liters of vinegar).
step: Convert to mass percent: grams of acetic acid per liter divided by grams of vinegar per liter, times $100$. Compare to the typical label value of $5\%$.
step: Estimate the uncertainty as half the range of the trials (half of the highest minus the lowest volume), and propagate it as a relative uncertainty to your final answer. Report the result with sensible significant figures, for example $4.99\pm0.15\%$.
step: Write a short report that lists your data, a sample calculation with units, the result with uncertainty, and at least three sources of error and how each would bias the result.
deliverable: A data table with all trials, units and significant figures, and a complete worked calculation.
deliverable: A final result for molarity and mass percent with an uncertainty and a comparison with the label value.
deliverable: A one-paragraph error analysis, including why the baking soda endpoint is imprecise and what you would change.
rubric: Balanced equation and a correct $1:1$ mole ratio are shown.
rubric: Every calculation step has units that cancel, and the final answer has appropriate significant figures.
rubric: Uncertainty is estimated from the trials and stated with the result.
rubric: The error analysis identifies specific sources (endpoint judgment, dissolved carbon dioxide, measurement resolution) and the direction of their effects.
rubric: The result is compared to the label value, with an honest statement of whether they agree within uncertainty.
safety: Use only kitchen materials with an adult present: white vinegar, baking soda, water and red cabbage. Do not taste any of the mixtures, keep vinegar away from your eyes, wipe up spills, and let the solutions go down the drain with plenty of water. Do not use laboratory chemicals at home; the sodium hydroxide data are supplied for analysis only.
tutor: Why does the baking soda titration fizz, and how does that carbon dioxide affect the endpoint?
tutor: How do I estimate the uncertainty in my final answer from three trials?
tutor: If my answer is $4.6\%$ and the label says $5\%$, how can I decide whether the difference is real?

### Week by week

- **Week 1, measurement:** significant figures (count digits; multiply or divide by fewest significant figures; add or subtract by fewest decimal places) and dimensional analysis with factors that cancel units.
- **Week 2, atoms:** $Z$ is the protons, $A=Z+N$, charge $=$ protons $-$ electrons, isotopes differ in neutrons, average atomic mass is a weighted average.
- **Week 3, electrons:** fill subshells in order, apply Hund's rule, cations lose the outer $n$ first; radius falls and ionization energy rises across a period.
- **Week 4, bonding:** $\Delta\text{EN}$ gives ionic, polar or nonpolar; count valence electrons for Lewis structures; VSEPR gives shape.
- **Week 5, naming:** balance charges for formulas, use Roman numerals for variable-charge metals, Greek prefixes for molecular compounds.
- **Week 6, the mole:** grams $\leftrightarrow$ moles through molar mass, moles $\leftrightarrow$ particles through $N_A$.
- **Week 7, reactions:** balance atoms and charge, classify, use solubility rules and spectator ions.
- **Week 8, stoichiometry:** mole ratio, limiting reagent (smallest product amount), percent yield.
- **Week 9, gases:** kelvin only, $PV=nRT$, Dalton's law.
- **Week 10, solutions:** $M=n/V$, $M_1V_1=M_2V_2$, molality, mass percent.
- **Week 11, acids and bases:** $\text{pH}=-\log_{10}[\text{H}^+]$, $\text{pH}+\text{pOH}=14$, $K_a$, buffers.
- **Week 12, rates and equilibrium:** rate laws, $K$, $Q$ and Le Chatelier.

### Self-check

- Can I find a limiting reagent without guessing?

### Common errors

- Using Celsius in a gas law, mL instead of L in molarity, or grams instead of moles in a ratio.
- Changing subscripts instead of coefficients when balancing.
- Comparing masses to find a limiting reagent.
- Dropping the negative sign or the pOH step when computing pH.
- Rounding in the middle of a calculation.

@exam num
prompt: A $10.00\ \text{mL}$ sample of vinegar is titrated with $0.500\ \text{M}$ NaOH, and three trials need $16.9\ \text{mL}$, $16.7\ \text{mL}$ and $16.8\ \text{mL}$ of base. Acetic acid reacts with NaOH in a $1:1$ ratio ($M=60.05\ \text{g/mol}$) and the vinegar has density $1.01\ \text{g/mL}$. What is the mass percent of acetic acid in the vinegar, to two decimal places?
answer: 4.99
check: (16.9 + 16.7 + 16.8) / 3 * 0.500 / 10.00 * 60.05 / (1.01 * 1000) * 100
tol: 0.05
unit: %
hint: Average the three volumes first, then find moles of NaOH from molarity times volume in liters.
hint: Moles of acetic acid equal moles of NaOH; divide by $0.01000\ \text{L}$ to get the molarity of the vinegar.
hint: Multiply molarity by $60.05$ to get grams per liter, then divide by the mass of one liter of vinegar ($1010\ \text{g}$) and multiply by $100$.
solution: The mean volume is $16.8\ \text{mL}$, giving $0.500\times0.0168=8.40\times10^{-3}\ \text{mol}$ of base and of acetic acid. The vinegar molarity is $8.40\times10^{-3}/0.01000=0.840\ \text{M}$. Mass concentration is $0.840\times60.05=50.4\ \text{g/L}$. One liter of vinegar has mass $1010\ \text{g}$, so the mass percent is $\dfrac{50.4}{1010}\times100=4.99\%$.

prompt: A student pours $25.0\ \text{mL}$ of $0.840\ \text{M}$ acetic acid onto an excess of baking soda: $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}+\text{NaHCO}_3\rightarrow\text{CH}_3\text{COONa}+\text{H}_2\text{O}+\text{CO}_2$. What volume of $\text{CO}_2$ forms if it is collected at $25\,^\circ\text{C}$ and $1.00\ \text{atm}$ and behaves ideally? Use $R=0.08206\ \text{L atm mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$ and give the answer in mL.
answer: 514
check: 0.0250 * 0.840 * 0.08206 * (25 + 273.15) * 1000
tol: 1%
unit: mL
hint: Find moles of acetic acid from molarity times volume in liters; baking soda is in excess.
hint: The mole ratio of acetic acid to carbon dioxide is $1:1$. Convert the temperature to kelvin.
hint: Use $V=\dfrac{nRT}{P}$ and convert liters to milliliters.
solution: $n=(0.840)(0.0250)=0.0210\ \text{mol}$ acetic acid, so $0.0210\ \text{mol CO}_2$. $T=298.15\ \text{K}$. $V=\dfrac{(0.0210)(0.08206)(298.15)}{1.00}=0.514\ \text{L}=514\ \text{mL}$.

@exam self
prompt: In the kitchen titration with baking soda, the cabbage-indicator endpoint is blurry, and the mixture fizzes. Explain at least three sources of error, state whether each makes the calculated acetic acid content too high or too low, and describe one change to the procedure that would improve the result. Use the ideas of equilibrium and Le Chatelier's principle in at least one explanation.
hint: Think about the indicator, the carbon dioxide produced, and how you read the volumes.
hint: Carbon dioxide dissolves in water to form a weak acid in an equilibrium that responds to its escape.
solution: Source 1: the indicator changes color gradually (the baking soda solution is only weakly basic, so the pH near the endpoint changes slowly), so the endpoint is hard to judge. If you stop early, too little base is recorded and the acid content comes out too low; if you overshoot, it comes out too high. Source 2: dissolved carbon dioxide forms carbonic acid, $\text{CO}_2+\text{H}_2\text{O}\rightleftharpoons\text{H}_2\text{CO}_3$, and this weak acid keeps the solution slightly acidic near the endpoint. By Le Chatelier's principle, as $\text{CO}_2$ escapes the equilibrium shifts toward gas, which slowly raises the pH, so the color drifts after you stop; stopping at the first color change tends to give a too-low volume and a too-low result. Swirling gently or letting the cup stand and releasing the gas before judging the color reduces this. Source 3: measurement resolution, such as reading a syringe to $\pm0.5\ \text{mL}$ or an imprecise baking soda mass, gives random error in either direction. Improvement: repeat three or more trials, add the last portions drop by drop, and compare to a color reference cup, or use a more precise instrument.
rubric: Identifies at least three distinct, plausible sources of error.
rubric: States the direction of bias (too high or too low) for each, with a reason.
rubric: Uses equilibrium or Le Chatelier's principle correctly, such as dissolved carbon dioxide and carbonic acid.
rubric: Proposes a specific, realistic change to improve the procedure.
