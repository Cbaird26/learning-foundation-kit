@article
A client interview has three jobs at once: **gather facts**, **learn what the client wants**, and **build a working relationship**. A lawyer who collects only facts leaves the client feeling processed; a lawyer who only listens may miss the legal elements. The NextGen bar exam treats client counseling as a foundational skill, so expect fact patterns that ask what a lawyer should say, ask or do next. (All clients and files in this course are hypothetical.)

### Before the meeting

Prepare. Run a **conflict check** (Model Rule 1.7) before the client discloses much. Identify the likely areas of law and the limitation periods that might apply, so that your questions are theory-driven rather than random. Decide what documents the client should bring. Arrange a private room: privilege depends on confidentiality.

### The stages

1. **Opening.** Greet, explain your role, explain confidentiality, and say what this meeting is and is not. A person who consults a lawyer about possible representation is a **prospective client** under Model Rule 1.18. Even if you decline the matter, you owe duties of confidentiality, and you may be barred from later representing someone materially adverse in the same or a substantially related matter if you received information that could be significantly harmful to the prospective client. The firm can avoid imputation only if the lawyer limited exposure to disqualifying information, is screened, receives no part of the fee, and written notice goes to the prospective client (Rule 1.18(d)(2)), unless both sides give informed consent.
2. **Client narrative.** Ask a broad open question ("Tell me what brought you here") and let the client talk without interruption. Use **active listening**: nods, short encouragers, paraphrase, and reflection of feeling.
3. **Chronological and theory-driven follow-up.** Return to the story in time order and probe for detail, using the **funnel**: begin with open questions ("What happened next?"), narrow to probing questions ("What exactly did the supervisor say?"), and finish with closed questions ("Was the letter dated before or after March 1?"). Leading questions ("You never missed a payment, right?") are efficient but suggest answers, so use them to confirm, not to discover.
4. **Closing.** Summarize, state what happens next, identify deadlines and documents, address fees, and ask "What have I not asked that I should have?"

### Fees and scope

Rule 1.5(b) requires the lawyer to communicate the scope of the representation and the basis or rate of the fee, preferably in writing, before or within a reasonable time after starting. A contingent fee must be in a writing signed by the client (Rule 1.5(c)). Rule 1.2(c) allows a limited-scope representation if reasonable and the client gives informed consent.

### Privilege and the room

The attorney-client privilege protects confidential communications between client and lawyer made to obtain legal advice. A third person whose presence is not reasonably necessary to the communication ordinarily destroys confidentiality; an interpreter or an assistant working for the lawyer usually does not. Confidentiality under Rule 1.6 is broader than privilege: it covers information relating to the representation, whatever its source.

### Difficult interviews

Clients omit bad facts out of shame or fear. Explain that you can only protect them from facts you know ("I would rather hear it from you than from the other side"). Ask about the worst fact directly and without judgment. Watch for client goals that differ from the legal claim: a client who says "I want to sue" may want an apology or a job back.

### Applying the method

Frame each step as IRAC for the interview itself: the **issue** (what do I need to learn?), the **rule** (which elements and which professional-responsibility rules?), the **application** (which questions, in what order?), and the **conclusion** (what will I tell the client we do next?).
@example
title: Planning the funnel for a hypothetical eviction consultation
problem: (Hypothetical.) Rosa, a tenant, tells you her landlord served a notice ending her month-to-month tenancy. You have 45 minutes. Outline the interview.
step: Opening (5 minutes): run the conflict check beforehand, explain confidentiality and that no fee is owed for this consultation, and note that a deadline may be running for her response to the notice.
step: Narrative (10 minutes): ask "Tell me what has happened with your apartment," and paraphrase back without interrupting. Listen for the date of the notice and any reason the landlord gave.
step: Follow-up (20 minutes): funnel from open ("What happened after you received it?") to probing ("What does the notice say, word for word?") to closed ("Is your rent current through this month?"). Ask for the lease and the notice.
step: Closing (10 minutes): summarize, state the next step (review of documents and the response deadline), explain the fee basis in writing, and ask what you have not asked that you should have.
@mistakes
- Treating a prospective client's disclosures as unprotected because no engagement letter has been signed; Rule 1.18 and privilege can both apply.
- Starting with closed or leading questions, which narrows the story to what the lawyer already expects.
- Letting a friend or relative sit in "for support" without considering the effect on privilege.
- Agreeing to a contingent fee orally; Rule 1.5(c) requires a signed writing.
