Preparation tells you what you want; tactics are how you pursue it, and ethics tells you where the lines are. (All clients and files here are hypothetical.)

### Common tactics

- **Anchoring and reasoned opening:** open with a number you can justify using objective criteria. An opening you cannot justify costs credibility.
- **Concession pattern:** make smaller and slower concessions as you near your target and ask for something in return ("If we move on price, can you move on timing?"). A final big jump signals that you have more room.
- **Packaging and logrolling:** bundle issues so each side gives on what it values least and gains on what it values most.
- **Contingent agreements:** when parties disagree on a prediction (the chance the claim succeeds, future sales), a deal can depend on what happens: a payment if a condition occurs.
- **Questions and listening:** ask why; you learn interests and the other side's reservation point.
- **Deadlines and walk-away:** a credible BATNA lets you decline without bluffing.

Hardball moves such as extreme demands, "good cop, bad cop," or threats can work against a counterparty who has no alternative, but they damage trust, invite retaliation, and may cross professional limits.

### What a lawyer may and may not say

**Model Rule 4.1(a)** forbids knowingly making a false statement of material fact or law to a third person. Rule 4.1(b) forbids failing to disclose a material fact when disclosure is necessary to avoid assisting a client's criminal or fraudulent act, unless Rule 1.6 prohibits disclosure. The comment explains that, under generally accepted conventions, some statements are ordinarily **not** regarded as statements of material fact: **estimates of price or value** of the subject of a transaction, and **a party's intentions as to an acceptable settlement** of a claim. So "my client will not take less than \$400,000" is a bargaining statement, not a representation of fact, while "my client has a competing offer for \$500,000" when none exists is a false statement of fact. Some states are stricter, and Rule 8.4(c) (dishonesty, fraud, deceit) adds an independent limit. A lawyer need not volunteer weaknesses, but may not lie about facts or law, or say affirmatively that a material fact exists when it does not.

Other rules that bear on negotiation:

- **Rule 1.2(a) and 1.4:** the client decides whether to settle, and offers must be communicated promptly. The lawyer may not exceed authority.
- **Rule 1.8(g):** a lawyer who represents two or more clients may not take part in an **aggregate settlement** unless each client gives informed consent in a writing signed by the client, after disclosure of the existence and nature of all the claims and of each person's participation.
- **Rule 5.6(b):** a lawyer may not participate in offering or making an agreement that restricts the lawyer's right to practice as part of settling a controversy, such as promising not to represent other claimants against the defendant.
- **Rule 4.2:** do not negotiate directly with a represented person without that person's lawyer's consent; the parties themselves may talk to each other.
- **Rule 4.4(a):** do not use means with no substantial purpose other than to embarrass, delay or burden a third person.
- **FRE 408** protects offers and statements in compromise negotiations from use to prove or disprove the validity or amount of a disputed claim; it does not bar use for another purpose such as proving bias.

### Offers of judgment

FRCP 68 lets a party defending a claim serve an offer of judgment at least 14 days before the trial date. The opposing party has 14 days after service to serve written notice of acceptance. If the offer is not accepted and the judgment finally obtained is not more favorable than the offer, the offeree must pay the costs incurred after the offer was made. Days are counted under FRCP 6(a): exclude the day of the event that triggers the period and count every day after it.

### The negotiation plan

A written plan (the deliverable for this block) states: the client's interests ranked; the issues; the BATNA with its computation; the **reservation point** and the reasons for it; the **target**; the opening offer and its justification; the concession plan; the other side's likely interests and BATNA; objective criteria; the client's authority; and ethical constraints (what you will not say).

### Applying the method

For an ethics question, classify the statement (fact, law, opinion/value, or intention), apply Rule 4.1 and the comment, then check Rules 8.4(c), 1.2(a) and 1.4 for authority and communication.
@example
title: Classifying negotiation statements under Rule 4.1
problem: (Hypothetical.) In a settlement talk a lawyer says: (a) "My client's claim is worth at least \$1 million." (b) "My client's lowest figure is \$600,000." (c) "Our expert has concluded the brakes failed," when no expert has so concluded. Which are problematic?
step: Statement (a) is an opinion about value; the Model Rule 4.1 comment treats estimates of value as ordinarily not material facts.
step: Statement (b) concerns a party's intentions as to an acceptable settlement, which the comment also treats as ordinarily not a statement of fact (even though some states are stricter).
step: Statement (c) asserts a fact, the existence of an expert conclusion, that is false and material. It violates Rule 4.1(a), and Rule 8.4(c).
step: Conclusion: only (c) is a clear violation. A safer version is "We expect our expert to testify the brakes failed" only if that is true.
@mistakes
- Treating every bargaining statement as protected "puffery"; false statements about facts or law are prohibited.
- Believing a lawyer may exceed the client's authority because the lawyer thinks it is best.
- Joining an aggregate settlement without each client's signed informed consent.
- Agreeing to a settlement term that bars the lawyer from representing other claimants.
