@article
Most legal disputes end by agreement, not judgment, so negotiation is the lawyer's most-used trial skill. The NextGen bar exam treats negotiation and dispute resolution as a foundational skill, testing preparation, strategy and ethical limits. (All clients and files here are hypothetical.)

### Two kinds of bargaining

**Distributive** (competitive, "fixed pie") bargaining divides one resource, like the price of a car: what one side gains the other loses. **Integrative** (problem-solving, "expanding the pie") bargaining looks for trades that give each side more of what it values most, because the parties value issues differently. Most legal negotiations contain both: parties may trade on timing, confidentiality, references, payment structure and apologies, and then fight over the money.

### Positions and interests

A **position** is what a party says it wants ("I want \$50,000"). An **interest** is the need or concern behind it ("I need to cover my medical bills and be done with this before my baby is born"). Fisher and Ury's principled negotiation urges four moves: separate the people from the problem; focus on interests, not positions; invent options for mutual gain; and insist on objective criteria (market value, a published index, precedent, expert appraisal). Interests reveal trades: a defendant that cares about reputation may pay more for confidentiality, while a plaintiff that cares about speed may accept less money now.

### The numbers that frame a deal

- **BATNA** (best alternative to a negotiated agreement): what the client will do if no deal is reached, measured as concretely as possible (expected value of trial, other buyers, other jobs), net of costs and discounted for delay and risk. Improve it before negotiating.
- **Reservation point** (walk-away point): the least favorable deal the client should accept. It is set by the BATNA, adjusted for the value of non-money terms, risk tolerance and transaction costs. Accepting a deal worse than the BATNA makes the client worse off than walking away.
- **Target** (aspiration): the realistic best outcome you will work toward.
- **ZOPA** (zone of possible agreement): the overlap between the parties' reservation points. In a price negotiation, if the buyer's maximum $B$ is at least the seller's minimum $S$, the zone runs from $S$ to $B$ and has width

$$B - S.$$

If $B < S$ there is no ZOPA on price alone, and a deal requires changing the alternatives or adding issues. You never know the other side's reservation point for sure: estimate it, and learn by listening.

### First offers and anchors

Numbers stated early act as **anchors** that pull later numbers toward them. A first offer that is aggressive but justified by objective criteria, and supported by reasons, tends to improve the final result. A wildly unjustified offer harms credibility and may end the discussion. Concessions should shrink as you approach your target and be tied to reciprocal movement.

### Authority and client control

The client decides whether to settle and for how much (Model Rule 1.2(a)), so a negotiation plan must be approved by the client, who sets the reservation point and gives the lawyer authority to reach it. Offers must be conveyed promptly (Rule 1.4). Settlement discussions are generally inadmissible to prove or disprove the validity or amount of a disputed claim under FRE 408, but may be used for another purpose, such as showing a witness's bias.

### A preparation checklist

1. Facts and law: strengths, weaknesses, likely range of outcomes.
2. Your client's interests, ranked, and the other side's likely interests.
3. Issues and trades (money, timing, confidentiality, non-monetary terms).
4. BATNA for each side, and how to improve yours.
5. Reservation point, target and opening, with reasons.
6. Objective criteria to justify your numbers.
7. Process: who attends, where, who speaks, how offers are documented, and what authority you have.

### Applying the method

State the problem (what is to be decided), the framework (interests, BATNA, ZOPA), the application (numbers and trades) and the recommendation (reservation point and plan).

title: Finding the zone of possible agreement
problem: (Hypothetical.) Ana will sell her delivery van to Ben. Ana's next best option is to sell to a dealer for \$14,000 net. Ben's next best option is to buy a comparable van for \$17,500. Is there a ZOPA, and how wide is it?
step: Identify the reservation points on price: Ana will not sell below \$14,000 (her BATNA), and Ben will not pay above \$17,500 (his BATNA).
step: Compare: Ben's maximum of \$17,500 exceeds Ana's minimum of \$14,000, so a ZOPA exists.
step: Compute its width: $17{,}500 - 14{,}000 = 3{,}500$.
step: Interpret: any price from \$14,000 to \$17,500 beats both parties' alternatives, so bargaining concerns how to divide the \$3,500 surplus, and objective criteria such as a published valuation guide may help.
@mistakes
- Confusing a position with an interest and never asking why.
- Setting a reservation point without reference to the BATNA, so the client accepts a deal worse than walking away.
- Ignoring costs and delay when valuing the alternative.
- Negotiating without the client's authority, or deciding for the client what to accept.
