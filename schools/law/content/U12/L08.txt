@article
A trial is a story told under rules. Your **case theory** is the combination of the **legal theory** (the claim or defense and its elements) and the **factual story** that makes the jury want to rule for your client. A **theme** is the short phrase that carries the story ("He knew, and he said nothing"). (All clients and files here are hypothetical.)

### What a good case theory does

1. **Fits the law:** it addresses every element of each claim or defense you must prove, or the element the other side must prove and cannot.
2. **Fits the evidence:** every important fact is supported by testimony or exhibits you can get admitted (relevance under FRE 401 and 402, subject to exclusion under FRE 403 when probative value is substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice, confusion, delay or waste of time).
3. **Is simple and credible:** the jury must be able to repeat it. It accounts for the bad facts instead of hiding them.
4. **Answers the other side's theory:** anticipate their story, and decide which facts you will concede and which you will contest.
5. **Is consistent with common sense and motive:** people act for reasons.

### The evidence matrix

A table lists each element down the side and, across the top, the witness or exhibit that proves it, the foundation required, the anticipated objection, and the cross-examination risk. It reveals missing proof early and lets you decide which witnesses to call, in what order, and which exhibits to offer.

### Burdens and standards

The **burden of production** is the duty to produce enough evidence to get an issue to the factfinder. The **burden of persuasion** is the duty to convince. In most civil cases the plaintiff must prove each element by a **preponderance of the evidence** (more likely than not). Fraud is often tested at "clear and convincing" in many states. In a criminal case the prosecution must prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt (*In re Winship* (1970)). Summary judgment under FRCP 56(a) is proper if there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. In a jury trial, a party may move for **judgment as a matter of law** under FRCP 50(a) before the case is submitted to the jury, if a reasonable jury would not have a legally sufficient evidentiary basis to find for the other party; a renewed motion under Rule 50(b) after verdict requires the earlier motion.

### Jury selection and pretrial rulings

- **Voir dire** (FRCP 47(a) lets the court examine prospective jurors or let the lawyers do it). Challenges for cause have no limit; **peremptory challenges** are limited, and in a federal civil case each side has three (28 U.S.C. § 1870).
- *Batson v. Kentucky* (1986) bars race-based peremptory strikes in a criminal case. The challenger makes a prima facie showing; the striking party gives a race-neutral explanation; the court decides whether the challenger has shown purposeful discrimination. The rule extends to civil cases (*Edmonson v. Leesville Concrete Co.* (1991)) and to defense strikes (*Georgia v. McCollum* (1992)); *J.E.B. v. Alabama ex rel. T.B.* (1994) applies equal protection to sex-based strikes.
- A federal civil jury has at least 6 and no more than 12 members, and unless the parties stipulate otherwise the verdict must be unanimous (FRCP 48).
- **Motions in limine** ask the court to rule before trial on admissibility. Under FRE 103(b), once the court rules definitively on the record, either before or at trial, a party need not renew an objection or offer of proof to preserve a claim of error for appeal.

### Settlement and theory

The strength of your theory sets your settlement range. A clear theory with strong admissible evidence raises your BATNA. Outline a settlement agreement with the structure from Week 7 (parties, consideration, release, no admission, confidentiality, dismissal, default and enforcement, boilerplate) and tailor each term to the client's interests and the theory (for example, a plaintiff's theme of "he never told us" may justify a demand for a statement of corrective action).

### Applying the method

For each case, write: the elements, the theme in one sentence, the evidence for each element, the bad facts and how you handle them, and the opponent's best theory and your answer.

title: Writing a theory and theme
problem: (Hypothetical.) A supplier sued a retailer for a \$60,000 unpaid shipment. The retailer says the goods were defective. You represent the supplier. Write a theory and theme.
step: Elements: a contract, delivery, acceptance (or failure to reject within a reasonable time), and non-payment. The retailer's defense is breach of warranty by defective goods.
step: Story: the retailer ordered the goods, took delivery on June 2, used or resold them for two months, and complained only after the invoice was overdue.
step: Theme: "They kept the goods, and they kept the money."
step: Evidence matrix: purchase order and invoice (contract, delivery), email thread (no complaint until August), sales records (resale), accounts-payable manager (non-payment), and a cross-examination plan for the retailer's quality manager on timing.
@mistakes
- Building a theory around facts that may be excluded or that no admissible witness can prove.
- Confusing a theme (a story phrase) with the legal theory (the elements).
- Ignoring the burden: forgetting which party must prove which element.
- Hiding bad facts instead of addressing them before the other side does.
