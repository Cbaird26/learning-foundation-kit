@article
Counseling begins after investigation. The client asks, in effect, "What should I do?" The lawyer's task is to supply the legal knowledge and the analysis that let the client decide well. (All clients and files here are hypothetical.)

### Who decides what

Model Rule 1.2(a) divides authority. The **client** decides the **objectives** of the representation and whether to settle a matter. In a criminal case the client also decides, after consulting the lawyer, what plea to enter, whether to waive a jury trial, and whether to testify. The lawyer must **consult** the client about the **means** of pursuing the objectives, and may take action impliedly authorized to carry out the representation. Decisions about tactics (which witnesses, which motions, which objections) ordinarily belong to the lawyer after consultation, though a lawyer should defer to a client's reasonable preferences.

The Supreme Court has applied this allocation. In *Jones v. Barnes* (1983) it held that appointed appellate counsel need not raise every nonfrivolous issue the client wants raised. In *McCoy v. Louisiana* (2018) it held that the Sixth Amendment gives a defendant the right to insist that counsel not concede guilt, even when counsel believes conceding is the best strategy, because the objective of maintaining innocence is the defendant's to decide.

### Communication and candid advice

Rule 1.4(b) requires the lawyer to explain a matter to the extent reasonably necessary to permit the client to make informed decisions. Rule 1.4(a)(1) requires prompt notice of any decision requiring informed consent. A settlement offer must be promptly communicated: a lawyer may not decide for the client that the client would refuse. Rule 2.1 requires the lawyer to exercise independent professional judgment and give candid advice, and allows reference to moral, economic, social and political considerations relevant to the client's situation.

### Limits on assistance

Rule 1.2(d) bars counseling or assisting a client in conduct the lawyer knows is criminal or fraudulent, but allows discussing the legal consequences of any proposed conduct and helping the client understand the law. Rule 1.4(a)(5) requires telling the client when the client expects assistance the Rules do not permit. If the client persists, Rule 1.16(a)(1) requires withdrawal when continuing would violate the Rules. Rule 1.14 governs clients with diminished capacity: maintain a normal relationship as far as reasonably possible, and take protective action only when reasonably necessary.

### A client-centered method

1. **Clarify objectives**, including non-legal ones (privacy, relationships, speed, reputation).
2. **Generate options**, including doing nothing, negotiating, litigating and alternative dispute resolution.
3. **Analyze consequences** of each: legal results, costs, time, probabilities, and effects on other interests.
4. **Compare** the options against the client's objectives and risk tolerance.
5. **Decide.** The client chooses. Offer a recommendation when asked or when needed for informed choice, but do not manipulate through selective facts or pressure.

### Putting numbers on risk

A rough **expected value** (EV) helps compare settlement with trial. For a plaintiff,

$$EV_{\text{trial}} = p \times V - C,$$

where $p$ is the probability of winning, $V$ the expected recovery if the plaintiff wins, and $C$ the cost of litigating. For a defendant, expected cost is $p \times J + C$ where $J$ is the judgment if the plaintiff wins. These numbers are estimates: say so, test them with ranges, and remember that a risk-averse client may rationally accept less than EV. Add timing (money now versus later), appeal risk, and collectability.

title: Comparing a settlement offer with trial
problem: (Hypothetical.) Maya's claim would be worth \$100,000 if she wins at trial. You estimate a 50% chance of winning, and it will cost \$20,000 to try the case. The defendant offers \$35,000. How should you counsel her?
step: Compute expected trial value: $0.5 \times 100{,}000 - 20{,}000 = 30{,}000$.
step: Compare: the \$35,000 offer exceeds the \$30,000 expected net, so settlement is attractive on these numbers.
step: Test the estimate: if the chance of winning is 60%, EV is \$40,000, so the answer is sensitive. Discuss time, stress, and Maya's tolerance for risk.
step: Communicate the offer promptly, give your recommendation if she wants it, and let Maya decide whether to accept, counter or try the case.
@mistakes
- Deciding on the client's behalf to reject an offer and not mentioning it; Rule 1.4 and Rule 1.2(a) require communicating it.
- Presenting a single option as if it were the only one, which defeats informed consent.
- Confusing means (the lawyer's domain after consultation) with objectives (the client's).
- Treating EV as the answer and ignoring the client's risk tolerance and non-legal goals.
