@article
Closing is the one time you may argue. It connects the evidence to the law and tells the jury what to decide and why. (All clients and files here are hypothetical.)

### What closing may contain

You may argue **reasonable inferences from the evidence admitted**, the credibility of witnesses by reference to the testimony, and the law as the court has instructed or will instruct. Model Rule 3.4(e) prohibits alluding to matters not supported by admissible evidence, asserting personal knowledge of facts in issue, and stating a personal opinion about the justness of a cause, witness credibility, or the guilt or innocence of an accused. The jury hears the **instructions** from the court, usually after closings; in federal civil cases, parties must request instructions in writing at the close of the evidence or at an earlier reasonable time the court sets (FRCP 51(a)), and objections must be made on the record when the court gives the opportunity, before the instructions and closings are delivered, or promptly after learning of an instruction (Rule 51(b)(2) and (c)(2)). Argue the law only as it will be instructed.

### Structure

1. **Theme and promise kept:** return to your opening theme and the evidence that delivered what you promised.
2. **The burden and the elements:** tell the jury what must be proved (preponderance, or beyond a reasonable doubt in a criminal case) and walk through each element with the evidence ("You heard Ms. Ortiz say...; Exhibit 5 shows...").
3. **Credibility:** explain why your witnesses are believable (perception, consistency, no motive) and why the other side's are not (bias, inconsistency, impossibility).
4. **Face the weaknesses:** address the best point against you and respond.
5. **The verdict form:** show how to answer each question and what to put in each blank.
6. **Remedy and ask:** state exactly what you ask the jury to do, and in a civil case justify the damages.

Use record cites in your notes and, with the court's permission, illustrative aids under FRE 107 (timelines, charts); a summary offered as evidence belongs under FRE 1006 and needs to be in evidence.

### Limits and objections

Improper argument includes: **misstating the evidence** or arguing facts not in evidence; **personal opinion** or vouching (Rule 3.4(e)); **appeals to passion or prejudice**; the **golden rule** argument (asking jurors to put themselves in a party's place), which most courts reject at least on liability and often on damages; and, in a criminal case, **comment on the defendant's silence**, which violates the Fifth Amendment (*Griffin v. California* (1965)). Prosecutors have a special duty to seek justice rather than only to win (*Berger v. United States* (1935)); burden-shifting argument and vouching for witnesses are common grounds for reversal. Object promptly and specifically (FRE 103 principles), ask for a curative instruction, and move for a mistrial if warranted. Failure to object may waive the issue, subject to plain-error review. In the usual order, the party with the burden argues first and may reserve time for rebuttal, limited to responding to the opponent's closing; the order varies by jurisdiction and criminal practice.

### Damages argument

Break damages into categories (economic: medical expenses, lost earnings; non-economic: pain and suffering) and show the arithmetic. Some courts permit counsel to suggest a number for non-economic damages, some restrict the practice, and some permit a per diem method; check local rules. Under **pure comparative fault** (the rule in a minority of states, with many states using modified comparative fault bars at 50% or 51%), the plaintiff's damages are reduced by the plaintiff's percentage of fault. Under modified comparative fault, a plaintiff at or above the bar recovers nothing. For example,

$$\text{Recovery} = (\text{economic} + \text{non-economic damages}) \times (1 - \text{plaintiff's fault share}).$$

### Applying the method

Write the closing as a list of headings, each a single sentence conclusion (for example, "Delta's driver did not look"), supported by two or three record cites, with the instruction and verdict-form question each heading answers.
@example
title: A damages argument with comparative fault
problem: (Hypothetical.) The jury finds lost wages of \$30,000 (six months at \$5,000), medical bills of \$12,000, and sets the plaintiff's fault at 10% under pure comparative fault. What is the plaintiff's recovery?
step: Add the damages: $30{,}000 + 12{,}000 = 42{,}000$.
step: Compute the plaintiff's share of fault: $10\% \times 42{,}000 = 4{,}200$.
step: Subtract: $42{,}000 - 4{,}200 = 37{,}800$.
step: In closing, argue the full damages figure and the fault question separately, because in many courts the judge makes the reduction and jurors are not told the effect of their fault finding; follow local practice.
@mistakes
- Arguing facts that were never admitted, or misstating a witness's testimony.
- Stating "I believe" or "I know" about credibility or the justness of the case.
- Using a golden rule argument.
- Forgetting the burden and the instructions, or failing to walk through the verdict form.
