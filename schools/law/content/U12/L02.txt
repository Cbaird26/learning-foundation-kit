@article
Facts win and lose cases, and you do not get them by waiting. Fact investigation is the planned search for **legally significant facts**: facts that tend to prove or disprove an element of a claim or defense. The NextGen bar exam treats investigation and analysis as a foundational skill, so expect fact patterns asking what to find, from whom, and what you may do to get it. (All clients and files here are hypothetical.)

### Start from the elements

List the likely claims and defenses, then the elements of each. For every element, sort the facts into **known**, **unknown** and **disputed**, and name a **source** for each unknown. A simple chart with columns for element, known facts, missing facts, source and priority keeps the plan honest. Add a **chronology**: in a dated timeline, gaps and contradictions show up quickly.

Rule 11(b)(3) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure requires, when you sign a pleading, that factual contentions have evidentiary support or, if specifically so identified, will likely have it after a reasonable opportunity for further investigation. The rule requires an inquiry reasonable under the circumstances before filing, so investigation comes first.

### Sources of facts

- **Testimonial:** the client, eyewitnesses, experts, and the adverse party's people.
- **Documentary and electronic:** contracts, emails, text messages, medical and business records, public records, and metadata.
- **Physical and demonstrative:** the object, the site, photographs, video.
- **Public information:** court filings, property and corporate records, and public social media content.

Informal investigation is cheap, fast, and does not alert the other side. Formal discovery (depositions under Rule 30, interrogatories under Rule 33, document requests under Rule 34, requests for admission under Rule 36, and subpoenas under Rule 45) is compelled and sworn. Scope under Rule 26(b)(1) is nonprivileged matter relevant to a claim or defense and proportional to the needs of the case. Key limits: 25 interrogatories including discrete subparts; 10 depositions per side; one day of 7 hours per deponent, unless the parties stipulate or the court orders otherwise. Effective December 1, 2025, amendments to Rules 16(b) and 26(f) require the parties to address how they will describe privileged material that is withheld, so plan privilege-log practice at the start.

### Preserve first

A duty to preserve evidence arises when litigation is reasonably anticipated. Send the client a **litigation hold** and, where appropriate, a preservation letter to the other side. For lost electronically stored information, Rule 37(e) allows measures no greater than necessary to cure prejudice, and the harshest sanctions (adverse-inference instruction, default or dismissal) only on a finding that the party acted with intent to deprive.

### Protected material

The **attorney-client privilege** protects confidential lawyer-client communications for legal advice; *Upjohn Co. v. United States* (1981) extends it to communications by corporate employees to counsel at management's direction for the purpose of legal advice, which is why investigators give an "Upjohn warning" that the lawyer represents the company, not the employee. **Work product** protects documents prepared in anticipation of litigation (*Hickman v. Taylor* (1947); Rule 26(b)(3)). Fact work product can be obtained on a showing of substantial need and inability to get the equivalent without undue hardship; opinion work product (mental impressions, conclusions, opinions, legal theories) receives near-absolute protection.

### Ethics limits

- **Rule 4.2:** do not communicate about the matter with a person you know is represented in it without that lawyer's consent. For an organization, the ABA comment reaches constituents who supervise or direct the matter, have authority to bind the organization, or whose act or omission may be imputed to it; states vary on how broad this is. Former employees are generally not covered, but do not seek privileged information from them.
- **Rule 4.3:** with an unrepresented person, do not state or imply that you are disinterested, and do not give legal advice other than to get a lawyer.
- **Rule 3.4:** do not unlawfully obstruct access to evidence, alter or destroy it, or offer an inducement to a witness that the law prohibits; paying a witness's reasonable expenses and a reasonable expert fee is allowed.
- **Rule 4.4(b):** if you receive a document you know or should know was inadvertently sent, promptly notify the sender.
- Pretextual or deceptive contact (for example, "friending" a represented person) risks violating Rules 4.2 and 8.4(c).

### Authenticating what you find

Think ahead to trial. Printouts of public web content, text messages and photographs must later be authenticated under FRE 901(a); certain electronic records can be authenticated by certification under FRE 902(13) and (14). Record who found each item, when, and how.

title: An elements chart for a hypothetical negligence claim
problem: (Hypothetical.) Client Ben slipped on a wet floor at a store and broke his wrist. List what to investigate for the breach element.
step: Name the element: breach requires that the store failed to use reasonable care, for example by failing to clean or warn about a spill it knew or should have known about.
step: List known facts: the floor was wet, there was no warning sign, and Ben fell at 2:10 p.m. Missing facts: how long the spill was there, who knew, and the store's cleaning schedule.
step: Match sources: surveillance video for how long; inspection logs and employee schedules by Rule 34 request; an employee witness by deposition; the manager's incident report.
step: Preserve first: send a preservation letter for the video and logs, because retention periods can be short.
@mistakes
- Waiting for formal discovery before preserving video, logs or devices that may be overwritten.
- Contacting a represented organization's supervisor directly because the person is "just an employee."
- Treating the investigator's witness memo as automatically privileged; it is work product, which can yield on substantial need.
- Counting discrete subparts as one interrogatory.
