### Novelty (§ 102)

Since the America Invents Act (AIA) took effect for applications with an effective filing date on or after March 16, 2013, the United States uses a **first-inventor-to-file** system. Under § 102(a)(1), a person is not entitled to a patent if the claimed invention was **patented, described in a printed publication, in public use, on sale, or otherwise available to the public** before the effective filing date. Under § 102(a)(2), it also fails if it was described in a patent or published application naming another inventor that was effectively filed before the claimed invention's effective filing date.

An invention is **anticipated** only if a single prior-art reference discloses **every element** of the claim, arranged as claimed. In *Helsinn Healthcare S.A. v. Teva Pharmaceuticals USA, Inc.* (2019), the Court held that a sale of the invention to a third party who is obliged to keep it confidential is still "on sale" under the AIA.

**Grace period.** Under § 102(b)(1), disclosures made **one year or less** before the effective filing date are not prior art if made by the inventor (or one who obtained the subject matter from the inventor), or if the inventor publicly disclosed the subject matter first. Most other countries have no comparable grace period, so a public disclosure can destroy foreign rights.

### Nonobviousness (§ 103)

A patent may not issue if the differences between the claimed invention and the prior art are such that the claimed invention **as a whole would have been obvious** before the effective filing date to a **person having ordinary skill in the art** (PHOSITA). *Graham v. John Deere Co.* (1966) set the inquiries:

1. the **scope and content** of the prior art;
2. the **differences** between the prior art and the claims;
3. the **level of ordinary skill** in the art; and
4. **objective indicia** (secondary considerations), such as commercial success with a nexus to the claimed features, long-felt but unsolved need, failure of others, copying, and industry praise.

*KSR International Co. v. Teleflex Inc.* (2007) rejected a rigid teaching-suggestion-motivation test and called for an expansive and flexible approach. Combining familiar elements according to known methods is likely obvious when it yields predictable results; a court may consider market demand, design needs, common sense and whether there were a finite number of identified, predictable solutions, making the solution "obvious to try." A reason to combine must be articulated, and hindsight is to be avoided. An invention can be novel but obvious.

### Disclosure (§ 112)

Section 112(a) requires the specification to contain:

- a **written description** showing the inventor possessed the claimed invention;
- **enablement**: teaching a skilled person how to make and use the full scope of the claim without undue experimentation (*Amgen Inc. v. Sanofi* (2023): claims to a whole genus of antibodies defined by function were not enabled where the specification disclosed only some species and a trial-and-error method); and
- the **best mode** the inventor contemplated. Failure to disclose the best mode is no longer a basis for invalidating a patent after the AIA (35 U.S.C. § 282(b)(3)(A)).

Section 112(b) requires claims to **particularly point out and distinctly claim** the invention, so that they inform skilled persons of the scope with **reasonable certainty** (*Nautilus, Inc. v. Biosig Instruments, Inc.* (2014)).

### Policy

The patent bargain: exclusivity for a limited time in return for disclosure that lets the public learn and build. Novelty and nonobviousness keep patents from removing what is already known or trivial; § 112 keeps the claim scope tied to what the inventor actually taught.

### Applying it

Identify the claim and effective filing date, list the prior art with dates, check whether one reference discloses each element (anticipation), apply Graham to obviousness with a reason to combine, and finally test § 112.

@example
title: Novel but obvious
problem: Ana's claim is to a water bottle with a replaceable carbon filter and a flow sensor that lights an LED when the filter should be replaced. Reference A (published 2018) shows a bottle with a replaceable carbon filter. Reference B (published 2019) shows a water pitcher with a flow sensor and LED indicating filter replacement. Ana filed in 2021. Is the claim patentable?
step: Novelty: neither reference discloses every element, because A lacks the sensor and B is a pitcher. The claim is not anticipated.
step: Graham: the scope of the prior art includes both references, which are in the same field of filtered drinking water. The difference is placing B's sensor on A's bottle.
step: A skilled person had a reason to combine, because B teaches the sensor solves the known problem of knowing when to replace a filter, and the combination uses each element for its established function with predictable results (KSR).
step: Secondary considerations such as commercial success or long-felt need could rebut this, but none are given. Conclusion: likely obvious under § 103.

@mistakes
- Treating anticipation as satisfied when references together supply the elements; that is obviousness.
- Applying the old first-to-invent rules to applications under the AIA.
- Requiring an express teaching to combine; KSR allows common sense and market demand.
- Forgetting that a grace period applies only to the inventor's own disclosures and not abroad.
