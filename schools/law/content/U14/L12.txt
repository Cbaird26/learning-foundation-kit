### Infringement

Under 15 U.S.C. § 1114(1), a person who uses in commerce a reproduction, counterfeit, copy or colorable imitation of a **registered** mark in connection with goods or services in a way **likely to cause confusion, mistake or deception** is liable. Section 43(a) (15 U.S.C. § 1125(a)(1)(A)) provides the same protection for **unregistered** marks against false designation of origin, sponsorship or affiliation likely to confuse. The elements are (1) a valid, protectable mark owned by the plaintiff, (2) the defendant's use in commerce, and (3) a **likelihood of confusion**.

### The likelihood-of-confusion factors

Courts use multifactor tests that are similar across circuits. The Second Circuit's *Polaroid Corp. v. Polarad Electronics Corp.* (1961) factors are:

1. **strength** of the plaintiff's mark (conceptual and commercial);
2. **similarity** of the marks (sight, sound, meaning, overall impression);
3. **proximity** of the goods or services;
4. likelihood that the plaintiff will **bridge the gap** into the defendant's market;
5. **actual confusion**;
6. the defendant's **good faith** (intent in adopting the mark);
7. **quality** of the defendant's goods; and
8. **sophistication** of buyers.

The Ninth Circuit's *AMF Inc. v. Sleekcraft Boats* (1979) factors include strength, proximity of goods, similarity, actual confusion, marketing channels, type of goods and degree of care, intent, and likelihood of expansion. The list is flexible, not a checklist, and no single factor controls. Confusion can include **forward**, **reverse** and **initial-interest** confusion, and confusion about **sponsorship or affiliation**, including after the sale.

### Dilution (§ 1125(c))

The owner of a **famous** mark may enjoin another's use of a mark that is likely to cause **dilution by blurring** (impairing distinctiveness) or **dilution by tarnishment** (harming reputation), **regardless of competition, likelihood of confusion or actual economic injury**. The owner must show that (1) its mark is **famous**, meaning widely recognized by the **general consuming public of the United States** as a designation of source (the Trademark Dilution Revision Act of 2006 eliminated "niche" fame), (2) the defendant uses a mark in commerce, (3) the defendant's use began after the plaintiff's mark became famous, and (4) the use is likely to cause blurring or tarnishment. Statutory exclusions cover **fair use** other than as a designation of the defendant's own goods (including parody, criticism and comment), news reporting, and **noncommercial use**.

### Jack Daniel's Properties v. VIP Products

In *Jack Daniel's Properties, Inc. v. VIP Products LLC* (2023), the defendant sold a dog toy that parodied a whiskey bottle and label, and also used the humorous mark as a brand. The Court held that the *Rogers v. Grimaldi* threshold test, which screens out Lanham Act claims against expressive works that have artistic relevance and are not explicitly misleading, **does not apply when the accused infringer uses a mark as a designation of source for its own goods**. The ordinary likelihood-of-confusion analysis applies, and a parody's humorous message may bear on whether confusion is likely. The Court also held that the dilution statute's **noncommercial use exclusion** does not shield a parody used as a source identifier for the defendant's own goods. The Court did not decide that parody is unprotected; it left Rogers for cases in which a mark is not used as a source identifier.

### Defenses

- **Classic (descriptive) fair use** (§ 1115(b)(4)): use, otherwise than as a mark, in good faith, of a descriptive term to describe one's own goods. Some confusion is compatible with the defense, and the plaintiff bears the burden of showing confusion (*KP Permanent Make-Up, Inc. v. Lasting Impression I, Inc.* (2004)).
- **Nominative fair use:** using another's mark to refer to the owner's own product (for example, a repair shop truthfully advertising that it services a brand of cars), using only what is needed and suggesting no sponsorship. Circuits treat it differently.
- Also: abandonment, genericness, laches and acquiescence, and the first-sale doctrine for genuine goods.

### Remedies

An **injunction** (§ 1116), with a rebuttable presumption of irreparable harm on a showing of a violation or likelihood of success (Trademark Modernization Act of 2020); **defendant's profits, plaintiff's damages and costs** (§ 1117(a)), subject to equity, with the plaintiff proving only the defendant's sales and the defendant proving costs and deductions; discretionary attorney's fees in exceptional cases; and enhanced statutory damages for counterfeiting (§ 1117(c)). *Romag Fasteners, Inc. v. Fossil, Inc.* (2020) held willfulness is not a precondition to a profits award, though it is an important consideration. *Abitron Austria GmbH v. Hetronic International, Inc.* (2023) held the Lanham Act's infringement provisions reach only domestic use in commerce, not foreign sales.

### Applying it

Identify the mark and the defendant's use, weigh each factor with facts (do not just list them), reach a conclusion on confusion, consider the dilution claim only if the mark is nationally famous, and then defenses and remedies.

@example
title: Weighing the factors for two marks
problem: Dara's company sells BRIGHTLEAF organic tea in supermarkets, with strong regional recognition. Rival launches BRYTLEAF herbal tea in the same supermarkets, with similar packaging colors. Several customers have mistakenly emailed Dara about Rival's tea. Apply the factors.
step: Strength: "Brightleaf" is arbitrary or suggestive for tea, so conceptually strong; regional recognition adds some commercial strength.
step: Similarity: the marks sound nearly identical and look alike, and the packaging colors add to similarity. Proximity: both are teas sold in the same channels, so close.
step: Actual confusion is shown by misdirected emails (though a few instances may be de minimis). Good faith and buyer sophistication: inexpensive grocery goods mean little care, and intent is unknown.
step: Weighing the factors together, likelihood of confusion is likely. Dilution is not available, because Brightleaf is not nationally famous.

@mistakes
- Treating the factors as a count; courts weigh them with the facts.
- Asserting dilution for a mark that is only locally or niche famous.
- Applying Rogers to a parody used as the defendant's brand after Jack Daniel's.
- Requiring proof of actual confusion; it is relevant but not required.
