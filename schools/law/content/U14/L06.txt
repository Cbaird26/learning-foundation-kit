@article
### Software

Computer programs are literary works (§ 101 defines a "computer program" as a set of statements or instructions used in a computer to bring about a certain result). Source and object code are protected as expression. Functional elements, methods of operation and ideas are not (§ 102(b)), and the filtering approach of *Computer Associates v. Altai* (1992) applies to non-literal elements. *Google v. Oracle* (2021) assumed copyrightability of API declaring code and found fair use. Section 117(a) lets the **owner of a copy** of a program make another copy or adaptation as an essential step in using it with a machine, or for archival purposes only. Courts distinguish owners from licensees: where a vendor grants a restricted license, the user may not be an "owner" for § 117 and first-sale purposes, and courts have differed on how to decide this.

### Digital distribution and performance

First sale (§ 109) covers the owner's lawfully made copy. In *Capitol Records, LLC v. ReDigi Inc.* (2d Cir. 2018), the court held that reselling digital music files through a service that reproduces them in transfer was not protected by first sale. In *American Broadcasting Cos. v. Aereo, Inc.* (2014), the Court held that a service that, through thousands of individual antennas, let subscribers watch broadcast television over the internet **performs publicly** under the Transmit Clause, because it was highly similar to a cable system. Sony and Grokster (Week 4) still govern device-maker liability.

### Anticircumvention (DMCA § 1201)

Section 1201(a)(1)(A) bars **circumventing a technological measure that effectively controls access** to a copyrighted work. Sections 1201(a)(2) and (b) bar **trafficking** in tools designed to circumvent access or copy controls. Circumvention is a violation separate from infringement. Courts split on whether a plaintiff must show a link to infringement: some circuits do not require one, and the Federal Circuit has required a nexus to protect rights. The Librarian of Congress, on the Register of Copyrights' recommendation, adopts temporary exemptions for particular classes of works in a rulemaking every three years (§ 1201(a)(1)(C)); examples have included certain good-faith security research and accessibility uses. Section 512 supplies safe harbors for online providers (Week 4).

### Artificial intelligence: what is unsettled

Three questions recur.

1. **Authorship of outputs.** See Week 2: human authorship is required; the degree of human contribution that suffices for AI-assisted works is open.
2. **Training on copyrighted works.** Developers copy large datasets of works to train models. Whether that copying is fair use is unsettled, and results so far depend on the model's purpose, how the training copies were obtained, and effects on markets. In *Thomson Reuters Enterprise Centre GmbH v. Ross Intelligence Inc.* (D. Del. 2025), the district court held that copying legal headnotes to train a non-generative legal-research tool was not fair use; in September 2026 the Third Circuit affirmed, in what appears to be the first federal appellate ruling on fair use in AI training, while noting in a footnote that the tool returned existing text rather than generating new expression, so generative AI remains an open question. In *Bartz v. Anthropic PBC* (N.D. Cal. 2025), the district court held that training a language model on lawfully purchased books was fair use but that building a central library from pirated copies was not protected by that ruling, and the case was later resolved by a class settlement. In *Kadrey v. Meta Platforms, Inc.* (N.D. Cal. 2025), the court ruled for the defendant on the record presented while stating that, with better evidence, harm to the market through output that competes with the plaintiffs' books could weigh against fair use. These are trial-level decisions that apply a flexible test and do not bind other courts; check for appellate developments. The Copyright Office's 2025 report on generative AI training likewise concludes that some training uses are likely fair and others are not, depending on the facts.
3. **Outputs that reproduce protected material.** A model's output may itself infringe if it copies protected expression; liability theories (direct, contributory, vicarious) are being tested.

For exam answers, state the competing factor-by-factor arguments (transformative purpose and non-expressive use on one side; commercial scale, copying of whole works, source of copies and market substitution or dilution on the other) and say plainly that no controlling Supreme Court decision exists.

### Applying it

Identify the technology use, the right implicated (reproduction, distribution, performance, circumvention), the owner's claim, any statutory limit (§§ 109, 117, 512, 1201 exemptions), and then fair use. For fair use of a technology, ask whether the use serves a different purpose, how much was copied and was it necessary, and whether it substitutes in a market the owner would exploit.

@example
title: A search tool that shows snippets
problem: A startup builds a search service that scans millions of published books and shows users short snippets from the text with the search term and a link to buy. A publisher sues over the scanning and snippets. Analyze fair use.
step: Factor 1: the service gives information about books (search and discovery), a purpose different from reading the books. It is commercial but serves a further purpose. This favors fair use under a Campbell and Warhol analysis of the specific use.
step: Factor 2: the books are creative and published, but this factor carries little weight here.
step: Factor 3: the entire books are copied internally, which is necessary for search, but the public sees only small snippets and cannot reconstruct the books. This is reasonable for the purpose.
step: Factor 4: snippets do not substitute for the books and may drive sales. Weighing the factors, fair use is likely, a result reached by the Second Circuit in Authors Guild v. Google (2015) on similar facts, though a service displaying large portions would change the weighing.

@mistakes
- Assuming that a technological use is fair simply because it is new or innovative.
- Treating circumvention as lawful whenever the underlying use would be fair; § 1201 is a separate prohibition subject to exemptions.
- Presenting district-court AI decisions as settled law or predicting how appeals will come out.
- Forgetting that § 117 protects owners of copies, not every licensee.

The broadcasters sue for infringing their public-performance right. What is the likely result under Aereo (2014)?
choice*: The broadcasters likely prevail, because the service performs the works publicly by transmitting them to many subscribers, much like a cable system.
choice: The service prevails, because each subscriber receives a separate private transmission from a separate antenna.
choice: The service prevails, because broadcasters have no public-performance right in broadcasts.
choice: The broadcasters prevail only if the service stores copies of the programs.
hint: Aereo looked at the service's similarity to cable.
solution: The Court held the service performs publicly under the Transmit Clause. Separate antennas did not change the substance, and storage of copies is not necessary for a performance.

prompt: Dev, who lawfully bought and owns a copy of a software program, makes one backup copy for archival purposes and keeps it on a shelf, intending to destroy it if he sells the original. The vendor sues, claiming the backup copy infringes. What is the likely result?
choice: The vendor wins, because any copy beyond the first is infringing.
choice: The vendor wins, because archival copies are permitted only for companies.
choice*: Dev wins, because § 117(a) permits the owner of a copy to make an archival copy, subject to its conditions.
choice: Dev wins, because software is not covered by copyright.
hint: Section 117 covers owners of copies.
solution: Section 117(a)(2) allows an owner of a copy to make an archival copy that is not used otherwise and is destroyed if ownership ends. Software is protected, and the right is not limited to companies.

@practice self
prompt: (Deliverable.) Apply the four fair-use factors to two uses and reach a conclusion on each. Use A: a documentary filmmaker includes a 12-second clip of a television news broadcast to critique the broadcast's coverage. Use B: a lyrics app copies the full lyrics of 5,000 songs and sells ads next to them without a license.
hint: Ask for each use whether it serves a different purpose and whether it could substitute for the original.
solution: Use A: factor 1 favors fair use because criticism is a different purpose, even though the film is commercial; factor 2 is neutral because news footage is largely factual and published; factor 3 favors fair use because 12 seconds is a small, tailored amount; factor 4 favors fair use because a clip used for critique does not substitute for the broadcast. Fair use is likely. Use B: factor 1 weighs against, because displaying lyrics serves the same purpose as the lyrics and is commercial; factor 2 weighs against because lyrics are creative; factor 3 weighs against because the entire work is copied; factor 4 weighs against because the app substitutes for licensed lyric services. Fair use is unlikely.
rubric: Applies all four factors to each use with facts.

@quiz mc
prompt: Eli's company sells a video recorder that customers use to record broadcast programs for later viewing at home. The device can also be used to copy purchased tapes illegally, but most uses are time-shifting of broadcasts. Studios sue the company for contributory infringement. What is the likely result under Sony (1984)?
choice: The studios win, because the company knows some customers infringe.
choice*: The company wins, because the device is capable of substantial noninfringing uses, including fair-use time-shifting.
choice: The studios win, because home recording is never fair use.
choice: The company wins, because contributory liability does not exist in copyright law.
hint: Recall the Betamax decision.
solution: Sony held that distributing a device capable of substantial noninfringing uses does not by itself create contributory liability, and treated time-shifting as fair use. Grokster later added inducement liability, and courts do recognize contributory liability.
