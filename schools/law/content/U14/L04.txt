@article
### The elements

A plaintiff proves infringement by showing (1) ownership of a valid copyright and (2) **copying of constituent elements of the work that are original** (*Feist*, 1991). Anyone who violates an exclusive right in § 106 infringes (§ 501(a)). Intent is not an element, so innocent infringement is still infringement, though intent affects damages.

### Two kinds of copying

1. **Actual copying** (factual copying). Direct evidence is rare, so plaintiffs prove it circumstantially by showing the defendant had **access** and that the works are **probatively similar**. Striking similarity can support an inference of copying even without proof of access. **Independent creation** is a complete defense: copyright does not stop two authors from creating the same work independently.
2. **Unlawful appropriation** (improper appropriation). The copying must be so extensive that the works are **substantially similar** as to protected expression.

### Tests for substantial similarity

Circuits differ; know the labels.

- **Ordinary observer test:** would an ordinary, reasonable observer, disregarding unprotectable elements, find the aesthetic appeal of the works the same? Many courts require the observer to look only at protected expression.
- **Extrinsic/intrinsic test** (Ninth Circuit): the extrinsic test objectively compares specific expressive elements after filtering out unprotectable ideas; the intrinsic test asks how the total concept and feel strikes the ordinary reasonable audience, and is for the jury.
- **Abstraction-filtration-comparison** (Second Circuit, *Computer Associates International, Inc. v. Altai, Inc.* (1992), for software): abstract the program into levels, filter out unprotectable elements (ideas, elements dictated by efficiency or external factors, public-domain material), then compare what remains. This builds on Judge Hand's abstractions approach in *Nichols v. Universal Pictures Corp.* (1930), which recognized that at some level of generality a plot is an unprotectable idea.
- **Total concept and feel** survives in some circuits but is criticized when it lets courts count unprotectable elements.

The key discipline is **filtration**: ideas, facts, scenes a faire, merger and public-domain elements do not count toward similarity. A work built from unprotectable elements may be protected only in a "thin" way, so only near-verbatim copying infringes.

### Secondary liability

Copyright has no statute defining secondary liability; courts developed it.

- **Contributory infringement:** knowledge of the infringing activity plus material contribution or inducement.
- **Vicarious liability:** the right and ability to supervise the infringing conduct plus a direct financial benefit.
- **Inducement:** distributing a device with the object of promoting its use to infringe, shown by clear expression or affirmative steps (*MGM Studios Inc. v. Grokster, Ltd.* (2005)).
- Selling a product capable of substantial noninfringing uses does not alone create contributory liability (*Sony Corp. of America v. Universal City Studios, Inc.* (1984)).
- The Digital Millennium Copyright Act (17 U.S.C. § 512) gives online service providers conditional safe harbors from monetary liability for infringement by users, if they meet conditions such as a notice-and-takedown procedure and a repeat-infringer policy.

### Remedies

- **Injunction** (§ 502), subject to the usual equitable factors from *eBay Inc. v. MercExchange, L.L.C.* (2006).
- **Actual damages plus profits** of the infringer attributable to the infringement and not already counted in actual damages (§ 504(b)); the plaintiff proves the infringer's gross revenue and the defendant proves deductible expenses and portions attributable to other factors.
- **Statutory damages** (§ 504(c)): the owner may elect, at any time before final judgment, between \$750 and \$30,000 per work infringed, as the court considers just, increased to as much as \$150,000 per work for willful infringement and reduced to as little as \$200 for innocent infringement. The unit is the work, not the number of infringements.
- **Attorney's fees and costs** (§ 505), discretionary.
- Limitations: three years from accrual (§ 507(b)); *Petrella v. Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer, Inc.* (2014) held laches cannot bar a damages claim brought within that period, and *Warner Chappell Music, Inc. v. Nealy* (2024) held that, where a claim is timely, damages are not capped at three years back (the Court assumed without deciding that the discovery rule applies).

### Applying it

Walk through: valid copyright; access and similarity (actual copying); filter out unprotectable elements; compare what remains under the governing test; consider defenses (independent creation, fair use, license); then remedies.

@example
title: Filtering a story
problem: Ana's novel is a thriller about a detective who, in a rain-soaked city, investigates a missing scientist and discovers the scientist faked her death to escape a corrupt lab. Ben's film has the same premise, a detective, a rain-soaked city, and a scientist faking her death, but different characters, dialogue, plot twists and structure. Ben had read Ana's novel. Is Ben liable?
step: Ownership is assumed. Access is shown because Ben read the novel, and the shared premise supports probative similarity, so actual copying may be inferred.
step: Unlawful appropriation requires substantial similarity of protected expression. Filter out the premise, the detective archetype, the rainy city setting (scenes a faire) and the general plot idea.
step: Compare what remains: specific characters, dialogue, sequence of events and twists. Those differ.
step: Conclusion: probably no infringement, because the overlap is in unprotectable ideas and stock elements. A court following Nichols would see the similarity at a level of abstraction too general to be protected.

@mistakes
- Treating access as enough to prove infringement without substantial similarity of protected expression.
- Counting ideas, stock elements and facts toward similarity instead of filtering them out first.
- Believing intent is required for infringement.
- Counting each copy as a separate unit for statutory damages; the unit is the work.
