@article
**The rule.** Copyright protects "original works of authorship fixed in any tangible medium of expression" (17 U.S.C. § 102(a)). The elements are **originality**, **authorship** and **fixation**. The listed categories are illustrative: literary works; musical works; dramatic works; pantomimes and choreographic works; pictorial, graphic and sculptural works; motion pictures and other audiovisual works; sound recordings; and architectural works.

### Originality

In *Feist Publications, Inc. v. Rural Telephone Service Co.* (1991), the Court held that originality requires **independent creation** plus a **modicum of creativity**. A telephone white-pages directory's names, towns and numbers are facts; facts are not original, and an alphabetical arrangement is so commonplace that it lacks the minimum creativity. The Court rejected the "sweat of the brow" theory, under which effort alone earns protection. A compilation can be protected for its original selection, coordination or arrangement (17 U.S.C. § 103), but protection is **thin** and does not reach the underlying facts. A derivative work (a translation or adaptation) is protected only as to the new material the author contributes.

### Fixation

A work is fixed when it is embodied in a copy or phonorecord by or under the authority of the author, sufficiently permanent or stable to be perceived, reproduced or communicated for more than a transitory duration (§ 101). An improvised, unrecorded dance or a tune only hummed is not fixed, and federal copyright does not subsist in it (state law may offer some protection). A live broadcast that is recorded simultaneously is fixed.

### The idea/expression dichotomy

Section 102(b) excludes "any idea, procedure, process, system, method of operation, concept, principle, or discovery" however described or embodied. *Baker v. Selden* (1879) held that copyright in a book explaining an accounting system did not give exclusive rights in the system or the use of the ruled forms needed to apply it. Two doctrines apply the dichotomy:

- **Merger:** when an idea can be expressed in only one or very few ways, the expression merges with the idea and is not protected.
- **Scenes a faire:** stock elements that flow naturally from a theme or setting (for example, a gunfight in a Western) are unprotected.

### Useful articles and separability

A "useful article" has an intrinsic utilitarian function (§ 101). Its design is protected only to the extent it incorporates pictorial, graphic or sculptural features that can be identified separately from, and are capable of existing independently of, its utilitarian aspects. In *Star Athletica, L.L.C. v. Varsity Brands, Inc.* (2017), the Court held that a feature qualifies if it (1) can be perceived as a two- or three-dimensional work of art separate from the useful article, and (2) would qualify as a protectable pictorial, graphic or sculptural work, either on its own or in another medium, if imagined separately. Applied to cheerleading uniforms, the surface designs qualified; the Court did not give copyright in the uniform's cut or shape as such, so copying the design on another article does not give rights to the useful article itself.

### Works not protected

Works of the U.S. government created by its officers or employees as part of their official duties are not copyrightable (§ 105). Titles, names and short phrases are generally not copyrightable. Facts, historical events and laws of nature are free for all to use.

### Artificial intelligence and authorship

The Copyright Office and courts require a **human author**. In *Thaler v. Perlmutter* (D.C. Cir. 2025), the court upheld refusal to register a work that the claimant identified as authored solely by an AI system, reading the Copyright Act to require human authorship. The Copyright Office's January 2025 report on copyrightability states that prompts alone generally do not make a user the author of AI output, while human-authored expression that is perceptible in the output, human selection and arrangement of AI material, and human modifications of it can be protected. Questions that remain open include how much human control or contribution suffices, how to treat iterative prompting, and what applicants must disclose. Courts and the Office are still working these out, and you should state both positions neutrally on an exam.

### Applying it

Take each work in turn. Ask: Is it a human creation? Is it fixed? Is any claimed element an idea, system, fact or useful feature? What independent, creative expression remains? Conclude element by element, because one work often has both protected and unprotected parts.

@example
title: A directory of local services
problem: Ben compiles a pamphlet listing 300 local plumbers by alphabetical name with phone numbers copied from public records. Rival Press copies the entire pamphlet. Ben also wrote a two-page introduction. Which parts are protected?
step: The names and numbers are facts. Under Feist, facts are not original, and effort in gathering them (sweat of the brow) is irrelevant.
step: Alphabetical listing is a mechanical, commonplace arrangement, so it lacks even a modicum of creativity. The compilation has no protectable selection or arrangement.
step: The two-page introduction is Ben's independently created prose, which is original and fixed, so it is protected expression.
step: Conclusion: Rival can copy the listings freely but not the introduction.

@mistakes
- Granting protection because the author worked hard; Feist rejects sweat of the brow.
- Holding that a compilation's copyright protects the underlying facts, rather than only original selection and arrangement.
- Confusing "fixed" with "published" or "registered."
- Treating a useful article as wholly unprotectable or wholly protected; the question is whether any feature is separable.
