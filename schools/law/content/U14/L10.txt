### What is a trade secret?

Trade secret law protects valuable confidential information. Nearly every state has adopted the **Uniform Trade Secrets Act (UTSA)**, and the **Defend Trade Secrets Act of 2016 (DTSA)** added a federal civil cause of action (18 U.S.C. § 1836(b)) for misappropriation of a trade secret related to a product or service used in or intended for interstate or foreign commerce. The DTSA does not preempt state law (§ 1838). Under § 1839(3), a **trade secret** is business or technical information of any form (formulas, methods, techniques, processes, programs, customer lists and so on) if (1) the owner has taken **reasonable measures** to keep it secret and (2) it derives **independent economic value** from not being generally known to, and not readily ascertainable through proper means by, others who could obtain economic value from it.

Reasonable measures include confidentiality agreements, access controls, marking documents, need-to-know policies and exit interviews. Information published or freely accessible is not secret. Disclosure under a confidentiality obligation does not destroy secrecy.

### Misappropriation

Misappropriation (§ 1839(5)) is:

- **acquisition** of a trade secret by a person who knows or has reason to know it was acquired by **improper means**; or
- **disclosure or use** without consent by one who used improper means to acquire it, or who knew or had reason to know that the knowledge came through a person who owed a duty to maintain secrecy, or through improper means.

**Improper means** include theft, bribery, misrepresentation, breach or inducement of a breach of a duty to maintain secrecy, and espionage. **Proper means** include **independent discovery** and **reverse engineering** of a lawfully obtained product (§ 1839(6)(B)). Unlike patent law, trade secret law offers no protection against a competitor who figures it out independently.

### Remedies

Under the DTSA: **injunction** against actual or threatened misappropriation (with limits so an injunction cannot prevent a person from entering an employment relationship and conditions on employment must be based on evidence of threatened misappropriation, not merely the information a person knows); **damages** for actual loss plus unjust enrichment not counted in actual loss, or a **reasonable royalty**; **exemplary damages** up to two times the damages awarded for willful and malicious misappropriation; and **attorney's fees** for bad-faith claims or willful and malicious misappropriation. In extraordinary circumstances, a court may order an ex parte **seizure** (§ 1836(b)(2)). The limitations period is three years from discovery or when it should have been discovered (§ 1836(d)).

**Whistleblower immunity (§ 1833(b)).** An individual is immune from liability for disclosing a trade secret in confidence to a government official or attorney solely to report or investigate a suspected violation of law, or in a sealed court filing. An employer must give notice of this immunity in any contract with an employee governing confidential information; if it does not, it cannot recover exemplary damages or attorney's fees against that employee.

### Relationship to other law

*Kewanee Oil Co. v. Bicron Corp.* (1974) held that state trade secret law is not preempted by federal patent law, because it protects different, confidential subject matter without a monopoly against independent discovery. *Ruckelshus v. Monsanto Co.* (1984) recognized that trade secrets can be property for Takings Clause purposes. Employment restrictions such as covenants not to compete are governed largely by state law, which varies.

### Patent versus trade secret

| | Patent | Trade secret |
|---|---|---|
| Subject matter | Eligible, novel, nonobvious, enabled inventions | Any valuable secret information |
| Term | 20 years from filing | Indefinite while secret |
| Disclosure | Required (published) | None; secrecy must be maintained |
| Independent invention | Infringes | Not misappropriation |
| Reverse engineering | Infringes | Permitted if product lawfully obtained |
| Cost and formality | Examination fees, attorney time | Security measures; no registration |

A process that is hard to reverse engineer from the product, and not likely to be independently discovered, fits secrecy. A product that reveals its secret when sold, or an invention others will likely discover soon, fits patent. A company may be unable to patent after publishing or selling the invention more than a year earlier (Week 8), and a secret user may also rely on the § 273 prior-commercial-use defense in some cases.

### Applying it

Identify the information, test secrecy and value, test measures, then ask how the defendant obtained and used it (proper or improper). For client advice, compare duration, cost, exposure to reverse engineering and the patentability risks.

@example
title: Ex-employee and a customer list
problem: Dara runs a roofing company. Her salespeople sign confidentiality agreements, and the customer database, with pricing and contact details built over years, is password protected. Ned, a salesperson, copies the database before quitting and uses it at a rival company. Does Dara have a claim?
step: Trade secret: the database is information with economic value not readily ascertainable (it took years to build and has pricing), and Dara used reasonable measures (agreements, passwords).
step: Misappropriation: Ned acquired it by copying in breach of his duty of confidentiality, which is improper means, and used it without consent.
step: Remedies: Dara can seek an injunction and damages under the UTSA or DTSA, and exemplary damages if the conduct was willful and malicious. Ned could claim whistleblower immunity only if he disclosed solely to report a suspected legal violation, which does not fit.
step: Conclusion: Dara likely prevails. A public list of customers or a database freely shared with outsiders would weaken the secrecy element.

@mistakes
- Treating information as a trade secret without reasonable secrecy measures.
- Calling reverse engineering of a lawfully purchased product misappropriation.
- Forgetting that independent discovery is a complete defense.
- Missing that an employee contract lacking the § 1833(b) immunity notice limits exemplary damages and fees against that employee.

hint: Compare duration, disclosure, reverse engineering and independent discovery.
solution: Patent: if the method is eligible, novel, nonobvious and enabled, a patent would give up to 20 years of exclusivity against even independent inventors, but the client must disclose the method publicly, pay for prosecution, and file within the grace period if it has sold coated parts. Recommendation: because independent discovery is likely within years and a patent would stop it for 20 years, patent protection is probably better if the method is patentable; if the client doubts patentability or the method could be kept tightly controlled, secrecy is workable.
