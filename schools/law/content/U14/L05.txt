@article
**The rule.** Section 107 provides that the fair use of a copyrighted work, for purposes "such as criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching (including multiple copies for classroom use), scholarship, or research," is not an infringement. The listed purposes are examples, not a safe list. Fair use is an **affirmative defense**: the defendant bears the burden. It is a flexible, case-by-case balancing of four non-exclusive factors, weighed together in light of copyright's purposes. Courts treat it as a mixed question of law and fact.

### The four factors

1. **Purpose and character of the use**, including whether it is commercial or for nonprofit educational purposes.
2. **Nature of the copyrighted work** (more creative works are closer to the core of copyright; factual works receive thinner protection; unpublished works get more protection, as in *Harper & Row, Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises* (1985)).
3. **Amount and substantiality** of the portion used in relation to the whole, both quantity and quality. In *Harper & Row*, a magazine's use of a few hundred words from a forthcoming memoir was not fair partly because it took "the heart of the book."
4. **Effect of the use on the potential market** for or value of the copyrighted work, including markets for licensing and derivative works that the owner would in general develop or license others to develop. Courts ask whether widespread similar use would substitute for the original.

### Transformative use and Campbell

In *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.* (1994), the Court held that a commercial parody of a song can be fair use. A use that adds something new, with a further purpose or different character, is more likely to be fair, and commercial character does not create a presumption of unfairness. **Parody** needs to mimic an original to make its point and so has a stronger claim to borrow than **satire**, which uses the original without commenting on it. The Court also rejected the idea that a harm to the market from criticism (as opposed to substitution) counts under factor four.

### Warhol

In *Andy Warhol Foundation for the Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith* (2023), the Court addressed one use: the foundation's licensing of an orange silkscreen of a musician to a magazine, for an article about that musician, where the artist had begun with a photographer's licensed photograph. The Court held that factor one asks whether the use has a **further purpose or different character**, a matter of degree, and that this is weighed against the commercial nature of the use. Adding new expression, meaning or message is relevant but not enough alone. Because the licensing use shared substantially the same purpose as the photograph (to illustrate a magazine story about the musician) and was commercial, the first factor favored the photographer. The Court did not decide whether the artist's creation of the underlying works was fair use, and it did not hold that all art based on photographs is infringing. After Warhol, "transformative" must be assessed by the purpose of the specific use challenged, not by the general idea that a work looks different.

### Software and technology

In *Google LLC v. Oracle America, Inc.* (2021), the Court assumed without deciding that the Java API's declaring code was copyrightable and held that Google's copying of about 11,500 lines of declaring code, to let programmers use their accrued skills in a new smartphone platform, was fair use. The Court stressed the functional nature of that code, the small share of the whole, the transformative new platform, and the lack of harmful market substitution. *Sony Corp. of America v. Universal City Studios, Inc.* (1984) found time-shifting of broadcasts at home to be fair use.

### Applying it

Write the use being challenged, apply each factor with facts and cases, and **weigh them together**; do not count factors mechanically. State which factor mattered most on those facts, and note any fact that would change the outcome. Unlike many bar answers, you can conclude "close case, but likely fair use" if your reasoning is clear.

@example
title: A critical review that quotes a book
problem: A reviewer for a nonprofit literary journal quotes two paragraphs (about 250 words) from a 90,000-word published novel to criticize its prose style. The novel's publisher sues. Apply the four factors.
step: Factor 1: criticism and comment is the kind of purpose § 107 names, and the review serves a different purpose than the novel (commentary versus entertainment). It is nonprofit, and the quotation supports the critique. This favors fair use.
step: Factor 2: the novel is creative and published. Creativity weighs against fair use somewhat, but this factor rarely decides cases of criticism.
step: Factor 3: 250 words from 90,000 is tiny, and the passages were chosen to illustrate the criticism, not as the heart of the book. This favors fair use.
step: Factor 4: a review does not substitute for the novel, and any harm from negative criticism is not cognizable harm (Campbell). Weighing all four, fair use is likely.

@mistakes
- Treating "transformative" as an automatic win after Warhol; the question is the purpose of the particular use, weighed against commerciality.
- Declaring commercial use presumptively unfair or nonprofit use automatically fair.
- Treating a small amount as always fair, or a large amount as always unfair; quality counts and a whole work may be copied if reasonable for the purpose.
- Counting factors rather than weighing them.
