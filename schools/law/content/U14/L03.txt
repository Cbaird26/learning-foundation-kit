@article
### Who owns the work

**Default rule.** Copyright vests initially in the **author** (17 U.S.C. § 201(a)). The author is usually the person who creates the expression, not the one who has the idea or pays for the work.

**Work made for hire.** Under § 201(b), the employer or commissioning party is treated as the author of a work made for hire. Section 101 defines two routes:

1. A work prepared by an **employee within the scope of employment**. In *Community for Creative Non-Violence v. Reid* (1989), the Court held that "employee" takes its meaning from the general common law of agency, applying factors such as the hiring party's right to control the manner and means, the skill required, the source of tools, the location, the duration of the relationship, the right to assign additional projects, payment method, benefits and tax treatment. A sculptor commissioned for one project was an independent contractor. Scope of employment is tested under the agency approach (Restatement (Second) of Agency § 228): work of the kind the employee is hired to perform, substantially within authorized time and space, and actuated at least in part by a purpose to serve the employer.
2. A **specially ordered or commissioned work** that falls within one of nine listed categories (for example, a contribution to a collective work, part of a motion picture or other audiovisual work, a translation, a supplementary work, a compilation, an instructional text, a test, answer material for a test, or an atlas) **and** is covered by a written instrument signed by both parties stating that it is a work made for hire.

An independent contractor's work outside those categories belongs to the contractor unless assigned. The commissioning party usually then holds, at most, an implied nonexclusive license.

**Joint works.** A joint work is prepared by two or more authors **with the intention that their contributions be merged into inseparable or interdependent parts of a unitary whole** (§ 101). Joint authors each own an undivided interest. Absent agreement, each may use or grant **nonexclusive** licenses without the others' consent, but must **account** to co-owners for their share of profits. A co-owner cannot grant an exclusive license alone.

**Transfers.** A transfer of ownership, including an exclusive license, is not valid without a **signed writing** (§ 204(a)). Nonexclusive licenses may be oral or implied. An author (except for a work made for hire) may **terminate** a post-1977 grant during a five-year window beginning 35 years after the grant, by serving advance written notice (§ 203).

### Duration

For works created on or after January 1, 1978 (§ 302):

- Individual author: life of the author plus **70 years**.
- Joint work: life of the last surviving author plus 70 years.
- Anonymous or pseudonymous works and works made for hire: **95 years from first publication or 120 years from creation, whichever expires first**.

Terms run to the end of the calendar year (§ 305).

### Exclusive rights

Section 106 gives the owner exclusive rights to: (1) reproduce the work; (2) prepare **derivative works**; (3) **distribute** copies to the public by sale or other transfer of ownership, rental, lease or lending; (4) **perform** literary, musical, dramatic, choreographic, motion-picture and similar works publicly; (5) **display** literary, musical, dramatic, choreographic, pictorial, graphic and sculptural works, and motion-picture images, publicly; and (6) perform sound recordings publicly by means of a digital audio transmission. These rights are subject to §§ 107-122. Important limits include fair use (§ 107) and the **first-sale doctrine** (§ 109): the owner of a lawfully made copy may sell or dispose of that copy without permission (*Kirtsaeng v. John Wiley & Sons, Inc.* (2013) applied it to copies lawfully made abroad). First sale limits distribution, not reproduction.

### Registration and remedies

Copyright exists on fixation, but a U.S. work generally must be registered, or registration refused, before suit (§ 411(a)); in *Fourth Estate Public Benefit Corp. v. Wall-Street.com, LLC* (2019), the Court held that registration occurs when the Copyright Office registers the work, not when the owner applies. Statutory damages and attorney's fees are generally available only when registration preceded the infringement (or came within three months of first publication) (§ 412).

### Applying it

Ask in order: Who created the expression? Was that person an employee within the scope of employment? If not, was it a commissioned work in a listed category with a signed writing? Were there co-authors with the intent to merge contributions? Has any transfer satisfied § 204(a)? Then compute the term.

@example
title: Employee or contractor?
problem: Dara runs a studio. Ned works for the studio full time with benefits and is paid a salary. During work hours, at Dara's request, he designs a poster. Separately, Dara pays Mia, an independent illustrator who uses her own studio and tools, to draw a poster; they sign nothing. Who owns each poster's copyright?
step: Ned is an employee under the common-law agency factors (salary, benefits, control, studio assignment) and created the poster as the kind of work he is hired to do, during work time, to serve the studio. It is a work made for hire, so the studio is the author.
step: Mia is an independent contractor under CCNV v. Reid (own tools and studio, no benefits).
step: A poster is not clearly within the nine commissioned-work categories, and there is no signed writing, so Mia is the author and owner.
step: Dara's studio probably holds an implied nonexclusive license to use the poster for the purpose for which it was created, but would need a signed writing to take ownership.

@mistakes
- Assuming the person who pays always owns the copyright.
- Treating any commissioned work as made for hire; it must fit one of nine categories and have a signed writing.
- Treating oral assignments of ownership as valid; § 204(a) requires a signed writing for transfers.
- Saying a joint owner may grant an exclusive license alone.

@practice ms
prompt: Which of the following are exclusive rights of a copyright owner under 17 U.S.C. § 106?
choice*: The right to make derivative works based on the work
choice*: The right to distribute copies to the public by sale
choice*: The right to perform a musical work publicly
choice: The right to prevent a lawful owner of a copy from reselling that copy
choice: The right to control private reading of a lawfully purchased book
hint: Remember the first-sale doctrine.
solution: Reproduction, derivative works, distribution, public performance and public display are exclusive rights. The first-sale doctrine allows the owner of a lawful copy to resell it, and copyright does not control private reading.

@practice self
prompt: Ana is employed full time as an app developer for Brightly Inc. On weekends, at home on her own laptop, she writes an unrelated puzzle game that Brightly has no interest in. Brightly claims the game is a work made for hire. Write an IRAC analysis.
hint: Use the agency factors: kind of work, time and space, purpose to serve the employer.
solution: Issue: Is the game a work made for hire, so that Brightly is the author? Rule: Under 17 U.S.C. §§ 101 and 201(b), a work prepared by an employee within the scope of employment belongs to the employer; scope is judged by agency principles, asking whether the work is of the kind the employee is employed to perform, occurs substantially within authorized time and space, and is motivated at least partly by a purpose to serve the employer. Application: Ana is an employee, and writing code is the kind of work she does, which favors Brightly. But she wrote the game on weekends, at home, on her own laptop, and for her own purposes, not to serve Brightly. The time-and-space and purpose factors weigh against Brightly. Conclusion: Probably not a work made for hire, so Ana is the author, subject to any signed assignment in her employment agreement and to facts on whether she used employer resources.
rubric: States the employee-within-scope rule and the three agency factors.
rubric: Applies each factor to the facts rather than treating employment alone as decisive.
rubric: Reaches a conclusion and notes the effect of any signed assignment.
