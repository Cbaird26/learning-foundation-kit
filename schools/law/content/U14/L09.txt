@article
### Claims define the right

The **claims** at the end of the patent define the scope of the exclusive right (35 U.S.C. § 112(b)). A claim has a **preamble**, a **transition** and a **body**. "Comprising" is open-ended: the claim covers anything that has the listed elements, even if it has more. "Consisting of" is closed and excludes unlisted elements. An **independent claim** stands alone; a **dependent claim** refers to an earlier claim and adds a limitation (§ 112(d)), so it is narrower.

### Claim construction

Construing the claims is a question for the judge (*Markman v. Westview Instruments, Inc.* (1996)). In *Phillips v. AWH Corp.* (Fed. Cir. 2005, en banc), claim terms have the ordinary meaning to a person of ordinary skill at the time of invention, read in the context of the **specification** and the **prosecution history** (the intrinsic evidence), with extrinsic evidence such as dictionaries and expert testimony of lesser weight. Under *Teva Pharmaceuticals USA, Inc. v. Sandoz, Inc.* (2015), subsidiary factual findings about extrinsic evidence get clear-error review, and the ultimate construction is reviewed de novo.

### Direct infringement

Under § 271(a), whoever without authority **makes, uses, offers to sell, or sells** a patented invention in the United States, or **imports** it, infringes. Infringement has two steps: construe the claim, then compare the accused product or process to it.

- **Literal infringement:** the accused device contains **every limitation** of at least one claim (the **all-elements rule**). Missing one element means no literal infringement, and adding elements does not avoid a "comprising" claim.
- **Doctrine of equivalents:** an accused device can infringe if it differs only insubstantially from a claim element, for example because it performs substantially the same function in substantially the same way to obtain the same result (*Graver Tank & Manufacturing Co. v. Linde Air Products Co.* (1950)). Under *Warner-Jenkinson Co. v. Hilton Davis Chemical Co.* (1997), equivalence is assessed element by element, not for the invention as a whole, so the doctrine cannot eliminate a limitation entirely (vitiation).
- **Prosecution history estoppel:** a narrowing amendment made for a reason related to patentability presumptively bars the patentee from claiming equivalents for the surrendered territory (*Festo Corp. v. Shoketsu Kinzoku Kogyo Kabushiki Co.* (2002)). The patentee can rebut by showing the equivalent was unforeseeable, the rationale was tangential, or some other reason. The doctrine also cannot reach prior art (ensnarement).

### Indirect infringement

- **Inducement (§ 271(b)):** actively inducing infringement requires knowledge that the induced acts infringe the patent (*Global-Tech Appliances, Inc. v. SEB S.A.* (2011), including willful blindness). A good-faith belief that the patent is invalid is not a defense (*Commil USA, LLC v. Cisco Systems, Inc.* (2015)).
- **Contributory infringement (§ 271(c)):** selling a component of a patented combination, knowing it was especially made for infringement, and which is not a staple article with substantial noninfringing use.
- Both require an underlying act of direct infringement. In *Akamai Technologies, Inc. v. Limelight Networks, Inc.* (Fed. Cir. 2015, en banc), direct infringement of a method claim performed by multiple actors can exist when one entity directs or controls the others or they act in a joint enterprise.

### Defenses

- **Invalidity** (§ 282): patents are **presumed valid**, and a challenger must prove invalidity by **clear and convincing evidence** (*Microsoft Corp. v. i4i Ltd. Partnership* (2011)).
- **Patent exhaustion:** an authorized sale of a patented item exhausts the patentee's rights in that item, regardless of post-sale restrictions and including foreign authorized sales (*Impression Products, Inc. v. Lexmark International, Inc.* (2017)). The patentee may still have contract remedies.
- Also: noninfringement, license, inequitable conduct, and the prior commercial-use defense of § 273.

### Remedies

- **Injunction** (§ 283), under the *eBay* factors.
- **Damages** (§ 284): "adequate to compensate for the infringement, but in no event less than a reasonable royalty," plus interest and costs. The court may **increase** damages up to **three times** the amount found; under *Halo Electronics, Inc. v. Pulse Electronics, Inc.* (2016), enhancement is reserved for egregious infringement, decided in the court's discretion.
- **Attorney's fees** (§ 285): in "exceptional cases," meaning a case that stands out for the substantive strength of a party's position or the unreasonable manner of litigating (*Octane Fitness, LLC v. ICON Health & Fitness, Inc.* (2014)).

### Applying it

Construe the claim; list its elements in a chart; compare each to the accused product (literal); if an element is missing, ask about equivalents and estoppel; then consider indirect liability, defenses and remedies.

@example
title: Literal infringement and equivalents
problem: Ben's patent claim reads: "A bottle cap comprising (a) a threaded ring, (b) a pull tab attached to the ring by a scored seam, and (c) a seal liner." Rival's cap has a threaded ring, a pull tab attached by a scored seam, a seal liner and a child-safety latch. A second Rival cap uses a strip with a perforated seam instead of a pull tab. Analyze infringement.
step: Construe the claim: "comprising" is open, so extra elements do not avoid infringement.
step: First cap: every element (a), (b) and (c) is present. The latch is an extra feature. Literal infringement.
step: Second cap: no pull tab, so (b) is missing literally. Consider equivalents: does the perforated strip perform substantially the same function in substantially the same way with the same result as the pull tab and seam? That is a fact question; the strip may be insubstantially different unless the patentee narrowed the term "pull tab" in prosecution.
step: Check estoppel and vitiation. If Ben amended the claim from "tear element" to "pull tab" to avoid prior art, Festo presumes he surrendered the strip, and the equivalents theory likely fails.

@mistakes
- Comparing the accused product to the commercial embodiment instead of to the claims.
- Treating extra features in the accused product as avoiding infringement of a "comprising" claim.
- Using the doctrine of equivalents to ignore a limitation entirely.
- Forgetting that indirect infringement requires direct infringement and knowledge.

@practice self
prompt: A patent claims "a fastener comprising a threaded shaft and a head having a hexagonal recess." Rival sells a fastener with a threaded shaft and a head with a star-shaped recess that works the same way with the same tool results. Nothing in prosecution narrowed the claim. Analyze literal infringement and the doctrine of equivalents.
hint: Construe "hexagonal" first, then ask whether a star-shaped recess is an insubstantial difference.
solution: Construction: "hexagonal recess" has its ordinary meaning, a six-sided recess. Literal infringement: the star recess is not six-sided, so a limitation is missing and there is no literal infringement. Equivalents: apply the function-way-result or insubstantial-differences test element by element. A star recess may perform the same function (receiving a driver) in a different way (different geometry and tool) with possibly the same result, which is a fact question. The patentee must show insubstantial differences, and a court will ask whether finding equivalence would vitiate "hexagonal." Because the claim chose a specific shape, a court may find that treating other shapes as equivalent would erase the limitation. Without estoppel, the claim is possible but uncertain. Conclusion: no literal infringement; equivalents depends on proof and vitiation.
