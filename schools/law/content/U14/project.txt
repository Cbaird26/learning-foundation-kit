title: IP audit memo for Tidebright Labs, Inc.
overview: You are an associate advising an invented start-up, Tidebright Labs, Inc., on what in its products, brand and software can be protected, how to protect it, and where it faces infringement risk. You will write a 2,000-word memo to the supervising partner using the facts in the supplied materials. This is a training exercise on invented facts, not legal advice.
goal: Map each Tidebright asset to copyright, patent, trademark and trade secret protection and explain what is not protectable.
goal: Apply the key tests from the course (originality and ownership, fair use and substantial similarity, eligibility, novelty and nonobviousness, secrecy measures, distinctiveness and likelihood of confusion) to specific facts.
goal: Identify unsettled questions about software and AI openly and advise on the risk without predicting how courts will rule.
step: Read the company profile and the asset facts below. Make an inventory chart with one row per asset (device design, device sensor method, app code, onboarding screens and artwork, logo, name, tagline, calibration algorithm, customer list, AI model) and columns for each regime.
step: Copyright: for the app code, artwork and logo, decide authorship and ownership (employee, freelancer, joint) under 17 U.S.C. §§ 101 and 201 and § 204(a), and note any § 102(b) limits and the open AI-authorship and AI-training questions.
step: Patent: analyze the sensor method for eligibility under § 101 (Alice), novelty and the one-year grace period of § 102(b)(1) given the trade-show demo and pilot sales, nonobviousness under § 103 using the prior art provided, and § 112 enablement.
step: Trade secret: analyze the calibration algorithm and customer list for secrecy, reasonable measures and value, and compare patent versus secrecy for the sensor method, including the effect of the pilot sales and the whistleblower-notice rule.
step: Trademark: classify TIDEBRIGHT, the tagline and the logo on the distinctiveness spectrum, assess registrability, and apply the likelihood-of-confusion factors to the competitor's TIDEBRITE mark. Consider whether dilution is available.
step: Infringement risks: assess the open-source license issue, the scraped photographs used to train the AI model (apply the four fair-use factors and state what is unsettled), and any risk that Tidebright's own product infringes the competitor patent described in the materials.
step: Draft the memo with a short question presented, brief answers, facts, analysis by regime, and a prioritized recommendations list. Aim for about 2,000 words, then revise for accuracy, hedging unsettled law and removing any rule you cannot name a source for.
material: **Company profile.** Tidebright Labs, Inc. is a six-person start-up that sells TideTrack, a handheld water-quality sensor, with a companion mobile app. Three employees (full-time, salaried, under employment agreements with confidentiality terms but no invention-assignment clause and no whistleblower-immunity notice) write the app code. A freelancer, Priya, wrote the app's onboarding module and drew the logo under an email exchange that says only "please send the files when finished"; she was paid per project and used her own tools. No written assignment exists.
material: **Device and sensor method.** The device has an ornamental rounded-triangle housing designed by a founder. Its main technical feature is a method of reading dissolved-oxygen levels by passing a signal through a layered membrane and applying a temperature correction before display. The method is described in general terms in a technical blog post that Tidebright published on March 12, 2026, but the exact layer thicknesses and the correction coefficients are not. Facts and dates: the founders demonstrated the device and described the method in general terms at a public trade show on March 10, 2026; Tidebright began offering pilot units to paying customers (under confidentiality agreements) on July 1, 2026; the earliest filing date Tidebright could use is November 15, 2026. A rival's published 2022 patent application describes a layered membrane sensor without the temperature correction. A 2023 journal article describes applying a temperature correction to a different type of oxygen sensor. A competitor, Aquavolt, holds a patent whose independent claim requires "a membrane having at least three layers, a temperature sensor, and a display"; the TideTrack device has a four-layer membrane, a temperature sensor and a display.
material: **Software and AI.** The app code includes about 1,000 lines taken from an open-source library under a license that requires derivative works to be released under the same license. The calibration algorithm is a proprietary formula stored in Tidebright's private code repository; only the three developers can access it. Tidebright's customer list (about 40 pilot and retail customers, with their pricing terms) is kept in a shared spreadsheet that all six employees and Priya can open, and it is not marked confidential. Tidebright also trained a machine-learning model that classifies photographs of water samples. The training set included 20,000 photographs scraped from a stock-photo website, whose terms prohibit scraping, and 5,000 photographs taken by Tidebright. The model outputs a label (for example, "turbid") and never displays the training photographs. Tidebright has not decided how much of its app's interface text and screens were produced with a generative AI tool; one founder says the tagline was drafted by an AI tool and then edited.
material: **Brand.** Tidebright uses TIDEBRIGHT (a coined combination of two ordinary words) as its company and product name on devices and packaging, the tagline "Clear Water, Clear Mind," and a logo of a wave inside a triangle. It has sold in three states for eight months and has not filed any applications. A competitor, Aquavolt Inc., sells a pond-water test kit under TIDEBRITE in five other states, with similar blue packaging, and has been using the mark for two years. Tidebright has received two misdirected customer-service emails intended for Aquavolt.
deliverable: A memo of about 2,000 words addressed to the supervising partner, with headings for each regime and a prioritized recommendation list.
deliverable: An asset inventory chart (one page) mapping each asset to the protections available, the key risk and the first action.
rubric: Correctly applies the elements and cases for each regime to the supplied facts (for example, no assignment from Priya, the Alice two-step, the grace period dates, the all-elements rule, the secrecy measures, the Abercrombie spectrum, the confusion factors).
rubric: Identifies the dates that matter (trade-show disclosure, pilot sales, filing date) and states the grace-period consequence accurately.
rubric: Treats AI authorship, AI training and similar questions as unsettled, presents both sides neutrally and avoids predicting outcomes.
rubric: Gives practical, prioritized recommendations (for example, signed assignments, patent versus secrecy decision, trademark filings, license compliance).
rubric: Is organized, concise, uses source names (statutes and cases) accurately and invents no rules or holdings.
safety: All parties, products and facts are invented for this exercise. The memo is practice analysis and is not legal advice about any real situation.
tutor: Which facts in the Tidebright materials fall inside or outside the one-year grace period under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b)(1), and what does that do to the patent versus trade-secret choice?
tutor: How should I handle the scraped-photograph training question without predicting an outcome, and which fair-use factors will matter most?
tutor: Can you quiz me on how to apply the Polaroid factors to TIDEBRIGHT and TIDEBRITE and tell me where my analysis is thin?

@review
### Week by week

- **Week 1, why protect IP:** incentive theory; Art. I, § 8, cl. 8; four regimes (copyright, patent, trademark, trade secret); preemption (Bonito Boats, 17 U.S.C. § 301) and Dastar.
- **Week 2, copyright subject matter:** original works fixed in a medium (§ 102(a)); Feist; § 102(b) excludes ideas and methods; merger and scenes a faire; Star Athletica separability; human authorship (Thaler) and open AI questions.
- **Week 3, ownership, duration, rights:** author owns; work made for hire (CCNV v. Reid; nine categories plus signed writing); joint works; § 204(a) signed writing; life plus 70, or 95/120 years for hire; § 106 rights and first sale.
- **Week 4, infringement:** ownership plus copying; access and similarity; substantial similarity and filtration; secondary liability; § 512; remedies (§ 504, § 505).
- **Week 5, fair use:** four factors weighed together; Campbell; Warhol (purpose of the specific use, commercial licensing); Harper & Row; Google v. Oracle.
- **Week 6, copyright and technology:** § 117; Aereo; § 1201; AI training and outputs as unsettled.
- **Week 7, patent eligibility and utility:** § 101 categories; Alice/Mayo two steps; Myriad; utility (Brenner); natural-person inventor (Thaler v. Vidal).
- **Week 8, novelty, nonobviousness, disclosure:** § 102 first-inventor-to-file and grace period; Graham and KSR; § 112 (Amgen, Nautilus).
- **Week 9, claims and infringement:** claim construction; all-elements rule; equivalents and Festo; § 271(b) and (c); exhaustion; remedies.
- **Week 10, trade secrets:** DTSA and UTSA elements; improper versus proper means; remedies and § 1833(b) notice; patent versus secrecy.
- **Week 11, trademark distinctiveness and registration:** the spectrum; secondary meaning; Wal-Mart; § 1052 bars; registration benefits and incontestability.
- **Week 12, confusion and dilution:** Polaroid and Sleekcraft factors; famous-mark dilution; Jack Daniel's; defenses and remedies.

### Self-check

- Can I state each regime's subject matter, duration and cost in one sentence?
- Can I walk the Alice two-step and the Graham inquiries without notes?
- Can I tell anticipation from obviousness and literal infringement from equivalents?
- Can I weigh (not count) the fair-use and confusion factors?
- Can I say what is unsettled about AI and give both sides without predicting?

### Common errors

- Protecting an idea or method with copyright.
- Assuming the person who pays owns the copyright.
- Treating Warhol's "transformative" as automatic fair use.
- Applying the old rules after the AIA or forgetting the grace period is one year and domestic only.
- Using combined references to argue anticipation.
- Treating incontestability as immunity from a genericness challenge.
- Reaching for Rogers when a parody is used as a brand.

@exam mc
prompt: A start-up hires Priya, a freelance developer who uses her own computer and sets her own hours, to write an app module. They exchange emails but sign no agreement about ownership. The start-up ships the module in its app, and Priya later licenses her module to a competitor. The start-up sues for infringement. Who is likely to prevail?
choice: The start-up, because it paid for the module and so is the author.
choice: The start-up, because software written for hire is automatically a work made for hire.
choice*: Priya, because as an independent contractor with no signed writing she owns the copyright, and the start-up likely has only an implied nonexclusive license.
choice: Priya, because software is not protectable by copyright.
hint: Apply CCNV v. Reid and § 204(a).
solution: Priya is an independent contractor under the agency factors, and software is not clearly within the nine commissioned-work categories and there is no signed work-made-for-hire writing. She owns the copyright, and an exclusive transfer would need a signed writing. The start-up holds, at most, an implied nonexclusive license, which does not support the infringement claim.

@exam self
prompt: A client's start-up has (a) a new process for purifying water that cannot be detected by examining the finished product, which it sold to a customer 15 months ago in confidence, (b) a brand name TIDEBRIGHT used for 8 months in three states, and (c) a mobile app whose code was written by employees. Write a short memo section (about 250 words) that matches each asset to protection, identifies the main obstacle, and gives one recommended action.
hint: For (a), check the one-year grace period and the effect of a sale.
solution: (a) Process: a patent is likely barred because the confidential sale 15 months ago is "on sale" under § 102(a)(1) and outside the one-year grace period of § 102(b)(1) (Helsinn). The process is hard to reverse engineer, so trade secret protection is available if the client uses reasonable measures (agreements, access controls); the obstacle is independent discovery and loss through carelessness. Action: document and tighten confidentiality measures. (b) Brand: TIDEBRIGHT, a coined combination, is likely suggestive or fanciful and so inherently distinctive. Rights exist in the areas of use under § 43(a); registration gives nationwide priority and presumptions. The obstacle is an unsearched conflicting mark under § 1052(d). Action: run a clearance search and file an application. (c) App: employees' code within the scope of employment is a work made for hire, so the client owns the copyright (§ 201(b)) as expression, not the underlying ideas. The obstacle is any third-party or open-source code. Action: audit the code and confirm employee agreements. Overall: patent unavailable for (a); rely on secrecy.
rubric: Recognizes that the sale 15 months ago is outside the grace period, so a patent is likely unavailable, and recommends secrecy measures.
rubric: Classifies the mark correctly and recommends clearance and registration.
rubric: States that employee code is a work made for hire and that copyright does not cover ideas.
