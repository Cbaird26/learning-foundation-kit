@article
**The rule.** Under 35 U.S.C. § 101, "[w]hoever invents or discovers any new and useful process, machine, manufacture, or composition of matter, or any new and useful improvement thereof, may obtain a patent." The four categories are **process**, **machine**, **manufacture** and **composition of matter**. Congress intended patentable subject matter to be broad, and *Diamond v. Chakrabarty* (1980) held that a human-made, genetically engineered bacterium is a manufacture or composition of matter ("anything under the sun that is made by man"). Other patent types include **design patents** (§ 171) for ornamental designs of articles of manufacture, which last 15 years from grant for applications filed on or after May 13, 2015, and plant patents. A utility patent runs 20 years from the earliest effective filing date (§ 154).

### Judicial exceptions

Three exceptions are outside § 101: **laws of nature**, **natural phenomena** and **abstract ideas**. They are the basic tools of science and technology, and monopolizing them would impede innovation more than it promotes it. The risk is that every invention uses such things, so the exceptions cannot swallow the rule.

### The Alice/Mayo two-step

In *Alice Corp. v. CLS Bank International* (2014), applying *Mayo Collaborative Services v. Prometheus Laboratories, Inc.* (2012):

1. **Step one:** is the claim directed to a law of nature, natural phenomenon or abstract idea? (Examples of abstract ideas include fundamental economic practices, such as intermediated settlement (*Alice*) and hedging (*Bilski v. Kappos* (2010)); the Court also held in *Bilski* that business methods are not categorically excluded and that the machine-or-transformation test is a useful clue but not the sole test.)
2. **Step two:** if so, does the claim contain an **inventive concept**, meaning additional elements sufficient to transform the claim into a patent-eligible application, and not merely well-understood, routine, conventional activity? Adding "apply it on a computer" or a generic computer does not suffice.

In *Mayo*, a method of adjusting a drug dose based on measured metabolite levels was directed to a law of nature and added only routine steps, so it was ineligible. In *Diamond v. Diehr* (1981), a process for curing rubber that used a known mathematical formula was eligible because the claim as a whole was an industrial process. In *Association for Molecular Pathology v. Myriad Genetics, Inc.* (2013), a naturally occurring DNA segment is a product of nature even if isolated, so it is ineligible, but complementary DNA (cDNA), which is not naturally occurring, is eligible. The Federal Circuit has held that claims directed to an improvement in computer functionality, such as a self-referential table that changes how a database operates (*Enfish, LLC v. Microsoft Corp.* (2016)), are not abstract at step one. Section 101 law remains contested, and reasonable observers disagree about its scope for software, diagnostics and AI; the point to know is the framework.

### Utility

An invention must be **useful**. The utility must be **specific, substantial and credible**. In *Brenner v. Manson* (1966), a process for making a chemical was not patentable because the applicant showed no specific benefit; a patent is not a hunting license for research with an unknown use. Total inoperability fails the requirement, but a small benefit is enough if real.

### Inventorship and AI

An inventor must be a natural person. *Thaler v. Vidal* (Fed. Cir. 2022) held that the Patent Act's "individual" means a natural person, so an AI system cannot be named as an inventor. USPTO guidance has addressed AI-assisted inventions and has changed over time. The open questions are how to measure a person's contribution to conception when AI generates candidate solutions, and what contribution suffices. State the rule that a natural person must have conceived the invention (under the general inventorship case law) and describe the uncertainty neutrally.

### Applying it

Take the claim and ask: (1) Is it a process, machine, manufacture or composition? (2) Is it directed to a law of nature, natural phenomenon or abstract idea (step one)? (3) If so, is there an inventive concept (step two)? (4) Is utility specific, substantial and credible? Note the issue, the rule, a claim-by-claim application and a conclusion.

@example
title: Software claim under Alice
problem: Claim 1 recites: "A method comprising: receiving on a computer the incomes and debts of a loan applicant; calculating on the computer a ratio of debt to income; and displaying on a screen a rating based on whether the ratio exceeds a threshold." Is it eligible?
step: The claim is a process, within § 101's categories, so move to the exceptions.
step: Step one: the claim is directed to comparing a debt-to-income ratio with a threshold, a longstanding financial practice and mathematical concept, so it is directed to an abstract idea.
step: Step two: the computer receives, calculates and displays using generic functions, which are well-understood, routine and conventional. Implementing the idea on a generic computer is not an inventive concept.
step: Conclusion: likely ineligible under Alice. A claim specifying a technical improvement in how the computer operates, as in Enfish, would be analyzed differently.

@mistakes
- Saying all software or all business methods are ineligible; Alice and Bilski reject categorical rules.
- Stopping at step one without applying the inventive-concept step.
- Treating "apply it on a computer" as an inventive concept.
- Confusing utility with commercial success; the utility requirement is only that the invention be specifically, substantially and credibly useful.
