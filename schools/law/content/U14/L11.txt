@article
### What a trademark is

A **trademark** is any word, name, symbol or device (or combination) used by a person to identify and distinguish their goods and indicate their source (15 U.S.C. § 1127). A **service mark** does the same for services. Trademark law serves consumers by reducing search costs and protects the owner's goodwill. Rights arise from **use in commerce**, not creation. Federal registration is optional but valuable. Unregistered marks are protected under § 43(a) of the Lanham Act (15 U.S.C. § 1125(a)) and state law, usually within the area of use and reputation.

### The distinctiveness spectrum

Courts classify word marks by how closely they relate to the product (the *Abercrombie & Fitch Co. v. Hunting World, Inc.* (2d Cir. 1976) spectrum):

| Category | Example (invented) | Protection |
|---|---|---|
| **Generic** | "Cola" for soft drinks | Never protectable or registrable |
| **Descriptive** | "Cozy Quilts" for quilts | Only with **secondary meaning** (acquired distinctiveness) |
| **Suggestive** | "Quilt Hearth" hinting at warmth | Inherently distinctive |
| **Arbitrary** | "Orchard" for laptops | Inherently distinctive |
| **Fanciful** | "Kryzon" for batteries | Inherently distinctive; strongest |

A descriptive term conveys an ingredient, quality, characteristic, function or purpose of the goods immediately. A suggestive term requires imagination to link to the goods. **Secondary meaning** exists when the primary significance of the term in the minds of consumers is the producer, not the product; evidence includes advertising, sales, length and exclusivity of use, unsolicited media coverage and consumer surveys. A term may be generic when consumers primarily understand it as the name of a type of product. In *USPTO v. Booking.com B.V.* (2020), the Court held that "Booking.com" was not generic as a whole, because consumers did not understand it as a name for a class of services, rejecting a rule that adding ".com" to a generic term is always generic. Genericness depends on consumer perception.

### Trade dress, product design and color

**Trade dress** is the overall look or packaging of a product or service. In *Two Pesos, Inc. v. Taco Cabana, Inc.* (1992), restaurant décor could be inherently distinctive. But in *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Samara Brothers, Inc.* (2000), **product design** trade dress can never be inherently distinctive and needs secondary meaning, because consumers do not usually see product design as a source identifier; product packaging may be inherently distinctive. In *Qualitex Co. v. Jacobson Products Co.* (1995), a color can be a mark if it has acquired secondary meaning. A **functional** feature cannot be protected as a mark (*TrafFix Devices, Inc. v. Marketing Displays, Inc.* (2001)); for unregistered trade dress, the claimant bears the burden of proving nonfunctionality (§ 1125(a)(3)).

### Registration

An applicant may register a mark on the Principal Register based on **use in commerce** (§ 1051(a)) or a bona fide **intent to use** (§ 1051(b)). Section 1052 lists bars to registration, including:

- a mark likely to cause confusion with a previously registered or used mark (§ 1052(d));
- a mark that is **merely descriptive** or **deceptively misdescriptive**, primarily geographically descriptive, or primarily merely a surname (§ 1052(e)), unless it has acquired distinctiveness under § 1052(f) (five years of substantially exclusive and continuous use is prima facie evidence);
- functional matter (§ 1052(e)(5)).

The Court struck down the bar on **disparaging** marks in *Matal v. Tam* (2017) and the bar on **immoral or scandalous** marks in *Iancu v. Brunetti* (2019), both as viewpoint-based. In *Vidal v. Elster* (2024), it upheld the "names clause" in § 1052(c), which bars registering a mark that is the name of a living person without consent, as applied to a mark criticizing a public figure; the Justices differed in their reasoning.

### Benefits and maintenance

Registration on the Principal Register gives: **constructive notice** of the claim of ownership, **prima facie evidence** of validity, ownership and exclusive right to use (§ 1057(b)), and **nationwide constructive-use priority** as of the filing date once registered (§ 1057(c)). After **five consecutive years** of continuous use following registration, the owner may file for **incontestability** (§ 1065), which makes the registration conclusive evidence of the right to use, subject to defenses; an incontestable mark cannot be challenged as merely descriptive (*Park 'N Fly, Inc. v. Dollar Park & Fly, Inc.* (1985)), but it can still be cancelled as generic, among other grounds (§ 1064(3)). The registrant must file a **declaration of use between the fifth and sixth year** (§ 1058) and **renew every 10 years** (§ 1059).

Priority among users generally goes to the first to use, with registration affecting later users.

### Applying it

Classify the mark relative to the goods (what it says about the goods), identify whether secondary meaning is needed and shown, check functionality for trade dress, and check § 1052 bars. Conclude on protectability, registrability and the remedy for any weakness.

@example
title: Classifying and registering
problem: Ana opens a tea shop and wants to register "Hot Tea Shop" for retail tea services, and also "Kryzon" for her branded tea blends. She has used "Hot Tea Shop" for eight months. Advise.
step: "Hot Tea Shop" describes the services, so it is generic or at best merely descriptive. A generic name can never be registered.
step: If merely descriptive, registration requires secondary meaning under § 1052(f). Eight months of use and no consumer-recognition evidence is likely insufficient, so the examiner would refuse under § 1052(e)(1).
step: "Kryzon" is a coined term with no meaning for tea, so it is fanciful and inherently distinctive. It can be registered without proof of secondary meaning, subject to a search for confusingly similar marks under § 1052(d).
step: Advice: build the brand on Kryzon, consider an intent-to-use application, and avoid relying on the descriptive shop name.

@mistakes
- Calling a term descriptive or suggestive based on whether it sounds clever instead of what it conveys about the goods.
- Requiring secondary meaning for arbitrary or fanciful marks.
- Treating product design as inherently distinctive; it needs secondary meaning (Wal-Mart).
- Treating incontestability as immunity from every challenge; genericness and other grounds remain.
