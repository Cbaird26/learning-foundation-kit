@article
Lemon juice tastes sour. Soap feels slippery. These are clues to two important groups of compounds.

### Acids and bases

An **acid** is a substance that releases hydrogen ions ($\mathrm{H^+}$) when dissolved in water. Acids taste sour, turn some indicators red or pink, and react with metals and with baking soda. Examples are vinegar (acetic acid), lemon juice (citric acid) and stomach acid. A **base** releases hydroxide ions ($\mathrm{OH^-}$) in water, tastes bitter, feels slippery and turns indicators blue or green. Examples are baking soda solution, soap and ammonia cleaner. **Never taste unknown chemicals** in the lab or at home.

### The pH scale

The **pH scale** tells how acidic or basic a solution is, usually from $0$ to $14$:

- $\mathrm{pH} < 7$ is **acidic**,
- $\mathrm{pH} = 7$ is **neutral** (pure water),
- $\mathrm{pH} > 7$ is **basic**.

The scale is **logarithmic**. A change of one pH unit means the hydrogen ion concentration changes by a factor of $10$. Lemon juice (pH about $2$) is $10^{3} = 1000$ times more acidic than a solution with pH $5$. The pH is related to the hydrogen ion concentration $[\mathrm{H^+}]$ (in mol/L) by

$$\mathrm{pH} = -\log_{10}[\mathrm{H^+}].$$

So a solution with $[\mathrm{H^+}] = 10^{-3}$ mol/L has pH $3$.

### Indicators and red-cabbage juice

An **indicator** changes color depending on pH. You can make one at home, with an adult helping, by soaking chopped red cabbage in warm tap water (never boiling) and keeping the purple liquid. It turns:

| Color | Approximate pH |
|---|---|
| Red or pink | 1 to 3 (strong acid) |
| Purple-pink | 4 to 6 |
| Purple | 7 (neutral) |
| Blue | 8 to 9 |
| Blue-green to green | 10 to 12 |
| Yellow-green | 13 to 14 |

To compare liquids, put a little of each in its own clear cup with equal amounts of cabbage juice and rank the colors from red to yellow. Colorless liquids work best, so you can see the indicator color. Always use clean cups and a different spoon for each, and wear eye protection, since even household cleaners can irritate eyes.

### Neutralization

When an acid and a base are mixed, they can cancel each other's effects and make water and a salt. This is called **neutralization**. It is why antacid tablets, which contain bases, relieve acid indigestion.

@example
title: From hydrogen ion concentration to pH
problem: A solution has $[\mathrm{H^+}] = 1 \times 10^{-4}$ mol/L. What is its pH, and is it acidic, neutral or basic?
step: Use $\mathrm{pH} = -\log_{10}[\mathrm{H^+}]$.
step: $\log_{10}(10^{-4}) = -4$, so $\mathrm{pH} = -(-4) = 4$.
step: Since $4 < 7$, the solution is acidic. With red-cabbage juice it would look purple-pink.

@mistakes
- Thinking pH $4$ is only a little more acidic than pH $5$. Each unit is a factor of $10$.
- Believing that bases are not dangerous because they are not sour. Strong bases, like drain cleaner, are as hazardous as strong acids.
- Treating a lower pH as a more basic solution. Low pH means more acidic.
- Judging by color in a colored liquid, such as a juice, instead of choosing colorless test liquids.
