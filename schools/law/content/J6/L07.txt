@article
A **force** is a push or a pull. Forces are measured in **newtons (N)**. A force has a size and a direction, so we often draw it as an arrow. Common forces include gravity, friction, the normal force from a surface, tension in a rope and applied pushes.

### Net force

Several forces can act on one object at the same time. The **net force** is their combined effect. Forces in the same direction add. Forces in opposite directions subtract. If a box is pushed right with $50\ \mathrm{N}$ while friction pulls left with $20\ \mathrm{N}$, the net force is $30\ \mathrm{N}$ to the right. **Balanced forces** give a net force of zero. **Unbalanced forces** change an object's motion.

### Newton's first law: inertia

An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion keeps moving at constant velocity (the same speed in a straight line), unless an unbalanced force acts on it. This tendency to keep doing what it is already doing is called **inertia**. More mass means more inertia. This is why passengers lurch forward when a car brakes suddenly: the car stops, but the passengers' bodies keep moving.

### Newton's second law: $F = ma$

The acceleration of an object depends on the net force and on its mass:

$$F_{\text{net}} = ma \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad a = \frac{F_{\text{net}}}{m}$$

With mass in kilograms and acceleration in $\mathrm{m/s^2}$, force comes out in newtons ($1\ \mathrm{N} = 1\ \mathrm{kg\,m/s^2}$). Pushing with twice the force gives twice the acceleration. The same push on twice the mass gives half the acceleration.

**Weight** is the force of gravity on an object: $W = mg$, where $g \approx 9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ near Earth's surface. Mass (in kg) is how much matter an object has. Weight (in N) is a force. Your mass is the same on the Moon, but your weight is smaller.

### Newton's third law: action and reaction

When one object pushes on a second object, the second pushes back on the first with a force of the same size in the opposite direction. These forces act on **different objects**, so they do not cancel. When you push a wall, the wall pushes back on you. A swimmer pushes water backward and the water pushes the swimmer forward.

@example
title: Using F = ma
problem: A $1200\ \mathrm{kg}$ car has a net forward force of $3000\ \mathrm{N}$. What is its acceleration?
step: Write the second law: $a = F_{\text{net}} / m$.
step: Substitute: $a = 3000 \div 1200$.
step: $a = 2.5\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ in the direction of the net force.

@mistakes
- Adding forces without paying attention to direction. Opposite forces subtract.
- Thinking that action and reaction forces cancel. They act on different objects.
- Confusing mass and weight. Mass is in kilograms; weight is a force in newtons.
- Believing that a moving object needs a constant net force to keep moving. At constant velocity the net force is zero.

@practice self
prompt: Explain with Newton's first law why passengers wearing no seat belts move forward when a car stops suddenly. Then explain what a seat belt does in terms of forces.
solution: Passengers and the car move forward together at the same velocity. When the brakes stop the car, the passengers have inertia and, with no unbalanced force on them, they keep moving forward at the same velocity until something stops them, such as the dashboard. A seat belt applies a backward force on the passenger's body, an unbalanced force that slows the person along with the car.
hint: Ask what force acts on the car and whether any force acts on the passenger.
rubric: States that the passenger keeps moving because of inertia.
rubric: Says a force is needed to change the passenger's motion.
rubric: Describes the seat belt as supplying the backward force on the passenger.
rubric: Uses the term inertia or Newton's first law correctly.
