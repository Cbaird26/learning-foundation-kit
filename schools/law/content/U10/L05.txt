@article
An **injunction** is a court order commanding or forbidding conduct, enforced by contempt. It is the central equitable remedy. A **prohibitory** injunction forbids conduct; a **mandatory** injunction compels action and is harder to get, because it changes rather than preserves the status quo. Injunctions come in three stages: the **temporary restraining order (TRO)**, the **preliminary injunction**, and the **permanent injunction** entered after trial.

### The preliminary injunction: the Winter test

A preliminary injunction preserves the status quo while the case is decided. Under *Winter v. Natural Resources Defense Council* (2008), the movant must establish all four factors:

1. a **likelihood of success on the merits**;
2. that it is **likely to suffer irreparable harm** in the absence of preliminary relief (a mere "possibility" of irreparable harm is not enough);
3. that the **balance of equities** tips in its favor; and
4. that an injunction is in the **public interest**.

The Court called a preliminary injunction an extraordinary remedy never awarded as of right. Some circuits retain a "sliding scale" or "serious questions" variant in which a strong showing on one factor can offset a weaker one, but irreparable harm remains a requirement. **Irreparable harm** means harm that cannot be adequately remedied by money damages later: loss of unique property, loss of goodwill or market position that is hard to quantify, or a constitutional violation (loss of First Amendment freedoms, "for even minimal periods of time," is irreparable injury under *Elrod v. Burns* (1976)). Purely monetary loss that can be compensated by damages is ordinarily not irreparable.

### The permanent injunction: the eBay test

After winning on the merits, a plaintiff seeking a permanent injunction must show: (1) irreparable injury; (2) that legal remedies are inadequate; (3) that the balance of hardships favors the plaintiff; and (4) that the public interest would not be disserved (*eBay Inc. v. MercExchange, L.L.C.* (2006)). The Court rejected categorical rules: a prevailing patent holder is not automatically entitled to an injunction, and a court may not deny one just because the patentee does not practice the patent.

### FRCP 65 mechanics

- **Notice.** A preliminary injunction requires notice to the adverse party (Rule 65(a)). A **TRO** may issue without notice only if specific facts show immediate and irreparable injury before the adverse party can be heard, and the movant's attorney certifies in writing the efforts made to give notice (Rule 65(b)(1)).
- **Duration.** An ex parte TRO expires at the time set by the court, not to exceed **14 days**, unless extended for good cause for a like period or the adverse party consents to a longer extension (Rule 65(b)(2)).
- **Security.** The court may issue a preliminary injunction or TRO only if the movant gives security in an amount the court considers proper to pay the costs and damages sustained by anyone wrongfully enjoined (Rule 65(c)).
- **Form and scope.** Every injunction must state its reasons, state its terms specifically, and describe in reasonable detail the acts restrained or required (Rule 65(d)(1)). It binds the parties, their officers, agents, servants, employees and attorneys, and other persons in active concert with them who receive actual notice (Rule 65(d)(2)).

### Limits on federal equitable power

*Grupo Mexicano de Desarrollo, S.A. v. Alliance Bond Fund, Inc.* (1999) held that a federal court has no power to issue a preliminary injunction freezing a defendant's assets to protect a potential money judgment on a **legal** claim. The Court looked to the equitable powers federal courts historically held. By contrast, a plaintiff who asserts an equitable interest in specific assets may seek to preserve them.

**Trump v. CASA, Inc.** (2025) addressed the scope of relief. The Court held that universal injunctions (orders that bar enforcement of a policy against everyone, not just the plaintiffs) likely exceed the equitable authority Congress gave federal courts under the Judiciary Act of 1789, because relief should be no broader than necessary to give the plaintiffs **complete relief**. The Court did not decide whether Rule 23(b)(2) class actions or APA vacatur can provide broader protection, and plaintiffs now often seek class certification or argue that complete relief to a particular plaintiff requires broader terms.

### Applying the rules (IRAC)

State the stage (TRO, preliminary or permanent), list the four factors, apply each to the facts with the strongest evidence first, and then check the scope of the order against complete relief and Rule 65(d). A strong merits showing cannot make up for the absence of likely irreparable harm.

@example
title: Applying Winter to a restaurant lease dispute
problem: Landlord threatens to lock out Tenant, a restaurant that holds a lease with 5 years remaining. Tenant moves for a preliminary injunction. Tenant shows a strong claim that Landlord has no valid ground to terminate, that a lockout would destroy its customer base and goodwill, and that Landlord would lose only a few weeks of a different tenant's rent if the lockout is delayed. Should the court issue the injunction?
step: Likelihood of success: Tenant shows a strong claim, so factor one favors Tenant.
step: Irreparable harm: loss of customer base and goodwill is hard to measure in money, so harm is likely and irreparable; factor two favors Tenant.
step: Balance of equities: Landlord loses only a delay in rent from another tenant, which is compensable, while Tenant loses its business; factor three favors Tenant.
step: Public interest: preserving a business and enforcing leases as written is neutral or favorable. The court should issue the injunction and require security under Rule 65(c).

@mistakes
- Treating a "possibility" of irreparable harm as enough, or letting a strong merits showing replace proof of likely irreparable harm.
- Omitting the Rule 65(c) security, or forgetting the 14-day limit on an ex parte TRO.
- Granting a nationwide injunction to a few plaintiffs without asking what relief is necessary to give them complete relief.
