@article
**Specific performance** is an equitable order directing a party to perform a contract. It is the contract analogue of an injunction. It is discretionary, and it is the exception: damages are the ordinary remedy for breach.

### The requirement: inadequate legal remedy

Specific performance is available only when damages would be an inadequate substitute for performance (Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 359). Section 360 lists factors: the difficulty of proving damages with reasonable certainty, the difficulty of procuring a suitable substitute, and the likelihood that an award of damages could not be collected. Applications:

- **Land.** Every parcel is treated as unique, so a buyer (and, in most states, a seller) can obtain specific performance of a contract for sale of land.
- **Goods.** Under UCC § 2-716(1), specific performance may be decreed where the goods are unique "or in other proper circumstances," for example where the buyer cannot reasonably cover. Standard goods available from many suppliers do not qualify, because the buyer can cover and recover damages.
- **Personal services.** Courts do not order a person to perform services, because of the supervision problem and the policy against involuntary servitude. A court may, however, enjoin a person with unique services from working for a competitor during the term, by a **negative injunction** (*Lumley v. Wagner* (1852)), when the contract's negative covenant is reasonable and damages would not be adequate.

### Other conditions

The terms must be **certain** enough for the court to frame an order (§ 362). The plaintiff must be **ready, willing and able** to perform its own obligations. The old requirement of mutuality of remedy has been abandoned. Courts also weigh **hardship** and the burden of supervising performance. If the seller can convey only part of the property, the buyer may usually take what the seller can convey with a proportional price reduction (**abatement**), unless the shortfall is a substantial part of the bargain.

### Equitable defenses

Because equity is discretionary, courts recognize defenses not available at law. They bar or limit coercive relief and restitution in equity:

- **Laches**: unreasonable delay in asserting a right that prejudices the defendant. It has no fixed period and is a defense to equitable relief; where Congress has set a limitations period for a damages claim, laches cannot bar the claim within that period (*Petrella v. Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer, Inc.* (2014); *SCA Hygiene Products v. First Quality Baby Products* (2017)).
- **Unclean hands**: the plaintiff's own inequitable conduct relating directly to the matter at issue (*Precision Instrument Manufacturing Co. v. Automotive Maintenance Machinery Co.* (1945)). It is not a punishment for unrelated misconduct.
- **Estoppel**: the plaintiff's words or conduct led the defendant to rely to its detriment.
- **Unconscionability, misrepresentation, mistake and undue hardship**: equity may refuse to enforce a bargain that is grossly unfair or that was procured unfairly.

### Equity and the jury

Equitable claims are tried to the judge. Where legal and equitable claims share common fact issues, the legal claims are decided by the jury first (*Beacon Theatres, Inc. v. Westover* (1959)).

### Preliminary injunction practice

In a contract case, the four *Winter* factors (Week 5) apply to a request for a preliminary injunction enforcing a negative covenant. Suppose a distributor agrees not to sell a rival brand during the term, and the manufacturer seeks a preliminary injunction when the distributor starts. Success on the merits depends on the covenant's validity; irreparable harm depends on loss of brand position and goodwill that cannot easily be measured; the balance of equities weighs the distributor's loss of income against the manufacturer's loss of control over its channel; the public interest concerns competition.

### Applying the rules (IRAC)

Ask: is there a valid, certain contract? Are damages inadequate? Is the plaintiff ready, willing and able? Is any equitable defense available? Is the decree enforceable without excessive supervision? Then compute any abatement or alternative damages.

title: Specific performance with abatement
problem: Seller agrees to sell 100 acres of uniform farmland for \$400,000. Seller turns out to own only 90 acres. Buyer wants specific performance of what Seller can convey. What price should Buyer pay?
step: Land is unique, so specific performance is available, and Buyer may take what Seller can convey with a price reduction.
step: The price per acre is $400{,}000/100 = 4{,}000$.
step: Abated price: $90 \times 4{,}000 = 360{,}000$.
step: Buyer pays \$360,000 for 90 acres, assuming the land is uniform in value.

@mistakes
- Awarding specific performance of a contract for standard goods that the buyer could buy elsewhere.
- Ordering an individual to perform personal services rather than considering a negative injunction.
- Using unclean hands for any misconduct of the plaintiff, rather than misconduct directly connected with the subject of the claim.
