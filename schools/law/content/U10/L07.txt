@article
**Punitive damages** (exemplary damages) are not compensation. They punish a defendant for egregious conduct and deter the defendant and others. Because they are a civil sanction imposed without the protections of criminal procedure, the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment limits them.

### When punitive damages are available

In tort, punitive damages are generally available when the defendant acted with **malice, fraud, oppression, or reckless or conscious indifference** to the plaintiff's rights; ordinary negligence is not enough, and many states require proof by clear and convincing evidence. In federal civil rights law, punitive damages are available under § 1983 on proof of reckless or callous indifference to federally protected rights (*Smith v. Wade* (1983)). In **contract**, punitive damages are generally not recoverable for breach, even a willful one (Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 355), except where the breach is also an independent tort. Many states cap punitive damages by statute, and some bar insurance for them or send a share to the state.

### The three guideposts: BMW v. Gore

*BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore* (1996) held a grossly excessive punitive award violates due process and set three guideposts:

1. the **degree of reprehensibility** of the defendant's conduct;
2. the **ratio** between the punitive award and the actual or potential harm to the plaintiff (compensatory damages); and
3. the **difference between the award and civil or criminal penalties** authorized or imposed for comparable misconduct.

The first guidepost is the most important. *State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co. v. Campbell* (2003) identified factors: whether the harm was physical rather than economic; whether the conduct showed indifference to or reckless disregard of the health or safety of others; whether the target was financially vulnerable; whether the conduct involved repeated actions rather than an isolated incident; and whether the harm resulted from intentional malice, trickery or deceit rather than mere accident. The presence of one factor may not sustain an award, and the absence of all of them makes an award suspect.

### Ratios

*State Farm* said that few awards exceeding a **single-digit ratio** between punitive and compensatory damages will satisfy due process, and that a ratio of about **4 to 1** may be close to the line, while a higher ratio may comport with due process where a particularly egregious act produced only a small amount of economic damage. When compensatory damages are substantial, a lesser ratio, perhaps only **1 to 1**, can reach the outermost limit. These are not bright-line caps. In maritime law, *Exxon Shipping Co. v. Baker* (2008) adopted a 1 to 1 ratio as the maximum under federal maritime common law, a rule of federal common law rather than a due process rule.

### Other limits

- **Nonparty harm.** A jury may consider harm to others to decide how reprehensible the conduct was, but may not use punitive damages to punish the defendant directly for harm to **nonparties** (*Philip Morris USA v. Williams* (2007)).
- **Out-of-state conduct.** A state may not punish a defendant for conduct that was lawful where it occurred or for dissimilar acts unrelated to the plaintiff's harm (*State Farm*; *Gore*).
- **Defendant's wealth.** Wealth cannot justify an otherwise unconstitutional award.
- **Review.** Courts of appeals review the constitutionality of a punitive award **de novo** (*Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Leatherman Tool Group, Inc.* (2001)), and states must provide meaningful judicial review (*Honda Motor Co. v. Oberg* (1994)). A court that finds an award excessive may order a **remittitur**, offering the plaintiff a reduced award or a new trial.
- **Statutory caps.** Title VII caps the sum of compensatory (future pecuniary and noneconomic) and punitive damages by employer size; for an employer with 201 to 500 employees the cap is \$200,000 (42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(3)(C)). Back pay is outside the cap.

### Applying the rules (IRAC)

Identify the award and compensatory damages. Go through the three guideposts. Compute the ratio. Check for nonparty and out-of-state punishment. Conclude whether the award is grossly excessive and what ratio the court should reduce it to.

@example
title: Evaluating an award under the guideposts
problem: A jury awards \$250,000 in compensatory damages (largely for emotional distress) and \$3,000,000 in punitive damages against an insurer that delayed paying a valid claim once, causing economic harm and distress, but no physical harm. Evaluate the punitive award.
step: Reprehensibility: the harm was economic and emotional, not physical; the conduct was an isolated delay, with some deceit but no threat to safety. This is reprehensible but only moderately so.
step: Ratio: $3{,}000{,}000/250{,}000 = 12$. That is a double-digit ratio, and the compensatory award is already substantial, which points toward a lower ratio, perhaps near 1 to 1.
step: Civil penalties: comparable regulatory fines for claim-handling violations are far smaller than \$3 million.
step: Conclusion: the award is likely grossly excessive, and the court should reduce it. Even a single-digit ratio would be \$2,250,000 at the top end, and with substantial compensatory damages the outermost limit may be nearer \$250,000.

@example
title: Capped combined damages
problem: A jury awards a Title VII plaintiff \$60,000 for emotional distress and \$400,000 in punitive damages against an employer with 350 employees. Using the \$200,000 combined cap for that size of employer, what may the plaintiff recover for these items?
step: The cap applies to the sum of compensatory (noneconomic) and punitive damages: $60{,}000 + 400{,}000 = 460{,}000$.
step: The sum exceeds the \$200,000 cap for an employer with 201 to 500 employees.
step: Recovery is limited to \$200,000 for these items. Back pay would be recoverable in addition.

@mistakes
- Treating the single-digit ratio as a fixed cap rather than a guide that depends on reprehensibility and the size of the compensatory award.
- Letting the jury punish the defendant for harm to nonparties, instead of limiting that evidence to reprehensibility.
- Counting the defendant's wealth as justification for a larger award.
