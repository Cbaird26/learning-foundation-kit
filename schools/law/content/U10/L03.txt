@article
**Restitution** returns to the plaintiff a gain the defendant should not keep. Damages ask what the plaintiff lost; restitution asks what the defendant gained. The two can differ sharply, and that gap is where remedies strategy lives.

### The principle and its elements

Under Restatement (Third) of Restitution and Unjust Enrichment § 1, a person who is unjustly enriched at the expense of another is subject to liability in restitution. Break that into three elements:

1. The defendant received a **benefit** (money, services, property, or a saved expense).
2. The benefit was obtained **at the plaintiff's expense**.
3. Keeping the benefit without paying is **unjust**: the plaintiff did not confer it as a gift, the defendant did not bargain for it under a valid contract, and the circumstances (mistake, fraud, duress, necessity, wrongdoing) make retention inequitable.

Older cases call the claim **quasi-contract** or **contract implied in law**. It is not a real contract: no promise is needed, and the law imposes the obligation to prevent unjust enrichment. Do not confuse it with a contract implied in fact, which rests on conduct showing actual agreement.

### Limits

- **Volunteers and officious intermeddlers.** A person who confers an unrequested benefit without necessity usually cannot recover. An exception covers **emergency services**: a professional (a doctor, say) who renders necessary services to someone unable to consent is generally entitled to restitution of reasonable value (Restatement (Third) § 20).
- **Express contract.** When a valid, enforceable contract governs the subject matter, restitution is generally unavailable for the same performance. If the plaintiff fully performed and only money is owed, the plaintiff is limited to the contract price (Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 373(2)).
- **Change of position.** A good-faith recipient is protected to the extent that, relying on the receipt, it changed its position so that repayment would be inequitable (Restatement (Third) § 65). Spending money it would have spent anyway does not qualify. A conscious wrongdoer cannot use the defense.

### Measuring restitution

- **Services:** the reasonable (market) value of the services, less any payments already received.
- **Money paid by mistake or on a failed contract:** the amount paid, often with interest.
- **Disgorgement of profit:** where the defendant took the plaintiff's property or opportunity through wrongdoing, the remedy may be the defendant's net profit attributable to the wrong, not the plaintiff's loss (Restatement (Third) § 51). Profit is net of legitimate costs and limited to what the wrong caused. A conscious wrongdoer gets less credit than an innocent one, and the rules vary by jurisdiction.

### Restitution after breach

A **nonbreaching** party may sue for expectation or, alternatively, for restitution of benefits conferred (Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 373), which is useful when expectation is hard to prove. A **breaching** party who partly performed was traditionally barred; the modern view lets that party recover the benefit conferred in excess of the damages it caused (§ 374), following the reasoning of *Britton v. Turner* (1834). The rule does not apply to a willful, material default in some courts.

### Choosing between damages and restitution

Ask four questions: (1) Which measure is larger on these facts? (2) Can each be proved with reasonable certainty? (3) Is the defendant's conduct wrongful enough to support disgorgement? (4) Does restitution bring advantages such as an equitable remedy (Week 4) with priority in bankruptcy? When the defendant's profit exceeds the plaintiff's loss, restitution usually wins; when the plaintiff's loss exceeds the defendant's gain, damages usually win.

### Applying the rules (IRAC)

Identify the benefit, who bore the cost, and why retention is unjust, then compute the measure the facts support. State the alternatives and why you chose one.

@example
title: Expectation versus restitution on a failed house sale
problem: Buyer agrees to buy a house for \$300,000 and pays a \$30,000 deposit. Seller wrongfully refuses to close. The house is worth \$340,000. Compare Buyer's expectation damages with restitution of the deposit.
step: Expectation: Buyer would have had a \$340,000 house for \$300,000, with \$30,000 already paid. Her net position is $340{,}000 - (300{,}000 - 30{,}000) = 70{,}000$.
step: Check: loss in value of the performance is $340{,}000 - 300{,}000 = 40{,}000$, plus the return of the \$30,000 deposit she paid on a house she will not receive, giving \$70,000.
step: Restitution of the deposit alone is \$30,000 (plus interest).
step: Expectation (\$70,000) is greater, so Buyer should sue for damages. If the market price were below \$300,000, restitution of the deposit might be the better claim.

@mistakes
- Recovering a benefit conferred under a valid express contract on a quasi-contract theory when only the contract price is owed.
- Treating every benefit as unjust: a volunteer or officious intermeddler has no claim, while an emergency professional may.
- Applying the change-of-position defense when the recipient merely spent money it would have spent anyway, or when the recipient is a conscious wrongdoer.

@practice self
prompt: Plaintiff's trade secret is misused by a competitor, who earns \$90,000 in net profit attributable to the secret. Plaintiff proves its own lost profit at only \$30,000. Plaintiff asks you whether to sue for damages or restitution. Write a short advice memo section analyzing the choice.
solution: Issue: which measure better serves the client. Rule: damages measure the plaintiff's loss; restitution measures the defendant's gain, and disgorgement of net profit attributable to the wrong is available against a wrongdoer (Restatement (Third) of Restitution § 51), subject to proving which profit the wrong caused and deducting legitimate costs. Application: the competitor's attributable profit (\$90,000) is three times Plaintiff's lost profit (\$30,000). The claim fits restitution because the competitor obtained the benefit by wrongdoing, so no change-of-position defense is available. The burden is to prove attributable net profit; the competitor will try to apportion some profit to its own efforts. Conclusion: seek disgorgement of the \$90,000, pleading damages in the alternative, and consider equitable relief (a constructive trust over traceable proceeds) because it gives priority if the competitor is insolvent.
