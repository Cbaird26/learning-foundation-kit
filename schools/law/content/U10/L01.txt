@article
A **remedy** is what a court gives a winning plaintiff. Before you ask what the plaintiff proved, ask what the plaintiff can get. Remedies fall into four families: **damages** (money measured by loss), **restitution** (money or property measured by the defendant's gain), **coercive equitable relief** (injunctions and specific performance, which command conduct), and **declaratory relief** (a statement of rights). Damages are legal remedies, tried to a jury where the Seventh Amendment applies; equitable remedies are decided by the judge and are available only when the legal remedy is inadequate.

### The compensatory principle

Compensatory damages aim to put the plaintiff where the law says the plaintiff should be. In tort that is the position before the wrong. In contract it is the position performance would have produced. Compensation is not punishment and not a windfall: the plaintiff recovers loss, not the defendant's wickedness.

### Three contract interests

Contract damages protect one of three interests (Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 344):

- **Expectation interest** (the default): the benefit of the bargain. Under § 347 it equals the loss in value of the other party's performance, plus other loss (incidental and consequential), minus cost or other loss avoided by not having to perform.
- **Reliance interest**: out-of-pocket expenditures made in preparing to perform or in performing (§ 349). Courts allow it when profits are too uncertain to prove, but it is reduced by any loss the plaintiff would have suffered anyway had the contract been performed.
- **Restitution interest**: the value of a benefit the plaintiff conferred on the defendant (§ 371), measured by the defendant's gain (Week 3).

In sales of goods, the UCC supplies formulas. A buyer who reasonably **covers** recovers the cover price minus the contract price, plus incidental and consequential damages, minus expenses saved (§ 2-712). A buyer who does not cover recovers market price minus contract price (§ 2-713). A seller whose buyer repudiates recovers contract price minus market price at the time and place of tender, plus incidental damages, minus expenses saved (§ 2-708(1)), or resale damages (§ 2-706). A **lost-volume seller** may recover lost profit instead under § 2-708(2) (Week 2).

### Tort damages and property

Personal-injury damages cover past and future medical expenses, lost earnings and noneconomic loss (pain and suffering). Future losses are **discounted to present value**, because a lump sum paid today can earn a return. For a payment $P$ received at the end of year $t$ with discount rate $r$, the present value is
$$PV = \frac{P}{(1+r)^t}.$$
For conversion of personal property, the usual measure is the value at the time and place of conversion, plus interest (some states allow the highest value within a reasonable time after learning of the conversion). For harm to land, the traditional measure is the lesser of the cost of repair and the diminution in market value (Restatement (Second) of Torts § 929 allows cost of restoration when reasonable).

### The economic-waste limit

When completion or repair would cost far more than it adds in value, courts avoid awarding cost. In *Jacob & Youngs v. Kent* (1921) the builder used a different brand of pipe of equal quality; the court held the owner could recover only the (negligible) difference in value, not the cost of tearing out the walls, because the breach was not willful and the cost was grossly disproportionate. *Peevyhouse v. Garland Coal & Mining Co.* (1962) applied similar reasoning to an unfinished restoration of strip-mined land and awarded diminution in value. Many courts treat a willful breach more harshly, and some allow cost when the plaintiff has a personal reason to restore.

### Nominal damages

When a legal right is violated but no loss is proved, the plaintiff may recover **nominal damages**, a token sum. In *Uzuegbunam v. Preczewski* (2021) the Court held that a request for nominal damages satisfies redressability when the plaintiff alleges a completed violation of a legal right.

### Applying the rules (IRAC)

Identify the claim, then the interest protected, then the formula, then the numbers. Always list the components: loss in value, incidental costs, consequential loss, and costs saved. A common bar structure gives you the contract price, the cover or market price and an expense or two, and asks for the total.

@example
title: Buyer covers after the seller fails to deliver
problem: Buyer contracts to buy 200 units from Seller at \$40 per unit. Seller does not deliver. Buyer reasonably covers by buying 200 comparable units at \$46 each and spends \$300 on incidental costs of arranging cover. Compute Buyer's damages under UCC § 2-712.
step: Name the formula: damages equal cover price minus contract price, plus incidental damages, minus expenses saved.
step: Cover cost: $200 \times 46 = 9{,}200$. Contract cost: $200 \times 40 = 8{,}000$. Difference: $1{,}200$.
step: Add incidental damages of \$300. No expenses were saved, so nothing is subtracted.
step: Total: $1{,}200 + 300 = 1{,}500$, so Buyer recovers \$1,500.

@example
title: Expectation versus reliance
problem: Ana is promised \$20,000 for producing a short film. Before the other party repudiates, Ana spends \$6,000 on preparation. She would have spent another \$9,000 to finish. Find her expectation and reliance damages.
step: Expectation equals the contract price minus the cost avoided: $20{,}000 - 9{,}000 = 11{,}000$.
step: Check the logic: expectation includes the \$6,000 already spent plus the \$5,000 profit she would have earned, which is $6{,}000 + 5{,}000 = 11{,}000$.
step: Reliance damages are the \$6,000 she actually spent, which she may elect if profit is hard to prove, provided the contract would not have lost money.
step: Expectation (\$11,000) is the larger figure and is the default measure.

@mistakes
- Subtracting the contract price from the cover price in the wrong direction, or forgetting that the buyer saves the unpaid price.
- Awarding both expectation and reliance damages together, which double counts the same expenditures.
- Awarding cost of repair automatically, ignoring the lesser-of rule and the economic-waste limit.
