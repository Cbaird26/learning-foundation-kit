@article
Once a plaintiff wins a judgment, the next question is whether it can be enforced elsewhere, for example where the defendant has assets. This is **recognition and enforcement of judgments**.

### The constitutional rule

Article IV, § 1 provides that "Full Faith and Credit shall be given in each State to the public Acts, Records, and judicial Proceedings of every other State." Congress implemented the Clause by statute, 28 U.S.C. § 1738, which also requires federal courts to give state judgments the same effect as the state's own courts. Several rules follow:

- **Judgments get the strongest form of credit.** A final judgment on the merits from a court with jurisdiction must be recognized and enforced in every state, and given the same **preclusive effect** (claim and issue preclusion) it has in the rendering state. The enforcing court may not relitigate the merits.
- **No public-policy exception.** The enforcing state may not refuse a judgment because it is based on a claim contrary to the enforcing state's policy, or because the rendering court made an error of law or fact (*Fauntleroy v. Lum* (1908)). *Baker v. General Motors Corp.* (1998) described the obligation to enforce judgments as "exacting," in contrast to the more flexible rule for sister-state statutes (Week 12).
- **Enforcement mechanics are local.** Full faith and credit governs the judgment's validity and effect, but the **methods of enforcement** (execution, garnishment, contempt) are governed by the law of the enforcing state (*Baker*). A court is not required to enforce another state's injunction in ways that would interfere with its own authority over its proceedings; *Baker* held that Michigan's injunction barring a former employee from testifying could not stop him from testifying in a Missouri case.
- **Registration.** Most states have adopted the Uniform Enforcement of Foreign Judgments Act, which lets a judgment creditor register an authenticated sister-state judgment and enforce it like a local one. A federal judgment can be registered in another federal district under 28 U.S.C. § 1963.

### Defenses that survive

- **Lack of personal or subject-matter jurisdiction**, but only if the question was not actually litigated. A defendant who never appeared may attack a default judgment collaterally for lack of jurisdiction. A defendant who appeared and litigated jurisdiction is bound by that determination (*Durfee v. Duke* (1963)); res judicata applies to jurisdictional issues actually litigated.
- **Lack of notice or denial of due process.**
- **Finality.** The judgment must be final. A judgment for installments (support or alimony) that cannot be modified retroactively is final as to accrued installments; modification of prospective support is governed by the rendering state's law, and federal law (28 U.S.C. § 1738B) requires recognition of child support orders.
- **Satisfaction or payment**, and rules about the **time limit to enforce**, which are governed by forum law.
- **Penal judgments.** A judgment for a penalty to the public is not entitled to full faith and credit; the test looks at whether the judgment's purpose is to punish an offense against the public rather than to redress a private wrong (*Huntington v. Attrill* (1892)).
- **Fraud in obtaining the judgment** (extrinsic fraud) is recognized by many courts.

### Divorce and status

Divorce decrees raise special issues because jurisdiction depends on **domicile**. In *Williams v. North Carolina* (1942, 1945), a sister state could re-examine whether the divorcing spouse truly had a domicile in the granting state when the other spouse did not appear. If the other spouse appeared and litigated, he or she cannot attack the decree later (*Sherrer v. Sherrer* (1948)). The Court also allows a "divisible divorce": a state may end the marriage without having jurisdiction to cut off support rights of a spouse who was not before it (*Estin v. Estin* (1948)).

### Foreign-country judgments

The Full Faith and Credit Clause applies only to sister-state judgments. A judgment from a **foreign country** is recognized under **comity** (*Hilton v. Guyot* (1895)) and, in most states, the Uniform Foreign-Country Money Judgments Recognition Act, which provides mandatory and discretionary grounds for nonrecognition, including a judicial system lacking impartial tribunals and procedures compatible with due process, and lack of personal jurisdiction. Those judgments are not treated as strongly as sister-state judgments.

### State sovereign immunity

A state is also immune from suit in another state's courts without its consent (*Franchise Tax Board v. Hyatt* (2019), overruling *Nevada v. Hall* (1979)). That decision protects states from private suits in sister states' courts.

### Applying the rules (IRAC)

Is the judgment from a sister state (Full Faith and Credit) or a foreign country (comity)? Was it final and on the merits? Was jurisdiction litigated? Does a surviving defense apply? Then compute the amount to be enforced, including any interest the governing law allows.

@example
title: Enforcing a partly paid judgment
problem: State X enters a \$50,000 judgment against Debtor, who appeared and defended the case. Debtor has paid \$10,000. The judgment creditor registers the judgment in State F, where Debtor holds assets. Assume the unpaid balance bears simple interest at 6% for one year, and that Debtor argues the underlying claim is against State F's public policy. What can the creditor enforce?
step: The judgment is a final judgment on the merits from a court with jurisdiction, so Full Faith and Credit applies.
step: State F cannot refuse enforcement because of its public policy, and cannot relitigate the merits.
step: Unpaid balance: $50{,}000 - 10{,}000 = 40{,}000$. Interest: $40{,}000 \times 0.06 = 2{,}400$.
step: The creditor may enforce \$42,400 using State F's enforcement procedures.

@mistakes
- Refusing to enforce a sister-state judgment because the enforcing state disapproves of the underlying claim.
- Allowing a collateral attack on jurisdiction by a defendant who appeared and litigated it.
- Applying full faith and credit rules to a judgment from a foreign country, which is governed by comity.
