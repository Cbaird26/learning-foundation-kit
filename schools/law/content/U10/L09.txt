@article
**Conflict of laws** asks three questions in a case with contacts to more than one state: (1) does the forum have **personal and subject-matter jurisdiction**? (2) which state's **law** governs (choice of law)? and (3) will the resulting **judgment** be recognized elsewhere (Weeks 11-12)? This week and the next address the second question. The choice matters when states differ: one allows recovery of damages another caps, or one bars a claim the other permits.

### The vested-rights theory

The **First Restatement of Conflict of Laws** (1934), drafted under the influence of Professor Beale, rested on the idea that rights **vest** under the law of the place where the last event necessary to create them occurred, and that a forum merely recognizes the right created elsewhere. The rules are territorial and mechanical, designed for certainty and predictability:

- **Torts: lex loci delicti.** The law of the place of the wrong governs. The place of the wrong is where the **last event necessary to make the actor liable** occurred, which in physical injury cases is the **place of injury**. If a defendant in State A negligently loads a truck whose load falls and injures someone in State B, State B law governs.
- **Contracts: lex loci contractus.** Questions of **formation and validity** are governed by the law of the **place of contracting**, where the last act necessary to form the contract occurred. For a contract formed by mailed acceptance, that is where the acceptance was dispatched (the mailbox rule). Questions of **performance** (manner, sufficiency, excuse) are governed by the law of the **place of performance**.
- **Property: lex situs.** Questions about land are governed by the law of the state where the land is located; for movables, courts often apply the law of the situs or of the owner's domicile.
- **Procedure: lex fori.** Matters of procedure are governed by the forum's law.

### Substance and procedure; characterization

The distinction between substance (governed by the chosen law) and procedure (forum law) is manipulable. Traditionally, **statutes of limitations** were procedural, so the forum's period applied even to a claim governed by another state's substantive law, and many states now soften this with **borrowing statutes** that apply the shorter period of the state where the claim arose. Rules about **damages** (measure and caps) are usually treated as substantive, so the law of the place of injury applies. **Characterization** (deciding whether an issue is one of tort, contract, property, or procedure) controls which rule applies, and courts sometimes use it to reach a preferred result.

### Escape devices

The rigid rules produced results courts found unjust, so they developed devices to avoid them: characterization (calling a claim a contract claim rather than a tort claim, or a rule procedural), the **public-policy exception** (the forum refuses to apply a foreign law that offends a strong forum policy), and **renvoi** (applying the foreign state's choice-of-law rules, which may point elsewhere; the First Restatement generally rejected renvoi for torts and contracts). Courts often call these escape devices because they allow forum law to be applied while claiming to follow the traditional rules. A minority of states still follow lex loci delicti for torts; most have moved to modern approaches (Week 10).

### Choice of law in federal court

In a diversity case, a federal court applies the **forum state's choice-of-law rules** (*Klaxon Co. v. Stentor Electric Manufacturing Co.* (1941)), so that the outcome does not depend on whether the suit is in state or federal court. After a transfer under 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a), the transferee court applies the choice-of-law rules of the transferor state when the defendant moved to transfer (*Van Dusen v. Barrack* (1964)), and also when the plaintiff did (*Ferens v. John Deere Co.* (1990)), so that transfer does not change the applicable law. When the transfer enforces a valid forum-selection clause, the transferee court applies its own rules (*Atlantic Marine Construction Co. v. U.S. District Court* (2013)).

### Applying the rules (IRAC)

Identify the issue (tort, contract, property, procedure), apply the First Restatement's connecting factor (place of injury, place of contracting or performance, situs, forum), check whether the forum uses another approach, and then apply the chosen state's substantive law to compute the result.

@example
title: Lex loci delicti
problem: Ana and Ben, both domiciled in State A, are driving in State B, where Ana negligently injures Ben. Ben sues Ana in State C, a lex loci delicti state. State B bars recovery by anyone more than 50% at fault; State A has pure comparative fault. Ben was 60% at fault. Which law governs, and can Ben recover?
step: The claim is a tort, so lex loci delicti applies. The place of the wrong is where the injury occurred: State B.
step: The parties' common domicile in State A is irrelevant under the First Restatement.
step: Apply State B's rule: a plaintiff more than 50% at fault is barred. Ben was 60% at fault.
step: Ben recovers nothing, though he would recover 40% of his damages under State A law.

@example
title: Contract validity versus performance
problem: A contract is signed in State M and is to be performed in State N. One party argues the promise lacks consideration; the other argues that delivery on a Saturday was not timely. Under the First Restatement, which state's law governs each issue?
step: Consideration goes to formation and validity, so the law of the place of contracting (State M) governs.
step: Timeliness goes to the manner and sufficiency of performance, so the law of the place of performance (State N) governs.
step: The court applies different states' laws to different issues, which is one feature that critics said made the First Restatement artificial.

@mistakes
- Applying the law of the parties' common domicile to a tort under the First Restatement, which looks to the place of injury.
- Treating limitations periods as substantive under the traditional approach and applying the claim state's period.
- In diversity, applying federal choice-of-law rules rather than the forum state's rules.
