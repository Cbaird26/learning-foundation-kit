@article
Compensatory damages are limited three ways. Think of them as three filters a claim must pass: **certainty**, **foreseeability** and **mitigation**. A loss that is real can still be unrecoverable because the plaintiff cannot prove it, because it was not foreseeable at contracting, or because the plaintiff could have avoided it.

### Certainty

Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 352 bars damages beyond the amount the evidence establishes with **reasonable certainty**. The distinction that matters is between the **fact** of damage and its **amount**. Where the fact of loss is established, courts allow a reasonable approximation of the amount even if exact proof is impossible, particularly when the defendant's wrong made precision difficult (*Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.* (1931)). The harder case is the **new business**: because it has no track record, lost profits are often too speculative, and many courts deny them, though a growing number allow them when proved by expert projections and comparable businesses. A plaintiff who cannot prove profits may fall back on reliance damages.

### Foreseeability

*Hadley v. Baxendale* (1854) holds that a breaching party is liable for (1) **general damages**, those arising naturally from the breach in the usual course of things, and (2) **special (consequential) damages**, which both parties contemplated at the time of contracting as a probable result, which usually requires the breaching party to have had notice of the special circumstances. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 351 restates this: loss is recoverable only if the party had reason to foresee it as a probable result when the contract was made. Under UCC § 2-715(2)(a), a buyer may recover consequential damages for losses resulting from needs of which the seller had reason to know at the time of contracting that the buyer could not reasonably prevent by cover or otherwise. Foreseeability is measured at contracting, not at breach, and notice after the contract is signed does not help.

Contract damages for emotional distress are generally unavailable unless the breach is of a kind likely to cause serious emotional disturbance (Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 353), as with some funeral or medical contracts.

### Mitigation (avoidable consequences)

The injured party cannot recover for loss it could have avoided without undue risk, burden or humiliation (Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 350). The burden of proving a failure to mitigate is on the **defendant**. Key points:

- **Reasonable efforts only.** The plaintiff need not succeed, only try reasonably; reasonable costs of mitigation are recoverable even if the effort fails.
- **Employment.** A wrongfully discharged employee must accept **comparable** employment but need not take work that is different or inferior (*Parker v. Twentieth Century-Fox Film Corp.* (1970)). Earnings from comparable work reduce damages.
- **Anticipatory repudiation and construction.** A party who is told not to continue cannot run up costs by finishing; in *Rockingham County v. Luten Bridge Co.* (1929) the contractor could not recover the cost of work done after the county repudiated.
- **Leases.** Most states now impose a duty on landlords to make reasonable efforts to relet after abandonment; a minority still permits the landlord to let the premises sit.
- **Torts.** A tort victim must use reasonable care to limit harm, for example by seeking reasonable medical treatment.

### The lost-volume seller

A seller with unlimited supply who loses a sale and then makes another has not mitigated: it would have made both sales. UCC § 2-708(2) gives the lost-volume seller its lost profit (including reasonable overhead) plus incidental damages, rather than the market-price formula.

### Applying the rules (IRAC)

Start with the full loss. Then ask three questions in order: Can the amount be proved with reasonable certainty? Was the loss foreseeable at contracting? Could the plaintiff reasonably have avoided any part of it? Subtract what fails each filter and add reasonable mitigation costs.

@example
title: Employee who refuses comparable work
problem: Dana is hired for 8 more months at \$6,000 per month and is wrongfully fired. After 2 months of unemployment she is offered a comparable job at \$6,000 per month for the rest of the term and refuses it without reason. Find her damages.
step: She was entitled to $8 \times 6{,}000 = 48{,}000$ in total over the remaining term.
step: The comparable job would have avoided the loss from month 3 onward, and her refusal was unreasonable, so the defendant proves a failure to mitigate.
step: Recoverable loss is limited to the 2 months of unemployment before the offer: $2 \times 6{,}000 = 12{,}000$.
step: Damages are \$12,000. The \$36,000 for months 3 through 8 is cut off by the avoidable-consequences rule.

@example
title: Special damages without notice
problem: Seller agrees to deliver a mixer to Buyer's bakery by May 1 for \$9,000. Seller knows only that Buyer runs a bakery. Delivery is two weeks late. Buyer proves \$1,400 in rental cost of a substitute mixer and \$25,000 in lost profit on a one-time catering contract that Seller never heard of. What does Buyer recover?
step: Identify general damages: rental of a substitute is a natural, foreseeable result of late delivery to a bakery, so \$1,400 is recoverable.
step: Identify special damages: the lost profit on the one-time catering job depends on circumstances not communicated at contracting.
step: Under *Hadley* and UCC § 2-715(2)(a), Seller had no reason to know of the special contract at the time of contracting.
step: Buyer recovers \$1,400 and not the \$25,000.

@mistakes
- Treating the plaintiff's duty to mitigate as a duty to succeed, or refusing to award the cost of reasonable but unsuccessful efforts.
- Judging foreseeability by what the defendant knew at the time of breach rather than at the time of contracting.
- Subtracting a replacement sale from a lost-volume seller's damages even though the seller could have made both sales.

prompt: A startup coffee chain sues a landlord that breached a lease before the chain opened. The chain claims \$400,000 in lost profits based on a consultant's projections. Write an IRAC analysis of whether the lost profits are recoverable and what the chain might recover instead.
solution: Issue: whether lost profits of a new business are proved with reasonable certainty. Rule: damages must be established with reasonable certainty (Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 352); the fact of loss must be shown, but the amount may be approximated; new businesses face the hardest burden because they lack a track record, though many courts admit expert projections grounded in data from comparable businesses.
