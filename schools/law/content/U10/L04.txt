@article
Restitution is a claim to value. A **constructive trust** or **equitable lien** turns that claim into a right in specific property. Both are equitable remedies, imposed by a court to prevent unjust enrichment; neither depends on any intent to create a trust.

### Constructive trust

A **constructive trust** (Restatement (Third) of Restitution § 55) makes the defendant hold identifiable property for the plaintiff and convey it to the plaintiff, because the defendant would otherwise be unjustly enriched. The usual requirements:

1. **Unjust enrichment**: the defendant holds property in which the plaintiff has a superior claim, typically because of fraud, breach of fiduciary duty, theft, mistake, or other wrongful or unconscientious conduct. Fraud is not necessary; mistake can suffice.
2. **Identifiable (traceable) property**: the plaintiff must trace its money or property into the thing it claims.
3. **No bona fide purchaser**: a good-faith purchaser for value without notice takes free of the trust, though the plaintiff can follow the proceeds.

Because the plaintiff is treated as the equitable owner, the plaintiff recovers the property **itself**, including its appreciation and profits, and, importantly, it is not just a general creditor if the defendant is insolvent. Courts vary on how a constructive trust fares in bankruptcy, but a plaintiff with a valid trust in traceable property can generally claim it ahead of unsecured creditors.

### Equitable lien

An **equitable lien** (§ 56) is a security interest in specific property to secure the plaintiff's restitution claim. It is used when the plaintiff's money was used to purchase or improve property but the plaintiff's claim is limited to the amount, or when the defendant's assets include both rightful and wrongful money. The lien secures the **amount** of the claim (typically with interest); it does not give the plaintiff the property's appreciation. Choose the constructive trust if the property has risen in value, and the lien if the property has fallen.

### Tracing

Tracing identifies where the plaintiff's property went. If the wrongdoer buys a house with stolen money, the house is traceable. When stolen money is **commingled** in a bank account with the wrongdoer's own funds, courts presume the wrongdoer spent his own money first. The **lowest intermediate balance rule** limits the plaintiff's claim to the lowest balance the account reached after the deposit, because later deposits of the wrongdoer's own money do not restore the plaintiff's funds. Equity does not let the plaintiff claim more than it contributed.

### Limits and federal applications

Remedies must remain equitable. In ERISA cases, *Great-West Life & Annuity Insurance Co. v. Knudson* (2002) held that "appropriate equitable relief" under § 502(a)(3) does not reach a plan's claim to general assets; *Sereboff v. Mid Atlantic Medical Services* (2006) allowed an equitable lien enforced against an identifiable settlement fund; and *Montanile v. Board of Trustees* (2016) held that a plan cannot recover from a participant's general assets once the identifiable fund has been dissipated on nontraceable items. Similarly, *Liu v. SEC* (2020) held that SEC disgorgement under the equity-based statute must not exceed the wrongdoer's net profits and must be awarded for victims.

### Choosing the remedy for a client

When a fiduciary or thief diverts the client's money, compare:

- **Damages** (money judgment): simple, but the client is an unsecured creditor.
- **Constructive trust** over traceable property: captures appreciation and gives priority, but requires tracing and bars recovery where a bona fide purchaser holds the property.
- **Equitable lien**: secures the amount when the property has not risen in value or when only part of the purchase price was the client's.

### Applying the rules (IRAC)

Identify the wrongful conduct or mistake and the unjust enrichment, trace the property, check for a bona fide purchaser, then pick the trust or the lien by comparing the property's present value with the amount traced.

@example
title: Tracing through a commingled account
problem: A trustee embezzles \$50,000 and deposits it in an account holding \$10,000 of his own money (balance \$60,000). He withdraws \$45,000 for a vacation (balance \$15,000), then deposits \$30,000 of his own earnings (balance \$45,000). How much of the account can the beneficiary claim under the lowest intermediate balance rule?
step: List the running balances after each transaction: \$60,000, then \$15,000, then \$45,000.
step: The trustee is presumed to have spent his own money first, but when the balance fell to \$15,000, at most \$15,000 of the beneficiary's money remained.
step: Later deposits of the trustee's own money cannot restore the beneficiary's funds, so the claim is capped at the lowest balance: \$15,000.
step: The beneficiary may claim \$15,000 from the account and is an unsecured creditor for the remaining \$35,000 (unless the \$45,000 spent can be traced into other property).

@mistakes
- Granting a constructive trust over property the plaintiff cannot trace, or over property held by a bona fide purchaser.
- Allowing the plaintiff to claim the current account balance instead of the lowest intermediate balance.
- Choosing an equitable lien when the property has appreciated and a constructive trust would capture the gain.
