### Declaratory judgments

A **declaratory judgment** states the parties' rights without ordering anyone to do anything. The federal Declaratory Judgment Act, 28 U.S.C. § 2201(a), provides that in "a case of actual controversy within its jurisdiction," a federal court "may declare the rights and other legal relations of any interested party seeking such declaration, whether or not further relief is or could be sought." Section 2202 allows further relief, such as an injunction, based on a declaration. FRCP 57 provides the procedure. Many states have adopted the Uniform Declaratory Judgments Act.

Key points:

- **Actual controversy.** The Act is limited by Article III. In *MedImmune, Inc. v. Genentech, Inc.* (2007) the Court asked "whether the facts alleged, under all the circumstances, show that there is a substantial controversy, between parties having adverse legal interests, of sufficient immediacy and reality to warrant the issuance of a declaratory judgment." A patent licensee could challenge the patent's validity without first breaching the license, because it should not be forced to risk liability to obtain a ruling.
- **No new jurisdiction.** The Act is procedural; it does not give a federal court subject-matter jurisdiction (*Skelly Oil Co. v. Phillips Petroleum Co.* (1950)). Jurisdiction depends on whether the coercive action the declaratory defendant could bring would arise under federal law or satisfy diversity.
- **Discretion.** A court may decline to hear a declaratory action even when jurisdiction exists, for example in favor of a parallel state proceeding (*Wilton v. Seven Falls Co.* (1995)).
- **Uses.** Insurance coverage disputes, patent validity, constitutionality of statutes, contract construction, and title questions. A declaratory judgment has the same preclusive effect as other judgments.

### Attorney's fees: the American Rule

Under the **American Rule**, each party pays its own attorney's fees, win or lose (*Alyeska Pipeline Service Co. v. Wilderness Society* (1975)). The main exceptions:

- **Statute.** Fee-shifting statutes, for example 42 U.S.C. § 1988 for prevailing parties in civil rights cases under § 1983. Prevailing plaintiffs ordinarily recover fees; prevailing defendants only when the claim was frivolous, unreasonable or without foundation (*Christiansburg Garment Co. v. EEOC* (1978)).
- **Contract.** A clause awarding fees to the prevailing party is generally enforced.
- **Common fund.** A lawyer whose work creates a fund for a group may be paid from it (*Boeing Co. v. Van Gemert* (1980)).
- **Bad faith.** A court may award fees under its inherent power for bad-faith conduct (*Chambers v. NASCO, Inc.* (1991)), and Rule 11 and other rules authorize sanctions.

### Prevailing party and the lodestar

A **prevailing party** has obtained a judicially sanctioned change in the legal relationship of the parties. *Buckhannon Board & Care Home, Inc. v. West Virginia Department of Health and Human Resources* (2001) rejected the "catalyst theory": a defendant's voluntary change of conduct does not make the plaintiff a prevailing party. *Lackey v. Stinnie* (2025) held that plaintiffs who win only a preliminary injunction, and whose case is then mooted by the government's change of position before a final ruling, are not prevailing parties under § 1988.

The court computes fees using the **lodestar**: reasonable hours multiplied by a reasonable hourly rate, a figure that is presumptively reasonable (*Perdue v. Kenny A.* (2010)). It excludes hours that are excessive or spent on distinct, unsuccessful claims, and may reduce the result when the plaintiff obtained only limited success (*Hensley v. Eckerhart* (1983)). Enhancements above the lodestar are rare.

### Procedure and Rule 68

A motion for fees must be filed within **14 days** after entry of judgment, unless a statute or order says otherwise (FRCP 54(d)(2)(B)). Under **Rule 68**, a defendant may serve an offer of judgment at least 14 days before trial; if the plaintiff rejects it and the judgment finally obtained is **not more favorable** than the offer, the plaintiff must pay the costs the defendant incurred after the offer. The plaintiff cannot recover its own post-offer costs. Where the underlying statute defines "costs" to include attorney's fees (as § 1988 does), post-offer fees are included (*Marek v. Chesny* (1985)). Rule 68 applies only when the plaintiff wins a judgment (*Delta Air Lines, Inc. v. August* (1981)).

### Applying the rules (IRAC)

For declaratory relief, test for an actual controversy and whether the court should exercise discretion. For fees, ask whether an exception to the American Rule applies, whether the movant is a prevailing party, and how the lodestar and any reduction or Rule 68 cost shifting work out.

@example
title: Lodestar with limited success
problem: A civil rights plaintiff's attorney worked 150 hours at a reasonable rate of \$300. The court finds that the plaintiff succeeded on the main claim but obtained only limited success overall and reduces the lodestar by 20%. What fee does the court award?
step: Lodestar: $150 \times 300 = 45{,}000$.
step: A 20% reduction removes $0.20 \times 45{,}000 = 9{,}000$.
step: Fee award: $45{,}000 - 9{,}000 = 36{,}000$.
step: Enhancements are rare, so the court's discretion generally operates to reduce rather than to increase the lodestar.

@example
title: Actual controversy for an insured
problem: An insurer denies that its policy covers a lawsuit against its policyholder, and the policyholder wants to know whether it has a duty to defend before spending money on its own lawyer. May the policyholder seek a declaratory judgment?
step: Identify the controversy: the parties have adverse legal interests about coverage.
step: Apply the *MedImmune* test: the dispute is substantial, immediate, and real, because the underlying suit is pending.
step: The policyholder need not first incur liability or breach anything to obtain a declaration.
step: The court has discretion to hear it, and ordinarily should, absent a parallel proceeding better suited to decide the question.

@mistakes
- Awarding attorney's fees to the winner without finding a statute, contract or recognized exception.
- Treating a favorable change in the defendant's conduct, or a mooted preliminary injunction, as making the plaintiff a prevailing party.
- Letting a Rule 68 plaintiff recover post-offer costs after a judgment no better than the offer.
