@article
The Constitution limits which state's law a forum may apply, but only loosely. Two clauses matter: the **Due Process Clause** (the choice must not be arbitrary or fundamentally unfair) and the **Full Faith and Credit Clause** (a state must respect other states' laws in some circumstances). In practice, these limits rarely bar a state from applying its own law.

### The standard

*Allstate Insurance Co. v. Hague* (1981) is the leading case. A Minnesota court applied Minnesota law to allow "stacking" of uninsured-motorist coverage for a Wisconsin resident killed in Wisconsin. A plurality held that for a state's substantive law to be selected in a constitutionally permissible manner, the state must have a **significant contact or significant aggregation of contacts, creating state interests, such that choice of its law is neither arbitrary nor fundamentally unfair**. The decedent had worked in Minnesota, his widow moved there after his death, and the insurer did business there. Justice Stevens concurred in the judgment on a narrower ground, and the plurality's test is the one courts apply.

*Phillips Petroleum Co. v. Shutts* (1985) applied the test to a nationwide class action. Kansas applied its own law to every class member's claim, though most class members and leases had no connection to Kansas. The Court held that applying Kansas law to those claims was unconstitutional because Kansas lacked the contacts necessary to create state interests. *Home Insurance Co. v. Dick* (1930) similarly barred Texas from applying its law to an insurance contract made and to be performed in Mexico, where Texas's only connection was the plaintiff's nominal Texas residence.

### Full faith and credit to statutes and procedure

The Full Faith and Credit Clause is much less demanding for **laws** than for judgments (Week 11). A forum need not substitute another state's statute for its own on a subject the forum may legislate about (*Pacific Employers Insurance Co. v. Industrial Accident Commission* (1939)). *Sun Oil Co. v. Wortman* (1988) held that a forum may apply its own **statute of limitations**, even a longer one, to a claim governed by another state's substantive law, because limitations periods were traditionally procedural and a state may treat them so.
*Franchise Tax Board v. Hyatt* (2016) held that the Clause prevents one state from applying its law to give a private party greater damages against a sister state's agency than the forum would permit against its own agencies. *Franchise Tax Board v. Hyatt* (2019) held that states retain sovereign immunity from private suits in the courts of other states, overruling *Nevada v. Hall*.

### Personal jurisdiction overlaps

Due process also limits personal jurisdiction. *Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial District Court* (2021) held that a claim may "relate to" a defendant's forum contacts without a strict causal link. *Mallory v. Norfolk Southern Railway Co.* (2023) held that the Due Process Clause does not bar a state from requiring an out-of-state corporation to consent to general jurisdiction as a condition of registering to do business, an approach some states use. Jurisdiction and choice of law are separate inquiries: a forum with jurisdiction may still lack the contacts to apply its own law under *Hague* and *Shutts*.

### Other constitutional limits

The **dormant Commerce Clause** limits state regulation of out-of-state commerce, though *National Pork Producers Council v. Ross* (2023) held that there is no per se rule against laws with extraterritorial effects. The Due Process Clause also limits punitive damages based on out-of-state conduct (*BMW v. Gore*, Week 7). The **Supremacy Clause** may displace state law, and in federal court *Klaxon* directs the forum state's choice-of-law rules (Week 9).

### The block deliverable: must a sister-state judgment be enforced?

Use this checklist. (1) Is the judgment from a sister state? (2) Is it final and on the merits? (3) Did the court have personal and subject-matter jurisdiction, and if the defendant defaulted, can the defendant challenge jurisdiction collaterally? (4) Was jurisdiction actually litigated? (5) Is any surviving defense (satisfaction, fraud, penal, lack of due process) present? If the court had jurisdiction, the enforcing state must enforce the judgment even if the claim violates its policy or the rendering state's choice of law was mistaken.

### Applying the rules (IRAC)

For a choice-of-law challenge, list the forum's contacts with the parties and the occurrence, decide whether they create state interests, and conclude whether applying forum law is arbitrary or unfair. For a judgment, apply the checklist.

@example
title: Does the forum have enough contacts?
problem: A decedent lived in State X and was killed there. His widow then moved to State F, where she sued the insurer. The decedent had worked in State F, and the insurer does business in State F. State F applies its own law to extend coverage. May State F constitutionally apply its own law?
step: List the contacts: State F was the decedent's place of work, the widow's residence at the time of suit, and a place the insurer does business.
step: Under *Hague*, the question is whether these aggregate to create state interests so the choice is not arbitrary or fundamentally unfair.
step: State F has an interest in its employees' coverage, in the welfare of its residents and in regulating businesses operating there.
step: Yes: the significant aggregation of contacts satisfies due process and full faith and credit, even if the better choice might have been State X's law.

@mistakes
- Treating the Full Faith and Credit Clause as requiring a state to apply the law of the place of injury; it requires little as to choice of law.
- Confusing personal jurisdiction with the constitutional limit on applying forum law.
- Refusing to enforce a sister-state judgment because the rendering court chose the wrong state's law.
