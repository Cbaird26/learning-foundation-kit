@article
By the 1960s many courts found the First Restatement's mechanical rules arbitrary. The landmark break was *Babcock v. Jackson* (1963). A driver and a passenger domiciled in New York were in an accident in Ontario, whose guest statute barred suits by passengers against drivers. The New York Court of Appeals refused to apply the Ontario statute: New York had the predominant interest in the question of the passenger's right to recover from a New York host, and the Ontario statute's purposes (preventing collusive suits against Ontario insurers and protecting Ontario hosts) were not implicated. The court looked to the law of the place with the most significant contacts and interests. Today most states use one of the modern approaches.

### Governmental interest analysis

Professor Currie proposed that courts identify the **policies** behind each state's rule and ask whether each state has an **interest** in applying it to these parties. Three situations follow:

- **False conflict.** Only one state has an interest, so that state's law applies.
- **True conflict.** Both states have an interest in applying their laws. Currie would apply forum law if the forum has an interest; other courts weigh the states' interests. California's **comparative impairment** approach asks which state's interest would be more impaired if its law were not applied (*Kearney v. Salomon Smith Barney, Inc.* (2006)).
- **Unprovided-for case.** Neither state has an interest. Courts often apply the forum's law, or the law of the state of the parties' common domicile or of the place of injury.

A state's interest in a **loss-allocation** rule (for example, who bears the cost of an accident) usually arises from the **domicile** of the parties, not from the place of the accident. A state's interest in a **conduct-regulating** rule (for example, traffic rules) usually arises from the place of conduct.

### The Second Restatement

The Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws (1971) is the most widely followed approach, in both tort and contract. It seeks the law of the state with the **most significant relationship** to the occurrence and the parties. Section 6 lists general principles: the needs of the interstate and international systems; the relevant policies of the forum; the relevant policies and interests of other states; protection of justified expectations; the basic policies underlying the field of law; certainty, predictability and uniformity of result; and ease in determining and applying the law.

- **Torts, § 145.** Contacts to be taken into account, evaluated by their relative importance to the issue: (a) the place where the injury occurred, (b) the place where the conduct causing the injury occurred, (c) the domicile, residence, nationality, place of incorporation and place of business of the parties, and (d) the place where the relationship, if any, between the parties is centered. Section 146 provides that in a personal-injury case the law of the state where the injury occurred applies unless another state has a more significant relationship.
- **Contracts, § 188.** Contacts: the place of contracting, negotiation, performance, the location of the subject matter, and the domicile, residence, place of incorporation and place of business of the parties. Absent an effective choice of law by the parties, the law of the state with the most significant relationship governs.
- **Party choice, § 187.** The law chosen by the parties to govern their contract is applied unless (a) the chosen state has no substantial relationship to the parties or the transaction and there is no other reasonable basis for the choice, or (b) application of the chosen law would be contrary to a fundamental policy of a state with a materially greater interest in the issue, whose law would apply absent the choice.

### Other approaches

A few states follow **Leflar's choice-influencing considerations** (predictability, interstate order, simplification of the judicial task, advancement of the forum's governmental interest, and application of the better rule of law, Minnesota for example), and a few simply apply **forum law**. A minority still follows the First Restatement for torts.

### Comparing outcomes (the block deliverable)

To decide which law governs a multistate tort under two approaches, write the two analyses side by side. Under the First Restatement, find the place of injury. Under interest analysis (or the Second Restatement), identify the issue, the policy of each state's rule, and each state's contact. Interest analysis and the Second Restatement often point to the same state, the parties' common domicile, in a false conflict when the accident happened elsewhere; the First Restatement then points to a different state, the place of injury.

### Applying the rules (IRAC)

State the approach the forum follows, name each state's rule and the policy behind it, apply the approach's test to the facts, and conclude which law governs and what result follows.

@example
title: Two approaches to one accident
problem: Ana and Ben, both domiciled in State A, collide in State B, where Ana injures Ben. State A has pure comparative fault; State B bars recovery by a plaintiff who is at all at fault (contributory negligence), a rule meant to protect defendants who live in State B. Ben is 30% at fault and proves \$300,000 in damages. Compare the result under the First Restatement and under interest analysis.
step: First Restatement: the place of injury is State B, so State B's contributory-negligence rule applies and Ben recovers \$0.
step: Interest analysis: State A's rule compensates injured persons in proportion to fault, and State A has an interest because both parties are its domiciliaries. State B's rule protects its own defendants, and Ana is not a State B defendant, so State B has no interest. This is a false conflict.
step: Apply State A's law: Ben recovers $300{,}000 \times (1 - 0.30) = 210{,}000$.
step: The Second Restatement (§§ 145-146) would likely reach the same result, because the parties' common domicile and the relationship centered in State A outweigh the accident's fortuitous location.

@example
title: A true conflict
problem: Plaintiff, domiciled in State A (which protects injured persons), sues Defendant, domiciled in State B (which protects defendants from liability beyond a statutory cap), in State A. The accident occurred in State C. What does interest analysis suggest?
step: State A has an interest in compensating its domiciliary under its rule.
step: State B has an interest in protecting its domiciliary under its cap.
step: Both states have an interest, so this is a true conflict. State C's only link is the location of the accident.
step: Under Currie's approach the forum, which has an interest, applies its own law; under comparative impairment the court asks whose policy would suffer more.

@mistakes
- Treating the place of the accident as the source of the state's interest in a loss-allocation rule.
- Calling a conflict true when only one of the two states has an interest in applying its rule to these parties.
- Applying a choice-of-law clause without testing the Second Restatement § 187 exceptions.

@practice self
prompt: Two roommates domiciled in State A are driving in State B, where the driver negligently injures the passenger. State A allows recovery of noneconomic damages without limit; State B caps them at \$150,000. The passenger sues in State A. Decide which law governs under (1) the First Restatement and (2) interest analysis, and state the outcome under each.
solution: (1) Under the First Restatement, lex loci delicti applies; the injury occurred in State B, and damages rules are substantive, so State B's \$150,000 cap applies. (2) Under interest analysis, State A's rule serves the policy of fully compensating persons injured; both parties are State A domiciliaries, so State A has an interest. State B's cap protects State B defendants or insurers from large judgments; the defendant is not a State B domiciliary, so State B's interest is weak or absent. This is a false conflict, and State A's law applies, with no cap. Outcome: the passenger can recover noneconomic damages in full under interest analysis but is limited to \$150,000 under the First Restatement. The Second Restatement (§§ 145-146) would probably also select State A because of the common domicile and the relationship centered there.
