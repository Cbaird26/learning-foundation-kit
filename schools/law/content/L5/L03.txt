@article
A crime ordinarily requires a **guilty mind** (**mens rea**) accompanying the guilty act. The mental state separates a murder from an accident and a theft from a misunderstanding. This lesson shows how the common law and the Model Penal Code (MPC) organize mental states.

### The common law

Common-law courts used a loose vocabulary.

- **Specific intent** means the defendant must act with a further purpose or awareness beyond the act itself. Classic specific-intent crimes include larceny (intent to permanently deprive), burglary (intent to commit a felony inside), robbery, forgery, attempt, conspiracy, solicitation and first-degree premeditated murder.
- **General intent** means only that the defendant intended the physical act (or acted with the awareness the crime requires). Battery and most traditional sex offenses are examples.
- **Malice** is a term of art. In murder it covers intent to kill, intent to cause serious bodily harm, and extreme recklessness.
- **Transferred intent.** If the defendant intends to harm A but because of bad aim injures B, the intent transfers to B for the same type of harm (a murder or battery). It does not transfer between different crimes.

The distinction matters for defenses such as mistake and intoxication. **Voluntary intoxication** is a defense at common law only to specific-intent crimes and only if it prevented the defendant from forming the required intent; it is no defense to general-intent crimes, and *Montana v. Egelhoff* (1996) held that a state may bar a jury from considering it on mens rea. Involuntary intoxication is a defense if it produced the equivalent of insanity or negated the mental state. MPC § 2.08 treats a person who is voluntarily intoxicated as aware of any risk of which a sober person would have been aware, so intoxication does not negate recklessness.

### The Model Penal Code

MPC § 2.02(2) defines four levels, from most to least culpable.

| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Purposely** | It is the actor's conscious object to engage in the conduct or cause the result; for a circumstance, he is aware of it or believes or hopes it exists. |
| **Knowingly** | He is aware that his conduct is of that nature or that the circumstance exists; for a result, he is aware it is practically certain to follow. |
| **Recklessly** | He consciously disregards a substantial and unjustifiable risk, and the disregard is a gross deviation from what a law-abiding person would do. |
| **Negligently** | He should be aware of a substantial and unjustifiable risk, and the failure to perceive it is a gross deviation from what a reasonable person would do. |

The difference between recklessness and negligence is **awareness**: the reckless actor perceives the risk, and the negligent actor does not but should have.

Four interpretive rules do most of the work.

1. **Element analysis** (§ 2.02(1)): culpability is assigned to each *material element* (conduct, result or attendant circumstance), so different elements can carry different mental states.
2. **Default** (§ 2.02(3)): if the statute does not state a mental state for an element, the prosecution must prove at least recklessness.
3. **Carry-through** (§ 2.02(4)): a stated mental state applies to all the material elements unless a contrary purpose plainly appears.
4. **Hierarchy** (§ 2.02(5)): proof of a higher level satisfies a lower one. Purpose satisfies knowledge, and knowledge satisfies recklessness.

Under MPC § 2.05, strict liability is permitted only for "violations" (non-criminal offenses punishable by fines).

Knowledge also covers **willful blindness**: under § 2.02(7), knowledge of a fact is established if the actor is aware of a high probability that it exists, unless he actually believes it does not.

### Silent statutes and strict liability

Legislatures often omit a mental state. Courts presume that Congress intends to require mens rea for serious crimes (*Morissette v. United States* (1952)), and they read a requirement of knowledge of the facts that make the conduct unlawful (*Staples v. United States* (1994), requiring knowledge of the characteristics that made a rifle a machine gun). The presumption is weaker for "public welfare offenses" carrying small penalties (*United States v. Balint* (1922)). Federal cases continue to apply the presumption: *Elonis v. United States* (2015) held that negligence is not enough for the federal threats statute, *Counterman v. Colorado* (2023) held that a true-threats prosecution requires at least recklessness under the First Amendment, and *Rehaif v. United States* (2019) required knowledge of one's prohibited status for a federal firearms offense. Statutory rape is the standard example of strict liability as to the age element.

### Policy

Mens rea ties punishment to blame and makes deterrence possible, because only choices can be influenced by threats. Strict liability is justified for regulatory offenses, where convictions are easier and penalties are mild.

### Applying it

List the elements, classify each as conduct, result or circumstance, find the stated or default mental state for each, and then test the facts against the definitions. Ask yourself, for any risk: did the defendant *notice* it?

@example
title: Mapping mental states onto elements
problem: Under an MPC-style statute, "a person who recklessly damages the property of another by fire commits arson." Drew, a camper, leaves a campfire burning in dry brush he knows is close to a neighbor's barn, goes to sleep, and the fire spreads to the barn. Does Drew satisfy the mental-state requirement?
step: Identify the material elements: conduct (leaving a fire), result (damage by fire), and circumstance (the property belongs to another).
step: Under § 2.02(4) the stated mental state, recklessness, applies to all material elements, so Drew must have consciously disregarded a substantial and unjustifiable risk of damage to property of another.
step: Application: Drew knew the brush was dry and the barn nearby, so he was aware of the risk, and leaving a fire to burn while asleep is a gross deviation from careful conduct.
step: Conclusion: Drew acted recklessly as to each element; the mental state is satisfied. If he had not noticed the barn but a reasonable person would have, he would have been merely negligent and would escape this statute.

@mistakes
- Equating recklessness with negligence; recklessness requires conscious awareness of the risk.
- Treating "intent" as only desire; at common law and under the MPC, practical certainty (knowledge) can be enough for intent-to-kill style crimes.
- Forgetting that intent does not transfer from one type of crime to another (a thrown rock aimed at a window does not give battery intent).
- Assuming a silent statute is strict liability; courts presume a mental-state requirement, and the MPC default is recklessness.
