@article
A mistake is relevant to criminal liability for one reason: it may show that the defendant lacked the **mental state** the crime requires. The question is always whether the mistake negates a required mental state, not whether the defendant was a good person.

### Mistake of fact at common law

The result depends on the type of mens rea.

- **Specific-intent crimes**: any *honest* mistake that negates the intent is a defense, even if unreasonable. A person who takes a coat from a rack, honestly believing it is his own, lacks the intent to permanently deprive and is not guilty of larceny.
- **General-intent crimes**: the mistake must be both honest and *reasonable*. Many jurisdictions apply this to the mistake about consent in sexual-assault statutes, and some apply it to the belief that force was necessary in self-defense.
- **Strict-liability crimes**: mistake is no defense. Most jurisdictions treat the victim's age in statutory rape as strict liability, so a reasonable belief that the victim was older is no defense (*Regina v. Prince* (1875); *Garnett v. State* (Md. 1993)). A minority allows a reasonable-mistake defense (*People v. Hernandez* (Cal. 1964)).

Some courts apply a **moral wrong** or **legal wrong** doctrine to deny the defense where the defendant would have been doing something immoral or illegal even if the facts were as believed. The MPC rejects this in favor of grading the defendant under the offense he thought he was committing (§ 2.04(2)).

### Mistake of fact under the MPC

MPC § 2.04(1)(a) makes ignorance or mistake about a matter of fact or law a defense if it **negatives the purpose, knowledge, belief, recklessness or negligence** required for a material element. The MPC therefore asks only whether the mistake is inconsistent with the required state. For purpose, knowledge and recklessness, an honest mistake suffices; for negligence, the mistake must be reasonable, because an unreasonable mistake is itself negligence. For statutory rape, § 213.6 provides that a reasonable belief that the child was older is a defense, except where the child is under ten.

### Mistake of law

The general rule is that **ignorance or mistake of law is no defense**. The justification is practical and moral: a rule that excuses honest ignorance would reward not learning the law and would make proof nearly impossible. The rule has recognized exceptions.

1. **The offense requires knowledge of the law.** If a statute makes "willful" violation a crime, the government may have to prove the defendant knew his conduct violated a known legal duty. In *Cheek v. United States* (1991), a good-faith belief, even if unreasonable, that the tax code did not impose a duty to pay negated willfulness; a belief that the tax laws are *unconstitutional* did not.
2. **Reasonable reliance on an official statement of the law** later held invalid or erroneous: a statute, a judicial decision, an administrative order or grant of permission, or an official interpretation by the public officer or body responsible for enforcing the law (MPC § 2.04(3)(b); due process cases such as *Raley v. Ohio* (1959) and *Cox v. Louisiana* (1965)). Advice from a *private* lawyer does not qualify.
3. **Fair notice.** *Lambert v. California* (1957) held that due process barred punishing a person for failing to register as a felon when she had no actual knowledge of the duty and the circumstances gave no reason to inquire. The case is narrow and applies to passive omissions.
4. **A collateral legal mistake that negates an element.** If a mistake about civil law, such as who owns the property, negates the intent the crime requires, it works like a mistake of fact. A person who honestly believes, because of a mistaken view of the law of ownership, that the property is his own lacks intent to steal (claim of right).

Recent federal cases show the same logic. In *Rehaif v. United States* (2019), the "knowingly" requirement in the firearms-possession statute applies to the defendant's status (for example, being an unlawful alien), so the government must prove he knew he was in the barred category (he need not know that possession was illegal). In *Ruan v. United States* (2022), the mental state "knowingly or intentionally" in the controlled-substances statute applies to the "except as authorized" clause, so a prescriber is convicted only if he knew or intended that he was acting without authorization.

### Applying it

Identify the element the defendant claims to have misunderstood. Ask whether it is a fact or the law, which mental state attaches to the element, and whether the mistake is inconsistent with that state. If it is strict liability, the mistake ends the inquiry. If it is a law mistake, check the four exceptions.

@example
title: Honest mistake and the type of intent
problem: Jo leaves a restaurant wearing a black wool coat that is identical to hers and in fact belongs to another diner. She honestly believes it is her coat, but a reasonable person would have checked the pockets and the size. She is charged with larceny. Is she guilty?
step: Larceny is a specific-intent crime: the defendant must take the property of another with intent to permanently deprive the owner.
step: The mistake is about a fact (whose coat it is), and it negates the intent to deprive another of property.
step: For specific-intent crimes an honest mistake suffices even if unreasonable, so the fact that a reasonable person would have noticed does not matter.
step: Conclusion: Jo is not guilty of larceny. A civil conversion claim may remain because the owner can still recover.

@mistakes
- Applying a reasonableness requirement to a specific-intent crime, where an honest mistake is enough.
- Letting a reasonable mistake of age excuse statutory rape in a majority jurisdiction, where the offense is strict liability as to age.
- Treating advice from a private lawyer as an official statement of the law.
- Confusing the *Rehaif* knowledge-of-status requirement with a requirement that the defendant know his conduct is illegal.
