@article
Some conduct that fits the definition of a crime is **justified**: society considers it right, or at least acceptable, under the circumstances. Self-defense and necessity are justifications. They are different from **excuses** (duress, insanity, next lesson), where the conduct is still wrong but the actor is not blameworthy. The difference matters for the theory of the defense: a justified actor does nothing wrong, so others may assist him, and he may not be resisted lawfully.

### Self-defense: the elements

A person may use force against another if:

1. **He reasonably believes** that force is **necessary** to protect himself;
2. against **unlawful** force;
3. that is **imminent** (about to happen, not a threat of future harm); and
4. the force he uses is **proportional**.

**Reasonable belief.** The test combines an objective standard with the defendant's circumstances: a reasonable person in his position. If he honestly and reasonably believes he is in danger, he is justified even if mistaken (a person who reasonably thinks that a gun is being drawn may shoot, though the object was a phone). If the belief is honest but unreasonable, the defense fails at common law. A **minority** of states recognize **imperfect self-defense**, which reduces murder to voluntary manslaughter. The MPC (§ 3.04) asks whether the actor *believes* force is immediately necessary, so an unreasonable belief can still justify, although liability for recklessly or negligently causing harm may remain (§ 3.09(2)).

**Proportionality.** **Nondeadly force** may be used against any unlawful force. **Deadly force** may be used only against a threat of **death or serious bodily harm** (and, in some states, forcible felonies such as rape or robbery). Using deadly force against a slap or a shove is excessive. MPC § 3.04(2)(b) allows deadly force only to protect against death, serious bodily harm, kidnapping or forcible sexual intercourse.

**Aggressor.** The initial aggressor generally cannot claim self-defense. Exceptions: (1) a **nondeadly aggressor** may defend himself if the victim **escalates** to deadly force; and (2) an aggressor who **withdraws** and communicates the withdrawal regains the right.

**Retreat.** The majority rule is that a person has **no duty to retreat** before using deadly force when attacked in a place he is lawfully present (*Brown v. United States* (1921), "Detached reflection cannot be demanded in the presence of an uplifted knife"). A **minority** requires retreat before deadly force if it can be done in complete safety, with a **castle doctrine** exception: no retreat inside one's home (many states except co-occupants, and some extend it to the workplace or vehicle). MPC § 3.04(2)(b)(ii) requires retreat if the actor knows he can retreat with complete safety, except in his dwelling or place of work. Many states have enacted "stand your ground" statutes that remove any duty to retreat where the person is lawfully present, and some create presumptions of reasonable fear against intruders.

**Defense of others.** A person may use force to defend a third party if he reasonably believes the third party is entitled to defend himself (the majority "reasonable belief" rule). A minority gives the defender only the rights the third party actually had.

**Burden of proof.** Once the defendant produces some evidence of self-defense, most states require the prosecution to disprove it beyond a reasonable doubt. The Constitution allows a state to treat it as an affirmative defense and place the burden on the defendant (*Martin v. Ohio* (1987)).

**Battered persons.** Courts admit expert testimony on battered-person syndrome to show the reasonableness of the defendant's belief in imminent danger, as in *State v. Kelly* (N.J. 1984), although some jurisdictions apply a strict imminence requirement to confrontations.

**Force by officers.** An officer may use deadly force against a fleeing suspect only if there is probable cause to believe the suspect poses a threat of serious physical harm (*Tennessee v. Garner* (1985)). In Fourth Amendment excessive-force claims, *Barnes v. Felix* (2025) holds that courts evaluate the totality of the circumstances and not merely the moment of the threat.

**Defense of property** does not permit deadly force. Spring guns and similar devices are not justified.

### Necessity (choice of evils)

The necessity defense is available when (1) the defendant faced a clear and imminent harm, often from natural forces; (2) he reasonably believed his act was necessary to avoid it; (3) there was no reasonable legal alternative; (4) the harm avoided was greater than the harm caused; and (5) he did not create the situation. MPC § 3.02 states it more generally as a choice of evils in which the harm avoided is greater than the harm that the law defining the offense seeks to prevent. A hiker who breaks into an empty cabin to survive a blizzard is justified in the trespass.

Necessity is traditionally **not a defense to intentional killing of an innocent person**. In *Regina v. Dudley and Stephens* (1884), shipwrecked sailors who killed and ate a cabin boy were convicted of murder, and in *United States v. Holmes* (1842) a crew member who threw passengers from an overloaded lifeboat was convicted of manslaughter. A prisoner who escapes from prison cannot ordinarily rely on necessity or duress unless, among other things, he reports promptly to authorities after the escape (*United States v. Bailey* (1980)).

### Policy

Justifications encourage socially valuable conduct, such as protecting oneself and others, while limiting vigilantism. Proportionality and imminence prevent self-help from replacing the courts.

### Applying it

Identify the force used and the force faced: was the belief reasonable, the threat imminent, and the response proportional? Check the aggressor and retreat rules in the jurisdiction. For necessity, weigh the harms and look for legal alternatives.

@example
title: Deadly force against nondeadly force
problem: Sam punches Ray once in the face during an argument. Ray, who is unarmed and no larger than Sam, pulls a pistol and shoots Sam dead. Does Ray have a self-defense claim?
step: Ray faced unlawful, imminent force (the punch), so force was permitted if proportional.
step: A punch is nondeadly force and does not threaten death or serious bodily harm, so only nondeadly force was justified.
step: Ray used deadly force, which is disproportionate here, and there is no evidence of an escalation to a deadly threat.
step: Conclusion: self-defense fails. Ray is guilty of murder or manslaughter depending on his mental state, and in a jurisdiction recognizing imperfect self-defense the honest but unreasonable belief might reduce it to manslaughter.

@mistakes
- Allowing deadly force against a threat that is not of death or serious bodily harm.
- Saying the initial aggressor can never claim self-defense; a nondeadly aggressor may defend after escalation, and a withdrawing aggressor regains the right.
- Requiring that the belief be correct, when it must only be honest and reasonable.
- Applying necessity to the intentional killing of an innocent.
