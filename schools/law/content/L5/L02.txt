@article
Criminal law punishes **conduct**, not bad thoughts or bad character. The physical component of a crime is the **actus reus**: a voluntary act, an omission where there is a legal duty to act, or possession. Every element beyond the mental state, including result and circumstance, is part of the actus reus broadly understood. This lesson covers the first element, the act.

### The voluntary act requirement

Liability requires a **voluntary act** (or omission). Under Model Penal Code (MPC) § 2.01(2), the following are *not* voluntary acts:

- a reflex or convulsion;
- a bodily movement during unconsciousness or sleep;
- conduct during hypnosis or resulting from hypnotic suggestion;
- a bodily movement that otherwise is not a product of the effort or determination of the actor, conscious or habitual.

The common law agrees in substance. In *Martin v. State* (Ala. 1944), a man arrested at home and carried onto a public highway by police was not guilty of public drunkenness, because his presence there was not voluntary.

**Time-framing.** The question is whether *some* voluntary act accompanies the required mental state and causes the harm. A driver who knows she is prone to blackouts, drives anyway and loses consciousness has committed a voluntary act (driving with that knowledge), so the involuntary movement at the moment of the crash does not excuse her. The earlier voluntary conduct carries the mens rea of recklessness or negligence.

### Possession

Possession is treated as an act (or a substitute for one). MPC § 2.01(4) says possession is an act if the possessor knowingly procured or received the thing possessed or was aware of his control of it for a sufficient period to have been able to terminate possession. Someone who is handed a package and does not know it holds drugs has not knowingly received contraband.

### Status is not an act

A statute cannot punish a mere condition. *Robinson v. California* (1962) struck down a law that made it a crime to be addicted to narcotics, holding that it inflicted cruel and unusual punishment for a status. *Powell v. Texas* (1968) upheld a conviction for public drunkenness, with a plurality distinguishing a status from the act of being drunk in public. The line is between punishing who you are (invalid) and what you do (valid).

### Omissions

The general rule is that **there is no criminal liability for failing to act unless the defendant had a legal duty to act** (and could have performed it). A stranger who can easily save a drowning child has a moral but ordinarily no legal duty to do so. Under MPC § 2.01(3), liability for an omission requires that the law defining the offense makes the omission sufficient or that a duty to perform the omitted act is imposed by law. Duties arise from:

1. **A statute** (for example, filing tax returns, or reporting child abuse; a few states also require bystanders to give reasonable aid in an emergency).
2. **A status relationship**: parent to minor child, and in many jurisdictions spouse to spouse.
3. **A contract**: a lifeguard, a nurse, a paid caregiver.
4. **Voluntary assumption of care**, where the defendant takes charge of a helpless person and secludes them so that others cannot help (*Jones v. United States* (D.C. Cir. 1962) is the classic discussion of these sources).
5. **Creation of peril**: a person who negligently or innocently puts another in danger must try to help.

Some courts add a duty of landowners and of those who control a dangerous instrument. Moral duties, including a romantic partner's, are not enough (*People v. Beardsley* (Mich. 1907): no duty from an unmarried companion who was not shown to have assumed care). Withdrawing life-sustaining treatment at a patient's or surrogate's request is generally analyzed as an omission by a physician who lacks a duty to continue unwanted treatment (*Barber v. Superior Court* (Cal. Ct. App. 1983)).

The omission must also be possible: the duty-holder must have been physically capable of acting and aware of the facts creating the duty.

### Policy

The act requirement protects against punishing thoughts, supports deterrence (only choices can be deterred), and respects autonomy. The duty limit on omissions prevents the law from making everyone responsible for everyone else and keeps the line between legal and moral obligation clear.

### IRAC for omissions

Issue: can this failure to act be a crime? Rule: omission plus legal duty plus capacity. Application: identify the source of the duty, then the capacity and the mental state. Conclusion.

@example
title: A paid caregiver's failure to act
problem: Lena is hired by a family to care for their bedridden grandfather overnight. One night she notices he is struggling to breathe, reads a book and does not call for help, and he dies. Could Lena's omission support a homicide conviction?
step: Issue: is Lena's failure to summon help a criminal omission, or is there no legal duty?
step: Rule: an omission supports liability only if the defendant had a legal duty and could perform it. A duty arises from a statute, a status relationship, a contract, a voluntary assumption of care, or creation of peril.
step: Application: Lena had a contractual duty to care for him and had voluntarily assumed his care. She could easily call for help and knew he was in distress, so the duty and capacity requirements are met.
step: Conclusion: the omission can support liability. Whether it is murder, manslaughter or no homicide depends on her mental state and on causation, which are the subjects of later lessons.

@mistakes
- Treating a moral duty to rescue a stranger as a legal duty.
- Finding "no act" at the final moment of a crash without asking whether earlier voluntary conduct with the required mental state caused it.
- Forgetting that a duty-holder must also be capable of performing the act.
- Saying status crimes are valid because the legislature can define crimes freely; Robinson holds that punishing a condition violates the Eighth Amendment.
