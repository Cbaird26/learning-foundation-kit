@article
When a crime defines a **result** (a death, an injury, a fire), the prosecution must prove that the defendant's conduct **caused** the result. Causation has two parts, both required: **actual cause** (cause in fact) and **proximate cause** (legal cause).

### Actual cause

The test is **but-for**: but for the defendant's conduct, would the result have occurred when and as it did? If yes, the defendant is not an actual cause. Two refinements:

- **Concurrent sufficient causes.** If two people independently inflict wounds, each enough to kill, and the victim dies, a strict but-for test would acquit both. Courts treat each as an actual cause (the "substantial factor" idea).
- **Acceleration.** A defendant who hastens a death, even by a short time, causes it. The victim would have died eventually, but not *when and how* he did.

The Supreme Court uses plain but-for causation when a federal criminal statute says an enhancement applies if death or injury "results from" the crime. In *Burrage v. United States* (2014), the Court held that the enhancement for distribution of a drug requires that the drug be a but-for cause of death, unless it was an independently sufficient cause. A drug that merely contributed to a death caused by several drugs, without which the victim might have lived, does not meet it.

### Proximate cause

Proximate cause limits liability to results that are a fair and **foreseeable** consequence of the defendant's conduct. The basic principle is that a result is too remote or accidental if it is unforeseeable or if another person's free choice makes the defendant an unfair target of blame. The law sorts intervening events this way.

- **Dependent (responsive) intervening causes** are reactions to the defendant's conduct: a victim who flees and is hit by a car, a rescuer who is injured, a doctor who treats the wound. They break the chain only if abnormal or bizarre in light of the risk.
- **Independent (coincidental) intervening causes** occur regardless of the defendant's act (a lightning strike, a stranger's unrelated attack). They break the chain unless foreseeable.
- **Free, deliberate and informed acts of a third party** (especially intentional or criminal acts) are usually superseding.
- **Victim's conduct.** Ordinary negligence or fright responses do not break the chain; extreme, unforeseeable behavior may.
- **Pre-existing conditions** (the "eggshell skull" rule): the defendant takes the victim as he finds him, so an unforeseeably fragile victim's death is still caused by the defendant.
- **Medical treatment.** Ordinary negligence in treating a wound is foreseeable and does not break the chain. Grossly negligent or intentional mistreatment may.

Cases show the line. In *People v. Kibbe* (N.Y. 1975), defendants robbed a drunk victim and abandoned him by a dark rural road at night; he was struck by a truck. Their conduct was a proximate cause because the victim's exposure to the road was foreseeable and the driver's negligence was not an unforeseeable event. In *Commonwealth v. Root* (Pa. 1961), a drag-race challenger was not a proximate cause when the other racer, trying to avoid an oncoming truck, swerved and died; the court required a more direct causal connection for criminal liability, and the deceased's own reckless choice intervened (many later courts have found the surviving racer a proximate cause in drag-race cases, so the result varies by jurisdiction). In *People v. Acosta* (Cal. Ct. App. 1991), a defendant whose reckless flight from police drew a chase was a proximate cause of a helicopter collision that killed those in the aircraft, because it was a foreseeable result of the chase.

### The Model Penal Code

MPC § 2.03 requires but-for causation and then asks whether the result is within the culpable mental state. For purposely or knowingly causing a result, it must be within the actor's purpose or contemplation, unless the actual result is too remote or accidental in its occurrence to have a bearing on the actor's liability or on the gravity of his offense. For recklessly or negligently caused results, the result must be within the risk of which the actor was or should have been aware, with the same remote-or-accidental qualification. In short, the MPC replaces "proximate cause" with a mens rea-based test.

### Policy

Causation rules aim at fair blame: the defendant is responsible for the harm his culpable conduct fairly produces but not for freak accidents or other people's independent choices.

### Applying it

State the but-for question first. Then identify each intervening event, classify it as dependent or independent, and ask whether it was foreseeable or a free, deliberate act of another.

@example
title: A fleeing victim
problem: Cal chases Dee with a knife, intending to frighten her. Dee runs into a busy street and is hit by a car and killed. Is Cal a cause of her death?
step: Actual cause: but for Cal's chase, Dee would not have run into the street when she did, so Cal is an actual cause.
step: Proximate cause: the intervening event, the car strike, followed from Dee's flight, a response to Cal's conduct (a dependent intervening cause).
step: A victim fleeing an armed attacker may run into traffic; that is a foreseeable response, and the driver's act, an accident, is not an unforeseeable, free and deliberate act.
step: Conclusion: Cal's conduct is the proximate cause of the death. The remaining question for a homicide charge is his mental state, which determines the grade of the offense.

@mistakes
- Asking only whether the victim would have died anyway; acceleration and concurrent sufficient causes still count.
- Treating any third-party negligence as superseding; ordinary negligence in foreseeable events does not break the chain.
- Applying foreseeability as to the victim's frailty; the eggshell rule says the defendant takes the victim as found.
- Forgetting that a free, deliberate criminal act of a third party is usually superseding.
