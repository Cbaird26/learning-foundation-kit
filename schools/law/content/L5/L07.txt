@article
**Manslaughter** is an unlawful killing without malice aforethought. It is the middle rung of criminal homicide: less culpable than murder, more than an accident. **Felony murder** is a different route to murder that bypasses the usual malice requirement.

### Voluntary manslaughter: heat of passion

An intentional killing (or one with intent to inflict serious harm) is reduced from murder to **voluntary manslaughter** when it is committed in the **heat of passion** caused by **adequate provocation**. Four elements are usually required:

1. **Adequate provocation**: conduct that would arouse sudden and intense passion in a *reasonable person*, causing him to lose self-control.
2. **Actual passion**: the defendant was in fact provoked and acted in the heat of passion.
3. **No cooling-off period**: not enough time elapsed for a reasonable person's passion to cool.
4. **Causal link**: the killing was caused by the passion.

Common-law categories of adequate provocation include a serious battery, mutual combat, an illegal arrest, a threat of deadly force, and discovering a spouse in the act of adultery. **Words alone** are traditionally not adequate (*Girouard v. State* (Md. 1991), taunts by a spouse); some courts now treat words conveying information, such as a confession of infidelity, as a jury question. The provocation test is objective, but modern courts allow the jury to consider some of the defendant's circumstances. Provocation also does not excuse retaliation against a third person who did not provoke the defendant, and a long delay lets passion cool.

A minority of states recognize **imperfect self-defense**: an honest but unreasonable belief in the need to use deadly force reduces murder to voluntary manslaughter.

**MPC § 210.3(1)(b)** treats a homicide that would be murder as manslaughter when committed "under the influence of extreme mental or emotional disturbance for which there is reasonable explanation or excuse," judged from the viewpoint of a person in the actor's situation as he believes it to be. It has no fixed provocation categories, no rule against words, and no cooling-off requirement, so it is broader than the common law.

### Involuntary manslaughter

An unintentional killing is **involuntary manslaughter** when it results from:

- **Criminal (gross) negligence**: a very high degree of carelessness that creates a high risk of death, below the depraved-heart standard. (Under the MPC, recklessly causing death is manslaughter, and negligently causing death is negligent homicide, § 210.4.)
- **Unlawful act (misdemeanor manslaughter)**: a killing during an unlawful act that is a misdemeanor or non-dangerous felony (in many states the act must be malum in se or dangerous).

### The felony-murder rule

Under the **felony-murder rule**, a killing committed during the commission or attempted commission of a felony is murder, regardless of the defendant's intent to kill. The felony supplies the malice. Limits imposed by courts and legislatures:

- **Inherently dangerous felony.** Many jurisdictions require the felony to be inherently dangerous to life, judged in the abstract or on the facts.
- **Merger doctrine.** A felony that is an integral part of the homicide, such as assault with a deadly weapon, merges into the killing and cannot support felony murder (*People v. Ireland* (Cal. 1966)).
- **Enumerated felonies.** Many statutes make a felony-murder killing first degree only in the enumerated felonies (commonly arson, rape, robbery, burglary and kidnapping) and second degree otherwise.
- **In the course of the felony.** The killing must occur during the felony, including flight to a place of temporary safety, but not after the felons have reached safety.
- **Causation.** The majority follows the **agency theory**: the rule applies only to killings by a felon or an accomplice acting in furtherance of the felony, so a killing by a victim or police officer, including of a co-felon, is not felony murder (*State v. Canola* (N.J. 1977)). A minority applies **proximate cause**, imposing liability for any death that is a foreseeable result of the felony (*Commonwealth v. Redline* (Pa. 1958) considered both approaches and chose the agency theory).
- **Co-felons.** Each participant is liable for a killing committed by another felon in furtherance of the felony.
- **The underlying felony** must be independent of the homicide, and the prosecution must prove its elements, including its mental state.

Constitutional limits apply to death sentences: *Enmund v. Florida* (1982) bars it for a getaway driver who did not kill, attempt to kill or intend that a killing occur, and *Tison v. Arizona* (1987) permits it for a major participant who showed reckless indifference to human life. A few states have abolished or sharply limited the rule. The MPC has no felony-murder rule; § 210.2(1)(b) instead creates a rebuttable presumption of extreme indifference when the actor is engaged in or fleeing from robbery, rape, arson, burglary, kidnapping or felonious escape.

### Policy

Supporters say felony murder deters dangerous felonies and violence in them; critics say it punishes accidental deaths as murder, with no relation to blame.

### Applying it

Is it a homicide? Intent to kill or extreme recklessness is murder. Provocation? Reduces to voluntary manslaughter. Neither? Consider involuntary manslaughter. Felony? Run the felony-murder checklist: dangerous felony, not merged, in the course, caused by a felon or accomplice (agency) or foreseeable (proximate cause).

@example
title: Heat of passion and cooling off
problem: Wes comes home to find his spouse in bed with Zed and, within moments, shoots Zed dead. Is Wes guilty of murder or manslaughter at common law?
step: He killed intentionally, so murder unless the killing is mitigated.
step: Provocation: discovering a spouse in the act of adultery is a traditional category of adequate provocation, and Wes was actually enraged.
step: Cooling off: he acted within moments of seeing the act, so a reasonable person's passion had not cooled, and the passion caused the killing.
step: Conclusion: voluntary manslaughter. If he had waited a day and then killed Zed, the passion would have cooled, and the killing would be murder.

@mistakes
- Treating words alone as adequate provocation under the traditional rule.
- Ignoring cooling-off time when the defendant acted after a long delay.
- Applying felony murder to a felony that merges with the killing, such as the assault that caused the death.
- Applying agency-theory states' rule in a proximate-cause jurisdiction, or the reverse, when a victim or police officer kills.
