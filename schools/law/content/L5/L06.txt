@article
**Homicide** is the killing of one human being by another. It is not always a crime: executions, killings in self-defense and accidents without culpability are lawful or not criminal. Criminal homicide divides into **murder**, **manslaughter** and (in MPC jurisdictions) **negligent homicide**. This lesson covers murder; the next covers manslaughter and felony murder.

### Common-law murder

At common law, **murder is the unlawful killing of a human being with malice aforethought**. "Malice aforethought" does not require ill will or planning. It is satisfied by any of four mental states:

1. **Intent to kill.**
2. **Intent to inflict serious bodily harm.**
3. **Depraved-heart (extreme recklessness) murder**: conduct showing an abandoned and malignant heart, a conscious disregard of a very high risk of death to others, without intent to kill (firing a gun into a crowd, or a deadly game such as Russian roulette; see *Commonwealth v. Malone* (Pa. 1946)).
4. **Intent to commit a felony** (felony murder), covered in the next lesson.

Each requires an unlawful killing: causation (previous lesson) and no justification such as self-defense. A **human being** must be alive at the time of the act, which is usually a live birth (a fetus is not a human being at common law, but many states have fetal-homicide statutes), and death is determined by modern medical criteria (cessation of circulatory and respiratory function or of all brain function).

### Statutory degrees

Most states divide murder into degrees to reserve the harshest penalty for the worst killings. A typical scheme provides:

- **First-degree murder**: a killing that is **willful, deliberate and premeditated**; killing by poison, torture or lying in wait; killing in the course of specified felonies (felony murder); and some statutory categories such as killing a police officer.
- **Second-degree murder**: all other murder: intentional killings without premeditation, intent to inflict serious bodily harm, and depraved-heart killings.

**Premeditation and deliberation.** *Premeditation* means the defendant thought about the killing beforehand; *deliberation* means he considered it in a cool state, free of the passion that would reduce the crime to manslaughter. The key question is how long the reflection must last. Most courts hold that no particular time is required, so long as there was time to reflect and the decision was not impulsive (*Commonwealth v. Carroll* (Pa. 1963) says the design to kill can be formed in a short time); a minority demands more evidence of actual reflection, such as planning, motive and manner of killing (*People v. Anderson* (Cal. 1968)). Evidence typically includes buying a weapon, lying in wait, motive and a method suggesting calculation. An intent formed in an instant in the heat of a quarrel is second-degree murder (or manslaughter if there was adequate provocation).

### The Model Penal Code

The MPC does not divide murder into degrees. Under § 210.2, criminal homicide is **murder** when it is committed **purposely or knowingly**, or **recklessly under circumstances manifesting extreme indifference to the value of human life**. The reckless-with-extreme-indifference category replaces depraved-heart murder, and it covers many cases of intent to inflict serious bodily harm, which has no separate category. A presumption of extreme indifference arises if the actor was committing or fleeing certain enumerated felonies. Homicide committed recklessly (without extreme indifference) is **manslaughter** (§ 210.3), and negligently caused death is **negligent homicide** (§ 210.4).

### Burden of proof

Due process requires the prosecution to prove every element of murder beyond a reasonable doubt (*In re Winship* (1970)). States may make provocation or similar matters an affirmative defense that the defendant must prove (*Patterson v. New York* (1977)), but not make the prosecution's burden on malice dependent on the defendant disproving it (*Mullaney v. Wilbur* (1975)).

### Policy

Degrees align punishment with culpability and make it easier to justify the most severe sanctions only for killings that show calculation or other aggravation. Critics note that "premeditation" is vague and may not track moral blame.

### Applying it

Ask in order: Was there a killing of a human being, unlawful and caused by the defendant? What was the mental state: intent to kill, intent to inflict serious bodily harm, extreme recklessness, or a felony? Was there premeditation and deliberation (first degree) or not (second)? Then compare the MPC.

@example
title: Premeditation and degrees
problem: In a jurisdiction that defines first-degree murder as "willful, deliberate and premeditated killing" and second-degree murder as all other common-law murder, Ora buys a pistol on Monday, learns her ex-partner's schedule, waits outside his work on Wednesday, and shoots him dead. How is it classified, and what is the MPC classification?
step: She killed a human being, unlawfully and with causation, so the question is the mental state and the degree.
step: She purposely killed him, which is malice aforethought.
step: Buying the weapon, tracking his schedule, and waiting are classic evidence of premeditation and deliberation: she had time to reflect and acted on a plan.
step: Common-law and statutory conclusion: first-degree murder. MPC: murder under § 210.2(1)(a) because she acted purposely; the MPC has no degrees, though the sentence may reflect the grade.

@mistakes
- Assuming premeditation requires days of planning; most courts require only time to reflect, even if brief.
- Treating "malice aforethought" as requiring ill will or advance planning.
- Missing depraved-heart murder when the defendant had no intent to kill.
- Forgetting that the MPC has no degrees and no separate intent-to-injure category.

@practice mc

She intended to kill him as she swung. Words alone are not adequate provocation there. How is the killing best classified?
choice: First-degree murder, because intent to kill is enough for first degree.
choice*: Second-degree murder, because she intended to kill but without premeditation and deliberation, and words alone are not adequate provocation.
choice: Voluntary manslaughter, because she was angry when she acted.
choice: Not a homicide crime, because the killing was spontaneous.
hint: What separates the two degrees, and is there adequate provocation?
solution: Intent to kill is malice, but first degree requires premeditation and deliberation, which an instant impulse lacks. Mere anger and words are not adequate provocation, so manslaughter is unavailable.

@practice mc
prompt: Rob points a revolver with a single live round at a friend, spins the cylinder, and pulls the trigger "for a thrill." The gun fires and kills the friend. Rob did not want his friend to die. Under the Model Penal Code, how is the killing best classified?
choice: Manslaughter only, because he lacked purpose or knowledge to kill.
choice*: Murder, because he killed recklessly under circumstances manifesting extreme indifference to the value of human life.

solution: Rob consciously disregarded a very high risk of death in a way that shows extreme indifference, which is murder under § 210.2(1)(b); common law reaches the same result as depraved-heart murder (*Malone*). Negligent homicide requires unawareness, and consent is not a defense to homicide.

@practice self

(1) Ana plans for a week and poisons her business partner's coffee, intending death. (2) Ben, in a bar argument, punches Cal once, intending only to hurt him; Cal falls, strikes his head and dies. (3) Dee, immediately after a sudden quarrel starts, pulls a gun and shoots Eli, intending to kill, with no provocation beyond words. (5) Common law: second-degree murder (intent to inflict serious bodily harm). rubric: Classifies item 2 as not murder at common law and as manslaughter or negligent homicide under the MPC.
