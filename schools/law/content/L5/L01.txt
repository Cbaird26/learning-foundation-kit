@article
Every criminal case rests on two questions: **why may the state punish at all**, and **was this person on fair notice that this conduct was punishable**? The first is the theory of punishment. The second is the principle of legality. Both frame everything else in the course.

### The purposes of punishment

Courts and legislatures usually justify punishment on one or more of these grounds.

- **Retribution** looks backward. The offender deserves punishment in proportion to blameworthiness. It is a limit as well as a justification: no one should be punished more than they deserve.
- **General deterrence** looks forward. Punishing this offender discourages *other people* from offending.
- **Specific (special) deterrence** aims to discourage *this offender* from reoffending.
- **Incapacitation** keeps the offender from committing crimes in the community while confined.
- **Rehabilitation** tries to change the offender through treatment, education or training.

Retribution is a deontological theory (justice requires it); the other four are utilitarian (punishment is justified by good consequences). Most sentences mix purposes, and exam questions usually ask you to name the one the facts emphasize. A sentence imposed "to send a message to the community" is general deterrence; one imposed "so that he cannot drive for ten years" is incapacitation; one imposed "because she deserves it, whether or not it deters anyone" is retribution.

### Proportionality

The Eighth Amendment bars cruel and unusual punishments. The Supreme Court has applied it categorically to the death penalty (for example, *Roper v. Simmons* (2005), no death penalty for offenders under 18; *Atkins v. Virginia* (2002), none for intellectual disability). For juveniles, *Graham v. Florida* (2010) bars life without parole for non-homicide offenses, and *Miller v. Alabama* (2012) bars mandatory life without parole for juvenile homicide offenders; *Jones v. Mississippi* (2021) held that a sentencer need not make a separate finding of permanent incorrigibility before imposing a discretionary life-without-parole sentence. For adult prison terms, successful challenges are rare: *Solem v. Helm* (1983) struck a life sentence for a recidivist's nonviolent crime, but *Harmelin v. Michigan* (1991) and *Ewing v. California* (2003) upheld very long sentences.

### The principle of legality

*Nullum crimen sine lege*: no crime without law. It has several constitutional and interpretive supports.

1. **Crimes are defined by legislatures.** There are no federal common-law crimes (*United States v. Hudson and Goodwin* (1812)). Most states have replaced common-law crimes with codes; a few still recognize some common-law offenses.
2. **Void for vagueness.** A criminal statute must define the offense clearly enough that ordinary people can tell what is prohibited and in a way that does not invite arbitrary enforcement (*Papachristou v. City of Jacksonville* (1972); *Kolender v. Lawson* (1983); *City of Chicago v. Morales* (1999)). The residual clauses in the Armed Career Criminal Act and in related statutes fell on this ground (*Johnson v. United States* (2015)).
3. **Ex post facto clauses** (U.S. Const. art. I, § 9 for Congress and § 10 for the states) forbid retroactive criminal laws: those that criminalize conduct that was innocent when done, or increase the punishment beyond what applied when the crime was committed. They restrain *legislatures*.
4. **Due process notice for judges.** Courts are not covered by the Ex Post Facto Clauses, but due process bars retroactive application of an unforeseeable judicial enlargement of a statute (*Bouie v. City of Columbia* (1964); *Rogers v. Tennessee* (2001) applies the same standard to judicial changes of common-law rules).
5. **Rule of lenity.** Genuine ambiguity in a penal statute is resolved in the defendant's favor.

### Applying it

For an exam, ask in order: (1) What purpose does the sentence or statute serve? (2) Did the legislature define the conduct, or is the court inventing a crime? (3) Would a person of ordinary intelligence know what is forbidden, and could police enforce it arbitrarily? (4) Was the law or its construction applied retroactively to the defendant's disadvantage?

@example
title: A retroactive increase in punishment
problem: On February 20, Ana commits a fraud offense punishable by up to 5 years in prison. On March 1 the legislature amends the statute to a maximum of 8 years and makes the amendment applicable to "all sentences imposed after March 1." Ana is convicted and sentenced in June. What is the maximum sentence, and why?
step: Issue: does applying the higher maximum to conduct that predates the amendment violate the Ex Post Facto Clause?
step: Rule: a law that increases the punishment for a crime after it was committed is an ex post facto law (*Calder v. Bull* (1798)). The relevant date is the date of the offense, not of conviction or sentencing.
step: Application: the offense date is February 20, when the maximum was 5 years. The amendment is retroactive and increases punishment.
step: Conclusion: Ana may be sentenced to no more than 5 years. The amendment is valid for offenses committed on or after March 1.

@mistakes
- Treating the Ex Post Facto Clause as a limit on courts; judicial retroactivity is tested under due process notice (*Bouie*).
- Confusing general deterrence (message to others) with specific deterrence (message to this offender).
- Assuming a vague statute is saved because the defendant's own conduct was plainly bad; a law that gives no standards can be void on its face even though some applications are clear (*Johnson v. United States* (2015)).
- Applying the date of conviction instead of the date of the offense in an ex post facto problem.

choice*: General deterrence, because the sentence aims to discourage others from offending.
choice: Specific deterrence, because the sentence aims to discourage this teller from stealing again.
choice: Incapacitation, because the teller cannot embezzle while in prison.
choice: Retribution, because the teller deserves punishment in proportion to her blameworthiness.
hint: Ask whose future behavior the judge is trying to influence.
solution: The judge's stated aim is to influence other employees, which is general deterrence. Specific deterrence concerns this offender, incapacitation concerns physical restraint, and retribution concerns desert, none of which the judge mentioned.
