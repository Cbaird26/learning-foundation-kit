@article
An **excuse** admits that the act was wrong but denies that the defendant is blameworthy enough to be punished. This lesson covers duress and insanity. Remember the contrast with justification: self-defense and necessity make the act acceptable; duress and insanity excuse the actor.

### Duress

At common law, **duress** is a defense if:

1. another person **threatened** the defendant with **imminent death or serious bodily injury** (to the defendant or to a close third person);
2. the defendant **reasonably believed** the threat was real and would be carried out;
3. a person of ordinary firmness would have yielded; and
4. the defendant had **no reasonable opportunity to escape** and **did not recklessly put himself** in the situation (for example, by joining a criminal gang).

Most jurisdictions do **not** allow duress as a defense to **intentional murder** (the defendant may not kill an innocent to save himself), though it may reduce the crime or be considered in sentencing.
**Necessity versus duress.** Necessity involves pressure from natural circumstances and is a justification measured by balancing harms; duress involves a threat by a human being and is an excuse.

**MPC § 2.09.** The MPC allows the defense where the actor was coerced to engage in conduct by the use of, or a threat to use, **unlawful force** against his person or another's that a person of **reasonable firmness in his situation** would have been unable to resist. It does **not** require imminence or limit the defense to serious injuries, and it is not limited by the type of crime, so it can apply to murder, though not if the actor recklessly placed himself in the situation (or, for negligence crimes, negligently). In federal prosecutions, the defendant bears the burden of proving duress by a preponderance of the evidence (*Dixon v. United States* (2006), a firearms case).

### Insanity

Insanity is a legal term, not a medical one. It is an affirmative defense in nearly all jurisdictions, a **complete defense** that usually results in commitment rather than release. Most states place the burden of persuasion on the defendant (*Leland v. Oregon* (1952) upheld that allocation), while a minority require the prosecution to disprove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt (*Jones v. United States* (1983) upheld automatic commitment after an insanity acquittal). The tests are:

- **M'Naghten rule** (the majority approach, following an 1843 English case): at the time of the act, the defendant, because of a **disease of the mind**, was laboring under such a **defect of reason** that he did not **know the nature and quality of his act** or did not **know that it was wrong**. It is a purely **cognitive** test. Some states define "wrong" as legally wrong, others as morally wrong.
- **Irresistible impulse** (a supplement in a few states): the defendant could not control his conduct because of mental disease.
- **Durham (product) test**: the act was the product of mental disease or defect (abandoned almost everywhere).
- **MPC § 4.01**: the defendant, as a result of mental disease or defect, **lacked substantial capacity either to appreciate the criminality (wrongfulness) of his conduct or to conform his conduct to the law**. It has a cognitive prong and a volitional prong and excludes abnormality manifested only by repeated criminal conduct.
- **Federal rule** (18 U.S.C. § 17, from the Insanity Defense Reform Act of 1984): the defendant must show, by **clear and convincing evidence**, that as a result of a **severe** mental disease or defect he was **unable to appreciate the nature and quality or the wrongfulness** of his acts. It dropped the volitional prong.

The Constitution leaves the choice to the states. *Clark v. Arizona* (2006) upheld a state rule that limited the insanity defense to moral incapacity and restricted the use of mental-disease evidence to the insanity defense, not to negate mens rea. *Kahler v. Kansas* (2020) held that due process does not require a state to adopt a moral-incapacity test, so a state may let mental illness negate the mental state without having a separate insanity defense.

Some jurisdictions allow **diminished capacity** evidence (often mental illness to show that the defendant did not form specific intent). **Voluntary intoxication** is not insanity, but settled insanity from long-term substance use can qualify.

### Related doctrines

- **Competency to stand trial** is a different question, asked at the time of trial: whether the defendant has a sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding and a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings (*Dusky v. United States* (1960)).
- **Infancy.** At common law, a child under seven could not be guilty, a child from seven to fourteen was presumptively incapable, and over fourteen was treated as an adult. Statutes and juvenile courts now control.
- **Entrapment** and **intoxication** are separate defenses.

### Policy

Excuses reflect the principle that punishment requires moral blameworthiness: it should not fall on those who could not make a free and rational choice. Critics worry about fabrication and about difficulty in defining mental disease.

### Writing the IRAC for a defense

For a self-defense problem: (1) State the **issue** as a question tied to the facts. (2) State the **rule**, with each element and the jurisdiction's variations. (3) **Apply** each element to specific facts, taking the best argument for each side and noting the weak points. (4) Give a **conclusion** that answers the question, including the offense of which the defendant is guilty if the defense fails.

@example
title: Duress and murder
problem: A gangster holds a gun to Dev's head and orders him to shoot Eli, a stranger, or be killed himself. Dev shoots Eli, who dies. In a jurisdiction following the common law, does Dev have a duress defense to murder?
step: The threat was of imminent death and came from a person, and Dev reasonably believed it, so the first elements are met.
step: The common-law rule is that duress is not a defense to intentional murder, because the law will not permit taking an innocent life to save one's own.
step: Under MPC § 2.09, the question would be whether a person of reasonable firmness would have been unable to resist, and the defense is not limited to non-homicides.
step: Conclusion: no defense at common law (though the threat may mitigate the sentence); the MPC would let the jury decide.

@mistakes
- Allowing duress as a defense to intentional murder in a common-law jurisdiction.
- Confusing duress (threat by a person, an excuse) with necessity (natural pressure, a justification).
- Mixing up M'Naghten (cognitive only) with the MPC test (cognitive and volitional).
- Writing an IRAC that states the rule but never applies it to specific facts, or stops without a conclusion on guilt.

@practice self
prompt: Write an IRAC answer. Ali and Bo argue in a bar. Bo, who is unarmed and about the same size as Ali, shoves Ali twice and says, "I'm going to give you a bloody nose." Ali, who could have stepped away, pulls a pocketknife and stabs Bo once in the chest, killing him. The jurisdiction does not require retreat and allows deadly force in self-defense only against a threat of death or serious bodily harm. Is Ali likely to prevail on self-defense, and of what offense is he likely guilty?
hint: Compare the force Ali faced with the force he used, and then address the retreat rule.
hint: Finish by classifying the killing if self-defense fails, and mention imperfect self-defense.
solution: Issue: Is Ali justified in using deadly force against Bo's shoving and threat to give him a bloody nose, and if not, what homicide offense does his act constitute? Rule: self-defense requires a reasonable belief that force is necessary to protect against imminent unlawful force, and the force used must be proportional; deadly force may be used only against a threat of death or serious bodily harm. In a no-retreat jurisdiction, the ability to retreat does not defeat the defense. Application: Bo's shoves and threat of a bloody nose were imminent unlawful force, but only nondeadly force: no weapon, equal size, and no threat of death or serious harm. Ali met it with deadly force, a knife to the chest, which is disproportionate. The lack of a duty to retreat does not make the force proportional. A claim that Ali reasonably feared serious harm is weak because the threatened injury was a bloody nose. Conclusion: self-defense likely fails. Stabbing the chest with a knife shows intent to kill or inflict serious bodily harm, so Ali is likely guilty of murder (second degree, with no premeditation shown), or voluntary manslaughter in a jurisdiction that recognizes imperfect self-defense if the jury finds an honest but unreasonable belief in the need for deadly force.
rubric: States the issue and the self-defense rule, including proportionality and the deadly-force limit.
rubric: Applies the rule to specific facts, comparing the nondeadly threat with the deadly response, and addresses the retreat rule.
rubric: Reaches a conclusion that classifies the offense (murder or, with imperfect self-defense, manslaughter).
