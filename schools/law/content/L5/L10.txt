@article
A person who does not himself commit a crime may still be liable for it because he **helped** someone else commit it. This is **accomplice liability** (also called aiding and abetting or complicity). It is a theory of liability for the *same* crime, not a separate offense, so the accomplice is punished as a principal.

### Parties to a crime

Common law divided participants into four groups, and they still help organize the analysis.

- **Principal in the first degree**: the person who commits the criminal act, or uses an innocent agent (a child or a person unaware of the crime) to do so.
- **Principal in the second degree**: a person who is present and aids or encourages the crime.
- **Accessory before the fact**: a person who helps beforehand and is absent at the crime.
- **Accessory after the fact**: a person who, knowing a felony was committed, helps the felon avoid arrest or conviction. This is **not** accomplice liability for the original crime; it is a separate, lesser offense (often called obstruction or hindering prosecution).

Most states have merged the first three, so each is an accomplice liable as a principal.

### Actus reus

The accomplice must **aid, abet, assist, encourage, or solicit** the principal. Any assistance is enough: supplying a weapon, acting as lookout, driving the getaway car, or encouraging words. The aid need not be a but-for cause of the crime. **Mere presence** at the scene, or mere knowledge, is not enough, and there is no duty to intervene unless a legal duty exists (for example, a parent). Presence combined with other facts can show encouragement. A **victim** of the crime, or a person the statute is meant to protect, cannot be an accomplice.

### Mens rea

The usual requirement is a **dual intent**: (1) the intent to assist the principal and (2) the intent that the principal commit the offense, or the purpose of promoting or facilitating it (MPC § 2.06(3)(a)). A few courts accept knowledge that one is helping a serious crime. The accomplice must also have the mental state the target crime requires. Under *Rosemond v. United States* (2014), for aiding and abetting the use of a firearm in a drug-trafficking crime, the accomplice must have **advance knowledge** that a confederate will carry a gun, which gives him a chance to walk away. Under MPC § 2.06(4), when the crime is defined by a result, an accomplice needs only the culpability the offense requires as to that result (so he may be liable for a recklessly caused death).

### Scope: natural and probable consequences

Under the majority **natural and probable consequences** doctrine, an accomplice is liable not only for the crime he intended to aid but also for any other crime by the principal that was a natural and probable (foreseeable) consequence of the target crime. The MPC rejects the doctrine: an accomplice is liable only for the crimes he aids with the required purpose. Several states have narrowed the doctrine for murder.

### The principal's status

An accomplice can be convicted even if the principal is not tried, is acquitted in a separate trial, or has a personal defense such as insanity or immunity, so long as the crime occurred (*Standefer v. United States* (1980), for federal aiding-and-abetting law). Under MPC § 2.06(2)(a), a person is also liable for conduct of an innocent or irresponsible person whom he causes to commit the act.

### Withdrawal

An accomplice may avoid liability by **withdrawing** before the crime is committed: he must **repudiate** his assistance, communicate that to the principals in time, and **neutralize** the assistance he gave (retrieve the weapon, warn the victim or police). MPC § 2.06(6)(c) requires that he terminate his complicity and wholly deprive it of effectiveness, or give timely warning to law enforcement or otherwise make proper effort to prevent the crime. Merely leaving is not enough if his help is still in use.

### Accomplice liability and conspiracy

Both let one person answer for another's crime, but they differ. Accomplice liability requires **aid** and applies to the crime aided (and, under the majority view, its natural and probable consequences). Conspiracy requires **agreement**, and under *Pinkerton* it extends to all reasonably foreseeable crimes of co-conspirators in furtherance of the conspiracy. A person can be both.

### Policy

Accomplice liability reflects that crimes often depend on helpers, and it gives helpers the same deterrent threat. The mental-state requirement and the rule that mere presence is insufficient keep it from reaching innocent bystanders.

### Applying it

For each person, ask: Did he provide aid or encouragement (actus reus)? Did he intend to help and that the crime be committed (mens rea)? What crimes are within his scope? Did he effectively withdraw?

@example
title: Natural and probable consequences
problem: Ola agrees to drive Pete to a store, wait outside, and drive him away after Pete robs it. Inside, Pete, without any plan to do so, punches a customer who blocks the door. Ola drives Pete away. The jurisdiction follows the majority rule. Is Ola liable for the battery?
step: Ola is an accomplice to the robbery: she aided it by driving, with intent that it be committed.
step: Under the natural and probable consequences doctrine, an accomplice is also liable for other crimes that are a foreseeable result of the target crime.
step: Violence against a bystander who blocks the exit is a foreseeable consequence of armed or forceful robbery.
step: Conclusion: Ola is liable for the battery. Under the MPC she would be liable only for the robbery, because she did not have the purpose to promote the battery.

@mistakes
- Treating presence at the scene as aiding or abetting without any assistance or encouragement.
- Treating an accessory after the fact as an accomplice in the original crime.
- Assuming the accomplice must have been present; an accessory before the fact is liable as a principal in most states.
- Confusing accomplice liability (aid) with conspiracy liability (agreement), and applying Pinkerton to an accomplice question.
