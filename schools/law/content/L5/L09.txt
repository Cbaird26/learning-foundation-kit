@article
**Inchoate crimes** are crimes of incomplete conduct. They punish a person who has taken steps toward a crime that has not happened, because the person is dangerous and shows criminal purpose. There are three: attempt, conspiracy and solicitation.

### Attempt

**Attempt** requires (1) the **intent** to commit the target crime and (2) an **act** that goes beyond mere preparation. Intent is the heart: attempt is a specific-intent crime even when the target crime can be committed recklessly. Attempted murder requires intent to kill (so there is no attempted depraved-heart murder or attempted felony murder). For attendant circumstances, most courts require only the culpability the target crime requires.

Courts use different tests for the act.

- **Last act test** (old): the defendant has done everything he thinks necessary. Rarely followed.
- **Proximity tests**: **dangerous proximity** (how close to success, how great the danger) and **physical proximity**.
- **Unequivocality test**: the act itself must clearly show the criminal purpose.
- **MPC substantial step** (§ 5.01(1)(c)): conduct that is a substantial step in the course of conduct planned to culminate in the crime and that is **strongly corroborative of the actor's criminal purpose**. Examples in § 5.01(2) include lying in wait, reconnoitering the place, unlawfully entering the place, and possessing materials designed for the crime. The MPC test reaches earlier than the common law.

**Impossibility.** *Factual impossibility* (the attempt cannot succeed because of circumstances unknown to the defendant: the pocket is empty, the victim is not in the bed) is not a defense. *Pure legal impossibility* is a defense: if the conduct is not a crime even if the facts were exactly as the defendant believed (for example, he does something he wrongly thinks the law forbids, such as importing a lawful item he believes is contraband by law), there is no attempt. The line between factual and "hybrid" legal impossibility (a defendant takes his own umbrella believing it is another's) is hard to draw, and the MPC (§ 5.01(1)) judges attempt by the circumstances as the actor believes them to be, abolishing most impossibility defenses.

**Abandonment.** At common law, once the attempt is complete (the act beyond preparation has occurred), later abandonment is no defense. The MPC (§ 5.01(4)) allows an affirmative defense of *voluntary and complete renunciation* of criminal purpose; withdrawal due to fear of detection or postponement is not renunciation.

**Merger.** Attempt merges into the completed crime, so a defendant cannot be convicted of both.

### Conspiracy

**Conspiracy** is (1) an **agreement** between two or more persons (2) with the **intent to enter the agreement and to achieve its unlawful objective**. The agreement can be implicit and shown by circumstances. Many jurisdictions, and the federal general conspiracy statute (18 U.S.C. § 371), require an **overt act** by any conspirator in furtherance; under MPC § 5.03(5), an overt act is required except for serious felonies. The overt act can be minor, such as buying materials.

- **Bilateral vs. unilateral.** At common law, conspiracy needs two genuine parties, so an agreement with an undercover agent who only pretends does not count. The MPC (§ 5.03(1)) is **unilateral**: one person who agrees with another (even a pretender) is guilty.
- **Wharton's rule.** Crimes that by definition require two participants (adultery, dueling) cannot be a conspiracy between only those two.
- **Pinkerton liability.** *Pinkerton v. United States* (1946) held that a conspirator is liable for all substantive crimes committed by co-conspirators in furtherance of the conspiracy that were reasonably foreseeable, even if he did not know of or participate in them. The MPC rejects this rule, and many states do.
- **Merger.** Conspiracy does **not** merge into the completed crime at common law, so both can be punished. The MPC (§ 1.07) does not allow convictions for both conspiracy and the completed offense and also limits convictions for several inchoate offenses aimed at the same crime (§ 5.05(3)).
- **Scope.** Whether there is one conspiracy or several depends on whether the parties shared a common agreement (*Kotteakos v. United States* (1946), a "wheel" without a rim is not a single conspiracy).
- **Withdrawal.** Withdrawal is not a defense to the conspiracy itself once the agreement exists, but a timely, communicated withdrawal ends liability for later crimes (the MPC, § 5.03(6), instead gives an affirmative defense to a defendant who thwarts the conspiracy's success under circumstances showing complete and voluntary renunciation of criminal purpose).

### Solicitation

**Solicitation** is asking, urging or commanding another to commit a crime **with the intent that the crime be committed**. It is complete when the request is made, even if the other person refuses. It merges into the target crime or attempt if the solicitee commits or attempts it. Offers to provide illegal material are not protected speech (*United States v. Williams* (2008)).

### Policy

Inchoate crimes allow intervention before harm, but they risk punishing thoughts. Intent and some objective act or agreement keep them tied to conduct.

### Applying it

For each defendant, ask: Did he intend the target crime? What did he do (act, agreement, request)? Choose the offense that fits, then check impossibility, abandonment, merger and grading.

@example
title: Preparation or attempt under the MPC?
problem: Tia plans to rob a bank. She buys a ski mask, drives to the bank with a loaded pistol, puts on the mask in the car in the parking lot and is arrested as she opens the car door to enter. Is she guilty of attempted robbery under the MPC?
step: Intent: she plainly intended to commit robbery, so the mental state is met.
step: Act: under § 5.01(1)(c), a substantial step strongly corroborative of criminal purpose is needed. Reconnoitering and possessing materials designed for the crime are listed examples.
step: Driving to the bank armed and masked is strongly corroborative of her purpose to rob.
step: Conclusion: guilty of attempted robbery under the MPC. Under a proximity test she might not have gone far enough, which shows why the MPC reaches earlier.

@mistakes
- Finding attempt for a reckless or negligent killing; attempt requires intent to commit the target crime.
- Treating all impossibility as a defense; only pure legal impossibility is one under the common law, and the MPC abolishes it for most cases.
- Merging conspiracy into the completed offense at common law; they are separate.
- Assuming withdrawal from a conspiracy erases the conspiracy; it only ends liability for later acts.
