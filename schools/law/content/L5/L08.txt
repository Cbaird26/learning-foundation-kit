@article
Property crimes arose at common law as separate offenses, each patched onto the one before to close a gap. The distinctions still matter on the bar exam and in states that have not consolidated theft statutes. The MPC (§ 223.1) consolidates larceny, embezzlement and false pretenses into a single offense of **theft**, but you should know the common-law elements.

### Larceny

**Larceny** is the (1) trespassory (2) taking and (3) carrying away (4) of the personal property (5) of another (6) with intent to permanently deprive.

- **Trespassory taking**: taking possession without the owner's consent. A person with mere *custody* (an employee handling the employer's goods) who takes them commits larceny; one with *possession* (a bailee) who keeps them has not trespassed.
- **Asportation**: any movement, however slight, of the property.
- **Personal property**: tangible movable things; land and services are not subject to larceny.
- **Intent to permanently deprive**, present at the time of the taking. It includes intent to keep the property for so long that its value is lost, or to dispose of it so that the owner is unlikely to recover it. A taker who intends to return the property has no larcenous intent (the MPC separately punishes unauthorized use of automobiles and other motor vehicles, § 223.9). A bona fide claim of right negates intent.
- **Larceny by trick**: the owner consents to *possession* because of the defendant's fraud and the defendant intends to keep the property. It is larceny because the owner gave up only possession.

### Embezzlement and false pretenses

**Embezzlement** is the fraudulent conversion of the property of another by a person lawfully in possession of it (a trustee, bank officer or agent). There is no trespass because the defendant held the property lawfully.

**False pretenses** requires that the defendant obtain *title* to the victim's property by a knowingly false representation of a past or existing material fact, with intent to defraud, and that the victim actually rely on it. The key difference from larceny by trick is whether the victim intended to transfer title (false pretenses) or only possession (larceny by trick). A promise of future conduct is generally not a misrepresentation of fact.

**Receiving stolen property** requires receipt of stolen property, knowledge or belief that it was stolen, and intent to deprive the owner.

Theft is graded by **value**, usually fair market value at the time and place of the taking (a petty offense below a statutory threshold, and a felony above it).

### Robbery

**Robbery** is larceny from a person or from his presence by force or by threat of immediate force. The force or threat must be used to take the property or to retain it as part of one continuous transaction. Taking by stealth (picking a pocket) is larceny, not robbery. Sudden snatching without force beyond that needed to take the item is usually larceny; some states treat it as robbery. The threat must be of immediate harm, so a threat of future harm is extortion (blackmail) rather than robbery. Armed robbery and robbery in the first degree are aggravated forms. Robbery is an enumerated felony for first-degree felony murder in most states.

### Burglary

At **common law**, burglary was the (1) **breaking** and (2) **entering** of (3) the **dwelling** (4) **of another** (5) at **night** (6) with **intent to commit a felony** inside.

- **Breaking** includes opening a closed door or window, even if unlocked, and constructive breaking by fraud or threat; entering through an open door is not a breaking.
- **Entry** is any part of the body, or an instrument used to commit the felony.
- **Intent** must exist **at the time of entry**. A person who enters lawfully and forms the intent later has not committed burglary.

Modern statutes broaden it: no breaking is required, any building or structure is covered, no night requirement, and the intent may be to commit any crime. Under MPC § 221.1 burglary is entry into a building or occupied structure with purpose to commit a crime inside unless the premises were open to the public or the entry was licensed.

### Policy

Burglary protects the security of the home and the safety of occupants; robbery combines a property offense with a violent threat; and the distinctions among larceny, embezzlement and false pretenses follow how the defendant got the property.

### Applying it

Ask in order: How did the defendant get the property (trespassory taking, lawful possession, or title through deceit)? What intent did he have, and when? Was there force or threat to a person? Was there an unlawful entry with intent to commit a crime inside?

@example
title: Larceny, breaking and the time of intent
problem: At 2 a.m., Ora opens the closed but unlocked front door of a neighbor's house, walks in, and takes a television, intending to sell it. At common law, what crimes did she commit?
step: Larceny: she took the television without consent, carried it away, and intended to keep it permanently, so each element is met.
step: Burglary: opening a closed door is a breaking even though it was unlocked, she entered a dwelling of another at night, and she had the intent to steal (larceny is a felony) at the time of entry.
step: Burglary is complete on entry, and larceny is a separate offense with different elements, so in most states she can be convicted of both (some courts merge them for sentencing).
step: Conclusion: common-law burglary and larceny. If she had entered through an open door, there would be no breaking and so no common-law burglary (though many modern statutes would still apply).

@mistakes
- Treating a bailee or agent's wrongful keeping as larceny; it is embezzlement because possession was lawful.
- Missing larceny by trick when the owner gave only possession: title passing separates false pretenses.
- Finding burglary when the intent to commit a crime was formed after a lawful entry.
- Calling pickpocketing robbery; robbery requires force or a threat.

@practice self
prompt: Ann and Bo plan an armed robbery of a jewelry store. Bo waits at the wheel of the getaway car. Ann enters, takes jewelry at gunpoint, and runs to the car; Bo drives off. A security guard chases on foot and, at a corner two blocks away, is still in pursuit when Ann fires at him and kills him. The jurisdiction follows the common law, uses the agency theory, and makes felony murder first-degree murder when the felony is robbery. In IRAC form, analyze Ann's and Bo's liability for felony murder.
hint: Check that the felony is proper, the killing is in the course of it, and who caused the death.
hint: Consider whether Bo is liable for a killing committed by his co-felon.
solution: Issue: whether Ann and Bo are liable for felony murder of the guard. Rule: a killing committed in the course of an inherently dangerous felony (including flight until the felons reach safety) by a felon or accomplice in furtherance of the felony is murder, and in this jurisdiction robbery makes it first degree. Application: armed robbery is an enumerated, dangerous felony that does not merge with the homicide. The guard was killed while the felons were still fleeing and being pursued, so it was in the course of the felony and before temporary safety. Ann pulled the trigger, so the agency theory is satisfied. Bo was a participant (the driver), the shooting was committed by a co-felon in furtherance of the escape, and robbery makes the death foreseeable. Conclusion: both are liable for first-degree felony murder. Neither Bo's lack of intent to kill nor the fact that he did not shoot is a defense, though a death sentence for Bo would be limited by *Enmund* and *Tison*.
rubric: Identifies robbery as a proper, enumerated felony and finds the killing in the course of it because of the continuing flight.
rubric: Applies the agency theory by noting that Ann, a felon, fired and that Bo is liable as a co-felon.
rubric: Concludes first-degree felony murder for both and notes that intent to kill is not required.
