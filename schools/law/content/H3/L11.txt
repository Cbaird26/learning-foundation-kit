@article
Real numbers live on a line. Complex numbers, $z = a + bi$ with $i^2 = -1$, need a plane. Plot $z$ at the point $(a, b)$: the horizontal axis is the **real axis** and the vertical axis is the **imaginary axis**. Adding complex numbers is then just adding vectors, and the **conjugate** $\bar{z} = a - bi$ is the mirror image of $z$ in the real axis.

### Modulus and argument

The **modulus** $|z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$ is the distance from the origin. The **argument** $\theta = \arg z$ is the counterclockwise angle from the positive real axis to the ray through $z$. It is determined only up to multiples of $2\pi$; its **principal value** lies in $(-\pi, \pi]$. This is polar coordinates again, with the same caution: decide the quadrant from the signs of $a$ and $b$ rather than trusting $\arctan(b/a)$.

Reading off $a = r\cos\theta$ and $b = r\sin\theta$ gives the **polar form**

$$z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) = re^{i\theta}, \qquad r = |z|.$$

The second equality uses **Euler's formula**, $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$. For now, read it as compact notation for the point on the unit circle at angle $\theta$. The next calculation shows why exponential notation is so natural.

### Multiplication is rotation and scaling

Multiply two unit-circle points and use the sum formulas from week 5:

$$(\cos\alpha + i\sin\alpha)(\cos\beta + i\sin\beta) = \cos(\alpha + \beta) + i\sin(\alpha + \beta).$$

So $e^{i\alpha}e^{i\beta} = e^{i(\alpha + \beta)}$: **angles add**. For general complex numbers,

$$|zw| = |z||w|, \qquad \arg(zw) = \arg z + \arg w.$$

Multiplying by $w$ **scales** by $|w|$ and **rotates** by $\arg w$. Multiplying by $i = e^{i\pi/2}$ is a quarter-turn. Division divides the moduli and subtracts the arguments.

### Powers and roots

Repeated multiplication gives **de Moivre's theorem**:

$$z^n = r^n e^{in\theta} = r^n(\cos n\theta + i\sin n\theta).$$

Run it backwards to find roots. Because $\theta$ is only defined up to $2\pi k$, the equation $w^n = re^{i\theta}$ has exactly $n$ solutions,

$$w_k = r^{1/n}\exp\left(i\,\frac{\theta + 2\pi k}{n}\right), \qquad k = 0, 1, \dots, n - 1,$$

equally spaced on a circle of radius $r^{1/n}$. The three cube roots of $1$, for example, are $1$, $e^{2\pi i/3}$ and $e^{4\pi i/3}$: the corners of an equilateral triangle.

### The link to real oscillations

For real $\omega t$, $\operatorname{Re}\left(e^{i\omega t}\right) = \cos\omega t$. As $t$ increases, $e^{i\omega t}$ circles the unit circle at $\omega$ radians per second, and its real part is its shadow on the horizontal axis: a cosine wave. Next week we use this to model oscillations.

```python
import cmath
z = -1 + 1j
print(round(abs(z), 4), round(cmath.phase(z), 4))   # 1.4142 2.3562
w = cmath.rect(2, cmath.pi/3)                       # 2*e^(i*pi/3)
print(round(w.real, 4), round(w.imag, 4))           # 1.0 1.7321
```

@example
title: Polar form and a power
problem: Write $z = -1 + \sqrt{3}\,i$ in polar form and use de Moivre's theorem to compute $z^3$.
step: The modulus is $|z| = \sqrt{1 + 3} = 2$.
step: For the argument, $\tan\theta = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{-1}$ has reference angle $\frac{\pi}{3}$. The point has $a < 0$ and $b > 0$, so it is in Quadrant II and $\theta = \pi - \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{2\pi}{3}$. Thus $z = 2e^{2\pi i/3}$.
step: Apply de Moivre: $z^3 = 2^3 e^{i\cdot 3\cdot 2\pi/3} = 8e^{2\pi i} = 8(\cos 2\pi + i\sin 2\pi) = 8$.
step: Check by direct multiplication: $z^2 = 1 - 2\sqrt{3}\,i - 3 = -2 - 2\sqrt{3}\,i$ and $z^3 = (-2 - 2\sqrt{3}\,i)(-1 + \sqrt{3}\,i) = 2 + 6 + (-2\sqrt{3} + 2\sqrt{3})i = 8$.

@example
title: Multiplication as rotation and scaling
problem: Compute $(1 + i)(\sqrt{3} + i)$ in polar form, and check it against direct multiplication.
step: Convert each factor: $1 + i = \sqrt{2}\,e^{i\pi/4}$ and $\sqrt{3} + i = 2e^{i\pi/6}$.
step: Multiply moduli and add arguments: the product is $2\sqrt{2}\,e^{i(\pi/4 + \pi/6)} = 2\sqrt{2}\,e^{5\pi i/12}$.
step: Convert back using $\cos 75^\circ = \frac{\sqrt{6} - \sqrt{2}}{4}$ and $\sin 75^\circ = \frac{\sqrt{6} + \sqrt{2}}{4}$: the real part is $2\sqrt{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{6} - \sqrt{2}}{4} = \sqrt{3} - 1$ and the imaginary part is $2\sqrt{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{6} + \sqrt{2}}{4} = \sqrt{3} + 1$.
step: Direct multiplication gives $\sqrt{3} + i + \sqrt{3}\,i + i^2 = (\sqrt{3} - 1) + (\sqrt{3} + 1)i$. The two methods agree, and the sum formulas of week 5 are what make the angle rule work.

@mistakes
- Computing the argument as $\arctan(b/a)$ without checking the quadrant.
- Adding the moduli or multiplying the arguments. In a product, the moduli multiply and the arguments add.
- Giving only one $n$th root. There are $n$ of them, equally spaced around a circle.
- Using degrees inside $e^{i\theta}$. The exponential form requires radians.

@practice num
prompt: The equation $z^3 = 8$ has three solutions in the complex plane. Find the one in the upper half-plane (positive imaginary part), written as $a + bi$.
answer: -1+sqrt(3)i
check: [z for z in roots([1, 0, 0, -8]) if z.imag > 0][0]
hint: Write $8 = 8e^{0i}$ and use the root formula with $n = 3$. The roots have modulus $8^{1/3}$.
hint: The arguments are $0$, $\frac{2\pi}{3}$ and $\frac{4\pi}{3}$. Which one lies in the upper half-plane, apart from the real root?
solution: The roots are $2e^{0}$, $2e^{2\pi i/3}$ and $2e^{4\pi i/3}$. The one with positive imaginary part is $2e^{2\pi i/3} = 2\left(-\frac{1}{2} + \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}i\right) = -1 + \sqrt{3}\,i$. (The other complex root is its conjugate $-1 - \sqrt{3}\,i$, and the real root is $2$.)

answer: -64
check: (sqrt(3) - I)**6
hint: Convert $\sqrt{3} - i$ to polar form. Be careful with the sign of the argument.
