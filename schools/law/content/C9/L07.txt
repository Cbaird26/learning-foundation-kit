@article
Two accounting estimates affect almost every company's profit: which inventory costs have been *sold* and how much of a long-lived asset has been *used up*. Both change the numbers without moving a dollar of cash, which is why they matter in contracts, taxes and fraud cases.

### Inventory cost flow

A retailer buys the same product at different prices during the year. When it sells a unit, which price goes to **cost of goods sold (COGS)**? The identity is:

$$\text{Beginning inventory} + \text{Purchases} = \text{Goods available for sale} = \text{COGS} + \text{Ending inventory}$$

Three common **cost-flow assumptions** divide the goods available for sale between COGS and ending inventory. They need not match the physical flow of goods.

- **FIFO (first-in, first-out)** assumes the oldest costs are sold first, so COGS uses the earliest layers and ending inventory carries the most recent costs.
- **LIFO (last-in, first-out)** assumes the newest costs are sold first. U.S. GAAP allows LIFO (and U.S. tax law allows it if the company also uses LIFO in its financial reports); international standards do not.
- **Weighted-average cost** divides total cost of goods available by total units to get one average unit cost.

In a period of **rising prices**, FIFO gives the lowest COGS and highest income; LIFO gives the highest COGS, lowest income and lowest taxable income. In falling prices the effects reverse. Over time the total cost of goods is the same; only the timing differs. Under GAAP, inventory is also reported at the lower of cost and net realizable value (for FIFO and average cost), so a write-down is recorded when the goods can be sold for less than they cost.

### Depreciation

A building or machine yields benefits for years, so **depreciation** allocates its cost over its **useful life**. It is an allocation, not a valuation: book value will not equal market value. For **straight-line** depreciation:

$$\text{Annual depreciation} = \frac{\text{Cost} - \text{Salvage value}}{\text{Useful life in years}}$$

Salvage value is the amount expected at the end of life. **Book value** (carrying amount) $=$ cost $-$ accumulated depreciation. Accelerated methods (such as double-declining balance) record more expense early. Land is not depreciated. Depreciation is a noncash expense, added back in the cash-flow statement. Tax depreciation follows separate tax rules and often differs from the book figure.

### Why lawyers care

Because both are estimates, a company's profit depends on choices. A switch of inventory method, a longer useful life, or higher salvage value can raise earnings without changing operations. Purchase agreements fix the accounting methods used for closing adjustments, and courts examine estimates for good faith in earn-out and fraud disputes. In valuing a business, book value of equipment is only a starting point.

@example
title: FIFO, LIFO, average and straight-line
problem: A store has 100 units at \$10 in beginning inventory, buys 200 units at \$12 and then 100 units at \$14, and sells 250 units. Find COGS and ending inventory under FIFO, LIFO and weighted average. Separately, a machine costing \$50,000 with salvage value \$5,000 and a five-year life is depreciated straight-line; find annual depreciation and book value after two years.
step: Goods available: $100(10) + 200(12) + 100(14) = 1{,}000 + 2{,}400 + 1{,}400 = 4{,}800$ for 400 units.
step: FIFO COGS $= 100(10) + 150(12) = 2{,}800$; ending inventory $= 50(12) + 100(14) = 2{,}000$. LIFO COGS $= 100(14) + 150(12) = 3{,}200$; ending $= 1{,}600$.
step: Weighted average cost per unit $= 4{,}800/400 = 12$. COGS $= 250 \times 12 = 3{,}000$; ending inventory $= 150 \times 12 = 1{,}800$. In rising prices FIFO gives the lowest COGS.
step: Depreciation $= (50{,}000 - 5{,}000)/5 = 9{,}000$ per year. After two years accumulated depreciation is $18{,}000$ and book value is $50{,}000 - 18{,}000 = 32{,}000$.
@mistakes
- Assuming the cost-flow method must match the physical movement of goods.
- Forgetting that FIFO gives the lowest COGS only when prices are rising.
- Subtracting salvage value twice, or forgetting to subtract it at all, in straight-line depreciation.
- Depreciating land, or treating depreciation as the way an asset's market value is tracked.
