@article
What is a business worth? The book value on the balance sheet is a historical-cost figure and rarely answers that. Valuation asks what a rational buyer would pay for the **future cash** the business can produce. Three approaches are standard.

### Income approach: discounted cash flow

The **discounted cash flow (DCF)** method forecasts the cash the business will generate, then finds the present value of each year's cash and of the value at the end of the forecast (the **terminal value**), using a discount rate that reflects risk:

$$V_0 = \sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t} + \frac{TV_n}{(1+r)^n}.$$

Two shortcuts for the terminal value, or for steady businesses. A **perpetuity** pays $C$ every period forever, so $PV = C/r$. A **growing perpetuity** whose next payment is $C_1$ and grows at rate $g$ forever (with $g < r$) has

$$PV = \frac{C_1}{r - g}.$$

This last form is the Gordon growth model for a stock paying a growing dividend. The result is highly sensitive: a business with next-year cash flow of \$90,000, $r = 11\%$ and $g = 3\%$ is worth \$1,125,000; at $r = 10\%$ it is worth \$1,285,714.

### Net present value

The **net present value (NPV)** of a project is the present value of its future cash flows minus the amount invested. A positive NPV means the project earns more than the discount rate. In a deal, a price below the PV of the cash flows is a good bargain; a price above it destroys value for the buyer, at that rate.

### Market approach: multiples

Compare the target with similar companies or transactions. A common multiple is **enterprise value** divided by EBITDA. Enterprise value (EV) is the value of the whole operating business, owned by lenders and shareholders together. To get to the value of equity:

$$\text{Equity value} = \text{EV} - \text{Debt} + \text{Cash}.$$

### Asset approach

Add up the fair value of assets and subtract liabilities. It is used for holding companies, liquidations and asset-heavy firms. It differs from book value because assets are restated to fair value and unrecorded items are included. When a buyer pays more than the fair value of identifiable net assets, the excess is recorded as **goodwill**.

### Why lawyers care

Valuation is the battleground of many legal disputes. Shareholders who dissent from a merger may seek **appraisal** of the fair value of their shares (in Delaware, under DGCL § 262); partners and spouses divide business interests; damages experts value lost businesses; and tax disputes turn on fair market value. Courts accept several methods but examine the assumptions, because the discount rate and the growth forecast can swing the value by large amounts. The standard of value (fair value, fair market value, investment value) is defined by statute, case law or contract, and discounts for minority interests or lack of marketability apply differently across jurisdictions and standards. For each valuation, ask who prepared the forecast, how reliable it is and what discount rate was used.

@example
title: DCF with a terminal sale
problem: A business is expected to generate \$60,000 of cash at the end of each of the next five years, and then be sold for \$400,000 at the end of year 5. At a 10% discount rate, what is it worth today? Round to cents.
step: PV of the five cash flows (ordinary annuity): $60{,}000 \times (1 - 1.1^{-5})/0.10 = 60{,}000 \times 3.790787 = 227{,}447.21$.
step: PV of the sale: $400{,}000/1.1^5 = 400{,}000/1.61051 = 248{,}368.53$.
step: Total value $= 227{,}447.21 + 248{,}368.53 = 475{,}815.74$.
step: A price above \$475,815.74 gives the buyer a negative NPV at a 10% discount rate; a higher discount rate would reduce the value.
@mistakes
- Using the perpetuity formula with $g \ge r$, which gives nonsense.
- Discounting the terminal value by one year too few or too many.
- Subtracting only debt when going from enterprise value to equity value, and forgetting to add cash.
- Treating book value, a multiple or a DCF number as the single objective value.

prompt: A buyer values a business with a DCF, then learns that the business is riskier than the buyer assumed. Holding the forecast cash flows constant, what is the proper effect?
choice*: Raise the discount rate, which lowers the present value.
choice: Lower the discount rate, which raises the present value.
choice: Leave the rate alone, since the cash flows are unchanged.
choice: Raise the terminal value to compensate for risk.
hint: Risk is reflected in the rate of return required by investors.
solution: More risk means a higher required return, so a higher discount rate and a lower value. Raising the terminal value would ignore the risk.
