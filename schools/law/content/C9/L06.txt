@article
Suppose a law firm finishes a client's work in December, sends the bill, and is paid in January. Did it earn revenue in December or January? **Cash-basis accounting** says January: record revenue when cash is received and expenses when cash is paid. **Accrual accounting** says December: record revenue when it is *earned* and expenses when they are *incurred*, whatever the cash timing. U.S. GAAP requires accrual accounting for public companies, because it better matches a period's efforts with its accomplishments.

### Revenue recognition and matching

Under the revenue standard (ASC 606), a company recognizes revenue when it satisfies a performance obligation by transferring promised goods or services to the customer, in the amount it expects to be entitled to. The **matching principle** records an expense in the same period as the revenue it helped produce. Sales commissions, cost of goods sold and wages belong to the period of the related sales, not the period they are paid.

### Adjusting entries

At period end, accountants make **adjusting entries** so that accounts reflect accrual-basis reality. There are four patterns:

- **Prepaid expense (deferral).** Cash was paid first; the expense comes later. Paying \$12,000 for a year of insurance creates an asset. Each month, \$1,000 moves to insurance expense.
- **Unearned revenue (deferral).** Cash was received first, so the company owes service. Received cash is a liability, then becomes revenue as the work is performed.
- **Accrued expense.** The expense was incurred but not yet paid, such as wages for days worked after the last payday. Debit expense, credit a payable.
- **Accrued revenue.** The revenue was earned but not yet billed or collected. Debit a receivable, credit revenue.

### Reconciling net income and cash

The two bases differ by the changes in working-capital accounts and by noncash items. Cash collected from customers $=$ revenue $-$ increase in accounts receivable (or $+$ a decrease). The indirect method of Week 5 is just that reasoning applied to every account:

$$\text{CFO} = \text{Net income} + \text{Depreciation} - \Delta\text{AR} - \Delta\text{Inventory} - \Delta\text{Prepaids} + \Delta\text{AP} + \Delta\text{Accruals}$$

where each $\Delta$ is the end-of-period balance minus the beginning balance.

### Why lawyers care

Accrual accounting is a source of **judgment**: when is a performance obligation satisfied, how much should be reserved for returns, how long is a customer contract? Bad judgment, or deliberate manipulation, shows up as revenue recognized early ("channel stuffing," bill-and-hold) and appears in securities enforcement and in disputes over earn-outs and purchase-price adjustments. Taxes follow separate rules (taxpayers may use a cash or accrual method depending on their size and type of business, and tax accounting differs in many details from GAAP), so a firm's tax return and financial statements can legitimately show different income.

@example
title: Adjusting entries and cash from customers
problem: On October 1 a firm pays \$12,000 for a one-year insurance policy. On November 1 it receives \$9,000 for six months of services starting then. Its employees earn \$1,000 per day for the three days after the last payday, December 29 to 31. Find the December 31 adjustments and the cash collected if revenue is \$100,000 and receivables rose by \$10,000.
step: Insurance: three months (October to December) have passed. Expense $= 12{,}000 \times 3/12 = 3{,}000$; the remaining prepaid asset is $9{,}000$.
step: Unearned revenue: two of six months are earned. Revenue $= 9{,}000 \times 2/6 = 3{,}000$; the remaining liability is $6{,}000$.
step: Accrued wages: three days at $1{,}000$ gives $3{,}000$. Debit wages expense, credit wages payable.
step: Cash collected from customers $= 100{,}000 - 10{,}000 = 90{,}000$, because \$10,000 of revenue is still receivable.
@mistakes
- Recognizing revenue when cash arrives even though the service has not been performed.
- Forgetting to accrue wages for days worked but not yet paid.
- Adding an increase in accounts receivable to cash instead of subtracting it.
- Believing that accrual net income and cash from operations should be equal in any single period.

@practice self
prompt: A company reports revenue of \$200,000 and total expenses of \$150,000, including depreciation of \$20,000. During the year accounts receivable rose by \$15,000, prepaid expenses rose by \$4,000 and accounts payable rose by \$6,000. All other working-capital accounts were unchanged. Compute net income, then reconcile it to cash from operations. Then verify with the direct method by computing cash collected from customers and cash paid for expenses.
hint: Net income comes first, then adjust for the noncash expense and each working-capital change.
hint: For the direct method, cash collected equals revenue minus the increase in receivables.
solution: Net income $= 200{,}000 - 150{,}000 = 50{,}000$. CFO $= 50{,}000 + 20{,}000 - 15{,}000 - 4{,}000 + 6{,}000 = 57{,}000$. Direct method: cash collected from customers $= 200{,}000 - 15{,}000 = 185{,}000$. Expenses other than depreciation are $150{,}000 - 20{,}000 = 130{,}000$. Cash paid is that amount less the increase in payables (\$6,000 was incurred but not paid) plus the increase in prepaids (\$4,000 was paid but not yet expensed): $130{,}000 - 6{,}000 + 4{,}000 = 128{,}000$. Check: $185{,}000 - 128{,}000 = 57{,}000$.
rubric: Net income of \$50,000 is computed first.
rubric: Depreciation is added back; the receivable and prepaid increases are subtracted; the payable increase is added.
rubric: CFO of \$57,000 is confirmed by the direct method.
