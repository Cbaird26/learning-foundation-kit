Every business, from a corner bakery to a public corporation, can be described by one sentence that always stays true. It is called the **accounting equation**:

$$\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Equity}$$

Think of it as two views of the same pile of resources. The left side says *what the business has*. The right side says *who has a claim on it*: creditors first, then the owners.

### The three elements

**Assets** are resources the business controls that are expected to provide future economic benefit: cash, accounts receivable (money customers owe), inventory, supplies, equipment, buildings. **Liabilities** are obligations to outsiders: accounts payable (bills owed to suppliers), notes payable (loans), wages payable, unearned revenue (cash received for work not yet done). **Equity** is the owners' residual claim. In a corporation it is called stockholders' equity; in a sole proprietorship, owner's equity. Rearranged, the equation says $\text{Equity} = \text{Assets} - \text{Liabilities}$, which is why equity is also called **net assets** or **book value**.

Equity changes in four ways. Owners' investments and revenues increase it. Owners' withdrawals (dividends, in a corporation) and expenses decrease it. So:

$$\text{Ending equity} = \text{Beginning equity} + \text{Investments} + \text{Revenues} - \text{Expenses} - \text{Withdrawals}$$

### Transaction analysis

An accounting **transaction** is an event that changes the entity's assets, liabilities or equity and can be measured in money. Signing a contract that nobody has performed yet is usually not a transaction; delivering the goods or paying the cash is. Every transaction keeps the equation balanced, in one of a few patterns:

- An asset goes up and another asset goes down (buy equipment for cash). Total assets do not change.
- An asset and a liability both go up (borrow from a bank; buy supplies on credit).
- An asset and equity both go up (owner invests cash; the business earns revenue).
- An asset and a liability both go down (pay a supplier with cash).
- An asset goes down and equity goes down (pay rent; owner withdraws cash).

Accounting also follows the **entity assumption**: the business is treated as separate from its owners. An owner's personal car is not a business asset unless the owner contributes it. U.S. GAAP (generally accepted accounting principles, set by the FASB and required for public companies by the SEC) records most assets at historical cost, not market value, so equity on the books can differ a great deal from what a buyer would pay.

### Why lawyers care

The equation is the skeleton of every financial statement you will read. Lawyers meet it in contract covenants ("maintain net worth of at least \$2 million"), in bankruptcy and creditor remedies, and in the law of voidable transfers: under the Uniform Voidable Transactions Act a debtor is generally insolvent when the sum of its debts is greater than all of its assets at a fair valuation. That is the balance-sheet test, and it is a statement about the equation, with one important twist: the law uses fair valuation, not book value. State corporate statutes limit dividends by similar tests. A document that does not balance is a red flag for error or fraud.

@example
title: Five transactions in the equation
problem: Ana starts a design studio. (1) She invests \$30,000 cash. (2) The studio buys a computer for \$8,000 cash. (3) It buys \$2,000 of supplies on account. (4) It earns \$5,000 cash for a logo job. (5) It pays \$1,200 cash for rent. Find total assets, liabilities and equity at the end.
step: Transaction 1: Cash $+30{,}000$. Assets $30{,}000 = $ Liabilities $0 +$ Equity $30{,}000$.
step: Transactions 2 and 3: the computer swaps cash for equipment (assets stay $30{,}000$). Supplies on account add an asset $+2{,}000$ and a liability (accounts payable) $+2{,}000$. Assets are now $32{,}000$; liabilities $2{,}000$; equity $30{,}000$.
step: Transaction 4: cash and revenue both rise by $5{,}000$. Assets $37{,}000$; equity $35{,}000$. Transaction 5: cash and equity (through rent expense) both fall by $1{,}200$. Assets $35{,}800$; equity $33{,}800$.
step: Check: assets of $35{,}800$ equal liabilities of $2{,}000$ plus equity of $33{,}800$. Equity also equals $30{,}000 + 5{,}000 - 1{,}200 = 33{,}800$.
@mistakes
- Treating a purchase of equipment for cash as a decrease in total assets; one asset replaced another.
- Counting a signed but unperformed contract as an asset or liability before anyone has delivered or paid.
- Putting an owner's withdrawal in with expenses; it reduces equity but is not an expense of earning revenue.
- Confusing book value (historical cost based) with fair value, which the law often uses.
