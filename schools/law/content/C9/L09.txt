@article
Would you rather receive \$10,000 today or \$10,000 in three years? Almost everyone chooses today, and not only because of impatience. Money received now can be invested, earning interest, and future money is also riskier. The **time value of money** turns this into arithmetic. It underlies loan payments, bond prices, lease accounting, business valuation and the way courts convert future losses into a present award.

### Compound interest and future value

With interest rate $r$ per period compounded each period, $P$ dollars become, after $n$ periods,

$$FV = P(1 + r)^n.$$

For example, \$20,000 at 5% per year for four years grows to $20{,}000 \times 1.05^4 = 24{,}310.13$ (rounded to cents). The interest earns interest: that is compounding. The rate and the period must match: for monthly payments use the monthly rate (annual rate divided by 12, in the simple convention) and the number of months.

### Present value of a lump sum

**Present value** runs the same formula backward. The value today of a single amount $F$ received in $n$ periods, discounted at rate $r$ per period, is

$$PV = \frac{F}{(1 + r)^n}.$$

The rate $r$ is the **discount rate**: it reflects both the time value and the risk of getting paid. The higher the discount rate or the longer the wait, the lower the present value.

### Annuities

An **annuity** is a series of equal payments at equal intervals. In an **ordinary annuity**, payments are made at the *end* of each period. The present value of $n$ payments of $C$ at rate $r$ is

$$PV = C \cdot \frac{1 - (1 + r)^{-n}}{r}.$$

In an **annuity due**, payments come at the *beginning* of each period, so every payment is one period closer and the present value is $(1 + r)$ times the ordinary-annuity value. Rent is typically an annuity due; loan payments and most bond coupons are ordinary annuities.

### Where lawyers use it

- **Damages.** Future lost earnings or lost profits are generally awarded at present value, so an expert discounts each future year's loss. Courts differ on the method and the discount rate, and often treat inflation and taxes in different ways, so a lawyer should know how the expert's rate and assumptions drive the number.
- **Settlements.** A structured settlement of \$30,000 a year for four years is worth far less than \$120,000 now; comparing offers means finding present values at a stated rate.
- **Interest on judgments.** Prejudgment and postjudgment interest are set by statute or contract and differ by jurisdiction.
- **Contracts and leases.** Liquidated damages, installment sales and lease payments all involve time value.

Every number in a calculation depends on assumptions (the rate, the timing, whether payments are at period ends). A good memo states them.

@example
title: A lump sum and an annuity
problem: Find the present value of \$10,000 to be received in three years at an 8% annual discount rate. Then find the present value of \$5,000 received at the end of each of the next four years at a 6% discount rate. Round to cents.
step: Lump sum: $PV = 10{,}000/1.08^3$. Since $1.08^3 = 1.259712$, $PV = 7{,}938.32$.
step: Annuity: $C = 5{,}000$, $r = 0.06$, $n = 4$. Then $(1.06)^{-4} = 0.792094$, so $1 - 0.792094 = 0.207906$.
step: The annuity factor is $0.207906/0.06 = 3.465106$.
step: $PV = 5{,}000 \times 3.465106 = 17{,}325.53$. The four payments total \$20,000 but are worth \$17,325.53 today.
@mistakes
- Using an annual rate with monthly payments, or discounting by the wrong number of periods.
- Adding up future payments without discounting.
- Treating the first payment of an ordinary annuity as occurring today.
- Forgetting that a higher discount rate lowers present value.
