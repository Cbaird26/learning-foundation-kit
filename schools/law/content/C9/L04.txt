@article
The **balance sheet** (statement of financial position) lists a company's assets, liabilities and equity at a single date. It is the accounting equation turned into a report, which is why total assets must equal total liabilities plus equity.

### Classified balance sheet

**Current assets** are cash and assets expected to be converted to cash or used up within one year (or the operating cycle, if longer). They are listed in order of liquidity: cash, short-term investments, accounts receivable (net of an allowance for doubtful accounts), inventory, prepaid expenses. **Noncurrent assets** include property, plant and equipment (PP&E), shown at cost less **accumulated depreciation**, plus intangible assets such as patents and goodwill.

**Current liabilities** are due within one year: accounts payable, wages payable, unearned revenue, taxes payable and the *current portion* of long-term debt. **Long-term liabilities** are due later: a ten-year bank loan, bonds payable, long-term leases. A loan that matures in 18 months is long-term today; after six months it moves to current, and a covenant breach can force a long-term loan into current.

**Equity** has two main pieces for a corporation: **contributed capital** (common stock and additional paid-in capital) and **retained earnings**, the cumulative profit kept in the business:

$$\text{Ending retained earnings} = \text{Beginning retained earnings} + \text{Net income} - \text{Dividends}$$

Two simple measures come straight from the classified balance sheet. **Working capital** $= $ current assets $-$ current liabilities. **Net PP&E** $= $ cost $-$ accumulated depreciation.

### From trial balance to statements

An **adjusted trial balance** lists every account after year-end adjustments. Revenue and expense accounts go on the income statement; the result, net income, flows into retained earnings. Asset, liability and equity accounts go on the balance sheet. The sequence is always:

1. Sort revenue and expense accounts and prepare the **income statement**; compute net income.
2. Compute ending retained earnings from the beginning balance, net income and dividends.
3. List assets, liabilities and equity; confirm the sheet balances.

### What the balance sheet leaves out

Under U.S. GAAP most assets are carried at historical cost less depreciation, so land bought decades ago may be worth far more than its book value, and self-created goodwill and brands generally do not appear at all. Liabilities for lawsuits appear only if a loss is probable and can be estimated (Week 8). Off-balance-sheet commitments, such as guarantees and some leases, are described in the notes to the financial statements, so lawyers read the notes with the statements.

Contracts frequently use balance-sheet terms: a net-worth covenant, a "working capital adjustment" in an acquisition agreement, a debt-to-equity limit in a loan. In every case the document should define whether numbers follow GAAP applied consistently with past practice, or an agreed accounting method, because the choice moves the figures.

@example
title: Building a classified balance sheet
problem: At year end a company has Cash \$30,000; Accounts Receivable \$20,000; Inventory \$40,000; Equipment at cost \$100,000; Accumulated Depreciation \$30,000; Accounts Payable \$25,000; current portion of long-term debt \$10,000; Long-term Debt \$50,000; Common Stock \$40,000. Find total assets, working capital and retained earnings.
step: Current assets $= 30{,}000 + 20{,}000 + 40{,}000 = 90{,}000$. Net equipment $= 100{,}000 - 30{,}000 = 70{,}000$. Total assets $= 160{,}000$.
step: Current liabilities $= 25{,}000 + 10{,}000 = 35{,}000$. Total liabilities $= 35{,}000 + 50{,}000 = 85{,}000$.
step: Working capital $= 90{,}000 - 35{,}000 = 55{,}000$.
step: Equity is the balancing figure: $160{,}000 - 85{,}000 = 75{,}000$. Retained earnings $= 75{,}000 - 40{,}000 = 35{,}000$.
@mistakes
- Listing accumulated depreciation as a liability instead of subtracting it from the equipment's cost.
- Forgetting that the current portion of long-term debt is a current liability.
- Adding net income to retained earnings but not subtracting dividends.
- Placing revenue and expense accounts on the balance sheet.

@practice self
prompt: Prepare an income statement and a classified balance sheet from this adjusted trial balance at December 31. Debit balances: Cash \$24,000; Accounts Receivable \$16,000; Inventory \$30,000; Equipment \$80,000; Cost of Goods Sold \$90,000; Salaries Expense \$40,000; Rent Expense \$12,000; Dividends \$5,000. Credit balances: Accumulated Depreciation \$20,000; Accounts Payable \$18,000; Long-term Notes Payable \$40,000; Common Stock \$50,000; Retained Earnings (beginning) \$9,000; Sales Revenue \$160,000. Ignore income tax.
hint: Build the income statement first; its net income is needed for retained earnings.
hint: Sort accounts: revenues and expenses to the income statement, everything else to the balance sheet.
solution: Income statement: Sales \$160,000 less COGS \$90,000 gives gross profit \$70,000; less salaries \$40,000 and rent \$12,000 gives net income \$18,000. Retained earnings end $= 9{,}000 + 18{,}000 - 5{,}000 = 22{,}000$. Balance sheet: current assets Cash \$24,000 + Receivables \$16,000 + Inventory \$30,000 = \$70,000; Equipment \$80,000 less accumulated depreciation \$20,000 = \$60,000; total assets \$130,000. Liabilities: Accounts Payable \$18,000 (current) and Notes Payable \$40,000 (long-term), total \$58,000. Equity: Common Stock \$50,000 plus Retained Earnings \$22,000 = \$72,000. Check: $58{,}000 + 72{,}000 = 130{,}000$.
rubric: Net income of \$18,000 is computed on the income statement before the balance sheet is built.
rubric: Dividends reduce retained earnings and do not appear as an expense.
rubric: Accumulated depreciation is subtracted from equipment; total assets are \$130,000.
rubric: The balance sheet balances (liabilities \$58,000 plus equity \$72,000).
