@article
The **income statement** (also called the statement of operations or profit-and-loss statement) reports a company's performance over a **period of time**, such as a quarter or a year. Where the balance sheet is a photograph at one date, the income statement is a film of a period. Its core idea is one line of subtraction:

$$\text{Net income} = \text{Revenues} - \text{Expenses}$$

### Revenues, expenses, gains and losses

**Revenues** are inflows from the company's main activity: sales of goods, fees for services, rent for a landlord. **Expenses** are the costs of earning them: cost of goods sold, wages, rent, utilities, depreciation, interest, income tax. **Gains and losses** come from incidental events, such as selling a building for more (gain) or less (loss) than its book value. Dividends paid to shareholders are *not* expenses; they are a distribution of profit and appear in the statement of changes in equity.

### The multi-step format

A merchandising or manufacturing company usually presents the statement in steps, so a reader can see where profit comes from:

| Line | How computed |
|---|---|
| Net sales | Sales less returns and allowances and discounts |
| Cost of goods sold (COGS) | Cost of the inventory sold |
| **Gross profit** | Net sales − COGS |
| Operating expenses | Selling, general and administrative (SG&A), depreciation |
| **Operating income** | Gross profit − operating expenses |
| Other income / expense | Interest expense, interest income, gains and losses |
| **Income before taxes** | Operating income ± other items |
| Income tax expense | Taxable-income-based charge |
| **Net income** | Income before taxes − income tax expense |

Interest expense is a financing cost, so it falls *below* operating income. This lets an analyst compare how efficiently two businesses operate regardless of how each is financed.

### Margins and per-share figures

A **margin** divides a profit line by net sales. Gross margin is gross profit over sales; **net margin** is net income over sales. **Earnings per share (EPS)** for a corporation with only common stock is net income divided by the weighted-average common shares outstanding. If preferred stock pays a dividend, subtract preferred dividends from net income first.

### What the income statement does not tell you

Net income is an accrual figure (Week 6): a company can be profitable and run out of cash, or lose money and have cash. It depends on estimates such as depreciation and bad debts, and on the timing of revenue recognition. Because public companies' reported earnings drive stock prices and executive pay, income-statement manipulation (recording revenue too early, hiding expenses) is the classic accounting fraud and the subject of many securities cases under Section 10(b) of the Securities Exchange Act and SEC Rule 10b-5.

### Reading it as a lawyer

Contracts often key off these lines: a purchase-price adjustment uses EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization), a royalty is a percentage of net sales, and a lender's covenant may require operating income to exceed interest by a stated multiple. The definition in the contract controls, so always read how "net sales" or "net profit" is defined: it may differ from the GAAP line. Any tax rate in a problem is an assumption for practice, not a statement of current law.

@example
title: From net sales to net income
problem: A retailer has net sales of \$500,000, cost of goods sold of \$300,000, operating expenses of \$120,000 and interest expense of \$10,000. Assume a flat 25% income tax rate. Find gross profit, operating income, income before taxes, net income and net margin.
step: Gross profit $= 500{,}000 - 300{,}000 = 200{,}000$.
step: Operating income $= 200{,}000 - 120{,}000 = 80{,}000$. Interest comes after this line.
step: Income before taxes $= 80{,}000 - 10{,}000 = 70{,}000$. Tax at 25% is $0.25 \times 70{,}000 = 17{,}500$.
step: Net income $= 70{,}000 - 17{,}500 = 52{,}500$. Net margin $= 52{,}500 / 500{,}000 = 0.105$, or $10.5$ percent.
@mistakes
- Subtracting interest expense before operating income, which mixes operating performance with financing choices.
- Treating dividends as an expense; they are not deducted to reach net income.
- Applying the tax rate to operating income or to sales instead of to income before taxes.
- Reading profit as cash received; the statement is on the accrual basis.
