@article
A single number from a financial statement says little. **Ratio analysis** divides one figure by another to make companies of different sizes and years comparable. Ratios fall into four families. Remember that each is a clue, not a verdict, and that a ratio is only as reliable as the statements behind it.

### Liquidity: can it pay bills soon?

$$\text{Current ratio} = \frac{\text{Current assets}}{\text{Current liabilities}}$$

$$\text{Quick ratio} = \frac{\text{Cash} + \text{Marketable securities} + \text{Receivables}}{\text{Current liabilities}}$$

The quick (acid-test) ratio leaves out inventory and prepaids, which are harder to turn into cash. A current ratio of 2.5 with a quick ratio of 0.6 suggests the company's liquidity depends heavily on selling inventory. Liquidity covenants in loan agreements often set a minimum current ratio.

### Leverage: how much debt?

$$\text{Debt-to-equity} = \frac{\text{Total liabilities}}{\text{Total equity}} \qquad \text{Debt ratio} = \frac{\text{Total liabilities}}{\text{Total assets}}$$

$$\text{Interest coverage (times interest earned)} = \frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Interest expense}}$$

EBIT is earnings before interest and taxes (roughly, operating income). High leverage magnifies returns in good years and raises the risk of default in bad ones. Lenders' covenants typically cap leverage and require minimum coverage; a breach can be an event of default that lets the lender accelerate the debt. Definitions vary (some analysts use only interest-bearing debt), so check the agreement's definitions.

### Profitability

$$\text{Gross margin} = \frac{\text{Gross profit}}{\text{Net sales}} \qquad \text{Net margin} = \frac{\text{Net income}}{\text{Net sales}}$$

$$\text{Return on equity (ROE)} = \frac{\text{Net income}}{\text{Average equity}} \qquad \text{Return on assets (ROA)} = \frac{\text{Net income}}{\text{Average total assets}}$$

Use **average** balances (beginning plus ending, divided by two) when comparing a flow from a period (net income) with a stock at a date (equity).

### Efficiency

$$\text{Inventory turnover} = \frac{\text{COGS}}{\text{Average inventory}} \qquad \text{Days sales outstanding} = \frac{\text{Average receivables}}{\text{Net sales}} \times 365$$

A falling turnover or rising days sales outstanding may signal slow-moving stock or customers who will not pay, which is a warning about the quality of earnings and sometimes about premature revenue recognition.

### Using ratios well

- **Trend.** Compare the company with itself over several years.
- **Peers.** Compare with a similar business; norms differ greatly by industry.
- **Quality.** Check that the numbers are comparable (same accounting methods, no one-time items).
- **Definitions.** In a contract, the stated formula controls, not the textbook one.

A lawyer reading a loan agreement or an acquisition model should be able to recompute each covenant from the statements, and to see which accounting choices (inventory method, depreciation, revenue timing) could move it. Ratios inform judgment but do not replace it, and they say nothing about legal exposure that is not on the books.

@example
title: A set of ratios
problem: A company has cash \$40,000, receivables \$50,000, inventory \$80,000 and prepaid expenses \$10,000; current liabilities \$100,000; total liabilities \$250,000; total equity \$250,000; EBIT \$90,000; interest expense \$15,000. Find the current ratio, quick ratio, debt-to-equity and interest coverage.
step: Current assets $= 40{,}000 + 50{,}000 + 80{,}000 + 10{,}000 = 180{,}000$. Current ratio $= 180{,}000/100{,}000 = 1.8$.
step: Quick assets $= 40{,}000 + 50{,}000 = 90{,}000$ (no marketable securities). Quick ratio $= 90{,}000/100{,}000 = 0.9$.
step: Debt-to-equity $= 250{,}000/250{,}000 = 1.0$.
step: Interest coverage $= 90{,}000/15{,}000 = 6.0$. EBIT covers interest six times.
@mistakes
- Including inventory in the quick ratio.
- Using only ending equity or assets for ROE and ROA when the comparison calls for averages.
- Comparing ratios across industries with different norms.
- Computing a covenant ratio with a textbook formula when the contract defines it differently.
