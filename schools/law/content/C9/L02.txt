@article
Last week you tracked transactions by writing the equation after each one. Real bookkeepers use **double-entry bookkeeping**: every transaction is recorded in at least two accounts, with total **debits** equal to total **credits**. The words only mean *left* and *right*. A debit is an entry on the left side of an account; a credit is an entry on the right.

### The rules

Because the equation is $\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Equity}$, accounts on the left side of the equation increase with a debit, and accounts on the right side increase with a credit.

| Account type | Increases with | Normal balance |
|---|---|---|
| Assets | Debit | Debit |
| Expenses | Debit | Debit |
| Owner withdrawals / dividends | Debit | Debit |
| Liabilities | Credit | Credit |
| Owner's capital / common stock / retained earnings | Credit | Credit |
| Revenues | Credit | Credit |

Expenses and withdrawals reduce equity, which increases with credits, so they increase with debits. Revenues increase equity, so they increase with credits. The opposite entry decreases each type. A "debit" is not good or bad; a debit to cash is good news and a debit to an expense is not.

### Journal entries and the ledger

A **journal entry** has a date, the account(s) debited, the account(s) credited, the amounts and a short explanation. The debited account is written first. A **compound entry** touches three or more accounts but still balances. For example, buying a \$12,000 machine with \$4,000 cash and an \$8,000 note:

- Debit Equipment 12,000
- Credit Cash 4,000
- Credit Notes Payable 8,000

Journal entries are then **posted** to the **general ledger**, which keeps one account (often drawn as a T) for each item. An account's balance is the sum of its increases minus its decreases, shown on its normal side.

### The trial balance

At the end of a period, list every ledger account with its debit or credit balance. Total debits must equal total credits. This **trial balance** catches arithmetic errors such as an entry with only one side posted. It does **not** prove accuracy: it will balance if you debit the wrong account, omit a whole transaction, or record the same wrong amount on both sides. That limit is why audits and internal controls (Week 12) exist.

### Why lawyers care

Journal entries are the original records behind financial statements. In litigation over breach of contract, partnership disputes or fraud, the question is often what the books showed, when an entry was made and who made it. The business-records hearsay exception (Federal Rule of Evidence 803(6)) is built on regularly kept records of this kind. Knowing that every entry has two sides lets you trace a payment: if cash was credited, find where the matching debit went.

@example
title: Three transactions and a trial balance
problem: A studio (1) is funded by an owner who invests \$20,000 cash, (2) buys \$3,000 of supplies on account, (3) bills a client \$7,000 for a completed project, not yet paid. Write the entries and check that debits equal credits.
step: Entry 1: Debit Cash 20,000; Credit Owner's Capital 20,000. An asset and equity both rise.
step: Entry 2: Debit Supplies 3,000; Credit Accounts Payable 3,000. An asset and a liability both rise.
step: Entry 3: Debit Accounts Receivable 7,000; Credit Service Revenue 7,000. The work is done, so revenue is earned even though no cash has arrived.
step: Totals: debits $20{,}000 + 3{,}000 + 7{,}000 = 30{,}000$ and credits $20{,}000 + 3{,}000 + 7{,}000 = 30{,}000$. The trial balance balances, and assets of $30{,}000$ equal liabilities of $3{,}000$ plus equity of $27{,}000$.
@mistakes
- Thinking debit always means increase; it increases assets and expenses but decreases liabilities, equity and revenue.
- Crediting Cash for a purchase on account; no cash moved, so credit Accounts Payable.
- Believing a balanced trial balance proves the books are right.
- Recording only the debit or the credit of an entry, so the ledger goes out of balance.
