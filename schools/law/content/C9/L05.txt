@article
A company can report a profit and still be unable to pay its bills. The **statement of cash flows** explains why, by reporting the actual cash that came in and went out during a period and where it came from. U.S. GAAP requires it alongside the balance sheet, income statement and statement of changes in equity. It reconciles beginning cash to ending cash:

$$\text{Ending cash} = \text{Beginning cash} + \text{CFO} + \text{CFI} + \text{CFF}$$

### Three sections

**Operating activities (CFO)** are the cash effects of the day-to-day business: cash from customers, cash paid to suppliers and employees, interest paid and received, and income taxes paid. **Investing activities (CFI)** are buying and selling long-term assets and investments: purchases of equipment (often called capital expenditures, or capex), proceeds from selling equipment, loans made to others. **Financing activities (CFF)** are raising and repaying capital: issuing stock or bonds, borrowing and repaying principal, paying dividends and buying back shares. Under U.S. GAAP, interest paid is operating, while dividends paid are financing (some other accounting systems classify them differently).

### The indirect method

The **direct method** lists cash collected and paid. Most companies use the **indirect method**, which starts with net income and adjusts it to cash:

1. **Add back noncash expenses**, chiefly depreciation and amortization. They reduced net income but no cash left.
2. **Remove gains and losses on asset sales.** Subtract a gain and add back a loss; the sale's whole cash proceeds belong in investing.
3. **Adjust for changes in operating working capital.** An *increase* in a current operating asset (receivables, inventory, prepaids) uses cash, so subtract it. A *decrease* releases cash, so add it. An *increase* in a current operating liability (payables, accrued liabilities) conserves cash, so add it. A *decrease* uses cash, so subtract it.

An increase in accounts receivable means sales were recorded that customers have not yet paid, so cash collected is lower than revenue. An increase in accounts payable means expenses were recorded but not yet paid, so cash paid is lower than expense.

### Free cash flow

Analysts often compute a rough measure of cash left after maintaining the asset base: **free cash flow** $=$ CFO $-$ capital expenditures. Definitions vary; a contract or valuation memo must say which one it uses.

### Why lawyers care

Cash-flow statements show liquidity, the ability to pay debts as they fall due. A debtor's "equity insolvency" (unable to pay debts as they become due) is a cash concept; the balance-sheet test is different. A company with positive earnings but negative operating cash flow for several years, for example because receivables keep ballooning, may be recording revenue it will never collect, a pattern that has appeared in securities-fraud cases. Lenders include covenants tied to cash flow, and buyers value businesses largely on the cash they produce (Week 10).

@example
title: Operating cash flow by the indirect method
problem: A company reports net income of \$52,000, depreciation expense of \$12,000, an increase in accounts receivable of \$8,000, a decrease in inventory of \$3,000 and an increase in accounts payable of \$5,000. It buys equipment for \$30,000, sells old equipment for \$4,000 (no gain or loss), borrows \$20,000 and pays dividends of \$10,000. Find CFO, CFI, CFF and the net change in cash.
step: Start with net income \$52,000 and add back depreciation: $52{,}000 + 12{,}000 = 64{,}000$.
step: Working capital: receivables up (subtract $8{,}000$), inventory down (add $3{,}000$), payables up (add $5{,}000$). $\text{CFO} = 64{,}000 - 8{,}000 + 3{,}000 + 5{,}000 = 64{,}000$.
step: $\text{CFI} = -30{,}000 + 4{,}000 = -26{,}000$. $\text{CFF} = +20{,}000 - 10{,}000 = +10{,}000$.
step: Net change in cash $= 64{,}000 - 26{,}000 + 10{,}000 = 48{,}000$.
@mistakes
- Subtracting depreciation again; it is added back because it was already deducted in net income without a cash payment.
- Reversing the sign of a working-capital change: an increase in receivables lowers cash, an increase in payables raises it.
- Putting the whole sale price of equipment in operating activities and also removing the gain.
- Classifying dividends paid as operating cash flow under U.S. GAAP.
