@article
Financial statements are prepared by management, which has an interest in how they look. **Audits** and **internal controls** exist to give outsiders confidence in the numbers.

### The audit

An independent **external audit** by a certified public accountant examines the statements and supporting evidence, such as confirmations from banks and customers, counts of inventory and testing of transactions. The auditor issues an opinion on whether the statements are presented fairly, in all material respects, in accordance with GAAP:

- **Unmodified (clean)** opinion: fairly presented.
- **Qualified** opinion: fair except for a specific, isolated problem.
- **Adverse** opinion: materially and pervasively misstated.
- **Disclaimer of opinion:** the auditor could not obtain enough evidence.

An audit provides **reasonable assurance**, not a guarantee, and tests samples rather than everything. Management, not the auditor, is responsible for the statements. For public companies the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 created the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board (PCAOB), restricts non-audit services that impair auditor independence, requires audit committees of independent directors, and requires the CEO and CFO to certify the financial statements (§ 302) and report on the effectiveness of internal control over financial reporting (§ 404). A false certification can bring civil and criminal liability.

### Internal controls

**Internal control** is the set of policies that protects assets, ensures accurate records and promotes compliance. The COSO framework is the standard model. Key activities:

- **Segregation of duties:** no one person should authorize a transaction, record it and hold the asset.
- **Authorization and approval** limits for payments and contracts.
- **Physical controls** such as safes and restricted inventory areas, and **access controls** over accounting software.
- **Independent verification:** reconciliations, counts and review by someone other than the preparer.
- **Documentation:** pre-numbered documents, support for every entry.

A **bank reconciliation** is the classic independent check: it explains the difference between the cash balance in the company's books and the bank statement. Bank side: add deposits in transit, subtract outstanding checks. Book side: subtract bank charges and NSF (insufficient funds) customer checks, add collections the bank made, such as notes. Both sides then equal the **adjusted (true) cash balance**.

### Fraud

Fraud falls into two broad types: misappropriation of assets (theft, fake vendors, skimming) and fraudulent financial reporting (inflating revenue, hiding liabilities). The **fraud triangle** says fraud tends to occur where three conditions meet: **pressure** (financial need, targets), **opportunity** (weak controls), and **rationalization** ("I'll pay it back").

Securities fraud claims arise under Section 10(b) of the Exchange Act and Rule 10b-5, which require, among other things, a material misrepresentation or omission and scienter (intent or severe recklessness). In *SEC v. Jarkesy* (2024), the Supreme Court held that when the SEC seeks civil penalties for securities fraud, the Seventh Amendment entitles the defendant to a jury trial in federal court. Auditors face liability too: under the *Ultramares* line of New York cases, an auditor's liability for negligence to third parties is limited to those in privity or a relationship close to it, while other states take broader views; many courts allow claims for fraud by anyone who justifiably relied.

### Reading statements as a lawyer

Before a deal or loan, lawyers combine the audit report, the notes, the ratios of Week 11 and an eye for control weaknesses. A going-concern paragraph, a late filing, a change of auditors, restated prior years or a qualified opinion each call for follow-up questions in diligence.

@example
title: Bank reconciliation
problem: A company's bank statement shows \$13,150. A deposit of \$650 has not yet reached the bank, and checks totaling \$1,200 are outstanding. The company's own books show \$12,650, but the bank charged a \$50 service fee that is not yet recorded. Find the adjusted cash balance from each side.
step: Bank side: $13{,}150 + 650 - 1{,}200 = 12{,}600$.
step: Book side: $12{,}650 - 50 = 12{,}600$.
step: The two sides agree at $12{,}600$, so the true cash balance is \$12,600. The books need an adjusting entry: debit bank fees expense \$50, credit cash \$50.
step: No entry is needed for the deposit in transit or outstanding checks; the company has already recorded them, and the bank will catch up.
@mistakes
- Adjusting the books for deposits in transit and outstanding checks; those are timing differences on the bank side.
- Adding an NSF check to the book balance instead of subtracting it.
- Believing a clean audit opinion guarantees there is no fraud.
- Letting the same employee approve invoices, issue payments and reconcile the bank statement.

@practice self
prompt: A company's financial statements show: cash \$30,000, accounts receivable \$60,000, inventory \$60,000 (these are all of its current assets); current liabilities \$100,000; total liabilities \$300,000; total equity \$200,000; EBIT \$60,000; interest expense \$12,000. Compute the current ratio, quick ratio, debt-to-equity ratio and interest coverage, and write a short paragraph assessing the company's liquidity and leverage for a prospective lender.
hint: Quick assets are cash plus receivables; leave out inventory.
hint: Interest coverage is EBIT divided by interest expense.
solution: Current assets $= 150{,}000$, so the current ratio is $150{,}000/100{,}000 = 1.5$. Quick ratio $= (30{,}000 + 60{,}000)/100{,}000 = 0.9$. Debt-to-equity $= 300{,}000/200{,}000 = 1.5$. Interest coverage $= 60{,}000/12{,}000 = 5.0$. Assessment: the company can cover current liabilities with current assets, but only if it sells inventory, because quick assets fall slightly short of current liabilities (0.9). Leverage is moderate-to-high, with \$1.50 of liabilities for each dollar of equity, but operating profit covers interest five times, so debt service looks manageable if earnings hold. A lender might ask for trend data, covenant protection on the quick ratio and leverage, and a review of the inventory's quality and the receivables' collectability.
rubric: Current ratio 1.5, quick ratio 0.9, debt-to-equity 1.5 and coverage 5.0 are computed correctly.
rubric: The paragraph interprets liquidity and leverage separately and notes the role of inventory.
rubric: The paragraph proposes at least one follow-up (trend, peers, covenants or inventory quality).
