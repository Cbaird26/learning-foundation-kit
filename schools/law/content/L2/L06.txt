@article
Causation has two parts, and both must be proved. **Actual cause** (cause in fact) asks whether the defendant's conduct was a necessary part of how the harm occurred. **Proximate cause** (legal cause) asks whether the defendant should be held responsible for the harm that happened, a question of policy and foreseeability.

### Actual cause

The basic test is **but-for**: but for the defendant's negligence, would the harm have occurred? If the harm would have occurred anyway, the defendant's conduct is not an actual cause. The plaintiff must prove it is more likely than not (over 50%) that the defendant's negligence was a but-for cause.

**Multiple sufficient causes.** If two independent negligent acts, each enough to cause the harm, combine to cause it (two fires that merge and burn a house), the but-for test fails for each. Courts use the **substantial factor test**: each defendant is liable if the conduct was a substantial factor in bringing about the harm (*Anderson v. Minneapolis, St. Paul & Sault Ste. Marie Ry.* (1920)).

**Uncertain causation among defendants.** Where two defendants were negligent but only one could have caused the harm, and the plaintiff cannot show which, *Summers v. Tice* (1948) shifted the burden of proof to the defendants. Two hunters negligently fired in the plaintiff's direction, and one pellet hit his eye; both were held liable, and the burden was on each to show his shot was not the cause. This is **alternative liability**, and it is usually limited to cases in which all potential defendants are before the court. In *Sindell v. Abbott Laboratories* (1980), the California Supreme Court adopted **market-share liability** for a generic drug (DES) whose manufacturers could not be identified: each manufacturer is liable for its share of the market, unless it proves it did not make the product that harmed the plaintiff. Few other states follow it.

**Loss of a chance.** If a negligent doctor reduces a patient's chance of survival from 40% to 15%, the traditional rule denies recovery because the patient was more likely than not to die anyway. A growing number of states allow recovery of the lost chance, often proportionally (the percentage lost times the full value of the harm). Others reject the doctrine.

### Proximate cause

Even if the defendant was an actual cause, liability is limited to harms within the scope of the risk that made the conduct negligent. The leading approaches are:

- **Foreseeability.** The defendant is liable for harm of a foreseeable type that results from the negligent conduct. *Palsgraf v. Long Island R.R.* (1928) used foreseeability to limit duty to foreseeable plaintiffs; *The Wagon Mound (No. 1)* (1961) rejected the older **direct consequences** test of *In re Polemis* and held that the harm must be of a foreseeable type. The **extent** of the harm need not be foreseeable, and the defendant takes the victim as found (the eggshell plaintiff rule).
- **Scope of the risk.** The Restatement (Third) § 29 states that an actor is liable for harms that result from the risks that made the conduct tortious.
- **Intervening and superseding causes.** An intervening act that is foreseeable does not cut off liability. Common examples are negligent medical treatment of an injury, rescue attempts (the **rescue doctrine** holds a defendant liable to rescuers injured in a foreseeable rescue) and foreseeable reactions to the peril. An unforeseeable, extraordinary intervening cause, particularly an intentional crime or an act of extraordinary negligence, can be **superseding** and relieve the original defendant, unless the crime was exactly the risk that made the conduct negligent (leaving a car unlocked with keys inside is such a case in some states).

### Applying the rules

Take each defendant separately. First test actual cause (but-for, then substantial factor if sufficient causes overlap, then alternative or market-share liability if identity is uncertain). Then test proximate cause: was the type of harm within the foreseeable risk, and did any intervening act break the chain?

@example
title: Two negligent drivers and one injury
problem: Drivers Abe and Bo each negligently speed through an intersection at the same moment and, by coincidence, both hit and kill a pedestrian, Cy, at the same time. Either impact alone would have killed Cy. Is either an actual cause?
step: Apply but-for to Abe: even without Abe's negligence, Bo's impact would have killed Cy, so Abe fails the but-for test. The same is true for Bo.
step: Because two sufficient causes combined, the but-for test would let both defendants escape, which is an unjust result.
step: Apply the substantial factor test: each driver's negligent impact was a substantial factor in bringing about the death.
step: Conclusion: both Abe and Bo are actual causes, and proximate cause is also satisfied, because a fatal collision is the foreseeable risk of speeding through an intersection.

@mistakes
- Using only but-for when two independent causes were each sufficient.
- Requiring that the extent of the harm be foreseeable, rather than the type. The eggshell plaintiff rule applies.
- Treating every intervening act as superseding. Foreseeable intervening acts, such as negligent medical care and rescue, do not cut off liability.
- Confusing actual cause (a factual question) with proximate cause (a legal and policy question).
