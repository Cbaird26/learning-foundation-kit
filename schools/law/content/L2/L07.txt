@article
Negligence requires **damages**: actual harm to a legally protected interest. Unlike intentional torts, nominal damages are not available for negligence without harm. This lesson covers the measure of damages and the situations in which the law departs from the general duty of reasonable care.

### Compensatory and other damages

**Compensatory damages** put the plaintiff in the position the plaintiff would have been in without the tort. They include **economic** losses (past and future medical expenses, lost earnings and earning capacity, property damage) and **noneconomic** losses (pain and suffering, loss of enjoyment of life, emotional distress). Future losses are reduced to **present value**. Under the **eggshell plaintiff** rule, the defendant is liable for the full extent of the harm to a plaintiff who is unusually susceptible. Under the **collateral source rule**, the defendant may not reduce damages by benefits the plaintiff received from independent sources, such as health insurance, although many states have modified the rule by statute. The plaintiff has a duty to **mitigate** damages by taking reasonable steps. **Punitive damages** punish and deter conduct that is malicious, fraudulent, or in reckless disregard of the safety of others, and are limited by due process (*State Farm v. Campbell* (2003), single-digit ratios). Many states have statutory caps on noneconomic or punitive damages, especially in medical malpractice. A **wrongful death** statute lets designated survivors recover for their losses, and a **survival** statute preserves the decedent's own claim for the estate.

### No duty to rescue

At common law, a person has **no duty to act affirmatively** to aid a stranger in peril, even at no risk to himself. Exceptions arise when there is a **special relationship** (common carrier and passenger, innkeeper and guest, shopkeeper and customer, employer and employee in some contexts, custodial relationships, parent and child), when the defendant **created the peril** (even innocently), or when the defendant has **voluntarily undertaken** to help, in which case the duty is to act with reasonable care and not leave the plaintiff worse off. **Good Samaritan statutes** in many states protect volunteers from liability for ordinary negligence in emergency aid. Under *Tarasoff v. Regents of the University of California* (1976), a mental health professional who determines, or under the standard of the profession should determine, that a patient poses a serious danger of violence to an identifiable victim has a duty to use reasonable care to protect the victim; the duty may be met by warning the victim or taking other steps. Many states have adopted versions of the rule, by case law or statute, while others have limited it.

### Landowners

The traditional common-law categories are **trespassers**, **licensees** and **invitees**. The owner owes trespassers only a duty to refrain from willful or wanton harm (with a duty to warn of known artificial dangers for known or anticipated trespassers in many states and special rules for **child trespassers**, below), licensees a duty to warn of known dangers that the licensee is unlikely to discover, and invitees a duty to inspect and use reasonable care. The **attractive nuisance** doctrine (Restatement (Second) § 339) protects children who are drawn to a dangerous artificial condition that the owner knows or should know is likely to attract them, when the risk outweighs the burden of eliminating it. A minority of states, led by *Rowland v. Christian* (1968), has abolished some or all of the categories in favor of a general duty of reasonable care; the Restatement (Third) § 51 retains a limited rule for flagrant trespassers. Duties in lease and retail settings may differ.

### Emotional distress

For **negligent infliction of emotional distress (NIED)**, states use one of three tests. Under the **impact rule**, the plaintiff needs physical contact (a minority). Under the **zone of danger** test, which many states use for direct victims and which the Restatement (Third) adopts, the plaintiff must have been placed in immediate risk of physical harm and suffered distress from that fear. Under the **bystander** rule (*Dillon v. Legg* (1968), narrowed in *Thing v. La Chusa* (1989)), a plaintiff who was not in danger may recover for distress from witnessing an injury to a close relative if the plaintiff is closely related to the victim, was present at the scene and aware that the injury was occurring, and suffers serious emotional distress. Some states also allow NIED where a special relationship (such as a hospital's mishandling of a body or a diagnosis) makes distress foreseeable.

### Pure economic loss

Under the **economic loss rule**, a plaintiff cannot recover in negligence for purely economic harm (lost profits, for example) without physical injury or property damage to the plaintiff, even if the loss was foreseeable. The rule protects defendants from indeterminate liability and channels commercial disputes into contract. Exceptions include professional negligence to known and intended beneficiaries, and some negligent misrepresentation claims.

### Applying the rules

For each claim, identify the duty and any special rule, then compute damages: sort losses into economic and noneconomic, check mitigation and collateral sources, and discount future losses.

@example
title: The bystander and the collateral source
problem: Lena sees a driver negligently run down her daughter, Mia, on a sidewalk twenty feet away. Lena was not in the path of the car. Mia's \$30,000 hospital bill was paid by Mia's health insurer. In a state following the Thing v. La Chusa bystander test and the common-law collateral source rule, can Lena recover for NIED, and what can Mia claim for medical expenses?
step: Lena was not in the zone of danger, so she cannot use the zone-of-danger test, but she may qualify as a bystander.
step: The bystander test is satisfied if she is closely related to the victim (a mother), was present at the scene and aware that the injury was occurring (she watched it), and suffered serious distress. Each element is met, so Lena has a claim.
step: Under the collateral source rule, the defendant may not reduce Mia's damages by what her insurer paid.
step: Conclusion: Lena can recover for NIED; Mia can claim the full \$30,000 in medical expenses (subject to any insurer subrogation).

@mistakes
- Imposing a duty to rescue a stranger. The general rule is no duty absent a special relationship, created peril or voluntary undertaking.
- Awarding emotional distress to a bystander who arrives after the accident or who is not closely related to the victim.
- Allowing recovery of purely economic loss in negligence when the plaintiff suffered no physical harm or property damage.
- Reducing damages for insurance payments the plaintiff received, despite the collateral source rule.
