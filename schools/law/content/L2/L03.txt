@article
A **privilege** or **defense** excuses what would otherwise be an intentional tort. The defendant has the burden of raising and proving it. Courts ask, for each defense, whether the privilege applies at all and whether the defendant stayed within its limits.

### Consent

**Consent** is a willingness that conduct occur, and it negates the contact or invasion as a wrong. It may be **express** (words or writing) or **implied** from conduct or custom (a person who holds out an arm for a vaccination in a line consents to the injection). Consent is measured objectively, by how a reasonable person would understand the plaintiff's outward behavior. Several limits apply.

- **Scope.** Consent covers only what a reasonable person would understand it to cover. A surgeon who operates on a different part of the body than the one the patient agreed to, absent an emergency, commits a battery (*Mohr v. Williams* (1905)). Sports participants consent to contact within the rules and customs of the game, not to flagrantly illegal acts.
- **Capacity.** Consent by a person who lacks capacity because of age, intoxication or mental condition may be ineffective.
- **Fraud and duress.** Consent induced by fraud about the essential character of the act, or by threat of force, is invalid. Fraud about a collateral matter does not invalidate it.
- **Illegality.** Courts have divided on consent to a criminal act such as a mutual fight. The Restatement (Second) § 892C treats consent as effective unless the criminal statute exists to protect a class of persons including the plaintiff (for example statutory rape).
- **Emergency.** Consent to treatment is implied for an unconscious patient who needs immediate treatment when no one can consent and a reasonable person would.

### Self-defense and defense of others

A person may use **reasonable force** to defend against what the person reasonably believes to be an imminent unlawful contact. The force must be proportionate: **deadly force** is privileged only when the defender reasonably believes it is necessary to prevent death or serious bodily harm. A reasonable mistake is excused, so the test is the reasonable appearance of danger, not actual danger. The privilege ends when the threat ends, and retaliation is not self-defense. Tort law varies on the **duty to retreat** before using deadly force. The Restatement (Second) § 65 requires retreat before deadly force if it can be done with complete safety (except from one's own home), but a majority of states impose no duty to retreat, and "stand your ground" statutes in many states extend this to any place the defendant is lawfully present. No duty to retreat applies to non-deadly force in any event. A person may use reasonable force to defend others, and most courts allow it if the defender reasonably believes the third person would be privileged to defend herself. The older rule that held the defender to the third party's actual rights is a minority view today.

### Defense of property and recapture

A person may use **reasonable force**, short of deadly force, to prevent the unlawful invasion of land or chattels. Deadly force is not allowed to protect property alone. Usually the owner must first ask the intruder to stop unless asking would be futile or dangerous. Mechanical devices that inflict deadly force, such as **spring guns**, are not privileged if the owner could not have used deadly force in person (*Katko v. Briney* (1971)). Recapture of chattels is privileged only in fresh pursuit of a wrongful taker, and with reasonable force.

### Necessity

**Public necessity** is a complete privilege: a person who damages or destroys property to avert an imminent public disaster, such as dynamiting a house to stop a spreading fire, is not liable (*Surocco v. Geary* (1853)). **Private necessity** privileges an entry or use to protect the defendant's own or another's person or property from a serious harm, but the defendant must pay for actual damage done. The owner may not forcibly resist the privileged entry. *Vincent v. Lake Erie Transportation Co.* (1910) applies the rule to a ship held at a dock in a storm.

### Policy

Consent protects autonomy, self-defense tolerates reasonable responses to danger, and the necessity rules assign the cost of saving property: the public benefits from public necessity, so the loss falls on the owner (under many statutes the government pays compensation); a private actor who benefits pays.

### Applying the rules

Ask whether the privilege exists, whether the defendant's belief was reasonable, whether the force was proportionate, and whether the defendant stayed within the scope. If the privilege applies only in part, the defendant is liable for the excess.

@example
title: A storm and a cabin
problem: Lost in a blizzard, Hiro breaks a window of a locked cabin owned by Ida to avoid freezing, and burns some of Ida's firewood. The window costs \$250 to replace and the firewood is worth \$40. Is Hiro liable?
step: Hiro entered Ida's land and used her chattels intentionally, which would be trespass to land and conversion or trespass to chattels, but there was a serious threat to his life.
step: This is private necessity: the entry is privileged, so Hiro is not liable for trespass, and Ida cannot lawfully eject him.
step: The privilege does not erase compensation for actual harm. Hiro must pay for the damage he caused.
step: Conclusion: no liability for the entry, but Hiro owes \$290 for actual damage (the window and the firewood).

@mistakes
- Treating private necessity as a complete defense. The defendant still owes actual damages.
- Allowing deadly force to protect property alone, or a spring gun when the owner could not have used deadly force in person.
- Ignoring scope. Consent to one procedure or contact does not cover another, and consent obtained by fraud about the essential nature of the act is invalid.
- Refusing the privilege because the threat turned out to be unreal. A reasonable mistake still supports self-defense.
