@article
These torts protect interests in land, personal property and emotional tranquility. As in Week 1, work element by element.

### Trespass to land

**Trespass to land** is an intentional physical invasion of land in the possession of another (Restatement (Second) of Torts § 158). The defendant must intend only to enter the land, or to cause a person or thing to enter it. The defendant need not intend to trespass or know the land belongs to someone else, so a good-faith **mistake** about boundaries or ownership is no defense (§ 164). No actual harm is required; nominal damages are available, and a trespasser who causes harm is liable for it. A person who is privileged to enter may become a trespasser by remaining after the license ends or by exceeding its scope. The tort protects possession, not title, so a tenant can sue the landlord for entry. Trespass can occur above and below the surface to a reasonable height and depth; flights at reasonable altitudes are not trespass.

Under **private necessity**, a person may enter land to protect life or property from a greater harm, but must pay for damage caused. *Vincent v. Lake Erie Transportation Co.* (1910) held that a ship's owner who kept a vessel tied to a dock during a storm was liable for the dock damage even though staying was reasonable. The privilege defeats a claim of wrongdoing but not the duty to compensate. (Public necessity, discussed in Week 3, is different.)

### Trespass to chattels and conversion

**Trespass to chattels** is an intentional interference with another's right of possession in personal property, resulting in dispossession for a substantial time, impairment of the chattel's condition, quality or value, or harm to the possessor or to someone or something in whom the possessor has a legally protected interest (§ 218). Damages are the actual loss, and there is no nominal-damages claim. A leading case on electronic contact is *Intel Corp. v. Hamidi* (Cal. 2003): unwanted mass email that did not impair the employer's computer system was not a trespass to chattels.

**Conversion** is an intentional exercise of dominion or control over another's chattel that so seriously interferes with the owner's right to control it that the defendant may justly be required to pay its full value (§ 222A). Courts weigh the extent and duration of the control, the defendant's intent to assert a right inconsistent with the owner's, good faith, the harm done and the inconvenience and expense caused. Because conversion is a forced sale, the standard remedy is the **fair market value** at the time and place of the conversion, plus interest, and consequential losses; the owner may instead seek **replevin** (return of the item). Good faith is no defense, so a buyer who purchased from a thief in good faith can be liable in conversion. Borrowing a bicycle for an afternoon is usually trespass to chattels at most; keeping it for a year or selling it is conversion.

### Intentional infliction of emotional distress

**Intentional infliction of emotional distress (IIED)** requires (1) **extreme and outrageous** conduct, (2) done intentionally or recklessly, (3) that causes (4) **severe** emotional distress (§ 46). Conduct is outrageous only if it goes beyond all bounds of decency and is regarded as atrocious and utterly intolerable in a civilized community. Mere insults, indignities, threats and annoyances do not qualify. Context matters: conduct directed at a known vulnerable person, abuse of a position of power, and the conduct of common carriers and innkeepers toward their customers are treated more strictly. Physical injury is not required, but the distress must be severe, and courts look for evidence such as medical treatment or disruption of daily life. A defendant whose outrageous conduct is directed at a third person can also be liable to that person's close family member who is present when it occurs, if severe distress results (§ 46(2)).

### First Amendment limits

In *Hustler Magazine v. Falwell* (1988), the Court held that a public figure cannot recover for IIED based on a parody unless he shows a false statement of fact made with actual malice. In *Snyder v. Phelps* (2011), the Court held that picketing at a funeral, on matters of public concern and in a public place and in compliance with police directions, was protected speech and could not support IIED liability. Speech on private matters receives less protection.

### Applying the rules

Ask what the defendant intended (only entry for trespass; dominion for conversion), what the plaintiff lost, and whether a privilege applies. For IIED, check outrageousness first, because it is where most claims fail.

@example
title: Borrow, keep, sell
problem: Mia lends her \$900 mountain bike to a neighbor, Noel, for the weekend. Noel keeps it for three months, then sells it to a stranger for \$700, and Mia learns the bike is gone. When Noel sold it, the bike's fair market value was \$900. Mia sues Noel. What claim does she have, and what are her basic damages?
step: Noel was entitled to possess the bike for a weekend. He then kept it for three months and sold it, exercising dominion inconsistent with Mia's rights, which is serious, lasting and not in good faith.
step: That interference is serious enough to be conversion and not merely trespass to chattels, because it justifies requiring Noel to pay the full value.
step: The measure is the fair market value at the time of the conversion, \$900, plus interest and any consequential losses. The \$700 price Noel received does not cap her recovery.
step: Conclusion: conversion; basic damages of \$900 plus interest and proven consequential losses.

@mistakes
- Requiring that a trespasser know the land belongs to another. Only intent to enter is required, so mistake is no defense.
- Treating every unauthorized use of a chattel as conversion. Conversion is reserved for serious interferences that justify a forced sale.
- Awarding IIED for insults or rude behavior. The conduct must be extreme and outrageous and the distress severe.
- Ignoring the First Amendment when the speech concerns a public figure or a public issue.
