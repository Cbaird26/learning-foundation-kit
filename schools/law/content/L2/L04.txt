@article
**Negligence** is conduct that falls below the standard of care and causes foreseeable harm. The plaintiff must prove five elements: (1) **duty**, (2) **breach**, (3) **actual cause**, (4) **proximate cause**, and (5) **damages**. This week we cover duty and the standard of care; Weeks 5 to 7 cover the rest.

### Duty

The general rule, from *Palsgraf v. Long Island R.R. Co.* (1928) and the Restatement (Third) of Torts: Liability for Physical and Emotional Harm, is that everyone owes a duty of reasonable care to avoid creating unreasonable risks of physical harm to others. In *Palsgraf*, the majority held that the duty runs to foreseeable plaintiffs, those in the zone of danger created by the conduct, and so a railroad was not liable to a bystander hurt by a package it could not have known was dangerous; the dissent would have extended liability to everyone harmed in a direct chain of events. Many special duty rules (no duty to rescue, landowners, emotional harm and economic loss) are covered in Week 7.

### The reasonable person standard

The standard of care is the conduct of an **ordinarily prudent person under the same or similar circumstances**. It is **objective**: the defendant's own good intentions, clumsiness or slowness do not lower it (*Vaughan v. Menlove* (1837), where a farmer who built a hayrick he honestly thought safe was held to the standard of a person of ordinary prudence). Variations to know:

- **Physical characteristics.** A person with a physical disability is held to the standard of a reasonable person with the same disability (a blind pedestrian must act as a reasonably careful blind person would).
- **Mental disability.** Most courts do not lower the standard for mental illness or low intelligence (Restatement (Third) § 11(c)), because liability aids victims and the defendant is in the best position to control the risk. Sudden unforeseeable incapacity, such as an unexpected heart attack at the wheel, can excuse.
- **Children.** A child is held to the standard of a reasonable child of **like age, intelligence and experience**. Many states hold that a very young child (often under five) cannot be negligent, and some courts hold older children to the **adult standard** when engaged in adult or dangerous activities such as driving a motor vehicle.
- **Professionals** are held to the knowledge and skill of a reasonably competent member of the profession in good standing, usually proven by **expert testimony**. In medicine, courts use a national or similar-locality standard of care, and many states also impose a duty to obtain informed consent based on what a reasonable patient would want to know.
- **Emergencies.** The reasonable person is judged in light of the emergency, if the defendant did not create it.
- **Custom** is relevant evidence of reasonable care but does not conclusively establish it.

### Negligence per se

If a defendant violates a statute, a court may adopt the statute as the standard of care. Most jurisdictions apply **negligence per se** if (1) the plaintiff is within the class of persons the statute was meant to protect and (2) the harm is of the type the statute was meant to prevent. A violation then establishes breach as a matter of law. The plaintiff must still prove causation and damages (the violation must have caused the harm). In *Martin v. Herzog* (1920), a driver who traveled at night without lights, in violation of a statute, was negligent per se. A violation is excused if, for example, compliance would have been more dangerous, the defendant was incapacitated, had no reason to know of the facts that made the statute apply, or could not comply despite reasonable effort. A minority of states treat a violation only as **evidence of negligence**. Compliance with a statute is evidence of due care but is not conclusive.

### Applying the rules

Start with the general duty, then state the standard. Ask whether a variation applies, whether a statute fits the harm and the plaintiff, and whether an excuse exists. Then compare the defendant's conduct to the standard (breach, Week 5).

@example
title: A statute that fits
problem: A city ordinance requires that all trucks carrying loose gravel be covered. Rex drives an uncovered gravel truck. A piece of gravel falls onto the road, and a car driver, Sal, swerves to avoid it and hits a tree. Separately, the ordinance's purpose is to prevent debris on roads. Does negligence per se apply?
step: The ordinance applies to Rex, who violated it by driving uncovered with loose gravel.
step: The ordinance was meant to protect motorists and others on the road from falling debris, and Sal's harm, a crash caused by debris, is that type of harm.
step: Sal is in the protected class and the harm is of the type the statute was aimed at, so a violation is negligence per se (the majority rule). There is no excuse on the facts.
step: Sal must still prove causation and damages, which seem satisfied. Conclusion: breach is established per se.

@mistakes
- Lowering the standard for a defendant who is clumsy, inexperienced or mentally ill. The standard is objective.
- Applying negligence per se without checking that the plaintiff is in the protected class and the harm is the type the statute was meant to prevent.
- Treating negligence per se as establishing liability. It establishes only breach; causation and damages are still required.
- Applying the child standard to a child engaged in a dangerous adult activity such as driving.
