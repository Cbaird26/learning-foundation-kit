@article
These torts protect interests in the use of land, reputation and personal privacy. Nuisance and the dignitary torts often turn on balancing, and defamation is deeply shaped by the First Amendment.

### Nuisance

A **private nuisance** is a **substantial and unreasonable interference** with the plaintiff's use and enjoyment of land. The interference is **substantial** if it would be offensive or seriously annoying to a normal person in the community, so oversensitive plaintiffs lose. It is **unreasonable** if the gravity of the harm outweighs the utility of the defendant's conduct (Restatement (Second) § 826), or, in some cases, if the harm is serious and compensation is feasible (§ 829A). The defendant's conduct may be intentional (knowing that the interference is substantially certain), negligent or reckless, or abnormally dangerous. Remedies are damages or an injunction. In *Boomer v. Atlantic Cement Co.* (1970), the court refused to shut down a cement plant that caused serious harm to neighbors and instead awarded permanent damages, balancing the equities because the economic cost of closing the plant was great compared with the damages. In *Spur Industries v. Del Webb Development Co.* (1972), a developer who moved a community next to a cattle feedlot obtained an injunction because of harm to the public, but had to indemnify the feedlot for the cost of moving. **Coming to the nuisance** is a factor, not a complete defense.

A **public nuisance** is an unreasonable interference with a right common to the general public (blocking a highway, polluting a river). It is generally enforced by public officials, and a private plaintiff may sue only for **special injury** different in kind from that suffered by the public.

### Defamation

**Defamation** is a false statement of fact that harms reputation. The elements are (1) a **defamatory** statement, one that tends to lower the plaintiff in the estimation of the community or deter others from associating with the plaintiff; (2) that is **of and concerning** the plaintiff; (3) **published** (communicated to at least one third person); (4) **falsity** and the required level of **fault**; and (5) **harm to reputation**. **Libel** (written or permanent) generally allows presumed damages. **Slander** (spoken) requires proof of special damages unless it falls within a **slander per se** category: accusing the plaintiff of a crime (generally a serious one), a loathsome disease, professional incompetence or unchastity. **Truth** is a defense, and a pure **opinion** that implies no provable facts is not actionable (*Milkovich v. Lorain Journal Co.* (1990)). There are **absolute privileges** (statements in judicial proceedings, legislative debates) and **qualified privileges** (a former employer's good-faith reference, or a report to someone with a common interest), which are lost if the speaker acts with malice or knows the statement is false. Under 47 U.S.C. § 230, providers of interactive computer services are not treated as the publisher of content from others.

**Constitutional rules.**

- **Public officials:** *New York Times Co. v. Sullivan* (1964) requires proof of **actual malice**, meaning knowledge that the statement was false or reckless disregard of whether it was false, by clear and convincing evidence. "Actual malice" is a term of art that has nothing to do with ill will. The rule was extended to **public figures** in *Curtis Publishing Co. v. Butts* (1967).
- **Private figures:** *Gertz v. Robert Welch, Inc.* (1974) lets states set their own fault standard, as long as it is not strict liability (most use negligence), and bars **presumed or punitive damages** without actual malice when the statement involves a matter of public concern. *Dun & Bradstreet v. Greenmoss Builders* (1985) held that presumed and punitive damages may be awarded without actual malice for private-concern speech. Under *Philadelphia Newspapers v. Hepps* (1986), a private-figure plaintiff must prove falsity when the speech is of public concern.
- A **public figure** is a person with general fame, or one who has voluntarily injected himself into a particular public controversy (a **limited-purpose public figure**).

### Privacy

The Restatement (Second) §§ 652A to 652E recognizes four torts:

1. **Intrusion upon seclusion**: an intentional intrusion into a private place or affair that would be highly offensive to a reasonable person (no publication needed).
2. **Public disclosure of private facts**: publicity of a private fact that is highly offensive to a reasonable person and not of legitimate public concern.
3. **False light**: publicity that places the plaintiff in a false light that is highly offensive to a reasonable person, with actual malice when the matter is of public interest (*Time, Inc. v. Hill* (1967)); not all states recognize it.
4. **Appropriation** of name or likeness for the defendant's commercial advantage.

### Applying the rules

@example
title: A public figure and a false story
problem: A newspaper prints that Dr. Rho, a famous television personality, accepted bribes to endorse a diet pill. The story is false. The reporter relied on a single anonymous source who had admitted lying in the past, and ignored Dr. Rho's offer of records disproving the claim. Dr. Rho sues for libel. Analyze.
step: The statement accuses her of a crime, is of and concerning her, was published and is false: factual falsity and defamatory meaning are established.
step: Dr. Rho is a public figure, so under Sullivan and Butts she must prove actual malice, knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard of the truth, by clear and convincing evidence. Negligence is not enough.
step: Reckless disregard requires serious doubts as to truth. Relying on a source known to be unreliable, and ignoring offered proof, supports a finding that the reporter had such doubts.
step: Conclusion: Dr. Rho likely satisfies the actual malice standard and can recover, including presumed damages, subject to the fact-finder's decision.

@mistakes
- Confusing constitutional actual malice with ill will. It means knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard.
- Applying negligence to a public figure, who must show actual malice.
- Treating an opinion as automatically protected. A statement implying a provable false fact can be actionable.
- Granting an injunction against a nuisance without balancing, or treating coming to the nuisance as a complete bar.
