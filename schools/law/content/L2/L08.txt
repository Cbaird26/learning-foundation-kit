@article
A plaintiff's own carelessness, or voluntary acceptance of a risk, can reduce or bar recovery. The law of these defenses changed from all-or-nothing rules to apportionment of fault.

### Contributory negligence

At common law, a plaintiff whose negligence contributed to the injury was **completely barred**, no matter how small the plaintiff's fault. A few jurisdictions still follow it (Alabama, Maryland, North Carolina, Virginia and the District of Columbia). The harsh result led to ameliorating doctrines, notably **last clear chance**: a plaintiff who negligently got into peril can recover if the defendant had the last clear chance to avoid the harm and failed to use it. The doctrine usually requires that the defendant knew or should have known of the plaintiff's helpless position. Contributory negligence is not a defense to intentional torts, and most courts do not apply it to reckless conduct.

### Comparative fault

Almost all states have replaced contributory negligence with **comparative fault**, by statute or by decision (*Li v. Yellow Cab Co.* (1975) in California). The jury assigns percentages of fault to the parties, and the plaintiff's recovery is reduced by the plaintiff's share.

- **Pure comparative fault.** The plaintiff recovers total damages reduced by the plaintiff's percentage of fault, however high. A plaintiff 90% at fault still recovers 10%. (Examples include California and New York.)
- **Modified comparative fault, 50% bar.** The plaintiff is barred if the plaintiff's fault is **equal to or greater than** the defendant's (50% or more), so the plaintiff recovers only if less than 50% at fault.
- **Modified comparative fault, 51% bar.** The plaintiff is barred only if the plaintiff's fault is **greater than** the defendant's (51% or more); a plaintiff exactly 50% at fault still recovers.

The formula under each system, when recovery is allowed, is

$$\text{Recovery} = \text{Total damages} \times (1 - \text{plaintiff's fault share}).$$

Where there are several defendants, states differ on whether the plaintiff's fault is compared with each defendant's or with the total of all defendants' fault. Many states also reduce recovery for a plaintiff's failure to wear a seat belt or to mitigate, though some limit or reject the seat belt defense. Comparative principles also apply to strict liability and products claims in most states.

### Assumption of risk

**Express assumption of risk** arises from a contract or waiver, such as a release signed before a recreational activity. Courts enforce it if clear and not against public policy; exculpatory clauses are often unenforceable when they cover gross negligence or recklessness or involve essential services, such as hospitals (*Tunkl v. Regents of the University of California* (1963)).

**Implied primary assumption of risk** means the defendant owed no duty because the risk is inherent in the activity. For example, a co-participant in an active sport owes only a duty not to injure recklessly or intentionally, and does not breach by ordinary negligence (*Knight v. Jewett* (1992)). It is a duty issue, so it is a complete bar to a negligence claim regardless of comparative fault.

**Implied secondary assumption of risk** means the plaintiff knowingly and voluntarily encountered a risk that the defendant negligently created. Most states, including those with comparative fault, merge secondary assumption into comparative fault: the jury treats the plaintiff's unreasonable choice as a share of fault and does not bar the claim outright. A few states retain it as a complete defense.

### Applying the rules

Identify the fault shares first, then the jurisdiction's rule, and then compute. State the bar first, if one applies, and compute only if the plaintiff survives it. Keep intentional conduct and reckless conduct in mind: comparative principles may not reduce recovery from an intentional tortfeasor.

@example
title: Same facts, three rules
problem: Pia suffers \$100,000 in damages. A jury finds Pia 40% at fault and the defendant, Quo, 60% at fault. What does Pia recover under pure comparative fault, a 50% bar and a 51% bar?
step: Compute Pia's reduction: 40% of \$100,000 is \$40,000.
step: Under pure comparative fault, Pia recovers \$100,000 minus \$40,000, or \$60,000.
step: Under a 50% bar, Pia is barred only if 50% or more at fault; at 40% she is not barred, so she also recovers \$60,000. The same is true under the 51% bar.
step: Conclusion: the three rules give the same result, \$60,000. They diverge only when Pia's fault is 50% or more, so check that boundary first.

@mistakes
- Using the wrong bar. Under the 50% bar a plaintiff at exactly 50% recovers nothing, but under the 51% bar the plaintiff recovers half.
- Applying contributory negligence as a complete bar in a comparative-fault state.
- Treating a signed waiver as always enforceable, even for recklessness or essential services.
- Applying comparative fault to reduce recovery for an intentional tort.
