@article
A plaintiff often wants to recover from someone other than, or in addition to, the person who committed the tort. Vicarious liability imposes liability on one party for another's tort. Rules on multiple defendants then allocate the burden among them.

### Respondeat superior

Under **respondeat superior**, an employer is vicariously liable for torts committed by an **employee** within the **scope of employment**. The employer need not be at fault. The policies are loss spreading, deterrence through the employer's control, and that the enterprise should bear the costs of its activities. The employee remains liable too, so the plaintiff can sue both.

- **Employee versus independent contractor.** The key question is the employer's **right to control** the manner and means of work (Restatement (Third) of Agency § 7.07), looking to factors such as supervision, tools, pay, duration and whether the work is part of the employer's regular business.
- **Scope of employment.** Conduct is within the scope if it is of the kind the employee is hired to perform, occurs substantially within the authorized time and space limits, and is motivated at least in part by a purpose to serve the employer. A **detour** (a minor deviation) remains within scope, but a **frolic** (a substantial departure for personal reasons) does not. The **coming-and-going rule** generally excludes commuting, with exceptions when the employee is on a special errand.
- **Intentional torts.** An employer is generally not liable for an employee's intentional torts, motivated by personal reasons, but is liable if the act was motivated by an intent to serve the employer, such as a bouncer's excessive force in ejecting a patron, or if it was foreseeable in light of the employment. In *Ira S. Bushey & Sons v. United States* (1968), a drunken sailor returned to a ship and opened drydock valves, damaging the dock; the court held the shipowner liable because the conduct, though not motivated by service, was foreseeable and characteristic of the enterprise. Many states take a narrower view.

### Independent contractors and direct liability

A party that hires an **independent contractor** is generally not vicariously liable for the contractor's negligence. Exceptions are for **inherently dangerous activities**, **nondelegable duties** (such as a landowner's duty to keep premises safe for the public, or a common carrier's duty), and **apparent agency** (holding out a person as one's agent to a plaintiff who reasonably relies, as in some hospital emergency-room cases). Separately, an employer may be **directly liable** for its own negligence in **hiring, supervising, training or retaining** an employee it knew or should have known was unfit, and for **negligent entrustment** of a car or a dangerous thing to someone likely to misuse it. Parents are generally not vicariously liable for a child's torts, absent statute or negligent supervision, and some states have **family purpose** or **owner-permissive-use** statutes that make vehicle owners liable.

### Joint tortfeasors

When several defendants cause a single indivisible harm, the traditional rule is **joint and several liability**: each defendant is liable for the whole judgment, and the plaintiff may collect all of it from any one defendant, who then seeks reimbursement from the others. Many states have abolished or limited the rule when comparative fault applies, leaving each defendant **severally liable** only for its own percentage, or retaining joint and several liability only for defendants above a threshold (for example 50% at fault) or for economic damages. Where harm is **divisible**, each defendant is liable only for its own part.

- **Contribution** lets a defendant who paid more than its share recover the excess from other tortfeasors, usually in proportion to comparative fault (older rules divide equally, or pro rata).
- **Indemnity** shifts the whole loss: a vicariously liable employer may seek full indemnity from the employee, and a retailer held strictly liable may seek indemnity from the manufacturer. Where contribution is available, many states have merged indemnity into comparative shares, except for vicarious liability.
- **Settlement.** A plaintiff's settlement with one defendant affects the others: under a **pro tanto** rule, the nonsettling defendants get a credit for the dollar amount paid; under a **proportionate share** rule (as in the Uniform Comparative Fault Act), the credit is the settling defendant's percentage of fault.

### Applying the rules

First decide whether another party is vicariously or directly liable. Then identify the jurisdiction's rule for multiple defendants and compute each party's share, noting insolvency risk, which falls on the defendants under joint and several liability and on the plaintiff under several liability.

@example
title: A delivery driver's detour
problem: Wren, a delivery driver for Quik Co., drives to a customer and, after the delivery, drives two blocks out of her way to buy lunch before returning. On the way, she negligently hits Sol. Is Quik liable?
step: Wren is an employee of Quik, so respondeat superior applies if she was within the scope of employment.
step: Stopping for lunch two blocks off the route is a minor deviation, a detour, and it is a personal act incidental to the job. Courts treat it as within the scope.
step: Driving to buy lunch on a delivery route is also not a substantial departure in time or space.
step: Conclusion: Quik is vicariously liable for Wren's negligence, and Sol may sue both, and Quik may have a claim for indemnity against Wren, though it rarely uses it.

@mistakes
- Holding an employer vicariously liable for every act of an independent contractor. The control test and exceptions matter.
- Treating any deviation as a frolic. A minor detour remains within the scope of employment.
- Confusing contribution (sharing the loss according to shares) with indemnity (shifting the entire loss).
- Ignoring insolvency. Under joint and several liability, a solvent defendant pays the shortfall, but not under several liability.
