@article
**Strict liability** imposes liability without proof of intent or negligence. The plaintiff must still prove actual cause, proximate cause and damages, but need not show a breach of reasonable care. The policy is that a person who engages in an activity that creates a risk of serious harm that cannot be eliminated by care should bear the costs of the activity as a cost of doing business, and that the activity's risks, not the defendant's carelessness, are the source of the harm.

### Animals

**Wild animals** (animals not customarily domesticated, such as a tiger or a venomous snake): the possessor is strictly liable for harm caused by the animal's dangerous propensities, even if the possessor exercised utmost care. A keeper is liable to anyone injured, with exceptions for trespassers in some states. **Domestic animals** (dogs, cats, horses): the owner is strictly liable only if the owner knew or had reason to know of the animal's **dangerous propensity**, which is the origin of the "**one-bite rule**" (although a known propensity may be shown by more than a prior bite). Otherwise the plaintiff must prove negligence. Many states have **dog-bite statutes** that impose strict liability for bites regardless of prior knowledge, with exceptions for provocation or trespass. Owners are also strictly liable for the physical damage that **trespassing livestock** cause to land, with the rule varying in open-range states.

### Abnormally dangerous activities

The English case *Rylands v. Fletcher* (1868) imposed strict liability on a landowner whose reservoir burst and flooded a neighbor's mine, based on a non-natural use of land. The American rule, in the Restatement (Second) of Torts §§ 519 and 520, is that a person who carries on an **abnormally dangerous activity** is strictly liable for harm to person, land or chattels resulting from the activity's abnormal danger. The court (not the jury) decides, weighing six factors:

1. a high degree of risk of harm;
2. a likelihood that the harm will be great;
3. inability to eliminate the risk by exercising reasonable care;
4. the extent to which the activity is not a matter of common usage;
5. inappropriateness of the activity to the place where it is carried on; and
6. the extent to which its value to the community is outweighed by its dangerous attributes.

Courts have treated **blasting with explosives** (*Spano v. Perini Corp.* (1969)), storing explosives or large quantities of toxic or flammable materials, and certain hazardous waste operations as abnormally dangerous, while ordinary activities such as driving, running a gas station or storing small amounts of household chemicals are generally not. The Restatement (Third) § 20 uses a narrower two-part test: the activity creates a foreseeable and highly significant risk of physical harm even when reasonable care is exercised, and it is not a matter of common usage.

### Scope and defenses

Strict liability extends only to harms that result from the **kind of danger** that makes the activity abnormally dangerous. A blaster is liable if flying debris injures a neighbor; he is not strictly liable if a worker trips on a wire at the site. Defenses include **comparative fault** (in most states, a plaintiff's unreasonable assumption of a known risk reduces recovery), an **abnormally sensitive** plaintiff's use or condition (for example, a plaintiff whose unusually delicate activity was damaged by a normal blast), and the plaintiff's trespass in some states. A plaintiff's ordinary negligence in failing to discover the danger is generally not a defense under the Restatement (Second) § 524, though modern comparative statutes may change the result. **Act of God** and the acts of third parties may bear on causation. Intervening acts that are within the risk do not bar recovery.

### Applying the rules

For animals, classify the animal and check for a statute. For activities, apply the factors and ask whether the harm results from the abnormally dangerous feature. Then check the defenses and compute any reduction.

@example
title: Blasting near a neighbor
problem: Dyna Corp. blasts rock with dynamite in a quarry in a populated area. Debris flies a quarter mile and wrecks Eve's greenhouse, a \$40,000 loss. Dyna used the best safety practices. Eve was unaware of the danger. Is Dyna liable?
step: Blasting with explosives in a populated area has a high risk of great harm that cannot be fully eliminated by care, is not common usage and is inappropriate to the place, so it is an abnormally dangerous activity.
step: Because the activity is abnormally dangerous, Dyna is strictly liable. Dyna's use of the best safety practices is not a defense, because strict liability does not turn on carelessness.
step: The harm, debris damage to nearby property, results from the very danger that makes blasting abnormally dangerous, so it is within the scope of liability.
step: Conclusion: Dyna is strictly liable for the \$40,000 loss, subject to any comparative-fault reduction, which does not apply because Eve was unaware of the danger.

@mistakes
- Requiring proof of negligence for an abnormally dangerous activity. The defendant's care is irrelevant to liability.
- Treating all dangerous activities as abnormally dangerous. Common activities such as driving, and activities whose risk can be controlled by care, are not.
- Imposing strict liability for harm outside the risk, such as an ordinary trip hazard at a blasting site.
- Applying the one-bite rule to a wild animal. Wild animals are strict liability without prior notice.
