@review
**Weeks 1 to 3: intentional torts and defenses.** Battery (intent to cause harmful or offensive contact, with transferred intent), assault (reasonable apprehension of imminent contact), false imprisonment (intentional confinement, awareness, no reasonable escape), trespass to land (intent to enter only), trespass to chattels and conversion (forced sale at fair market value), IIED (extreme and outrageous, severe distress, First Amendment limits). Defenses: consent and its scope, self-defense with proportional force, no deadly force for property, and public versus private necessity.

**Weeks 4 to 7: negligence.** Five elements: duty, breach, actual cause, proximate cause, damages. Reasonable person standard (objective, with adjustments for physical disability, children, professionals), negligence per se (class and harm fit, excuses), the Hand formula (breach if $B < P \times L$), custom (T.J. Hooper), res ipsa loquitur. Causation: but-for, substantial factor, alternative and market-share liability, loss of chance, foreseeability and superseding causes. Special duties: no duty to rescue and its exceptions, Tarasoff, landowners, NIED, economic loss rule. Damages: compensatory, collateral source, eggshell plaintiff, punitive limits.

**Week 8: defenses to negligence.** Contributory negligence (complete bar, last clear chance), pure comparative fault, modified comparative fault (50% bar and 51% bar), express, primary and secondary assumption of risk.

**Weeks 9 and 10: strict liability and products.** Wild and domestic animals, abnormally dangerous activities (Restatement (Second) §§ 519-520), manufacturing, design and warning defects, consumer-expectation and risk-utility tests, reasonable alternative design.

**Weeks 11 and 12: dignitary torts and multiple parties.** Nuisance (substantial and unreasonable, Boomer and Spur), defamation (Sullivan actual malice for public officials and figures, Gertz for private figures), four privacy torts, respondeat superior, independent contractors, joint and several liability, contribution and indemnity.

**Self-check list.**
- Can I state the elements of each tort without notes?
- Can I compute recovery under pure and modified comparative fault, and under several and joint-and-several liability?
- Can I explain when intent is transferred and when it is not?
- Can I apply both design-defect tests to a set of facts?
- Can I classify a defamation plaintiff and state the fault standard?

**Common errors.** Treating intent as intent to harm; applying negligence per se without a class-and-harm fit; using but-for for two sufficient causes; treating private necessity as a complete defense; applying the wrong bar rule at exactly 50% fault; confusing actual malice with ill will; and treating independent contractors as employees.

prompt: Dana, injured when a delivery van driven by an employee, Eli, hit her in a crosswalk, proves \$480,000 in total damages. The jury finds Dana 20% at fault, Eli 50% and a store, Market, 30%. The jurisdiction uses modified comparative fault barring a plaintiff whose fault is greater than the defendant's, retains joint and several liability, and Market is insolvent. Eli's employer is vicariously liable for Eli. How much can Dana collect from the employer in dollars?
answer: 384000
check: 480000 * (1 - 0.20)
hint: First apply Dana's comparative fault to the total damages.
hint: Under joint and several liability, each liable defendant owes the whole reduced judgment, and the employer answers for Eli.
solution: Dana's fault does not bar her (20% is less than each defendant's share). Her recovery is \$480,000 times 0.80, or \$384,000. Because liability is joint and several and the employer is vicariously liable for Eli, the employer can be made to pay the full \$384,000, with a right of contribution against Market that is worthless because Market is insolvent.

@exam mc
prompt: Gabe, a delivery driver, runs a red light in violation of a statute designed to protect cross traffic, and strikes Hope's car. Hope, who was driving 15 miles per hour over the limit, would have avoided the collision had she been driving the limit. Gabe's employer sent him on a route that predictably required speed. The state uses pure comparative fault. Which is the best statement?
choice: Hope is barred from recovery because she was speeding.
choice: Gabe is not liable because Hope's speeding was the actual cause.
choice*: Gabe's violation is negligence per se, and Hope's recovery is reduced by her share of fault, with the employer likely vicariously liable if Gabe was within the scope of employment.
choice: Hope recovers nothing from the employer because employers are never liable for their drivers.
hint: Apply negligence per se, comparative fault and respondeat superior in turn.
solution: Running the light violates a statute aimed at preventing intersection collisions (negligence per se). Hope's speeding is comparative fault, which reduces but does not bar recovery in a pure comparative state. The employer is liable under respondeat superior for a driver acting within the scope of employment.

@exam self
prompt: A restaurant owner hires a cleaning contractor, which negligently leaves a wet floor unmarked. A customer slips, breaks an arm and is told by the restaurant manager, who says he is detaining her until the police come, that she cannot leave until she signs a statement admitting she was careless. She stays for thirty minutes because she believes she cannot leave. Write an issue-spotting analysis identifying the claims against the restaurant, the contractor and the manager, with the key rules.
hint: List defendants and claims, then give the rule and application for each.
solution: Against the contractor: negligence (duty to keep the floor safe, breach by leaving it wet and unmarked, actual and proximate cause of the fall, damages); the contractor is the direct tortfeasor. Against the restaurant: the owner of premises owes invitees a duty of reasonable care and may have a nondelegable duty to keep premises safe, so it can be liable despite hiring an independent contractor, and may also be directly negligent if it knew or should have known of the hazard. Against the restaurant and manager: false imprisonment, because the manager intentionally confined her by an assertion of legal authority and she was aware and believed she could not leave; the shopkeeper's privilege does not apply because she was not suspected of theft. The restaurant is vicariously liable if the manager acted within the scope of employment, which is arguable because the act was motivated by the employer's interests. Defenses and damages: comparative fault for the fall if she failed to notice an obvious spill, and compensatory damages for the arm, with nominal and possibly punitive damages for the detention. Joint and several liability or several liability would determine how the contractor and the restaurant share a judgment.
rubric: Identifies negligence (contractor), premises liability or nondelegable duty (restaurant) and false imprisonment (manager).
rubric: States the elements and applies facts, including the assertion of legal authority and the absence of shopkeeper's privilege.
rubric: Addresses vicarious liability, comparative fault, damages and allocation among defendants.
