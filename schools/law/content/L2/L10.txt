@article
**Products liability** holds sellers responsible for injuries caused by defective products. Its history shows how the law moved from privity (the defendant owed a duty only to a direct buyer) to negligence, then to strict liability. *MacPherson v. Buick Motor Co.* (1916) held that a manufacturer owes a duty of reasonable care to foreseeable users of a product that is dangerous if negligently made, even without privity. *Greenman v. Yuba Power Products* (1963) adopted strict liability in tort for defective products, and Restatement (Second) of Torts § 402A put the rule in a form most states follow.

### Elements of strict liability

Under § 402A, a plaintiff must show (1) the defendant is a **commercial seller** of the product (a manufacturer, distributor or retailer in the chain of distribution, but not a casual seller); (2) the product was **defective** when it left the defendant's control and was **unreasonably dangerous** (a phrase the Restatement (Third) drops); (3) the defect was an **actual and proximate cause** of the plaintiff's injury; and (4) damages. Fault is not required: a defendant is liable even if it exercised all possible care. Bystanders may recover in most states. **Pure economic loss** from a defective product that damages only itself is generally left to contract and warranty law.

### Three kinds of defect

1. **Manufacturing defect.** The product departs from its intended design (a bottle with a flaw that makes it explode). Liability is strict, and the product is compared with the manufacturer's own specifications.
2. **Design defect.** The whole product line is made as intended, but the design is unreasonably dangerous. Two main tests apply:
   - The **consumer-expectation test** asks whether the product is more dangerous than an ordinary consumer would expect when used in a reasonably foreseeable way. It works for simple products and obvious failures, but is less suited to complex products.
   - The **risk-utility test** asks whether the risks of the design outweigh its benefits, considering the gravity and likelihood of harm, feasible alternative designs, their cost and effect on utility, and the user's ability to avoid harm. The Restatement (Third) of Torts: Products Liability § 2(b), followed in many states, requires the plaintiff to show a **reasonable alternative design (RAD)** whose adoption would have reduced the foreseeable risk and made the product not reasonably safe without it. Some states, such as California in *Barker v. Lull Engineering Co.* (1978), allow either test and put the burden on the defendant to show the benefits of the design outweigh its risks once the plaintiff shows the design caused the injury.
3. **Warning (marketing) defect.** The seller fails to give adequate instructions or warnings of foreseeable risks that are not obvious, when the absence of the warning makes the product not reasonably safe (§ 2(c)). There is no duty to warn of obvious dangers, and a warning does not cure a design that could reasonably be made safer.

### Other theories

**Negligence** requires proof that the manufacturer failed to use reasonable care in design, manufacture or warnings. Under the **Uniform Commercial Code**, an **implied warranty of merchantability** (§ 2-314) arises when a merchant sells goods, and an **express warranty** (§ 2-313) arises from affirmations of fact or promises. Warranty claims have notice and disclaimer rules and, in some states, privity limits on remote buyers.

### Defenses

Most states apply **comparative fault** to products claims, reducing recovery for the plaintiff's unreasonable use of a known danger. **Misuse** that was not reasonably foreseeable can defeat liability or reduce recovery, but foreseeable misuse is within the manufacturer's duty. Some states recognize a **state-of-the-art** defense (the design conformed to what was technologically feasible), and compliance with government safety standards is evidence but not a complete defense in most states. **Federal preemption** can bar state claims that conflict with federal regulation, and **statutes of repose** cut off claims after a fixed period regardless of injury.

### Applying the rules

Start with the type of defect, because it determines the test. For a design claim, state both tests and the jurisdiction's choice, identify a reasonable alternative design and compare its cost and benefits. Then address causation and defenses.

@example
title: A design-defect claim under two tests
problem: A tractor sold by Agri Co. has no rollover protective structure. A farmer, Ned, is crushed when it tips on a slope. A cage structure costing \$400 per tractor was available and would have prevented the injury. The farmer says an ordinary consumer would expect a tractor to be reasonably safe on a slope. Analyze under both tests.
step: Consumer-expectation test: a jury could find an ordinary consumer would not expect a tractor used on a slope to lack basic rollover protection, so the tractor is more dangerous than expected. The test favors Ned if a jury finds that expectation reasonable.
step: Risk-utility test: the risk (fatal crushing, a likelihood that is significant on slopes) is great, and a \$400 cage is a small cost that preserves the tractor's utility.
step: Under a RAD requirement, the \$400 cage is a feasible reasonable alternative design that would have prevented the injury.
step: Conclusion: Ned likely prevails on a design-defect claim under either test, subject to defenses such as comparative fault.

@mistakes
- Requiring the plaintiff to prove the manufacturer was careless. Strict liability looks at the product.
- Calling a warning a cure for every design defect. A feasible safer design may still be required.
- Applying § 402A to a casual seller, or to a seller not in the business of selling the product.
- Treating the consumer-expectation test as the only design test. Many states use risk-utility or require a reasonable alternative design.
