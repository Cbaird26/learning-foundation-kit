@article
**Breach** asks whether the defendant's conduct fell below the standard of care: what would a reasonable person have done under the circumstances? The plaintiff must prove breach by a preponderance of the evidence, usually by comparing the defendant's behavior with the costs and benefits of safer alternatives.

### Foreseeability and risk-benefit analysis

A reasonable person takes precautions against risks that are foreseeable and unreasonable. Courts weigh the likelihood and severity of harm against the cost of avoiding it, and the social utility of the activity. The best-known formulation is Judge Learned Hand's in *United States v. Carroll Towing Co.* (1947): liability depends on whether the **burden** of adequate precautions ($B$) is less than the **probability** of harm ($P$) times the **gravity** of the resulting loss ($L$):

$$\text{Breach if } B < P \times L.$$

If a precaution that would cost \$300 would prevent a one-in-ten chance of a \$10,000 loss, then $P \times L = \$1,000$, which is greater than $B = \$300$; failing to take the precaution is negligent. The formula is a way of organizing reasoning, and courts rarely have numbers precise enough to apply it mechanically. It is the foundation of the economic approach to negligence, which asks whether the defendant failed to take a cost-justified precaution. Critics note that it cannot value human life and dignity without difficulty, and that it neglects distributional concerns.

### Custom

Evidence that the defendant followed or violated the **custom** of the industry is relevant but not conclusive. In *The T.J. Hooper* (1932), tugboat owners were negligent for not equipping tugs with radios, even though no tugs had them, because a whole industry may lag behind reasonable precautions. Compliance with custom is evidence of due care, and departure from custom is evidence of negligence. For professionals, custom usually sets the standard of care, with limited exceptions for obviously unreasonable customs.

### Proving breach with circumstantial evidence

When the defendant has no actual notice of a hazard, as in many slip-and-fall cases, the plaintiff may show that the defendant created the hazard, or had **constructive notice** because the condition existed long enough that a reasonable inspection would have found it.

### Res ipsa loquitur

**Res ipsa loquitur** ("the thing speaks for itself") lets the jury infer negligence from the accident itself. In *Byrne v. Boadle* (1863), a barrel of flour fell from a warehouse window onto a passerby. The traditional elements, which Restatement (Second) § 328D restates in modern form, are: (1) the event is of a kind that ordinarily does not occur in the absence of negligence; (2) the instrumentality was within the defendant's **exclusive control** (a modern formulation asks whether other responsible causes, including the plaintiff's conduct and third parties, are sufficiently eliminated by the evidence); and (3) the event was not due to any voluntary action or contribution by the plaintiff. The usual effect is a **permissible inference**: the jury may, but need not, find negligence. A minority of states treat it as a rebuttable presumption that shifts the burden of production, and a very few shift the burden of persuasion. Res ipsa typically arises with surgical instruments left in a patient, elevators that fall and falling objects.

### Applying the rules

For breach, state the standard, list the precautions the defendant could have taken, and compare the burden with the probability and magnitude of harm. Use custom as support but not as a conclusion. If there is no direct evidence of what the defendant did, consider res ipsa. Keep breach separate from causation: a defendant may be careless without having caused the harm.

@example
title: The Hand formula in numbers
problem: A shop owner knows that a floor cleaner leaves a slippery film and that there is a 4% chance each cleaning that a customer will fall and suffer a \$20,000 injury. A \$500 warning sign and mat would eliminate the risk. Did the owner breach by failing to take that precaution?
step: Identify the variables: the burden of precaution B = \$500; the probability P = 0.04; the loss if it happens L = \$20,000.
step: Compute the expected loss: P times L = 0.04 times 20,000 = \$800.
step: Compare: B = \$500 is less than PL = \$800, so the precaution is cost-justified and the owner who skipped it fell below the standard.
step: Conclusion: breach is likely. A jury would still weigh other factors, such as the utility of the activity, but the numbers favor the plaintiff.

@mistakes
- Using custom as conclusive evidence of due care; an entire industry may be negligent (The T.J. Hooper).
- Reversing the Hand formula: breach occurs when the burden is less than the expected loss, not greater.
- Applying res ipsa when the defendant did not have exclusive control or when the plaintiff may have caused the accident.
- Confusing breach with causation: carelessness is not liability unless it caused the harm.
