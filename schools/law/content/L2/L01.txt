@article
Intentional torts protect interests in bodily integrity, freedom of movement and peace of mind. Each one has an **intent** element and a **conduct** element, and the exam question is almost always whether each element is satisfied on the facts. Remember that in tort law "intent" does not mean malice or a desire to hurt. It means a purpose to bring about a result, or knowledge to a substantial certainty that the result will follow (Restatement (Second) of Torts § 8A).

### Battery

**Battery** is (1) an act by the defendant, (2) done with intent to cause a harmful or offensive contact with the plaintiff's person, or an imminent apprehension of such contact, and (3) a harmful or offensive contact that results (Restatement (Second) § 13). Three points drive most problems.

- **Offensive contact** is contact that offends a reasonable sense of personal dignity. The plaintiff need not be aware of it at the time (a kiss on a sleeping person is a battery), and the contact may be with anything closely connected to the body, such as a hat, a plate in the hand or a seat being pulled out.
- **Intent** is measured by what the defendant meant to do. In *Garratt v. Dailey* (1955), a child who moved a chair as someone sat down was liable if he knew with substantial certainty that she would try to sit where the chair had been. In *Vosburg v. Putney* (1891), a boy who kicked a classmate's leg was liable for the full, unexpected injury even though he meant only a light touch; the intent to make a contact the plaintiff had not consented to was enough, and the **eggshell plaintiff** rule makes the defendant take the victim as found.
- Many courts speak of **single intent** (intent to make the contact) and others of **dual intent** (intent to make a harmful or offensive contact). The Restatement and many states require the actor to intend the contact to be harmful or offensive, or to know that it is substantially certain to be so.

**Transferred intent** applies to five torts: battery, assault, false imprisonment, trespass to land and trespass to chattels. If A swings at B and hits C, A is liable to C for battery (and to B for assault if B saw the swing). Intent also transfers between those torts.

### Assault

**Assault** is (1) an act intended to cause a harmful or offensive contact or an imminent apprehension of one, and (2) that causes the plaintiff a reasonable apprehension of an imminent harmful or offensive contact (Restatement (Second) § 21). Apprehension means anticipation, not fear. The following limits recur: words alone are generally not enough without an act; threats of future harm are not imminent; a conditional threat ("I would hit you if we weren't in church") is not an assault because the condition negates the threat of present harm; and the plaintiff must be aware of the threat, so an unseen gun aimed at your back is not an assault. The defendant must also appear to have the present ability to carry the threat out.

### False imprisonment

**False imprisonment** is (1) an act or omission intended to confine the plaintiff within fixed boundaries, (2) an actual confinement, and (3) the plaintiff's awareness of the confinement or actual harm from it (Restatement (Second) § 35). Confinement can be by physical barriers, physical force, threats of force against the plaintiff, duress against property, or an assertion of legal authority. **Moral pressure** and threats to reputation are not enough, and there is no confinement if a reasonable means of escape is known to the plaintiff. Confinement may be short. Merchants have a **shopkeeper's privilege** (recognized at common law and by statute in most states) to detain a suspected shoplifter briefly, if they have reasonable cause, use reasonable means and detain for a reasonable time to investigate.

### Remedies and policy

Nominal damages are available for intentional torts without proof of loss, so a technical battery is actionable. Compensatory damages include medical expenses, lost earnings and pain and suffering, and punitive damages may be awarded for malice or reckless indifference, subject to the due process limits in *State Farm v. Campbell* (2003), where the Court said few awards exceeding a single-digit ratio to compensatory damages will satisfy due process. The policy is to deter breaches of the peace and to vindicate dignity and autonomy.

### Applying the rules

Work element by element: identify the act, ask what the defendant intended or knew to a substantial certainty, ask what contact or apprehension or confinement resulted, then check for a defense (Week 3). Do not skip transferred intent when the wrong person is hurt.

@example
title: The wrong target
problem: Pat, angry at Quinn, throws a heavy mug at Quinn across a crowded cafe. Quinn sees it coming and ducks. The mug hits Robin, who had her back turned, and breaks her shoulder. Identify the torts.
step: Pat acted with the purpose of causing a harmful contact with Quinn, and Quinn saw the mug coming and reasonably anticipated an imminent contact. Pat is liable to Quinn for assault, even though no contact with Quinn occurred.
step: The mug struck Robin. Pat did not intend to hit Robin, but battery is a transferred-intent tort, so Pat's intent to batter Quinn carries over to Robin.
step: Robin did not see the mug coming, so she has no assault claim; assault requires awareness of the threat. She has a battery claim for a harmful contact.
step: Conclusion: assault to Quinn, battery to Robin. The eggshell plaintiff rule makes Pat liable for the full extent of Robin's broken shoulder, even if a healthier person would have been bruised.

@mistakes
- Treating intent as intent to harm. Intent is a purpose to cause the contact, or knowledge to a substantial certainty that it will occur.
- Finding assault when the plaintiff never knew of the threat, or when the threat was merely of future harm or only conditional.
- Finding false imprisonment when the plaintiff could have left by a known, reasonable route, or was held only by moral pressure.
- Forgetting transferred intent when the wrong person is hit or the defendant meant a different tort.
