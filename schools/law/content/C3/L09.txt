@article
**Distributive justice** asks how the benefits and burdens of social cooperation, such as income, wealth, education, health care and opportunity, should be shared. Other forms of justice matter too: **corrective justice** (repairing wrongs, as in tort law), **retributive justice** (deserved punishment, studied next week) and **procedural justice** (fair process). This lesson focuses on distribution.

### The original position

The **Rawlsian** approach is a social-contract argument. Imagine people choosing the basic rules of society from an **original position**, behind a **veil of ignorance**: they do not know their class, talents, race, gender, religion or conception of the good. Because no one knows where they will end up, they must choose fair terms. The argument concludes that they would choose two principles, in priority order:

1. **Equal basic liberties** for all.
2. Social and economic inequalities are permitted only if (a) attached to positions open to all under **fair equality of opportunity**, and (b) they work to the **greatest benefit of the least advantaged** (the **difference principle**).

The reasoning for (b) is often described as **maximin**: choose the arrangement whose worst position is best. A critic replies that rational chooser might maximize **expected** or **average** well-being instead, accepting some risk of a poor position for high gains. Defenders reply that the stakes (a whole life) and the lack of probability information make caution reasonable.

### Rival views

- **Utilitarian.** Choose the distribution with the highest **total** or **average** welfare. Critics say this ignores how welfare is spread, and could sacrifice some for the many. Utilitarians reply that diminishing marginal utility (an extra dollar adds more welfare for a poor person) often supports redistribution.
- **Libertarian (entitlement theory).** Justice concerns the *history* of holdings, not the pattern. A holding is just if acquired justly, transferred voluntarily, or rectified after injustice. Taxing earnings to redistribute is, on this view, a form of forced labor or a violation of property rights. Critics ask how original acquisition could be just given unequal starting points, and whether the theory leaves many in desperate need.
- **Luck egalitarian.** Inequalities due to **brute luck** (talent, family, health) are unjust, while those arising from free choices may be acceptable. Critics say it is harsh toward people who choose badly and demeaning in its inquiry into who is responsible for need.
- **Desert.** People should receive according to merit or contribution. Critics note that talents and efforts are themselves influenced by luck, and measuring desert is difficult.
- **Sufficientarian.** What matters is that everyone has *enough*, not equality. Critics ask what counts as enough and why inequality above the threshold never matters.

### Patterned and historical

A **patterned** theory says that a distribution is just if it matches a pattern (equal, by need, by merit). A **historical** theory says that it depends on how the distribution came about. Libertarians argue that any pattern is disrupted by free exchange; patterned theorists reply that markets rest on legal rules that society chooses and so are open to justification.

### Connections to law and policy

Law constantly distributes: through taxation, property rules, contract law, anti-discrimination law and public benefits. Whether progressive taxation or a particular program is just is a contested question on which these theories differ, and this course asks you to understand the arguments, not to settle them.

@example
title: Ranking distributions by three criteria
problem: Three groups of equal size receive incomes (in thousands) under three policies. Policy A: 10, 20, 60. Policy B: 25, 30, 35. Policy C: 18, 40, 50. Which would a maximin chooser pick, and which has the highest average?
step: Find the worst-off group's income under each policy: A gives 10, B gives 25, C gives 18.
step: The maximin criterion picks the policy with the highest minimum: B (25).
step: Compute averages: A is $90/3 = 30$, B is $90/3 = 30$, and C is $108/3 = 36$.
step: C has the highest average (and total), so a utilitarian focused on income would pick C, and a maximin chooser picks B.
step: Note the lesson: the theories disagree when a gain in total comes at the cost of the worst off.
@mistakes
- Confusing the veil of ignorance with a claim that people are equal; it is a device for fair choice under ignorance of one's position.
- Treating the difference principle as requiring equality; it permits inequality that benefits the least advantaged.
- Assuming libertarian theory denies all duties of justice; it holds that justice is about rights and the history of holdings.

@practice self
prompt: Explain in about 120 words why someone choosing behind a veil of ignorance might choose the difference principle, and give one objection.
hint: Think about what a person who does not know their position would fear.
solution: Behind the veil I do not know whether I will be among the most or least advantaged. Because the choice shapes my whole life, and I lack information on probabilities, I may reason that I should make the worst position as good as possible, since I cannot risk ending up there with little. This supports the difference principle, which allows inequality only where it helps the least advantaged. An objection is that rational choosers might instead maximize average welfare or accept a floor guarantee plus higher inequality above it, since the maximin rule is extremely cautious; another is that the veil may build fairness into the premises.
