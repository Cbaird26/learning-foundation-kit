@article
**Deontology** (from the Greek word for duty) holds that some acts are right or wrong in themselves, whatever their consequences. Where consequentialism says "make things as good as possible," deontology says there are **constraints** on what you may do to people in pursuit of any goal.

### Kantian ethics

The Kantian tradition grounds morality in reason. Its central principle, the **categorical imperative**, has several formulations.

- **Universal law:** act only on a maxim (your principle of action) that you could will to be a universal law. Test a lying promise: if everyone made false promises when convenient, promising would collapse, so the maxim defeats itself.
- **Humanity:** treat humanity, in yourself and others, never merely as a means but always also as an end. You may use a taxi driver as a means of transport; you may not deceive or coerce her so that she cannot refuse.

Duties are **perfect** (strict, such as not to deceive or kill) or **imperfect** (broad, such as to help others, which leaves discretion over when and how). Kantians stress **intention** and **respect for persons**: a person has dignity, not a price.

### Rights

A **right** is a claim that others must respect. **Claim rights** correlate with duties: if Ana has a right against Ben to be paid, Ben has a duty to pay. Some rights are **negative** (not to be interfered with) and some **positive** (to be provided with something). Rights-based deontology treats rights as **side constraints**: they limit what may be done to a person even to benefit others. **Threshold deontology** says constraints hold until the stakes become catastrophic; critics ask where the threshold lies and why it is not lower.

### Doing and allowing, and double effect

Many deontologists distinguish **doing harm** from **allowing harm**, and **intending** harm from **merely foreseeing** it. The **doctrine of double effect** says that an act with a bad side effect may be permissible if (1) the act itself is not wrong, (2) the bad effect is not intended, either as an end or as a means to the good, (3) the good effect is not produced by the bad one, and (4) there is a proportionate reason. A doctor who gives pain relief that foreseeably hastens a dying patient's death is the standard example; critics doubt that intention can bear so much weight.

### Objections

- **Rigidity:** if lying is always wrong, may you not lie to a person at your door who is hunting an innocent victim? Kantians reply with strategies such as refusing to answer or limiting the duty; critics find these evasive.
- **Conflicts of duty:** the view offers little guidance when duties collide.
- **The paradox of deontology:** it seems odd to forbid one killing even where it would prevent several killings by others.

### The rescue dilemma and law

In an **agent-relative** view, you have special duties to your children, friends and clients that an impartial calculation does not recognize. Utilitarianism is **agent-neutral**. This is the key contrast in the rescue case. Notice also that the law of rescue is not the same as ethics: in most U.S. states there is no general legal duty to rescue a stranger; common exceptions include a special relationship, a peril the person created, and a rescue already begun. Moral duties are often wider than legal ones.

@example
title: Applying both theories to a rescue
problem: Dev can reach only one of two drowning groups: his own child, or five strangers. Apply a utilitarian and a deontological analysis.
step: Utilitarian: counting impartially, five lives outweigh one, so Dev should save the five; his child's identity is not a morally relevant difference.
step: Deontological (agent-relative): Dev has a special duty as parent that he does not have toward strangers; many deontologists hold that this makes it permissible, or required, to save his child.
step: Check the imperfect duty of beneficence: it does not require saving any particular stranger, so choosing his child violates no perfect duty to the five.
step: Note objections: critics of the deontological answer say it overweights personal ties when five lives are at stake; critics of the utilitarian answer say it requires detachment from one's family.
@mistakes
- Treating Kantian ethics as a version of "follow the rules"; the test is whether a maxim can be universalized and whether persons are treated as ends.
- Reading "never merely as a means" as "never as a means" at all; using someone as a means with their consent and respect is fine.
- Assuming a moral duty to rescue implies a legal duty; most U.S. jurisdictions impose none on a stranger.

prompt: This is the deliverable for weeks 5 and 6. A bystander, Eli, sees five hikers swept toward a weir and the only way to reach them is to take a neighbor's canoe without asking, which will wreck it. Apply both utilitarianism and deontology, and state where they agree or differ.
hint: For each theory state the test, then apply it to the facts, then compare.
solution: A utilitarian compares outcomes: five lives saved against the loss of a canoe, so Eli should take it. A deontologist looks at constraints and duties. The neighbor has a property right, but most deontologists, including threshold views, hold that this right is overridden when needed to save lives at small cost, and the neighbor would plausibly consent; Eli should compensate the neighbor afterward. Both therefore recommend taking the canoe, but for different reasons: welfare for the utilitarian, the weak weight of property against life and probable consent for the deontologist. They would diverge if the only means were to harm an innocent person, which a deontologist would treat as a constraint.
