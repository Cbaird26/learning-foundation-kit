step: Choose one of the two supplied questions below (or a question of similar scope approved by your tutor or instructor) and write your thesis in one sentence.
step: Choose two theories (for example consequentialism and deontology, or contractualism and virtue ethics). Write one paragraph per theory stating what it asks and what it implies for your question, using the facts in the supplied case.
step: Reconstruct your main argument as numbered premises and a conclusion. Check validity and mark each premise you expect a critic to challenge.
step: Identify the strongest objection to your thesis (from the opposite theory, a rival position or a counterexample) and state it in a form its defenders would accept (the principle of charity).
step: Draft a reply to the objection. Say whether the objection is answered fully, partly or only at a cost, and what remains uncertain.
step: Write the full paper in this order: introduction and thesis; the case and key definitions; argument with theory one; argument with theory two; the strongest objection; your reply; a short conclusion. Aim for 2,000 words.
step: Revise using the rubric: cut any sentence that does not advance the argument, define key terms on first use, and check each factual or legal statement against your readings.
material: **Question A (punishment).** A state is reviewing its sentencing law for repeat property offenses. One proposal is to lengthen sentences because studies in the state suggest that more certain detection deters more than longer sentences do, and the proposal would pair longer sentences with more patrols. Opponents say the longer sentences would exceed what offenders deserve for non-violent crimes. Supporters say that protecting the public and discouraging offenses justifies them. Question: on what basis, if any, should the state lengthen these sentences?
material: **Question B (privacy).** A city plans to install cameras at intersections that record license plates and keep the data for 90 days, accessible to police only with a warrant, for investigating serious crimes. Supporters point to solved cases and safety. Critics point to the risk of tracking lawful movements and of data misuse. Question: is the city's plan ethically justified, and if so, on what conditions?

## Review guide for week 13

### Key ideas, week by week

- **Week 1: Argument and method.** An argument has premises and a conclusion. Validity is about form (true premises cannot yield a false conclusion); soundness adds true premises. Modus ponens and modus tollens are valid; affirming the consequent and denying the antecedent are not. Use charity and number premises.
- **Week 2: Knowledge and skepticism.** Justified true belief, Gettier cases, foundationalism, coherentism and infinitism, and the closure-based skeptical argument with its Moorean, contextualist and externalist replies.
- **Week 3: Mind, free will, responsibility.** Dualism and physicalism; determinism, hard determinism, libertarianism and compatibilism; Frankfurt-style cases; voluntary acts in criminal law.
- **Week 4: Personal identity.** Numerical versus qualitative identity; soul, animalist and psychological-continuity views; circularity, transitivity and fission.
- **Week 5: Consequentialism.** Impartial welfare, expected value, act and rule versions, and objections from rights, demandingness and integrity.
- **Week 6: Deontology and rights.** Universal law and humanity formulations, side constraints, double effect, claim rights and duties, and the rescue dilemma.
- **Week 7: Virtue and care ethics.** Flourishing, the mean, practical wisdom and the action-guidance objection; attentiveness, responsibility, competence and responsiveness.
- **Week 8: Social contract.** Hobbesian, Lockean and Rousseauian accounts; contractualism; consent objections; promise-keeping.
- **Week 9: Justice.** Original position, difference principle, maximin; utilitarian, libertarian, luck-egalitarian, desert and sufficientarian views.
- **Week 10: Punishment.** Retributive, deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation, mixed and restorative views; expected sanction.
- **Week 11: Applied ethics.** Privacy, the harm principle, speech, paternalism and informed consent, with *Katz*, *Carpenter*, *Brandenburg*, *Counterman* and *Cruzan*.
- **Week 12: Professional ethics.** Role morality, the adversarial conception, Rule 1.6 confidentiality and its exceptions.

### Self-check list

- Can you reconstruct any argument from a passage in numbered form and test its validity?
- Can you state each of the four main ethical theories' central test and its best-known objection?
- Can you present both sides of a contested question fairly, without declaring a winner unless the task asks for a thesis?
- Can you compute expected values, expected sanctions and maximin choices?
- Can you explain one major idea aloud without notes?

### Most common errors

- Confusing valid with sound, or rejecting an argument because the conclusion is unwelcome.
- Treating compatibilism as a denial of determinism, or utilitarianism as majority rule.
- Stating a theory's conclusion without its reason; the reason is what distinguishes the theories.
- Presenting an objection in a weak form (a straw man).
- Treating confidentiality or any moral duty as absolute, and treating law and ethics as the same thing.

@exam num
prompt: A town considers raising the probability of conviction for a theft offense from 0.25 to 0.75 with patrols, keeping a 4-year sentence. Assume each additional expected year of sanction deters 12 offenses, each offense avoided is worth 5 welfare units, and the enforcement costs 100 units. What is the net change in welfare in units, according to a simple utilitarian calculation?
answer: 20
check: (0.75 * 4 - 0.25 * 4) * 12 * 5 - 100
hint: First compute the change in expected sanction in years.
hint: Then multiply by the offenses deterred and by 5 units each, and subtract the cost.
solution: Expected sanction rises from $0.25 \times 4 = 1$ year to $0.75 \times 4 = 3$ years, an increase of 2. That deters $2 \times 12 = 24$ offenses, worth $24 \times 5 = 120$ units. Subtracting 100 gives a net of 20 units. A retributivist would ask a different question: whether the sentence is deserved.
@exam self
prompt: Ana, a lawyer, learns that her client has been filing false expense reports that cost a charity money, but no one is in danger. In about 250 words, analyze her confidentiality dilemma with two ethical theories and the Model Rules' structure, and say how the facts differ from a case where someone faces serious bodily harm.
hint: Identify the competing duties, then the rule structure, then apply two theories.
hint: Compare the severity and nature of the harm with the physical-harm exception.
solution: Ana owes the client confidentiality under Rule 1.6(a); the charity's financial interest is on the other side. The physical-harm exception in Rule 1.6(b)(1) does not fit, since the harm is financial and no one faces death or substantial bodily harm; other exceptions, such as ones for crimes or frauds in which the lawyer's services are used, depend on her involvement and the jurisdiction's version of the rule. A consequentialist compares the financial loss with the damage to client trust and the profession, and will often favor confidentiality with efforts to persuade the client to repay. A deontologist weighs fidelity against honesty and not assisting wrongdoing, and holds that Ana must not assist the fraud (Rule 1.2(d)) but need not reveal it unless an exception applies. A virtue ethicist asks what integrity and courage require, such as counseling the client to stop. Compared with a threat of physical harm, the financial harm is less severe and less likely to justify disclosure, so confidentiality weighs more heavily.
rubric: Identifies the competing duties and the structure of Rule 1.6.
rubric: Applies two theories accurately to the facts.
rubric: Explains why financial harm differs from physical harm in the rule.
rubric: Reaches a qualified conclusion, noting the role of jurisdiction and Ana's own involvement.
