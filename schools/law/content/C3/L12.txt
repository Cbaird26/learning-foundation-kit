@article
Professionals have duties that ordinary people do not. A doctor keeps patient information confidential; an accountant protects a client's records; a lawyer advocates for a client. **Role morality** is the idea that **roles generate special obligations**, and sometimes permissions, that differ from ordinary morality. The central question is whether those role duties are genuinely moral or merely rules of the job.

### The standard conception of the lawyer's role

The traditional, **adversarial** picture of the lawyer has three principles.

- **Partisanship**: the lawyer pursues the client's lawful objectives zealously.
- **Neutrality**: the lawyer does not judge the client's goals or character.
- **Non-accountability**: the lawyer is not morally blamed for the client's lawful aims, which the Model Rules reflect by stating that representing a client does not endorse the client's views (Rule 1.2(b)).

The **justification** is mostly institutional: a system in which each side is vigorously represented before an impartial judge is the best way to find the truth, protect rights and give everyone access to the law. A consequentialist can defend the role by its overall results; a Kantian by respect for the client's autonomy and the right to be heard; a contractualist by principles that no one could reasonably reject when they might be the accused.

*Critics* raise three points. First, an **adversarial excuse** can justify wrongdoing: a lawyer who helps a client harm others hides behind the role. Second, **good lawyering and good morals** can diverge, so a lawyer's own moral integrity is at stake. Third, critics argue the system may favor those who can pay. Defenders reply that the role has limits: lawyers may not assist crime or fraud, must be candid with tribunals (Rule 3.3), and may give moral advice. Rule 2.1 allows a lawyer to refer to moral, economic, social and political considerations in giving candid advice.

### Confidentiality

Confidentiality supports the **trust** that makes professional help possible: if clients fear disclosure, they will withhold facts and receive worse advice, so the duty serves both clients and the justice system. Under Model Rule 1.6(a), a lawyer must not reveal information relating to the representation unless the client gives informed consent, the disclosure is impliedly authorized to carry out the representation, or an exception applies. The duty is broader than the evidentiary attorney–client privilege.

The Model Rules also allow disclosure in listed circumstances. For example, Rule 1.6(b)(1) permits a lawyer to reveal information to the extent reasonably necessary to prevent reasonably certain death or substantial bodily harm. Other exceptions concern crimes or frauds that use the lawyer's services, securing legal advice about compliance, and complying with law or court orders. States adopt their own versions, and some are broader or narrower; some require, not just permit, disclosure in certain cases. Medical professionals face similar tensions: courts in some states, following *Tarasoff v. Regents of the University of California* (1976), recognize a duty for therapists to take reasonable steps to protect an identifiable person from a patient's serious threat, and the details differ among jurisdictions.

### Analyzing a dilemma with the theories

1. **Consequentialist**: compare the harm prevented by disclosure with the harm to trust in the profession if confidences are broken. Rule consequentialism favors a general practice of confidentiality with narrow exceptions.
2. **Deontological**: weigh the duty of fidelity to the client against the duty not to let others be seriously harmed; consider whether the client's information may be used as a mere means.
3. **Virtue ethics**: ask what integrity, courage, honesty and compassion require, and what a person of practical wisdom would do.
4. **Care ethics**: attend to relationships with the client and those at risk, and look for responses that preserve both, such as persuading the client to act.
5. **Rules and law**: identify the governing rule and whether disclosure is permitted or required, and disclose no more than is necessary.

### Method

A strong analysis (a) states the facts and the competing duties, (b) identifies the applicable professional rule, (c) applies at least two theories, (d) notes where they agree or differ, and (e) reaches a reasoned, qualified conclusion. It is a pattern you will use throughout law school, where ethics is both a body of rules and an area of examination.

@example
title: Analyzing a confidentiality dilemma
problem: A client tells his lawyer in confidence that he plans to set fire to a building where people live next week. The lawyer is unsure if he is serious. Analyze the lawyer's dilemma.
step: Facts and duties: a duty of confidentiality to the client; the interest of potential victims in not being killed or harmed.
step: Rule: Model Rule 1.6(b)(1) permits disclosure to the extent reasonably necessary to prevent reasonably certain death or substantial bodily harm. The lawyer must assess whether harm is "reasonably certain."
step: Consequentialist: the possible loss of life weighs heavily against the cost to trust if the threat is credible; if it is a joke, disclosure would cost trust for little gain.
step: Deontological and virtue views: fidelity to the client is a real duty, but it does not require helping to bring about grave harm to innocents; practical wisdom suggests first trying to talk the client out of it, and disclosing only what is needed.
step: Conclusion: if the lawyer reasonably believes the threat is real, the rule permits disclosure, and several theories support it; if not, he should seek to dissuade the client and consult ethics counsel.
@mistakes
- Treating confidentiality as absolute; the Model Rules list exceptions.
- Confusing the ethical duty of confidentiality with the evidentiary attorney–client privilege, which is narrower.
- Using a role as a complete excuse: role morality gives special duties but does not remove all ordinary moral limits.

hint: Follow the five-part method from the article: facts, rule, two theories, comparison, qualified conclusion.
solution: Eli owes the client confidentiality under Model Rule 1.6(a), which supports candid communication. Third parties face possible serious injury. The key rule question is whether disclosure is permitted: Rule 1.6(b)(1) allows disclosure to prevent reasonably certain death or substantial bodily harm, and other exceptions may apply if Eli's services were used in a fraud; the answer depends on how likely and how severe the injury is and on the jurisdiction's version of the rule. A consequentialist weighs the injuries to buyers against the damage to client trust, and may favor disclosure if the danger is serious and likely, and the rule is limited. A deontologist weighs fidelity against the duty not to allow grave harm to innocents; the client's information may not be used as a means to injure others. Both theories favor first urging the client to correct the defect and disclosing only what is reasonably necessary if that fails and the harm is reasonably certain. If the harm is only financial or speculative, the rules and theories lean toward continued confidentiality.
