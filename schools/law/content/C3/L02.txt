@article
**Epistemology** asks what knowledge is, how we get it, and whether we have any. You use the word constantly ("I know the exam is Friday"), and philosophy asks you to say precisely what you mean.

### Knowledge as justified true belief

The traditional analysis says that you **know** that $p$ when three conditions hold:

1. **Truth**: $p$ is true. You cannot know what is false.
2. **Belief**: you believe $p$.
3. **Justification**: you have good reasons for believing $p$.

Each condition is plausible. A lucky guess that happens to be right is true belief without justification, and most people deny it is knowledge.

### Gettier cases

A **Gettier case** is a situation with justified true belief that does not look like knowledge. Suppose Ana looks at a clock that reads 3:00 and forms the belief that it is 3:00. The clock stopped yesterday, but Ana happens to look at exactly 3:00. Her belief is true, and she was justified in trusting a clock, yet she does not know. Her truth is a matter of luck. Gettier cases show that the three conditions are not **sufficient**.

Proposed repairs include adding a "no false lemmas" clause (the belief must not rest on a false intermediate belief), **reliabilism** (the belief must come from a reliable process), and **safety** (the belief could not easily have been false). Each faces counterexamples, and many philosophers now treat knowledge as a basic notion rather than an analysis of parts.

### Sources and the regress

**Rationalists** hold that some knowledge, such as arithmetic, comes from reason independently of experience; **empiricists** hold that all substantive knowledge comes from experience.

Whatever the source, a **regress of justification** threatens. If belief A is justified by B, and B by C, where does the chain end? Three answers:

- **Foundationalism**: some beliefs are basic and justified without needing support from other beliefs (for example, perceptual or self-evident beliefs); the rest rest on them.
- **Coherentism**: beliefs are justified by fitting together in a mutually supporting web.
- **Infinitism**: the chain of reasons is infinite, and justification is a matter of how far reasons are available on demand.

### Skepticism

**Skepticism** holds that we lack knowledge in some area. The classic **global** version uses a radical possibility: you might be dreaming, deceived by an evil demon, or a brain in a vat fed experiences. Reconstructed:

1. If I know that I am reading this page, then I know that I am not a brain in a vat.
2. I do not know that I am not a brain in a vat.
3. Therefore, I do not know that I am reading this page.

The argument is valid (modus tollens on the closure principle that knowledge extends across known implications). Replies target the premises. A **Moorean** reply says I am more certain that I am reading than that premise 2 is true, so I run the argument backwards. A **contextualist** reply says "know" is context-sensitive: the standards in a philosophy seminar are higher than in daily life. An **externalist** reply says knowledge depends on facts about reliability that I need not be able to prove.

Do not confuse the skeptic with a cynic. The skeptic's argument is a puzzle that tests our theories of justification, and a good answer must say which premise fails and why.

@example
title: Reconstructing the dreaming argument
problem: Reconstruct: "I cannot tell by any feature of experience whether I am awake. So I cannot know that I am awake. If I cannot know that I am awake, I cannot know that the desk in front of me exists. So I cannot know the desk exists."
step: Identify the final conclusion: C. I cannot know that the desk exists.
step: Number the premises in order: P1. No feature of experience tells me whether I am awake. P2. If P1, then I cannot know that I am awake. P3. If I cannot know that I am awake, then I cannot know the desk exists.
step: Add the intermediate conclusion: from P1 and P2, I cannot know that I am awake.
step: Test the form: two uses of modus ponens, so the argument is valid.
step: Locate the weak points: a critic can attack P1 (some features, such as coherence over time, may distinguish waking) or P3 (a closure-style premise).
@mistakes
- Treating the Gettier problem as showing the three conditions are unnecessary rather than insufficient.
- Reading skepticism as the claim that nothing is true; it is a claim about knowledge or justification.
- Replying to the brain-in-a-vat argument by saying "that is silly" without identifying which premise is false.
