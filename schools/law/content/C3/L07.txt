@article
Consequentialism and deontology ask, "What should I do?" and answer with a principle about acts. **Virtue ethics** changes the question: "What kind of person should I be?" Right action is what a **virtuous person** would characteristically do in the circumstances.

### Virtue ethics

The Aristotelian tradition is built on a few ideas.

- **Eudaimonia** (flourishing, a good human life) is the final end. It is not a feeling but a life lived well, in accordance with excellence.
- A **virtue** is a stable trait of character, such as courage, honesty or justice, acquired by **habituation**: we become brave by doing brave acts.
- The **doctrine of the mean** holds that each virtue lies between an excess and a deficiency. Courage lies between rashness and cowardice; generosity lies between wastefulness and stinginess. The mean is relative to the situation and the person, not a fixed arithmetic midpoint.
- **Practical wisdom** is the skill of seeing what a situation requires and acting well. It is what unifies the virtues and what rules cannot replace.

A virtue ethicist evaluates not only acts but **motives, emotions and perception**. Someone who returns a lost wallet only to avoid being seen acts correctly but not virtuously.

### Objections to virtue ethics

- **Action-guidance.** "Do what the virtuous person would do" seems circular or unhelpful for a person who does not know what that is. *Reply:* virtue theory offers rules of thumb, the "v-rules" (be honest, avoid cruelty), and models of exemplars.
- **Cultural variation.** Societies disagree about the virtues. *Reply:* there may be a core set grounded in human nature and shared needs, while details differ.
- **Situationism.** Experiments suggest that behavior depends heavily on circumstance, so stable character traits may be rare. *Reply:* the theory describes an ideal, and the research may show only that few people achieve it.
- **Conflict.** Honesty and kindness can conflict; practical wisdom is said to resolve it, but critics find that unsatisfying.

### Care ethics

**Care ethics** begins from the fact that people are **dependent** and live in **relationships** that carry particular responsibilities. It stresses attention to the needs of particular others, rather than impartial rules, and often challenges the picture of the moral agent as an independent, self-sufficient chooser. Care involves four commonly listed elements: **attentiveness** (noticing need), **responsibility** (taking it on), **competence** (doing the caring well) and **responsiveness** (checking how the recipient actually experiences it).

Critics raise **parochialism** (do we neglect distant strangers?), **paternalism** (does the carer decide what the cared-for needs?) and the risk of **exploitation** (care work has often fallen on women and been undervalued). Defenders say care is compatible with justice and corrects its blind spots, while some argue that care supplies a complete alternative to the justice-focused theories.

### Comparing the four theories

| Theory | Central question | Typical strength | Typical worry |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consequentialism | Which act produces the best outcome? | Explains why welfare matters | Can override individuals |
| Deontology | Which acts respect duties and rights? | Protects persons | Can be rigid |
| Virtue ethics | What would a person of good character do? | Attends to motive and development | Weak action guidance |
| Care ethics | What do these relationships require? | Attends to dependence and particulars | Partiality, paternalism |

These theories often agree in ordinary cases, and the **disagreements** show where each theory's special commitments lie. This is the method used in the second half of the course.

@example
title: Four questions about one case
problem: A manager notices that a colleague's report, which the manager helped prepare, contains a serious error that no one else has found. Frame the case under each of the four theories.
step: Consequentialist: Which course, correcting or staying silent, leads to the best overall results for the team, clients and the organization, counting everyone's welfare?
step: Deontologist: Does the manager have a duty of honesty to those who rely on the report, and would staying silent treat clients merely as means?
step: Virtue ethicist: What would honesty, courage and fairness require, and what would the manager's silence do to her character?
step: Care ethicist: What does the relationship with the colleague require, such as raising the error privately and supportively, while attending to clients who depend on the report?
step: Compare: all four likely favor correcting the error, but they differ on how and why, especially on the support owed to the colleague.
@mistakes
- Treating the doctrine of the mean as "moderation in everything"; some acts, such as cruelty, have no mean.
- Saying virtue ethics ignores action; it holds that right action flows from character, and it still gives guidance.
- Treating care ethics as mere kindness; it is a theory about responsibility within relationships.

hint: For each theory, name its central question and what feature of the case it highlights.
solution: A virtue ethicist asks what honesty, courage, compassion and justice require of the nurse and what response a person of practical wisdom would choose; the focus is the nurse's character and perception. The care ethicist focuses on the relationship: the patient's dependence, the family member's responsibilities and pressures, and the nurse's responsibility to attend and respond. The care ethicist would likely ask how the patient actually experiences the care and how to support the family member while protecting the patient. Both would reject ignoring the problem, but the virtue ethicist stresses the agent's excellence and the care ethicist the web of relationships.
