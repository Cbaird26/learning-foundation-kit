@article
Two questions sit under every theory of blame. What kind of thing is a mind? And can we ever be responsible for what we do, given that everything seems to have causes?

### Mind and body

**Substance dualism** says that mind and body are two different kinds of thing: the mind is non-physical. Its attraction is that conscious experience seems unlike anything in physics. Its main difficulty is the **interaction problem**: how can a non-physical mind cause an arm to move without breaking the pattern of physical causes? Dualism also struggles with evidence that brain damage changes thought and personality.

**Physicalism** holds that mental states are physical states. The **identity theory** says a pain simply is a type of brain state. **Functionalism** says a mental state is defined by its causal role (what causes it and what it causes), so in principle a different physical system could have the same mental states. The standard objection is the **hard problem of consciousness**: even a full physical or functional account may leave out *what it is like* to feel pain.

### Determinism and free will

**Determinism** is the thesis that every event is fully caused by earlier events together with the laws of nature. Three main positions follow:

| Position | Determinism true? | Free will compatible with determinism? | Free will? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hard determinism | yes | no | no |
| Libertarianism (the metaphysical view, not the political one) | no | no | yes |
| Compatibilism | may be | yes | yes |

**Incompatibilists** argue: if determinism is true, our acts follow from the past and the laws, over which we have no control; so we have no control over our acts. **Compatibilists** reply that free will is acting on your own desires and reasons, without coercion or compulsion; being caused is not the same as being forced. Libertarians face the question of how undetermined events could be *controlled* rather than random.

### Responsibility

Many people accept the **principle of alternate possibilities**: you are morally responsible for an act only if you could have done otherwise. A **Frankfurt-style case** challenges it. Suppose Ben decides on his own to take a bribe, and a hidden device would have forced the same choice if he had wavered; it never activates. Ben could not have done otherwise, yet he seems responsible. If so, responsibility depends on the actual source of the act, not on alternatives.

A related view, associated with the **reactive attitudes**, says that blame, resentment and gratitude are part of human life; to ask whether they are justified, we should ask when they are appropriate, such as excusing an act done by accident or under duress. A skeptic about free will replies that these attitudes may be unjustified and that we should replace blame with forward-looking responses such as prevention and repair.

### A note on law

Criminal law requires a **voluntary act** and, for most serious crimes, a culpable mental state (*mens rea*). Under the Model Penal Code, reflexes, convulsions and movements during sleep are not voluntary acts. This suggests the law treats responsibility as dependent on how an act came about, which fits the compatibilist idea of reasons-responsiveness, though the law itself does not take a stand on metaphysics. Excuses such as duress and insanity mark conditions under which ordinary blame is withdrawn.

title: Classifying a view on free will
problem: A speaker says: "Every choice I make was caused by my character and circumstances. But when I choose without threats or compulsion, I am free, and I deserve credit and blame." Classify the view.

step: Match to the table: determinism may be true and free will exists. That is compatibilism.
step: Note the objection: an incompatibilist asks whether causes outside my control, such as my upbringing, undermine ownership of my character.
@mistakes
- Treating compatibilism as the denial of determinism; it accepts that determinism may be true.
- Confusing metaphysical libertarianism with the political ideology of the same name.
- Thinking that a Frankfurt-style case shows that Ben had a choice; it is meant to show he was responsible without one.

@practice self
prompt: In about 120 words, explain the difference between a hard determinist and a compatibilist, using one concrete example of a choice.
hint: Say what each view claims about determinism, then what each concludes about responsibility.
solution: Both may accept that every choice has prior causes. The hard determinist infers that nobody has the kind of free will needed for moral responsibility, because we do not control the causes. The compatibilist denies that inference. Take a student who chooses to study tonight. The hard determinist says that her character and past fixed the choice, so she is not ultimately responsible. The compatibilist says that she acted freely: she chose according to her own reasons, with no coercion or compulsion, and would have chosen differently had her reasons been different. The dispute is over what "free" must mean for responsibility.
