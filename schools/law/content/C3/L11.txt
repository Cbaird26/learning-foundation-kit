@article
**Applied ethics** takes general theories and asks what they imply for concrete questions. This lesson looks at three: privacy, speech and autonomy. On each, the aim is to understand the strongest arguments on the main sides, since reasonable people disagree.

### Privacy

**Privacy** has been understood as **control** over information about oneself, as **limited access** to oneself, and as **appropriate flow of information** within context (the contextual-integrity view: medical details flow properly from patient to doctor but not to an employer). Arguments for its value include **autonomy** (people need space to think and experiment), **dignity**, **intimacy** and relationships, and **protection against abuse** of power.

Arguments for surveillance appeal to **security** and **efficiency**: detecting crime, preventing harm, and verifying behavior. Opponents reply that surveillance can chill lawful activity, that data can be misused or leaked, and that "nothing to hide" misunderstands the value of privacy, which is not only concealing wrongdoing. Proponents respond that safeguards, warrants and oversight can limit abuse.

In U.S. constitutional law, the Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable searches. Under *Katz v. United States* (1967), a search occurs when the government intrudes on a person's reasonable expectation of privacy. In *Carpenter v. United States* (2018), the Court held that obtaining a person's historical cell-site location records is a search, ordinarily requiring a warrant, even though a third party holds the data. And in *Chatrie v. United States* (2026), the Court held that obtaining a person's location data from Google with a geofence warrant is a Fourth Amendment search. These cases show law grappling with the same questions of control and appropriate use.

### Speech

The **harm principle** says that power may rightly be used against a person's will only to prevent harm to others, not merely for the person's own good. For speech, the question is which speech can be restricted. Main justifications for protecting speech:

- **Truth** (the "marketplace of ideas"): open debate helps expose error. Critics note that falsehood and manipulation can spread.
- **Autonomy**: people have a right to form and share their views.
- **Democracy**: self-government needs open discussion of public affairs.
- **Tolerance and distrust of authority**: officials cannot be trusted to decide which ideas are acceptable.

Arguments for **limits** appeal to harm: incitement, true threats, defamation and harassment can injure. Others argue that **offense** or hate speech harms dignity and equality; opponents answer that offense alone is too vague a standard and that restrictions are prone to abuse. In law, *Brandenburg v. Ohio* (1969) protects advocacy of force or lawbreaking unless it is directed to inciting imminent lawless action and is likely to produce it, and *Counterman v. Colorado* (2023) requires at least recklessness for a true-threats prosecution.

### Autonomy and paternalism

**Autonomy** is self-rule: choosing according to one's own values. **Paternalism** interferes with a person's choices for their own good. **Hard** paternalism overrides competent choices (a ban on a risky activity); **soft** paternalism intervenes only when the choice is not fully voluntary or informed (stopping a person from crossing a bridge they do not know is unsafe). Defenders of paternalism point to cognitive bias and limited information; critics say that it disrespects persons and that officials have no special knowledge of what is good for individuals.

In medicine, **informed consent** requires that the patient be competent, informed and acting voluntarily. In *Cruzan v. Director, Missouri Department of Health* (1990), the Court assumed a competent person has a constitutionally protected interest in refusing unwanted medical treatment, and held that a state may require clear and convincing evidence of an incompetent patient's wishes before withdrawing treatment.

### Applying the theories

A consequentialist weighs benefits and harms of surveillance, speech limits or paternalism. A Kantian stresses respect for persons and consent. A virtue ethicist asks what honesty, respect and prudence require. A care ethicist attends to dependence and trust. Each gives different weights, which explains why the debates continue.

@example
title: Analyzing workplace monitoring of email
problem: An employer proposes to read all employee email sent from work accounts to prevent leaks. Identify the main arguments on each side.
step: State the interests: the employer's interest in security and property; employees' interests in privacy, autonomy and trust.
step: For monitoring: security, accountability, and the fact that the accounts belong to the employer; clear notice reduces unfairness.
step: Against monitoring: chilling effect, loss of trust, intrusion into personal communication, and less intrusive alternatives (monitoring only flagged content).
step: Apply theories: a consequentialist compares the harms of leaks to the harms of lost trust; a Kantian asks whether employees are treated with respect and informed; a care ethicist notes the effect on relationships.
step: Conclude with conditions, such as notice and proportionality, that different theories could accept without settling every disagreement.
@mistakes
- Treating the "nothing to hide" argument as settled; critics say privacy protects autonomy and guards against misuse, not only concealment.
- Equating the harm principle with a ban on all speech that causes any offense; the principle requires harm to others.
- Treating soft paternalism (interfering to secure voluntary choice) as the same as hard paternalism.
