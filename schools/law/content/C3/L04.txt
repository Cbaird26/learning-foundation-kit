@article
What makes you the same person as the child in your oldest photograph? Your body has changed, your beliefs have changed, and most of your cells have been replaced. Yet you may be held to a promise you made or blamed for a deed you did years ago. The question of **personal identity** asks what makes a person at one time the same person as a person at another.

### Two kinds of identity

**Qualitative identity** means exact similarity: two identical twins, or two new coins of the same mint, are qualitatively identical. **Numerical identity** means being one and the same thing: you now and you at age five are numerically one person though qualitatively different. Numerical identity is **transitive** (if $A=B$ and $B=C$ then $A=C$) and obeys the indiscernibility principle (identical things share all properties).

### Main views

- **The soul view**: you are an immaterial soul that continues; the same soul, the same person. It faces the evidence problems of dualism, and it gives no observable test for who is who.
- **The bodily (animalist) view**: you are a living human organism; you continue as long as the biological life continues. It handles infancy and dementia well but has trouble with thought experiments in which a brain moves to a new body.
- **The psychological-continuity view**: you continue as long as there is an overlapping chain of memories, intentions, beliefs and character. Memory alone was the earliest version.

### Objections to the memory view

1. **Circularity.** Genuine memory is itself defined as remembering your own experiences, so memory presupposes identity rather than explaining it. The reply is to use *quasi-memory*, an experiential link defined without reference to identity.
2. **Transitivity.** A person may remember being a child, and an old person may remember being that adult, but not the child. The old person is the same as the adult, and the adult is the same as the child, so memory must be a *chain* of overlapping links, not a direct connection.
3. **Fission.** Imagine your brain is divided and each half is transplanted into a new body, and each resulting person has your memories and character. Both have the same continuity with you, and they are not identical to each other. Since $A=B$ and $A=C$ would force $B=C$, you cannot be identical to both. Some conclude that you are identical to neither, to one arbitrarily, or that identity is not what matters in survival.

### Why identity matters to law and ethics

Responsibility seems to need a **same-person link**: we blame the defendant for the act of the person who did it. Amnesia raises a puzzle. If psychological continuity is lost, is the later person responsible? Most legal systems hold that a defendant with amnesia may be tried, since the body and the legal person continue; philosophers who stress psychology can reply that what matters for blame is continuity of character and intention, which usually remain. Property, contracts, wills and promises all assume that one person persists.

### Does blame need free will?

This lesson's deliverable asks you to take a position on a question linking weeks 3 and 4: **is free will needed for blame?** Consider the options. A hard determinist says blame is unjustified because no one controls their causes; one version replaces blame with prevention and repair. A compatibilist says blame is fitting when an agent acts from reasons-responsive character. A libertarian says blame needs the power to do otherwise. A good paper states a thesis, gives an argument, and answers the strongest objection to it fairly.

@example
title: Testing the memory view against fission
problem: Person $P$'s brain is divided and transplanted into two bodies, producing $Q$ and $R$, each with $P$'s full memories. Can the memory view say that $P=Q$ and $P=R$?
step: Suppose $P=Q$ and $P=R$.
step: Identity is transitive and symmetric, so $Q=R$.
step: But $Q$ and $R$ are two people living separate lives from this point, so $Q \neq R$.
step: The supposition is contradictory. So $P$ is identical to at most one of them, and the memory view, which gives them equal claims, cannot choose.
step: Options: deny identity to both, choose arbitrarily, or say what matters is continuity rather than identity.
@mistakes
- Using "identical" loosely: twins are qualitatively but not numerically identical.
- Thinking the fission case shows that identical persons can be two; it shows the opposite, since identity cannot hold with both.
- Writing a position paper that only lists views without choosing one and answering an objection.

@practice self
prompt: Deliverable for weeks 3 and 4. In about 200 words, write a short paper on this question: is free will needed for blame? State a thesis, give your main argument, and answer the strongest objection fairly.
hint: Pick one view from week 3, state your thesis in one sentence, then write the best objection to it.
solution: One defensible line: free will in the sense of ultimate, uncaused choice is not needed for blame, but control is. Blame responds to what a person's act expresses about her reasons and attitudes toward others. A person who acts from her own reasons, can understand why the act was wrong, and could have been moved by those reasons to act otherwise is a fitting target for blame; a person who acts under compulsion or without understanding is not. The strongest objection is the incompatibilist argument that the causes of one's character lie outside one's control, so no one deserves blame. A reply is that this proves too much: it would make praise and all reactive attitudes unfitting, and the practice of holding people to standards also serves ends such as protecting trust. A hard determinist may still think the reply fails; a fair paper notes the cost of each view.
