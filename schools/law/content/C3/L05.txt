@article
**Normative ethics** asks what makes actions right or wrong. **Consequentialism** gives a simple answer: the rightness of an act depends only on its consequences. The best-known version is **utilitarianism**: an act is right when it produces at least as much overall well-being as any alternative, counting everyone's well-being **impartially** (a stranger's welfare counts as much as your own).

### The core idea

Three ingredients define the view.

1. **Consequentialism**: only outcomes matter.
2. **Welfare**: the good to be promoted is well-being. *Hedonistic* utilitarianism counts pleasure and pain; *preference* utilitarianism counts satisfied preferences.
3. **Aggregation and impartiality**: add up the welfare of all affected, with equal weight for each person.

Because outcomes are uncertain, a practical version compares **expected** welfare: weight each outcome by its probability. If option A saves 4 lives for certain and option B has a 50% chance of saving 10, B has an expected value of $0.5 \times 10 = 5$ lives, so B wins on expectation, though some will feel B is riskier.

### Act and rule versions

**Act utilitarianism** applies the principle to each individual act. **Rule utilitarianism** asks which *rules*, if generally followed or accepted, would produce the best outcomes, and calls an act right when it conforms to those rules. The rule view can defend promise-keeping and honesty even in a case where one lie would help, since a practice of lying would erode trust. Critics reply that if the rule's only justification is its good outcomes, it should be broken when breaking is better (the charge of **rule worship**); defenders respond with more refined rule sets.

### Objections and replies

- **Rights and justice.** Suppose punishing an innocent person would calm a riot and prevent several deaths. Simple act utilitarianism seems to permit it. *Reply:* in realistic cases, secret injustice has large long-run costs; rule utilitarians add that rules protecting individuals do better overall. *Counter:* these replies make the view depend on contingent facts.
- **Demandingness.** If every dollar not spent on yourself could relieve someone else's suffering, morality seems to require near-total sacrifice. *Reply:* accept the demand, or use a *satisficing* or rule-based version that is less demanding.
- **Integrity.** The view treats my own projects and relationships as just another source of welfare, which seems to alienate me from my own commitments.
- **Measurement.** Comparing welfare across people is hard, and adding up small benefits across many people against a severe harm to one is controversial.

### Strengths

Consequentialism explains why ethics seems to be about *making things better*, why doing nothing can be a choice with consequences, and why moral rules seem to have a point. Law and policy use this style of reasoning in **cost–benefit analysis**, although most legal systems also protect individual rights that resist simple aggregation.

### The trolley problem

In the **switch case**, a runaway trolley will kill five unless you divert it to a track where it will kill one. A utilitarian says divert: four more lives are saved. In the **footbridge case**, you can stop the trolley only by pushing a large person off a bridge. A utilitarian says the arithmetic is the same, yet many people judge that pushing is wrong. Explaining the difference is a central task for rival theories, which you will study next.

@example
title: Comparing options by expected welfare
problem: A clinic can fund Program X, which gives 30 patients 4 extra healthy days each for certain, or Program Y, which has a 40% chance of giving 20 patients 12 extra healthy days each and otherwise nothing. Which has the higher expected benefit?
step: Compute X: $30 \times 4 = 120$ healthy days.
step: Compute the payoff of Y if it works: $20 \times 12 = 240$ healthy days.
step: Weight by probability: $0.4 \times 240 = 96$ healthy days.
step: Compare: $120 > 96$, so X has the higher expected benefit.
step: Note what this ignores: a rights-based critic may care about who receives the benefit, and risk-averse reasoning may favor the certain option even more.
@mistakes
- Treating utilitarianism as "the majority wins"; it adds up welfare, not votes, and a small group can lose a great deal.
- Confusing consequentialism (the theory of right action) with egoism (maximizing only your own welfare).
- Forgetting the probability weight when an outcome is uncertain.
