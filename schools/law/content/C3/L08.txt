@article
Why should you obey the government? Why is political power legitimate? **Social contract theory** answers: because the governed would agree, or have agreed, to the terms. Authority is justified by **consent**, real or hypothetical, rather than by tradition, force or divine right.

### The state of nature

The tradition begins with a thought experiment: imagine people **without government**, the **state of nature**, and ask what they would choose.

- **Hobbesian view.** The state of nature is a "war of all against all": with scarcity and mutual fear, life is insecure, and each has reason to strike first. The contract creates a strong sovereign to keep peace. The reasoning has the form of a **prisoner's dilemma**: each does better by defecting whatever others do, yet all are worse off when all defect.
- **Lockean view.** People have **natural rights** to life, liberty and property in the state of nature, governed by a law of nature. They form government to protect those rights better through impartial judges. Government is **limited** and is held in trust: if it violates rights, the people may replace it.
- **Rousseauian view.** The contract forms a community guided by the **general will**, the common good as citizens deliberate about it; each person obeys laws they have a part in making.

Founding documents echo this tradition. The Declaration of Independence refers to governments "deriving their just powers from the consent of the governed."

### Contractualism in ethics

**Contractualism** extends the idea to morality: an act is wrong if it is forbidden by principles that no one could **reasonably reject** as a basis for general agreement. The test asks each affected person whether they could reasonably reject a principle, which gives weight to individual claims and to **justifiability to each person**, not to totals. It explains why we owe reasons to those affected and why aggregate benefit does not outweigh a serious burden on one.

### Objections

- **Consent is missing.** Few have agreed to anything. **Tacit consent** (residence, using public services) is a weak substitute, since you cannot easily leave. **Hypothetical consent** asks what you *would* agree to, but a hypothetical contract is arguably no contract at all; its force must come from the fairness of the procedure.
- **Exclusion.** Historical versions excluded women, enslaved people and the poor. Modern versions must say who is a party and how those who cannot take part (children, people with severe cognitive disability, animals, future generations) are protected.
- **Historical doubt.** The state of nature never existed. Defenders say that the idea is a device for testing justification, not a claim of history.

### Promises

A **promise** creates a moral obligation by giving another person a reason to rely on you. Philosophers differ on why. A **consequentialist** may say the practice of promising creates trust and coordination. A **Kantian** says that breaking a promise treats the promisee merely as a means or cannot be universalized. A **contractualist** says a principle allowing broken promises whenever convenient could be reasonably rejected by those who rely on promises. A **virtue ethicist** appeals to fidelity and integrity. The law enforces only some promises: a contract generally requires an agreement and consideration, so many moral promises are not legally binding, and some legal duties exist without promises.

@example
title: Comparing two theories on a promise
problem: Maya promised to help Omar move on Saturday. That morning she is offered a chance to attend a conference talk that would help her career. Compare consequentialist and Kantian analyses.
step: State the facts that matter: the promise, Omar's reliance (he arranged a truck and has no other helper), and Maya's gain.
step: Consequentialist: compare total welfare. Omar's lost time and trust in Maya's word and the effect on the promising practice against Maya's gain. If Omar can hire help cheaply, the balance may favor the talk; if not, it favors helping.
step: Kantian: can Maya will the maxim "break promises when something better comes up"? It cannot be universalized, and it uses Omar as a means, so she must keep the promise unless Omar releases her.
step: Compare: the consequentialist answer depends on details; the Kantian answer is firmer. A critic of the Kantian view asks whether any promise may yield to a heavy competing duty.
@mistakes
- Treating hypothetical consent as if it were actual consent: a hypothetical agreement has force only through the fairness of its terms.
- Saying that the state of nature is meant as a historical claim.
- Assuming that every moral promise is a legal contract, or the reverse.
