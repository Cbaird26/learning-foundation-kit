@article
Punishment deliberately imposes a burden on someone because of an offense. That is morally unusual: normally, harming people is wrong. So punishment needs a **justification**. Philosophers distinguish three questions: **why punish at all** (the general justifying aim), **whom** may we punish (who is eligible), and **how much** (proportion). This lesson sets out the main views and the strongest objections to each. These are contested questions on which thoughtful people disagree, and the goal is to understand the arguments.

### Retributivism

**Retributivism** says that punishment is justified because wrongdoers *deserve* it. It is **backward-looking**: the justification is the offense, not future benefit. Core ideas:

- **Desert.** The guilty deserve a response proportioned to the wrong and the offender's culpability.
- **Proportionality.** The punishment should fit the gravity of the crime (the old idea of "an eye for an eye" is sometimes read as a proportionality principle).
- **Limits.** Only the guilty may be punished, and not beyond what they deserve.
- **Expressive version.** Punishment communicates society's condemnation and affirms the victim's worth.

*Objections.* (1) Why is it good to add suffering to a wrong already done? Critics call desert an unexplained intuition, or disguised vengeance. (2) Fair desert is hard to measure, and social disadvantage may affect culpability. (3) If free will is doubtful (week 3), desert may be unfounded. Defenders reply that desert is a deeply shared judgment, that it respects offenders as responsible agents, and that it limits punishment.

### The utilitarian approach

A **utilitarian** (consequentialist) view justifies punishment only by its **benefits**, because punishment itself is an evil (it causes suffering). It is **forward-looking** and gives these aims:

- **General deterrence**: threat of punishment discourages others.
- **Specific deterrence**: punishment discourages the offender from re-offending.
- **Incapacitation**: confinement prevents further crimes during the sentence.
- **Rehabilitation**: treatment and education reduce future offending.

A deterrence argument uses **expected sanction**: the probability of being caught and convicted times the severity. If the chance of conviction is 0.25 and the sentence is 8 years, the expected sanction is $0.25 \times 8 = 2$ years. Evidence in practice often suggests that the *certainty* of punishment deters more than its severity, though findings vary by offense.

*Objections.* (1) **Punishing the innocent**: if a false conviction would deter, the view seems to permit it. Replies: it would be exposed, destroy trust, and a rule against it has better results. (2) **Excessive punishment**: deterrence might justify severe penalties for minor crimes if they are very effective. (3) **Using persons as means**: it treats the offender as a tool for social ends. (4) **Empirical uncertainty**: whether deterrence works often depends on information that people lack.

### Mixed and other views

**Mixed (hybrid) theories** use retribution to set the *limits* (only the guilty, nothing disproportionate) and consequences to choose within them. This is sometimes called **limiting retributivism**. In law, legislatures and courts typically list several purposes together: the Model Penal Code includes deterrence, correction and rehabilitation, and protection against excessive or arbitrary punishment, and in *Gregg v. Georgia* (1976), the Supreme Court recognized retribution and deterrence as legitimate purposes of punishment in upholding a capital sentencing scheme.

**Restorative justice** focuses on repairing harm: the offender acknowledges responsibility, the victim is heard, and the community participates in repair. Critics ask whether it can adequately address serious crimes and whether it expresses sufficient condemnation. Proponents see it as complementing or partly replacing punishment. Some **abolitionist** and **communicative** views ask whether punishment is justified at all or whether it should aim to engage offenders in moral reflection.

### Putting the views side by side

Retributivism and utilitarianism can agree on a sentence while disagreeing about *why*, and they diverge in test cases: a very effective deterrent penalty for a minor crime, a guilty person who poses no future risk, or a framed scapegoat.

@example
title: Comparing two views on a sentence
problem: A town considers raising the penalty for repeated shoplifting. Analyze the proposal using a retributive and a utilitarian lens, using a deterrence calculation: currently the chance of conviction is 0.2 and the penalty is 10 days in jail; the proposal would raise the chance of conviction to 0.4 through more patrols, leaving the penalty at 10 days.
step: Retributive question: is the penalty proportionate to the wrong and the culpability of repeat shoplifting? Nothing in the proposal makes it excessive, so a retributivist would focus on proportion, not on effects.
step: Utilitarian calculation: current expected sanction is $0.2 \times 10 = 2$ days. The proposal gives $0.4 \times 10 = 4$ days.
step: The utilitarian then asks if the extra deterrence justifies the cost of patrols and the burden on those convicted.
step: Compare: the retributivist asks what each offender deserves; the utilitarian asks what policy produces the best overall results.
@mistakes
- Equating retributivism with vengeance or severity; a retributivist insists on proportion and denies punishing more than is deserved.
- Assuming a utilitarian approves of any punishment that deters; it counts the suffering of punishment as a cost.
- Treating the mixed view as proof that the debate is settled; it still has to explain how to weigh the aims.
