@article
A function is **continuous** at a point when its graph has no break there: you can pass through the point without lifting your pencil. Calculus needs a precise version, and it is built directly on limits.

### The definition

A function $f$ is **continuous at $x = a$** when all three conditions hold:

1. $f(a)$ is defined;
2. $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ exists;
3. the two agree: $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$.

A function is continuous **on an interval** if it is continuous at every point of it (at an endpoint of a closed interval, use the one-sided limit from inside).

Most functions you meet are continuous wherever they are defined: polynomials, $\sin x$, $\cos x$, $e^x$, $\ln x$ for $x > 0$, and roots. Sums, products, compositions and quotients (where the denominator is nonzero) of continuous functions are continuous. So a rational function is continuous everywhere except where its denominator is zero.

### Three kinds of discontinuity

When a condition fails, the way it fails has a name.

| Type | What happens at $x = a$ | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Removable | the limit exists, but $f(a)$ is missing or different | $\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}$ at $x=1$ |
| Jump | left and right limits exist but differ | $\lfloor x \rfloor$ at $x=1$ |
| Infinite | $f(x) \to \pm\infty$ from at least one side | $\frac{1}{x^2}$ at $x=0$ |

A removable discontinuity is a "hole": redefine the single value $f(a)$ to equal the limit and the hole is filled. Jumps and infinite discontinuities cannot be repaired this way.

### Making pieces meet

A piecewise function is continuous at a seam only if the pieces meet there. Find the limit from each side and set them equal to each other and to the function value. This is how you choose an unknown constant so that two formulas join up.

### The Intermediate Value Theorem

Hike along a continuous trail from 100 m of altitude up to 400 m and you must pass 250 m at some point. That is the **Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT)**.

**IVT.** If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and $N$ is any number between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, then $f(c) = N$ for at least one $c$ in $[a, b]$.

Its most common use is root finding. If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and $f(a)$, $f(b)$ have opposite signs, then $f$ has a root in $(a, b)$ (take $N = 0$). Evaluating at the midpoint and keeping the half where the sign changes is the **bisection method**.

Two warnings. Continuity on the whole closed interval is essential: $f(x) = 1/x$ has $f(-1) < 0 < f(1)$ but no root, because of the break at $0$. And if the endpoint values have the same sign, the theorem says nothing: there may or may not be a root.

@example
title: Choosing a constant to remove a hole
problem: Let $f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - 9}{x - 3}$ for $x \neq 3$ and $f(3) = k$. Find the value of $k$ that makes $f$ continuous at $x = 3$.
step: Continuity at $3$ requires $\lim_{x \to 3} f(x) = f(3) = k$, so find the limit first. Direct substitution gives $\frac{0}{0}$, so simplify before substituting.
step: Factor the numerator: $x^2 - 9 = (x - 3)(x + 3)$. For $x \neq 3$ the factor $x - 3$ cancels, leaving $f(x) = x + 3$.
step: Take the limit of the simplified expression: $\lim_{x \to 3} (x + 3) = 6$. The limit ignores the value of $f$ at $x = 3$ itself.
step: Set $k$ equal to the limit: $k = 6$.
step: Check the three conditions: $f(3) = 6$ is defined, the limit $6$ exists, and they are equal. The graph is the line $y = x + 3$ with the hole filled.

@example
title: Showing that a root exists
problem: Show that $x^3 + x - 1 = 0$ has a solution between $0$ and $1$, then decide which half of that interval contains it.
step: Let $g(x) = x^3 + x - 1$. It is a polynomial, so it is continuous on $[0, 1]$.
step: Evaluate the endpoints: $g(0) = -1 < 0$ and $g(1) = 1 > 0$. Since $0$ lies between $g(0)$ and $g(1)$, the IVT gives some $c$ in $(0, 1)$ with $g(c) = 0$.
step: Bisect. At the midpoint, $g(0.5) = 0.125 + 0.5 - 1 = -0.375 < 0$.
step: Now $g(0.5) < 0$ and $g(1) > 0$ have opposite signs, so the root lies in $(0.5, 1)$.
step: Bisect again: $g(0.75) = 0.421875 + 0.75 - 1 = 0.171875 > 0$, so the root lies in $(0.5, 0.75)$.

@mistakes
- Checking only that the limit exists. Continuity also needs $f(a)$ to be defined and equal to that limit.
- Calling a vertical asymptote "removable". Only a finite limit can be repaired by redefining $f(a)$.
- Using the IVT when $f$ is not continuous on the whole closed interval, or concluding "no root" when the endpoint values have the same sign.

@practice num
prompt: Let $f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - 5x + 6}{x - 2}$ for $x \neq 2$ and $f(2) = k$. Find $k$ so that $f$ is continuous at $x = 2$.
answer: -1
check: lim(lambda x: (x**2 - 5*x + 6)/(x - 2), 2)
hint: Continuity needs $k$ to equal $\lim_{x \to 2} f(x)$. Substituting $x = 2$ gives $\frac{0}{0}$, so simplify first.
hint: Factor the numerator as $(x - 2)(x - 3)$ and cancel the common factor.
solution: For $x \neq 2$, $f(x) = x - 3$, so $\lim_{x \to 2} f(x) = -1$. Continuity requires $k = -1$.

prompt: Let $f(x) = \dfrac{\lvert x \rvert}{x}$ for $x \neq 0$. What kind of discontinuity does $f$ have at $x = 0$?
choice: Removable: the limit exists, so defining $f(0)$ repairs it.
choice*: Jump: the one-sided limits are $-1$ and $1$.
choice: Infinite: $f$ blows up near $0$.
choice: None: $f$ is continuous at $0$.
hint: Evaluate $f$ at a small positive $x$ and at a small negative $x$.
hint: For $x > 0$, $\lvert x \rvert = x$; for $x < 0$, $\lvert x \rvert = -x$.
solution: For $x > 0$, $f(x) = 1$; for $x < 0$, $f(x) = -1$. The one-sided limits are finite but different, so the discontinuity is a jump, and no choice of $f(0)$ can repair it.

@practice num
prompt: Find $k$ so that $f$ is continuous at $x = 3$, where $$f(x) = \begin{cases} kx + 1, & x \le 3, \\ x^2 - 2k, & x > 3. \end{cases}$$
answer: 8/5
check: root(lambda k: (k*3 + 1) - (3**2 - 2*k), -10, 10)
hint: Continuity at $3$ means the left limit, the right limit and $f(3)$ all agree. Which formula gives the left limit?
hint: The left limit (and $f(3)$) is $3k + 1$, and the right limit is $3^2 - 2k$. Set them equal and solve for $k$.
solution: Left side: $3k + 1$, which is also $f(3)$. Right side: $9 - 2k$. Setting them equal gives $5k = 8$, so $k = \frac{8}{5}$. Check: $3(1.6) + 1 = 5.8$ and $9 - 3.2 = 5.8$.

@practice list
prompt: Find every value of $x$ at which $f(x) = \dfrac{x + 2}{x^2 + 2x - 3}$ is discontinuous. Enter the values separated by commas.
answer: -3, 1
check: allroots(lambda x: x**2 + 2*x - 3, -10, 10)
hint: A rational function is continuous wherever its denominator is nonzero. Where is the denominator zero?
hint: Factor $x^2 + 2x - 3$ into two linear factors.
solution: $x^2 + 2x - 3 = (x + 3)(x - 1)$, which is zero at $x = -3$ and $x = 1$. The numerator is nonzero at both, so these are infinite discontinuities. At $x = -2$ the numerator is zero, but $f(-2) = 0$ is perfectly fine.

@quiz num
prompt: Find the value of $a$ that makes $f$ continuous at $x = 0$, where $f(x) = \dfrac{\sqrt{x + 4} - 2}{x}$ for $x \neq 0$ and $f(0) = a$.
answer: 1/4
check: lim(lambda x: (sqrt(x + 4) - 2)/x, 0)
hint: You need $\lim_{x \to 0} f(x)$. Direct substitution gives $\frac{0}{0}$, so try multiplying the top and bottom by the conjugate $\sqrt{x + 4} + 2$.
hint: The new numerator is $(x + 4) - 4 = x$, which cancels the $x$ in the denominator.
solution: Multiplying by the conjugate gives $\frac{(x + 4) - 4}{x(\sqrt{x + 4} + 2)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x + 4} + 2}$ for $x \neq 0$. As $x \to 0$ this tends to $\frac{1}{4}$, so $a = \frac{1}{4}$.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which of the following guarantee, by the Intermediate Value Theorem alone, that $h(x) = 0$ has a solution in $(1, 2)$? Select all that apply.
choice*: $h$ is continuous on $[1, 2]$, with $h(1) = -3$ and $h(2) = 4$.
choice: $h$ is continuous on $[1, 2]$, with $h(1) = 2$ and $h(2) = 5$.
choice: $h(1) = -1$ and $h(2) = 1$, with nothing known about continuity.
choice*: $h$ is continuous on $[1, 2]$, with $h(1) = 0.5$ and $h(2) = -0.5$.
hint: The IVT needs two things: continuity on the whole closed interval, and a target value between the endpoint values.
hint: For a root the target is $0$. Does $0$ lie between $h(1)$ and $h(2)$ in each case, and is $h$ known to be continuous?
solution: The options with $h(1) = -3,\ h(2) = 4$ and with $h(1) = 0.5,\ h(2) = -0.5$ have continuity and endpoint values of opposite sign, so $0$ lies between them. The option with $h(1) = 2,\ h(2) = 5$ has no sign change, so the theorem is silent. The option with no continuity information lacks continuity: $h$ could jump over $0$.
