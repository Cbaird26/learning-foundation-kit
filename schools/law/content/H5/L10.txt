@article
How do you find the area of a region with a curved boundary? Slice it into thin rectangles, add up their areas, and let the slices get thinner. The same construction measures distance travelled from a speed graph, or total mass from a density. It is the **definite integral**.

### Riemann sums

Take a function $f$ on $[a, b]$ and split the interval into $n$ equal pieces of width

$$\Delta x = \frac{b - a}{n}, \qquad x_i = a + i\,\Delta x.$$

In each piece $[x_{i-1}, x_i]$ choose a sample point $c_i$ and build a rectangle of height $f(c_i)$. The total is a **Riemann sum**:

$$S_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(c_i)\,\Delta x.$$

Common choices are the **left** endpoint $c_i = x_{i-1}$, the **right** endpoint $c_i = x_i$, and the **midpoint**. For an increasing function the left sum is too small and the right sum too big; for a decreasing function it is the other way round.

For $f(x) = x^2$ on $[0, 1]$ with $n = 4$ we have $\Delta x = \frac{1}{4}$. The left sum is $\frac{1}{4}\left(0 + \frac{1}{16} + \frac{4}{16} + \frac{9}{16}\right) = \frac{7}{32} \approx 0.219$, the right sum is $\frac{15}{32} \approx 0.469$, and the midpoint sum is $\frac{21}{64} \approx 0.328$. The exact area is $\frac{1}{3}$.

### The definite integral

If $f$ is continuous, the Riemann sums approach a single limit as $n \to \infty$, whatever sample points are used. That limit is the **definite integral**:

$$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(c_i)\,\Delta x.$$

### Signed area and net change

The integral counts area **above** the $x$-axis as positive and area **below** it as negative, so it measures **net** (signed) area. If $v(t)$ is velocity, then $\int_a^b v\,dt$ is the **displacement**, the net change in position. The **total distance** travelled is $\int_a^b \lvert v \rvert\,dt$, which counts every stretch of motion as positive.

For simple shapes you can read integrals straight off the geometry: rectangles, triangles and circles. For example, $\int_{-3}^{3} \sqrt{9 - x^2}\,dx$ is the area of the upper half of a circle of radius $3$, namely $\frac{9\pi}{2}$.

### Properties

- $\int_a^a f\,dx = 0$ and $\int_b^a f\,dx = -\int_a^b f\,dx$.
- $\int_a^c f\,dx + \int_c^b f\,dx = \int_a^b f\,dx$.
- $\int_a^b [\alpha f + \beta g]\,dx = \alpha \int_a^b f\,dx + \beta \int_a^b g\,dx$.
- If $f$ is odd, then $\int_{-a}^{a} f\,dx = 0$, because the area on the left cancels the area on the right.

@example
title: Bounding an area with left and right sums
problem: Use $n = 4$ rectangles to find the left and right Riemann sums for $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}$ on $[1, 3]$, and use them to bound $\displaystyle\int_1^3 \frac{1}{x}\,dx$.
step: The width is $\Delta x = \dfrac{3 - 1}{4} = \dfrac{1}{2}$, so the subinterval endpoints are $1,\ 1.5,\ 2,\ 2.5,\ 3$.
step: The left sum uses the first four endpoints: $L_4 = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(1 + \tfrac{2}{3} + \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{2}{5}\right) \approx \tfrac{1}{2}(2.567) \approx 1.283$.
step: The right sum uses the last four endpoints: $R_4 = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(\tfrac{2}{3} + \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{2}{5} + \tfrac{1}{3}\right) = \tfrac{1}{2}(1.9) = 0.95$.
step: The function is decreasing, so each left rectangle pokes above the curve and each right rectangle sits below it. Therefore $R_4 < \displaystyle\int_1^3 \frac{1}{x}\,dx < L_4$, that is, between $0.95$ and $1.283$.

@example
title: Displacement versus distance
problem: A particle has velocity $v(t) = t - 2$ m/s for $0 \le t \le 6$. Find its displacement and the total distance it travels.
step: The graph of $v$ is a line that crosses the $t$-axis at $t = 2$. It lies below the axis on $[0, 2]$ and above it on $[2, 6]$.
step: Below the axis there is a triangle with base $2$ and height $2$, of area $\tfrac{1}{2}(2)(2) = 2$. Its signed value is $-2$.
step: Above the axis there is a triangle with base $4$ and height $4$, of area $\tfrac{1}{2}(4)(4) = 8$. Its signed value is $+8$.
step: Displacement is the signed total: $-2 + 8 = 6$ m. Total distance adds the sizes: $2 + 8 = 10$ m.

@mistakes
- Treating the integral as total area. Regions below the $x$-axis contribute negative values.
- Using the wrong width. It is $\Delta x = \frac{b - a}{n}$, not $\frac{1}{n}$, and every term must be multiplied by it.
- Mixing up the endpoints. A left sum starts at $a$ and stops one step short of $b$; a right sum starts one step after $a$ and ends at $b$.
- Confusing displacement with distance. Distance needs $\lvert v \rvert$, so split the interval where $v$ changes sign.

prompt: Find the left-endpoint Riemann sum $L_4$ for $f(x) = x^3$ on $[0, 2]$ using $n = 4$ equal subintervals. Give an exact value.
answer: 9/4
check: sum((2*i/4)**3 for i in range(4))*(2/4)
hint: The width is $\Delta x = \frac{2 - 0}{4} = 0.5$. A left sum uses the left end of each subinterval.
hint: The left endpoints are $0,\ 0.5,\ 1,\ 1.5$. Add $f$ at these four points and multiply by $\Delta x$.
solution: $L_4 = 0.5\left(0^3 + 0.5^3 + 1^3 + 1.5^3\right) = 0.5(0 + 0.125 + 1 + 3.375) = 0.5(4.5) = \frac{9}{4}$. The function is increasing, so this underestimates the true value, $4$.

@practice num
prompt: A cyclist's speed is recorded every $10$ seconds: at $t = 0, 10, 20, 30, 40$ s the speeds are $v = 0, 4, 7, 9, 10$ m/s. Estimate the distance travelled in the $40$ s by averaging the left-endpoint sum and the right-endpoint sum.
answer: 250
unit: m
check: (sum([0, 4, 7, 9])*10 + sum([4, 7, 9, 10])*10)/2
hint: Each speed reading is a rectangle height and each rectangle has width $10$ s. Distance is speed times time.
hint: The left sum uses $0, 4, 7, 9$ and the right sum uses $4, 7, 9, 10$. Find each, then average.
solution: Left sum: $(0 + 4 + 7 + 9)(10) = 200$ m. Right sum: $(4 + 7 + 9 + 10)(10) = 300$ m. Since the speed is increasing these are an underestimate and an overestimate, and their average is $250$ m.

@practice num
prompt: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_{-3}^{3} \sqrt{9 - x^2}\,dx$ by recognising the region under the graph. Give an exact value.
answer: 9pi/2
check: quad(lambda x: sqrt(9 - x**2), -3, 3)
hint: If $y = \sqrt{9 - x^2}$ then $x^2 + y^2 = 9$ with $y \ge 0$. What shape is that?
hint: It is a half-disc of radius $3$. The area of a full disc is $\pi r^2$.
solution: The graph is the upper half of the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 9$, so the integral is the area of a half-disc of radius $3$: $\frac{1}{2}\pi(3)^2 = \frac{9\pi}{2}$.

@practice num
prompt: A particle's velocity is $v(t) = 3 - t$ m/s for $0 \le t \le 5$. Find the total distance it travels.
answer: 6.5
unit: m
check: quad(lambda t: abs(3 - t), 0, 5)
hint: Total distance is the area between the graph of $v$ and the $t$-axis, with every region counted as positive. Where does $v$ change sign?
hint: The velocity is zero at $t = 3$. Find the area of the triangle on $[0, 3]$ and the triangle on $[3, 5]$ separately.
solution: On $[0, 3]$ the velocity is positive, a triangle of base $3$ and height $3$ with area $4.5$. On $[3, 5]$ it is negative, a triangle of base $2$ and height $2$ with area $2$. Total distance $= 4.5 + 2 = 6.5$ m. (The displacement would be $4.5 - 2 = 2.5$ m.)

@practice self
prompt: Let $f(x) = x^2$ on $[0, 2]$ with $n = 4$. (a) Sketch the left and right rectangles. (b) Compute $L_4$ and $R_4$. (c) Explain, using the shape of the graph, why $L_4 < \int_0^2 x^2\,dx < R_4$, and describe what happens to the gap $R_n - L_n$ as $n \to \infty$.
hint: Here $\Delta x = 0.5$. The left sum uses $x = 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5$ and the right sum uses $x = 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2$.
hint: For an increasing function, which rectangles lie under the curve and which poke above it? Compare $R_n$ with $L_n$: most terms are shared.
solution: (b) $L_4 = 0.5(0 + 0.25 + 1 + 2.25) = 1.75$ and $R_4 = 0.5(0.25 + 1 + 2.25 + 4) = 3.75$. (c) Since $x^2$ is increasing on $[0, 2]$, every left rectangle lies under the curve and every right rectangle covers it, so $L_4 < \int_0^2 x^2\,dx < R_4$. (The exact value is $\frac{8}{3}$.) The two sums share all terms except the first and last, so $R_n - L_n = \big(f(2) - f(0)\big)\Delta x = 4 \cdot \frac{2}{n}$. This tends to $0$ as $n \to \infty$, so both sums are squeezed to the integral.

@quiz mc
prompt: Which definite integral equals $\displaystyle\lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(1 + \frac{2i}{n}\right)^3 \frac{2}{n}$?
choice*: $\displaystyle\int_1^3 x^3\,dx$
choice: $\displaystyle\int_1^2 x^3\,dx$
choice: $\displaystyle\int_0^2 x^3\,dx$
choice: $\displaystyle\int_1^5 x^3\,dx$
hint: Match the sum to $\sum f(c_i)\,\Delta x$. The factor $\frac{2}{n}$ is $\Delta x = \frac{b - a}{n}$, so what is $b - a$?
hint: The sample points are $c_i = a + i\,\Delta x = 1 + \frac{2i}{n}$. What is $a$, and what function is applied to $c_i$?
solution: $\Delta x = \frac{2}{n}$ means $b - a = 2$. The sample points $c_i = 1 + \frac{2i}{n}$ are right endpoints starting from $a = 1$, so $b = 3$. The function applied to each sample point is $f(x) = x^3$. The limit is $\int_1^3 x^3\,dx$.

@quiz num
prompt: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_{-3}^{3} (x^3 + 2)\,dx$.
answer: 12
check: quad(lambda x: x**3 + 2, -3, 3)
hint: Split the integral into $\int_{-3}^{3} x^3\,dx$ and $\int_{-3}^{3} 2\,dx$.
hint: One piece is the integral of an odd function over a symmetric interval. The other is the area of a rectangle.
solution: $x^3$ is odd, so $\int_{-3}^{3} x^3\,dx = 0$: the area on the left cancels the area on the right. The constant gives a rectangle of width $6$ and height $2$, with area $12$. So the integral is $12$.
