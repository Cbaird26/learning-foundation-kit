@article
Joinder rules decide **who** can be in the case and **what claims** the parties may bring. They promote efficiency and consistent results while protecting those who should be heard.

### Joining claims: Rule 18

A party asserting a claim, counterclaim, cross-claim or third-party claim may join, as independent or alternative claims, as many claims as it has against an opposing party. The claims need not be related. Whether a federal court can hear them is a separate jurisdictional question (supplemental jurisdiction, lesson 4).

### Counterclaims and cross-claims: Rule 13

- **Compulsory counterclaim** (Rule 13(a)): a claim the pleader has against an opposing party at the time of service of the pleading that **arises out of the same transaction or occurrence** as the opposing party's claim and does not require adding parties over whom the court lacks jurisdiction. It **must** be pleaded or it is **barred** in a later action. Compulsory counterclaims fall within supplemental jurisdiction.
- **Permissive counterclaim** (Rule 13(b)): any other claim against the opposing party. It may be pleaded but need not be, and generally needs its own jurisdictional basis.
- **Cross-claim** (Rule 13(g)): a claim against a **coparty** that arises out of the same transaction or occurrence as the original action or a counterclaim. Cross-claims are always optional.

### Joining parties

**Permissive joinder: Rule 20.** Persons may join as plaintiffs, or be joined as defendants, if (1) the right to relief arises out of the **same transaction, occurrence or series** of transactions or occurrences, and (2) a **common question of law or fact** will arise. Misjoinder is not a ground for dismissal (Rule 21), and the court may order separate trials (Rule 20(b), Rule 42(b)).

**Impleader: Rule 14.** A defending party may serve a summons and complaint on a nonparty "who is or may be liable to it for all or part of the claim against it." The third-party claim must be **derivative**: indemnity, contribution or subrogation. It is not proper simply because the third party is liable to the plaintiff or on a separate claim. A defendant may implead without leave if it files the third-party complaint within **14 days** after serving its original answer; later, it needs leave of court (Rule 14(a)(1)). The plaintiff may assert claims against the third-party defendant (Rule 14(a)(3)), subject to jurisdiction. Supplemental jurisdiction covers the defendant's impleader claim.

**Required parties: Rule 19.** A person must be joined if feasible when (A) in the person's absence, the court cannot accord **complete relief** among existing parties, or (B) the person claims an interest in the subject of the action and disposing of it in the person's absence may (i) **impair or impede** the person's ability to protect that interest, or (ii) leave an existing party at **substantial risk of inconsistent obligations**. If a required person cannot be joined (for example, joinder would destroy diversity or defeat venue), the court applies Rule 19(b) to decide whether, "in equity and good conscience," the action should proceed or be dismissed, weighing (1) prejudice to the absent person or existing parties, (2) whether the judgment can be shaped to reduce prejudice, (3) the adequacy of a judgment without the person, and (4) whether the plaintiff would have an adequate remedy if the action were dismissed (*Provident Tradesmens Bank & Trust Co. v. Patterson*, 1968). Joint tortfeasors are not required parties merely because they are jointly liable (*Temple v. Synthes Corp.*, 1990). The defense is raised under Rule 12(b)(7).

**Intervention: Rule 24.** A nonparty may intervene **as of right** on timely motion if she claims an interest relating to the property or transaction that is the subject of the action, is so situated that disposing of the action may impair or impede her ability to protect the interest, and existing parties do not adequately represent it, or if a federal statute gives an unconditional right. A court may permit intervention if the applicant has a claim or defense sharing a common question of law or fact with the main action, considering delay and prejudice.

**Interpleader: Rule 22 and 28 U.S.C. § 1335.** A stakeholder facing adverse claims to a single fund or property may join the claimants to avoid multiple liability. **Rule 22 interpleader** needs ordinary jurisdiction (complete diversity between stakeholder and claimants, with more than \$75,000 in controversy). **Statutory interpleader** under § 1335 requires only **minimal diversity** between two adverse claimants and \$500 or more in controversy, with nationwide service (28 U.S.C. § 2361).

### Class actions: Rule 23

A representative may sue on behalf of a class if **all four** requirements of Rule 23(a) are met: (1) **numerosity** (joinder impracticable), (2) **commonality** (common questions of law or fact; *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 2011, requires a common contention capable of classwide resolution), (3) **typicality** (the representative's claims are typical), and (4) **adequacy** (fair and adequate protection of the class). The class must also fit one of the Rule 23(b) categories: (b)(1) risk of inconsistent adjudications or impairment of absent members' interests, (b)(2) injunctive or declaratory relief for the class as a whole, or (b)(3) **predominance** of common questions and **superiority** of the class device (*Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor*, 1997). A (b)(3) class requires the best notice practicable and a chance to opt out. The court must certify the class, and an order granting or denying certification may be appealed by permission under Rule 23(f). Under the Class Action Fairness Act, federal courts may hear large class actions on minimal diversity.

### Policy

Joinder rules favor resolving related disputes together to avoid repetition and inconsistent results, but they must respect jurisdictional limits and due process rights of absent persons.

@example
title: Compulsory counterclaim and impleader
problem: Ana sues Ben for damages from a car accident, alleging Ben's negligence. Ben believes Ana was speeding and that her mechanic, Cal, negligently repaired her brakes. Ben's answer, served March 6, denies negligence and includes no claims. Ben later wants to sue Ana for his own injuries from the same accident and to bring in Cal. What are his options?
step: Ben's claim against Ana for his own injuries arises out of the same occurrence as Ana's claim, so it is a compulsory counterclaim under Rule 13(a). It had to be pleaded in his answer, and omitting it likely bars a later suit.
step: Ben cannot bring Cal in as a defendant for Ana's brake claims by Rule 14 unless Cal "is or may be liable to Ben" for all or part of Ana's claim. If Cal's repair negligence was a cause, Ben may seek contribution from Cal.
step: Timing: Ben may implead without leave within 14 days after serving his answer (through March 20); after that he needs leave of court.
step: Conclude that Ben risks losing his counterclaim but may still seek leave to amend his answer under Rule 15 and to implead Cal for contribution.
@mistakes
- Treating all counterclaims as optional; a compulsory counterclaim is lost if not pleaded.
- Using Rule 14 to bring in a person who is liable to the plaintiff but not derivatively to the defendant.
- Treating a required party (Rule 19) as automatically requiring dismissal; the court first seeks joinder and then applies the Rule 19(b) factors.
- Confusing joinder rules with jurisdiction; a joined claim must still have a jurisdictional basis.

@practice self
prompt: Zoe, a State A citizen, sues Yan, a State B citizen, in federal court for \$100,000 for a car collision. Yan wants to (1) sue Zoe for Yan's own damages from the same collision, and (2) bring in Xia, a State A citizen who may have caused the collision, to share liability. Write a short memo on Yan's procedural options and the jurisdictional issues.
hint: Use Rules 13(a) and 14, and § 1367 for supplemental jurisdiction.
solution: (1) Yan's claim against Zoe arises from the same occurrence, so it is a compulsory counterclaim under Rule 13(a) and must be pleaded in Yan's answer or it is barred. It falls within supplemental jurisdiction (§ 1367(a)), and it need not have independent diversity or an amount in controversy over \$75,000. (2) Yan may implead Xia under Rule 14(a) if Xia is or may be liable to Yan for all or part of Zoe's claim (contribution or indemnity), without leave if done within 14 days after serving the answer. Section 1367(a) permits Yan's impleader claim because § 1367(b) restricts only claims by plaintiffs (and Xia and Yan are diverse in any event). But Zoe cannot assert a direct claim against Xia, a State A citizen like Zoe, in the diversity case because § 1367(b) bars it as inconsistent with complete diversity. Conclusion: Yan should plead the counterclaim and implead Xia promptly; Zoe would need to sue Xia in state court.
rubric: Identifies the counterclaim as compulsory under Rule 13(a) with its consequence.
rubric: Identifies impleader under Rule 14 as derivative liability and states the 14-day rule.
rubric: Applies § 1367(a) and (b) correctly to defendant's claims and the plaintiff's claim against Xia.
rubric: Concludes with a practical recommendation.
