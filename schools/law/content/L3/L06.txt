@article
A federal court hearing a state-law claim (in diversity or under supplemental jurisdiction) must decide whether to apply a **state rule** or a conflicting **federal rule**. The **Erie doctrine** answers that question.

### Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins (1938)

*Erie* overruled *Swift v. Tyson* (1842) and held that there is **no general federal common law**. A federal court in diversity applies state substantive law, including state common law as declared by the state's highest court. The ruling rests on the Rules of Decision Act (28 U.S.C. § 1652) and on constitutional limits: federal courts cannot make general common law for the states. The policy concerns were uniformity within a state and fairness to those who could not choose a federal court.

If the state's highest court has not ruled, the federal court must **predict** how that court would decide, giving proper regard to decisions of intermediate courts. A federal court may also **certify** a question to the state's supreme court where available.

### Procedure and substance: the framework

Federal courts apply federal procedural law, so the real question is whether a given rule is "substantive" (state law governs) or "procedural" (federal law governs). The modern analysis comes from three Supreme Court strands.

**1. Is there a federal statute or constitutional provision on point?** If a valid federal statute directly governs, it controls (*Stewart Organization, Inc. v. Ricoh Corp.*, 1988, where § 1404(a) governed transfer despite a state-law forum rule).

**2. Does a Federal Rule of Civil Procedure directly cover the issue? (*Hanna v. Plumer*, 1965).** If a Federal Rule is "sufficiently broad" to control the point in dispute, so that it directly collides with state law, the Rule applies if it is valid under the **Rules Enabling Act** (28 U.S.C. § 2072) and the Constitution. The Act authorizes Rules of "practice and procedure" that do not "abridge, enlarge or modify any substantive right." The Court has never held a Federal Rule invalid under the Act. In *Hanna*, Rule 4(d)(1) (service by leaving papers at the defendant's residence) governed over a state requirement of in-hand service. In *Burlington Northern Railroad Co. v. Woods* (1987), a federal appellate rule on discretionary penalties for frivolous appeals displaced a state automatic-penalty statute. In *Shady Grove Orthopedic Associates v. Allstate Insurance Co.* (2010), a fractured Court held that Rule 23 governed over a New York law barring class actions for statutory penalties. The plurality asked only whether the Rule answers the question; Justice Stevens, concurring, would ask whether the state rule is so bound up with state substantive rights that applying the Rule would violate the Enabling Act. Courts often follow his narrower view.

**3. If no Rule collides, apply Erie's twin aims.** Under *Hanna*, the court asks whether failing to apply the state rule would lead to **forum shopping** (a different outcome in federal court as a reason to choose it) and **inequitable administration of the laws** (a result that gives one side an advantage only because of the forum). *Guaranty Trust Co. v. York* (1945) set the outcome-determinative test, which *Hanna* narrowed so that every difference does not count. *Byrd v. Blue Ridge Rural Electric Cooperative* (1958) allows the court to weigh **countervailing federal interests**, such as the federal jury trial tradition. *Gasperini v. Center for Humanities, Inc.* (1996) applied state law on excessive verdicts while allocating review between the trial and appellate court in the federal manner.

### Statutes of limitations

Statutes of limitations are substantive for Erie purposes: they affect outcome and invite forum shopping. A federal court in a diversity case applies the state's **limitations period**, its **accrual** rules, its **tolling** rules, and the state rule for **when an action is commenced**. *Walker v. Armco Steel Corp.* (1980) held that FRCP 3 (an action is commenced by filing a complaint) does not conflict with a state rule that commencement for limitations purposes occurs on **service**; Rule 3 governs the time periods within the federal rules, not the state limitations period. So a claim filed in federal court within the limitations period, but served after the state period expired under a state service requirement, is barred. Federal claims follow federal limitations law instead.

### Choice of law

*Klaxon Co. v. Stentor Electric Manufacturing Co.* (1941) holds that a federal court in diversity applies the **forum state's choice-of-law rules**, so that the choice-of-law outcome is the same as in state court. On a transfer, *Van Dusen* and *Atlantic Marine* adjust that rule (see the previous lesson).

### Scope

Erie applies to state claims in federal court, including claims under supplemental jurisdiction. It does not apply to federal claims; federal law governs those. Issues of federal judicial administration, such as the allocation of functions between judge and jury, may be governed by federal law under *Byrd*.

### Applying it

Identify the conflict, ask whether a federal statute or Rule directly covers it, test the Rule's validity, and if no Rule collides, apply the twin aims. For limitations questions, answer that state law governs.
@example
title: Rule 3 and a state service requirement
problem: A State X statute requires a plaintiff to serve the defendant within two years of the injury for the claim to be timely, and treats the action as commenced when the defendant is served. Pru, injured 20 months earlier, files a diversity complaint in federal court in State X, then serves the defendant 27 months after the injury. FRCP 3 says an action is commenced by filing a complaint. The defendant moves to dismiss as time-barred. How should the court rule?
step: Identify the conflict: State X treats service as the commencement of the action for limitations purposes; Rule 3 treats filing as commencement.
step: Apply Walker v. Armco Steel. Rule 3 is not intended to displace a state's limitations scheme or to toll state periods. It governs the steps within a federal case.
step: Apply the twin aims: if the federal court allowed this late service, plaintiffs would file in federal court to avoid the state period (forum shopping), and the defendant would be treated differently from a defendant in state court (inequitable administration).
step: Conclude: State X law governs when the action commenced. Service came after two years, so the claim is time-barred, and the motion should be granted.
@mistakes
- Treating all "procedural" matters as controlled by federal law without checking whether a Federal Rule directly covers the point.
- Applying the twin-aims analysis when a valid Federal Rule collides with state law; the Rule governs (Hanna).
- Concluding that FRCP 3 tolls or sets a state limitations period (Walker).
- Applying the federal court's own choice-of-law rules in a diversity case instead of the forum state's (Klaxon).
