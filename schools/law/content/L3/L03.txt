@article
Federal courts are courts of **limited jurisdiction**. A federal court needs a constitutional grant (Article III, § 2) and a statute that implements it, and the party invoking federal jurisdiction bears the burden of showing both. Unlike personal jurisdiction, **subject-matter jurisdiction (SMJ) cannot be waived or conferred by consent**. A court must dismiss at any time it finds SMJ lacking (FRCP 12(h)(3)), and a defect can be raised for the first time on appeal. The motion is FRCP 12(b)(1).

### Federal question: 28 U.S.C. § 1331

Section 1331 gives district courts jurisdiction over civil actions "arising under the Constitution, laws, or treaties of the United States." Two cases shape it.

**The well-pleaded complaint rule.** *Louisville & Nashville Railroad Co. v. Mottley* (1908) holds that a federal question must appear on the face of the plaintiff's properly pleaded complaint. A federal defense, even one that the plaintiff anticipates, does not create federal jurisdiction. The same is true of a federal counterclaim (*Holmes Group, Inc. v. Vornado Air Circulation Systems*, 2002).

**Federal law creates the claim.** The ordinary case is one in which federal law creates the cause of action (a Title VII claim, a federal civil rights claim, a federal securities claim).

**State claims with a federal issue.** In a small group of cases, a state-law claim "arises under" federal law. *Grable & Sons Metal Products, Inc. v. Darue Engineering & Manufacturing* (2005) and *Gunn v. Minton* (2013) require a federal issue that is (1) necessarily raised, (2) actually disputed, (3) substantial, and (4) capable of resolution in federal court without disrupting the federal-state balance. In *Gunn*, a state-law legal malpractice claim that depended on a patent question did not qualify, because the federal issue was not substantial to the federal system as a whole.

### Diversity: 28 U.S.C. § 1332

Diversity jurisdiction requires (1) **complete diversity** of citizenship, meaning no plaintiff shares citizenship with any defendant (*Strawbridge v. Curtiss*, 1806), and (2) an **amount in controversy exceeding \$75,000**, exclusive of interest and costs. "Exceeding" means more than \$75,000; a claim for exactly that amount does not qualify.

**Citizenship.**
- An individual is a citizen of the state of her **domicile**: physical presence plus intent to remain. Residence is not the same thing. A U.S. citizen domiciled abroad is "stateless" for diversity and cannot sue or be sued under § 1332(a)(1).
- A corporation is a citizen of every state where it is **incorporated** and of the state where it has its **principal place of business** (§ 1332(c)(1)), which is its "nerve center," where officers direct and control its activities (*Hertz Corp. v. Friend*, 2010).
- A partnership or LLC takes the citizenship of **all of its members** (*Carden v. Arkoma Associates*, 1990), traced through every layer of ownership.
- The legal representative of an estate takes the decedent's citizenship (§ 1332(c)(2)).
- Aliens: § 1332(a)(2) covers citizens of a state versus citizens or subjects of a foreign state; alien-versus-alien suits are not covered, and a lawful permanent resident is treated as a citizen of the state of domicile for this purpose.

**Timing.** Diversity is measured when the complaint is filed (*Grupo Dataflux v. Atlas Global Group*, 2004). Later changes in citizenship do not defeat jurisdiction.

**Amount in controversy.** The amount the plaintiff claims in good faith controls unless it appears to a **legal certainty** that the claim is really for less (*St. Paul Mercury Indemnity Co. v. Red Cab Co.*, 1938). A single plaintiff may **aggregate** all claims against a single defendant, related or not. Multiple plaintiffs may not aggregate separate claims, except for claims to enforce a single title or right in which they share a common undivided interest. Attorney's fees count if a statute or contract allows them. Interest and costs are excluded. In a suit for an injunction, courts value the object of the litigation (a majority view takes the plaintiff's viewpoint).

**Exceptions.** Federal courts decline to hear divorce, alimony and child-custody matters (the domestic-relations exception, narrowly read in *Ankenbrandt v. Richards*, 1992) and will not probate a will or administer an estate, though they may hear related claims that do not interfere with probate (*Marshall v. Marshall*, 2006). The **Class Action Fairness Act**, 28 U.S.C. § 1332(d), relaxes the rule for large class actions: minimal diversity and more than \$5 million in controversy, with exceptions.

### Policy

Federal question jurisdiction places national law before federal judges. Diversity jurisdiction was designed to protect out-of-state litigants from local bias. Critics argue that the premise is weak today; because it exists, Congress has restricted it with the amount-in-controversy and complete-diversity requirements.

### Applying it

Check each basis separately. For § 1331, look only at the plaintiff's claim as pleaded. For § 1332, list every party's citizenship, test complete diversity, then compute the amount in controversy. A single nondiverse defendant destroys diversity.
@example
title: Corporate and LLC parties
problem: Ana, domiciled in State A, sues Lex LLC and Brightco, Inc. in federal court for \$90,000 in breach of contract damages. Brightco is incorporated in State B with its headquarters (nerve center) in State A. Lex LLC has two members: Ben, domiciled in State B, and Carla, domiciled in State C. Does the federal court have diversity jurisdiction?
step: Identify citizenship. Ana is a citizen of State A. Brightco is a citizen of State B (incorporation) and State A (principal place of business). Lex LLC is a citizen of State B and State C through its members.
step: Test complete diversity. Brightco is a citizen of State A, the same state as Ana, so diversity is incomplete.
step: Amount in controversy: \$90,000 exceeds \$75,000, so that requirement is met, but it cannot cure the lack of diversity.
step: Conclude: no diversity jurisdiction. Unless a federal question exists, the case must be dismissed (or brought in state court), and the defect cannot be waived.
@mistakes
- Treating residence as citizenship, or treating an LLC like a corporation (it takes its members' citizenship).
- Counting an anticipated federal defense as a federal question (Mottley).
- Thinking that a claim of exactly \$75,000, or one that exceeds that figure only through interest and costs, meets § 1332.
- Believing the parties can waive or consent to subject-matter jurisdiction.
