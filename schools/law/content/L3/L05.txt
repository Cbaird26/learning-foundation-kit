@article
**Venue** answers a different question from jurisdiction: given that a court system has power over the case and the parties, **which district** is the proper place for the action? The rules protect defendants from inconvenient forums and place cases near the events and witnesses. Venue is a **statutory** requirement, not a constitutional one, and it is **waivable**.

### General venue: 28 U.S.C. § 1391(b)

A civil action may be brought in:

1. a judicial district in which **any defendant resides**, if **all defendants** are residents of the state in which the district is located;
2. a district in which a **substantial part of the events or omissions** giving rise to the claim occurred, or in which a substantial part of the property that is the subject of the action is situated; or
3. if there is **no district** in which the action may otherwise be brought, any district in which any defendant is **subject to the court's personal jurisdiction** with respect to the action (the fallback).

The prongs are alternatives. The fallback applies only if the first two prongs produce no proper district. More than one district may satisfy prong (2): "substantial part" does not mean the "most" substantial part.

### Residency: § 1391(c)

- A natural person resides where she is **domiciled**.
- An entity with the capacity to sue and be sued (a corporation or unincorporated association), as a **defendant**, resides in any district where it is subject to personal jurisdiction with respect to the civil action in question. In a state with several districts, § 1391(d) lets a corporation reside in any district within which its contacts would suffice for personal jurisdiction if that district were a separate state.
- A defendant **not resident in the United States** may be sued in any district, and its residence is disregarded when deciding whether all defendants reside in one state (§ 1391(c)(3)).

Venue statutes for particular claims (for example, patent infringement under 28 U.S.C. § 1400(b)) may override § 1391.

### Objecting to venue

Improper venue is raised by FRCP 12(b)(3). The defense is waived under **FRCP 12(h)(1)** if omitted from the first Rule 12 motion (when one is filed) or the first responsive pleading. Venue is not an element of subject-matter jurisdiction, and a court need not have personal jurisdiction to transfer a case (*Goldlawr, Inc. v. Heiman*, 1962).

### Remedies and transfer

**28 U.S.C. § 1406(a): wrong venue.** If venue is improper, the court must dismiss or, "in the interest of justice," transfer to a district where the action could have been brought. Transfer is usually preferred, because it avoids statute-of-limitations problems.

**28 U.S.C. § 1404(a): convenience transfer.** Even where venue is proper, a district court may transfer "for the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice" to any district where the action **might have been brought** or to which all parties have consented. Courts weigh private factors (access to proof, witnesses, cost) and public factors (court congestion, local interest, the law to be applied). The plaintiff's choice receives deference but is not conclusive. In *Van Dusen v. Barrack* (1964) and *Ferens v. John Deere Co.* (1990), the Supreme Court held that on a § 1404(a) transfer, the **transferee court applies the choice-of-law rules of the transferor state**, so a convenience transfer does not change the applicable law. (By contrast, after a § 1406(a) transfer for improper venue, the transferee state's choice-of-law rules apply.)

**Forum-selection clauses: *Atlantic Marine Construction Co. v. U.S. District Court* (2013).** A valid forum-selection clause is enforced through a § 1404(a) motion (or forum non conveniens, if the chosen forum is state or foreign), not through § 1406(a) or Rule 12(b)(3), because venue is "proper" under § 1391 regardless of the clause. The court gives the clause **controlling weight** in all but the most exceptional cases: the plaintiff's choice of forum gets no weight, the parties' private interests are deemed to favor the chosen forum, and the transferee court applies its **own** choice-of-law rules, so the plaintiff does not keep the transferor state's law.

**Forum non conveniens.** When the proper alternative forum is a state court or a foreign nation (so that § 1404(a) is not available), a court may dismiss if the alternative forum is adequate and the private and public interests favor it (*Gulf Oil Corp. v. Gilbert*, 1947; *Piper Aircraft Co. v. Reyno*, 1981). Courts may decide forum non conveniens before deciding jurisdiction (*Sinochem International Co. v. Malaysia International Shipping Corp.*, 2007).

**Other transfers.** 28 U.S.C. § 1631 allows transfer to cure a want of jurisdiction. 28 U.S.C. § 1407 permits consolidation of related federal cases in different districts for pretrial proceedings before one judge (multidistrict litigation). New FRCP 16.1, effective December 1, 2025, addresses early management of such proceedings.

### Policy

The statute's two-sided design balances plaintiff choice against fairness to defendants; transfer lets courts fix a poor choice without penalizing the plaintiff, and *Atlantic Marine* gives effect to bargained-for forums.

### Applying it

First test § 1391(b)(1) through (3) in order. If venue is improper, use § 1406(a). If venue is proper but another forum is better, use § 1404(a). If a clause points to a forum, apply *Atlantic Marine*. Do not confuse venue with personal jurisdiction.
@example
title: Choosing the venue
problem: Orla (domiciled in State O) sues Pike Co. (a State P corporation subject to jurisdiction in State P only) in a federal district in State O for breach of a supply contract. The contract was negotiated and performed in State P, and Pike's only contacts with State O are one unsolicited order from Orla. Does § 1391(b) make the State O district proper?
step: Prong (b)(1): the only defendant, Pike, resides in State P (where it is subject to jurisdiction for this action). Venue is proper in State P districts where Pike resides, but not in State O under this prong.
step: Prong (b)(2): the events (negotiation and performance) occurred in State P, and nothing substantial occurred in State O, so prong (b)(2) points to State P as well.
step: Prong (b)(3): the fallback applies only if no district works. Because a State P district works, the fallback is unavailable.
step: Conclude that venue is improper in State O. If Pike objects under Rule 12(b)(3) in time, the court should dismiss or, more likely, transfer to a State P district under § 1406(a) in the interest of justice.
@mistakes
- Treating the plaintiff's residence as a venue basis; § 1391(b) looks at the defendants' residence and the events or property.
- Using the fallback (b)(3) when another district satisfies (b)(1) or (b)(2).
- Raising venue under § 1406(a) to enforce a forum-selection clause; Atlantic Marine requires § 1404(a) or forum non conveniens.
- Forgetting that a convenience transfer under § 1404(a) carries the transferor's choice-of-law rules (except in a forum-clause case).
