### The right to a jury

The **Seventh Amendment** preserves the right to a jury "in Suits at common law" in federal court. It applies to claims that are **legal** in nature (those for which damages were available at common law in 1791 or that are analogous) and not to purely equitable claims. Courts consider the nature of the claim and the remedy sought, with the remedy the more important factor (*Tull v. United States*, 1987; *Chauffeurs, Teamsters & Helpers Local No. 391 v. Terry*, 1990). Where a case joins legal and equitable claims with common facts, the jury decides the legal issues first so that the equitable decision does not preclude them (*Beacon Theatres, Inc. v. Westover*, 1959; *Dairy Queen, Inc. v. Wood*, 1962). In *SEC v. Jarkesy* (2024), the Court held that the Seventh Amendment entitles a defendant to a jury trial when the SEC seeks civil penalties for securities fraud, because that remedy is legal in nature.

**Procedure.** A party must demand a jury **in writing** within **14 days after the last pleading directed to the issue** is served (Rule 38(b)); failure to demand waives the right (Rule 38(d)). Federal juries have at least 6 and no more than 12 members and, unless the parties stipulate otherwise, the verdict must be unanimous (Rule 48). Peremptory challenges may not be used to discriminate on race or sex, even in civil cases (*Edmonson v. Leesville Concrete Co.*, 1991, race; *J.E.B. v. Alabama ex rel. T.B.*, 1994, sex).

### Judgment as a matter of law: Rule 50

A party may move for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) at any time before the case goes to the jury if "a reasonable jury would not have a legally sufficient evidentiary basis" to find for the other side on an issue (Rule 50(a)). The standard resembles summary judgment, now applied to the trial record; the court draws all inferences for the nonmovant and does not weigh credibility. **If the motion is denied, the movant may renew it after the verdict** under Rule 50(b), within **28 days after entry of judgment**. A **renewed motion requires a prior Rule 50(a) motion** on the same ground: without it, the court of appeals cannot review the sufficiency of the evidence (*Unitherm Food Systems, Inc. v. Swift-Eckrich, Inc.*, 2006).

### New trial and altering the judgment: Rule 59

A motion for a **new trial** (Rule 59(a)) may be granted for any reason for which a new trial has been granted in federal court, such as a verdict against the great weight of the evidence, prejudicial error, or an excessive award. The judge may weigh the evidence and credibility. A motion for a new trial, or to **alter or amend a judgment** (Rule 59(e)), must be filed within **28 days after entry of judgment**.

### Relief from judgment: Rule 60(b)

On motion, the court may relieve a party from a final judgment for (1) mistake, inadvertence, surprise or excusable neglect; (2) newly discovered evidence that could not have been found in time to move for a new trial; (3) fraud, misrepresentation or misconduct by an opponent; (4) a void judgment; (5) a satisfied or discharged judgment; or (6) any other reason that justifies relief. The motion must be made within a reasonable time, and for grounds (1) through (3) **no more than one year after entry of judgment**. Rule 60(b) is not a substitute for appeal.

### Equitable relief

A **preliminary injunction** requires a likely success on the merits, likely irreparable harm, a favorable balance of equities and the public interest (*Winter v. NRDC*, 2008). In *Trump v. CASA, Inc.* (2025), the Supreme Court held that universal injunctions likely exceed the equitable authority that Congress has given federal courts: relief should be no broader than necessary to give the plaintiffs before the court complete relief. The decision left open class actions under Rule 23 and relief under the Administrative Procedure Act.

### Appeal

**The final judgment rule.** Courts of appeals have jurisdiction over **final decisions** of district courts (28 U.S.C. § 1291): decisions that end the litigation on the merits and leave nothing but execution. An order denying a motion to dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction, denying summary judgment, or ordering discovery is not final. Rule 54(b) lets the court enter final judgment on fewer than all claims or parties if it expressly determines there is "no just reason for delay."

**Exceptions.** (1) **Injunction orders**: granting, denying or modifying injunctions are appealable immediately (§ 1292(a)(1)). (2) **Certified interlocutory appeal** (§ 1292(b)): the district judge states in the order that it involves a controlling question of law as to which there is substantial ground for difference of opinion and that an immediate appeal may materially advance the termination of the litigation; the court of appeals has discretion to accept it, and the petition is due within 10 days. (3) **Collateral order doctrine** (*Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 1949): orders that conclusively resolve an important issue separate from the merits and unreviewable after final judgment (for example, denial of qualified immunity or of absolute immunity). The Court has declined to extend it to discovery privilege rulings (*Mohawk Industries, Inc. v. Carpenter*, 2009). (4) **Class certification orders** may be appealed by permission within 14 days (Rule 23(f)). (5) **Mandamus** is extraordinary.

**Timing.** The notice of appeal must be filed within **30 days** after entry of the judgment (**60 days** if the United States is a party) (FRAP 4(a)(1)). Timely motions under Rules 50(b), 52(b), 59 and certain others **toll** the time until the order disposing of the last such motion (FRAP 4(a)(4)).

**Standards of review.** Questions of law are reviewed **de novo**; findings of fact after a bench trial are reviewed for **clear error** (Rule 52(a)(6)); discretionary rulings are reviewed for **abuse of discretion**; and jury verdicts are reviewed deferentially, upheld if supported by substantial evidence. Errors that do not affect substantial rights are **harmless** (Rule 61).

Identify the stage, the motion available, the deadline and the standard the court will apply. Note whether the jury right was preserved, and whether the order is appealable now or only after final judgment.
@example
title: Preserving a sufficiency challenge
problem: After a jury returns a verdict for the plaintiff on a contract claim, entered as judgment on March 3, the defendant, who never moved for JMOL before the case went to the jury, moves under Rule 50(b) on March 20 to set aside the verdict as unsupported by the evidence. How should the court rule, and what other motion might the defendant have made?
step: A Rule 50(b) motion renews a Rule 50(a) motion made before submission to the jury. The defendant made no such motion.
step: The 50(b) motion is therefore procedurally improper, and the court may deny it, even though it was filed within 28 days after entry of judgment.
step: The defendant's alternative is a Rule 59 motion for a new trial, which does not require a prior motion and which may be based on the verdict being against the great weight of the evidence. It was due within 28 days after judgment (by March 31).
step: Conclude: deny the Rule 50(b) motion; the defendant should move for a new trial under Rule 59 before the deadline.
@mistakes
- Believing a party can seek JMOL after the verdict without having moved before submission to the jury.
- Using Rule 60(b) to correct ordinary legal errors that should be raised on appeal.
- Treating any significant pretrial order, such as the denial of a motion to dismiss, as immediately appealable.
- Forgetting that a jury demand must be made within 14 days after the last pleading directed to the issue and is waived otherwise.
