@article
Jurisdiction under *International Shoe* comes in two forms.

### General (all-purpose) jurisdiction

A court with **general jurisdiction** can hear any claim against the defendant, even one unrelated to the forum. For an individual, the paradigm forum is domicile. For a corporation, *Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations v. Brown* (2011) and *Daimler AG v. Bauman* (2014) hold that general jurisdiction exists where the corporation is "at home": its **place of incorporation** and **principal place of business** are the paradigm forums. *BNSF Railway Co. v. Tyrrell* (2017) rejected the idea that "doing business" in a state, even substantial business, makes a corporation at home there. An "exceptional case" may exist (the example is *Perkins v. Benguet Consolidated Mining Co.*, 1952, where wartime operations were run from the forum), but it is rare.

**Consent** is an alternative route. *Mallory v. Norfolk Southern Railway Co.* (2023) held that the Due Process Clause does not bar a state from requiring a foreign corporation that registers to do business there to consent to general jurisdiction. Only states with such statutes are affected, and the Court left open dormant Commerce Clause arguments.

### Specific (case-linked) jurisdiction

**Specific jurisdiction** requires a connection between the forum, the defendant and the **claim**. The standard analysis has three parts:

1. **Purposeful availment (or direction).** The defendant must have deliberately reached into the forum, so that it could foresee being sued there (*Hanson v. Denckla*, 1958; *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 1985). Contacts must be the defendant's own choice, not random, fortuitous or the unilateral activity of the plaintiff or a third party. *Walden v. Fiore* (2014) held that the relationship must arise from contacts the defendant creates with the forum itself, not merely contacts with a forum resident.
2. **The claim arises out of or relates to those contacts.** *Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial District Court* (2021) held that this phrase does not require strict causation: Ford had systematically marketed, sold and serviced the vehicle models in the forum states, and the plaintiffs were injured there, so the claims "relate to" Ford's forum activities. By contrast, *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court* (2017) held that California lacked specific jurisdiction over claims by nonresident plaintiffs who were not injured or prescribed the drug there.
3. **Reasonableness (fair play and substantial justice).** Courts weigh the burden on the defendant, the forum state's interest, the plaintiff's interest in effective relief, the interstate judicial system's interest in efficiency, and shared substantive social policies (*World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 1980; *Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court*, 1987). Strong contacts make it a rare defendant who defeats jurisdiction on this factor alone.

### Special problems

- **Stream of commerce.** *World-Wide Volkswagen* held that a car dealer's foreseeability that a car might travel to Oklahoma was not enough. Justices split in *Asahi* (a "stream of commerce plus" view requiring additional conduct directed at the forum, versus a view that awareness of the flow suffices), and a plurality in *J. McIntyre Machinery, Ltd. v. Nicastro* (2011) found no jurisdiction over a foreign manufacturer that did not target the forum state. After *Ford*, a manufacturer that serves a market for its product in the forum is subject to jurisdiction over in-state injuries from that product.
- **Intentional torts.** *Calder v. Jones* (1984) found jurisdiction where the defendants' intentional conduct was expressly aimed at the forum and the harm was felt there. After *Walden*, injury to a plaintiff who happens to live in the forum is not enough.
- **Contracts.** A contract with a forum resident does not by itself establish minimum contacts; courts look to negotiations, contemplated future dealings, terms and course of performance (*Burger King*).
- **Federal claims with no state forum.** FRCP 4(k)(2) lets a federal court exercise jurisdiction over a defendant who is subject to no state's courts, for a federal claim, if the defendant has sufficient contacts with the United States as a whole.

### Procedure

The plaintiff bears the burden of showing jurisdiction once it is contested. A motion under FRCP 12(b)(2) tolls the time to answer; if the court denies it, the answer is due 14 days after notice of the ruling (FRCP 12(a)(4)(A)). Remember that the defense is waived if omitted from the first Rule 12 motion or responsive pleading (FRCP 12(h)(1)).

### Applying it

For an out-of-state company, first ask whether it is "at home" (incorporation, principal place of business, or consent by registration where the statute provides). If not, analyze specific jurisdiction: availment, relatedness, then reasonableness.
@example
title: Out-of-state manufacturer and a local injury
problem: Brightway Co. is incorporated and headquartered in State A. It sells power tools through a distributor in State B and maintains a regional service center in State B. A State B resident is injured in State B by a Brightway tool and sues in State B. Does the court have personal jurisdiction over Brightway?
step: General jurisdiction first: Brightway is at home only in State A (incorporation and headquarters). Selling and servicing in State B does not make it at home there (Daimler, BNSF).
step: Specific jurisdiction, availment: Brightway deliberately serves the State B market through a distributor and a service center, so it purposefully availed itself of the forum.
step: Relatedness: the injury occurred in State B from a product Brightway markets and services there, which fits Ford ("arise out of or relate to," without strict causation).
step: Reasonableness: the burden on a manufacturer with a State B service center is modest, and State B has a strong interest in injuries to its residents. Conclude that specific jurisdiction exists.
@mistakes
- Treating substantial "doing business" in a state as making a corporation "at home" there; that is the argument BNSF rejected.
- Stopping the specific-jurisdiction analysis at contacts and omitting the relatedness and reasonableness steps.
- Using the plaintiff's contacts or injury alone as the defendant's contacts (Walden).
- Applying Bristol-Myers Squibb to a plaintiff injured in the forum, or misreading Ford to eliminate relatedness altogether.
