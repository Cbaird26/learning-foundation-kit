@article
**Preclusion** makes a final judgment binding in later litigation. It protects defendants from repeated suits, promotes finality and conserves judicial resources, and it prevents inconsistent results. It has two branches. Many courts use "res judicata" for both; this lesson uses **claim preclusion** and **issue preclusion**.

### Claim preclusion (res judicata)

A final judgment on the merits bars a later action by the same parties (or their privies) on the **same claim**. It bars not only what was litigated but everything that **could have been** litigated: the first judgment **merges** a winning claim and **bars** a losing one.

**Elements.**
1. **Same parties or privity** (a successor, assignee, or one in a legal relationship with a party such that the party represented the nonparty's interests).
2. **A valid final judgment on the merits** by a court of competent jurisdiction.
3. **The same claim.** Most courts, following the Restatement (Second) of Judgments § 24, use the **transactional test**: all claims arising from the same transaction or series of connected transactions are one claim, regardless of the legal theories or remedies sought. A few courts still use the narrower "primary rights" or "same evidence" tests.

**What counts as a judgment on the merits.** Judgments after trial, summary judgment, directed verdict, default judgment, a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal (which is not rendered non-final by a co-plaintiff's appeal: *Federated Department Stores, Inc. v. Moitie*, 1981) and dismissal with prejudice count. By Rule 41(b), an involuntary dismissal operates as an adjudication on the merits **unless** it is for lack of jurisdiction, improper venue or failure to join a required party under Rule 19. A voluntary dismissal without prejudice is not preclusive (but see the two-dismissal rule in Rule 41(a)(1)(B)).

**Limits.** A later action is not barred as to claims that **arose after** the first judgment, claims the first court reserved or could not hear (for example, because of a limit on its jurisdiction), or where the parties agreed to split the claim. A **compulsory counterclaim** (Rule 13(a)) not asserted is barred in later litigation, and the transactional test and Rule 13(a) work together.

### Issue preclusion (collateral estoppel)

Once an issue of fact or law is actually litigated and determined by a valid final judgment, that determination is **conclusive in a later action between the parties** (and, in some cases, with others), even on a different claim.

**Elements.**
1. The issue is **identical** to the one in the prior action.
2. The issue was **actually litigated** (a default or a stipulation ordinarily does not satisfy this).
3. The issue was **actually decided** and **necessary (essential) to the judgment**.
4. There was a **valid final judgment** on the merits.
5. The party against whom preclusion is asserted was a party (or in privity) and had a **full and fair opportunity** to litigate.

Preclusion does not apply when the party against whom it is asserted had a **lower burden** in the first case, or when new law or incompatible procedures make it unfair. For example, a criminal acquittal (beyond a reasonable doubt) usually does not bar a civil case in which the plaintiff must prove the same facts by a preponderance of the evidence.

### Mutuality

Traditionally, only parties to the first action could use it. Most modern courts have abandoned **mutuality**:

- **Defensive nonmutual issue preclusion** (a defendant uses a prior judgment against a plaintiff who lost on the issue against someone else) is generally allowed (*Blonder-Tongue Laboratories, Inc. v. University of Illinois Foundation*, 1971).
- **Offensive nonmutual issue preclusion** (a plaintiff uses a prior judgment against a defendant who lost) is allowed in federal court at the trial court's **discretion** (*Parklane Hosiery Co. v. Shore*, 1979). Courts decline when (a) the new plaintiff could easily have joined the earlier suit (a "**wait-and-see**" plaintiff), (b) the defendant had little incentive to defend vigorously the first time, (c) the judgment conflicts with other judgments, or (d) procedures available in the later case, such as a jury, were not available earlier. *Parklane* held that using issue preclusion in this way did not violate the Seventh Amendment.

A minority of states still require mutuality.

### Who is bound: nonparties

Due process generally prohibits binding a person who was not a party to the earlier case. *Taylor v. Sturgell* (2008) rejected "virtual representation" and listed the recognized exceptions: (1) an agreement to be bound, (2) a pre-existing substantive legal relationship (such as successor and assignor), (3) adequate representation (for example, class actions with proper procedures), (4) control of the earlier litigation, (5) a party who uses a proxy to relitigate, and (6) a special statutory scheme.

### Which court's law governs

Under the Full Faith and Credit Act, 28 U.S.C. § 1738, a federal court gives a **state-court judgment** the preclusive effect that the rendering state's courts would give it. For a **federal-court judgment**, federal law governs the effect of a federal-question judgment. For a federal **diversity** judgment, *Semtek International Inc. v. Lockheed Martin Corp.* (2001) held that federal common law applies and adopts the preclusion law of the **state in which the federal court sits**, unless that law conflicts with federal interests. That holding means a Rule 41(b) dismissal "on the merits" in a diversity case does not automatically bar suit in other states if the state's law would not so provide.

### Applying it

For a two-lawsuit problem: (1) identify the prior judgment, its finality and the court; (2) test claim preclusion (parties, merits, same transaction), (3) if it fails, test issue preclusion (the five elements and mutuality), (4) identify whose preclusion law governs, and (5) conclude.
@example
title: A two-lawsuit problem
problem: In Suit 1, Ana sues Ben for property damage to her car in a collision, and a jury finds Ben 100 percent at fault; judgment is entered and becomes final. Ana then sues Ben in Suit 2 for her personal injuries from the same collision. Ben also wants to use the Suit 1 finding in a separate case brought by Cy, a passenger in Ana's car, who sues Ben for his injuries. What are the results?
step: Ana v. Ben, claim preclusion: the same parties, a valid final judgment on the merits, and a claim from the same transaction (the collision) under the transactional test. Ana's personal injury claim could have been brought in Suit 1, so Suit 2 is barred.
step: Cy v. Ben, claim preclusion does not apply because Cy was not a party or in privity with Ana, and he never had his day in court.
step: Cy v. Ben, issue preclusion: Cy can try to use the finding that Ben was at fault offensively, because Ben was a party who fully litigated the issue (nonmutual offensive use). The court has discretion under Parklane, considering whether Cy could have joined Suit 1.
step: Conclude: Ana's second suit is barred. Cy is not bound by the judgment, but he may be able to use Ben's loss on fault against Ben.
@mistakes
- Treating a dismissal for lack of jurisdiction, venue or failure to join a party as a judgment on the merits.
- Applying claim preclusion only to claims actually litigated, ignoring those that could have been.
- Binding a nonparty to a prior judgment because her interests were similar (rejected in Taylor v. Sturgell).
- Applying issue preclusion to an issue that was not actually decided or was not necessary to the judgment.
