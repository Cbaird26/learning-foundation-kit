@article
**Discovery** is the pretrial process by which parties obtain information from each other and from nonparties. Its purposes are to avoid trial by surprise, narrow the issues and lead to settlement or summary judgment. The Federal Rules favor broad discovery limited by proportionality.

### Scope: Rule 26(b)(1)

Parties may obtain discovery of any **nonprivileged** matter that is **relevant to any party's claim or defense** and **proportional to the needs of the case**, considering the importance of the issues at stake, the amount in controversy, the parties' relative access to information, their resources, the importance of the discovery in resolving the issues, and whether the burden or expense outweighs the likely benefit. Information need **not be admissible** in evidence to be discoverable. Courts must limit discovery that is unreasonably cumulative, obtainable from a more convenient source, or outside the scope (Rule 26(b)(2)(C)). Electronically stored information (ESI) that is not reasonably accessible because of undue burden or cost need not be produced unless good cause is shown (Rule 26(b)(2)(B)).

### Timing and planning

- **Rule 26(f) conference.** The parties must confer as soon as practicable, and at least 21 days before the scheduling conference or order, to discuss claims and defenses, settlement, disclosures, preservation, ESI, privilege issues and a **discovery plan**. Generally, discovery cannot begin before the conference (Rule 26(d)(1)).
- **Rule 16(b).** The judge issues a **scheduling order** limiting time to join parties, amend pleadings, complete discovery and file motions. It is modified only for good cause and with the judge's consent.
- **Initial disclosures: Rule 26(a)(1).** Without waiting for a request, each party discloses within 14 days after the Rule 26(f) conference (unless stipulated or ordered otherwise): individuals likely to have discoverable information that it may use, documents and ESI it may use, a computation of damages, and applicable insurance agreements.
- **Expert disclosures: Rule 26(a)(2)** (at least 90 days before trial absent order or stipulation), and **pretrial disclosures: Rule 26(a)(3)** (at least 30 days before trial). Whether the expert's testimony is admissible is governed by Federal Rule of Evidence 702, amended December 1, 2023, so that the proponent must demonstrate to the court that it is more likely than not that each requirement is met.
- **Recent changes.** Effective December 1, 2025, amendments to Rules 16(b) and 26(f) require the parties to address, at the Rule 26(f) conference and in the discovery plan, how they will describe privileged or protected material that is withheld (the timing and method of the privilege log under Rule 26(b)(5)(A)), and permit the scheduling order to cover it. New Rule 16.1, also effective December 1, 2025, governs early management of multidistrict litigation.
- **Duty to supplement:** Rule 26(e). **Signature certification:** Rule 26(g).

### Privilege and work product

**Attorney-client privilege** protects confidential communications between client and lawyer made to obtain legal advice. It covers the communication, not the underlying **facts**: a client must disclose facts even if she told them to the lawyer (*Upjohn Co. v. United States*, 1981, which also extended the corporate privilege to communications with employees about matters within their duties, made to enable the lawyer to advise the company).

**Work product** (*Hickman v. Taylor*, 1947; Rule 26(b)(3)) protects documents and tangible things prepared in anticipation of litigation by or for a party or its representative. **Ordinary** work product (such as witness statements) is discoverable on a showing of **substantial need** and inability to obtain the substantial equivalent without **undue hardship**. **Opinion** work product (the lawyer's mental impressions, conclusions, opinions and legal theories) is given nearly absolute protection. Rule 26(b)(4) protects draft expert reports and most attorney-expert communications; a consulting (nontestifying) expert may be deposed only in exceptional circumstances.

A party withholding information as privileged or protected must **expressly claim** the protection and describe the nature of the withheld items so the other side can assess the claim (Rule 26(b)(5)(A), the privilege log). An inadvertent disclosure can be clawed back under Rule 26(b)(5)(B) and Federal Rule of Evidence 502, and a court may enter a Rule 502(d) order protecting against waiver. A **protective order** under Rule 26(c) for "good cause" protects a party from annoyance, embarrassment, oppression or undue burden.

### The devices

| Device | Rule | Key limits |
|---|---|---|
| Deposition (oral or written) | 30, 31 | 10 per side; 1 day of 7 hours per deponent absent stipulation or order |
| Interrogatories | 33 | 25 per party, including discrete subparts; answers within 30 days |
| Requests for production | 34 | Documents, ESI, tangible things, entry on land; 30 days |
| Physical or mental examination | 35 | By court order on good cause; condition must be in controversy |
| Requests for admission | 36 | Matter is **admitted** unless answered or objected to within 30 days |
| Subpoena | 45 | Nonparties; documents and testimony |

### Enforcement: Rule 37

A party must confer in good faith before moving to **compel** (Rule 37(a)(1)). Failure to obey an order exposes the party to sanctions. A party that fails to disclose information or a witness required by Rule 26(a) or (e) may not use it at trial, on a motion or at a hearing unless the failure was **substantially justified or harmless** (Rule 37(c)(1)). Under Rule 37(e), if ESI that should have been preserved is lost because a party failed to take reasonable steps, the court may order measures no greater than necessary to cure prejudice, and only upon finding **intent to deprive** may it presume the information was unfavorable, instruct the jury that it may or must presume so, or dismiss or enter default.

### Applying it

Ask whether the request is relevant and proportional, whether a privilege or protection applies and was properly claimed, which device fits, and what the time limit is.
@example
title: Scope and work product
problem: In a product liability suit, the plaintiff asks the manufacturer for (a) an engineer's internal email, written before the injury, discussing a defect in the design, and (b) the report of an investigator the manufacturer's lawyer hired after the suit was threatened, summarizing interviews of three witnesses. The manufacturer objects to both. How should the court rule on each?
step: Request (a): the email is relevant to the defect and was not prepared in anticipation of litigation, so it is not work product. It is not a communication with counsel, so it is not privileged. It is discoverable, even if it may be inadmissible, subject to proportionality.
step: Request (b): the report was prepared by a representative of a party after litigation was anticipated, so it is ordinary work product (Rule 26(b)(3)).
step: Ordinary work product is discoverable on a showing of substantial need and inability to obtain the equivalent without undue hardship. Because the witnesses can be interviewed or deposed by the plaintiff, the plaintiff likely cannot show it, unless a witness is now unavailable.
step: Conclude: compel production of (a); sustain the objection to (b) absent a showing of substantial need. The facts the witnesses know remain discoverable through depositions.
@mistakes
- Believing information must be admissible to be discoverable.
- Treating facts as privileged because the client told them to a lawyer.
- Forgetting that work product protection is qualified for ordinary work product but nearly absolute for opinion work product.
- Ignoring the numerical limits (10 depositions per side, 25 interrogatories) and the 30-day response time.

@practice self
prompt: A plaintiff seeks all of the defendant's emails over ten years about a product that causes \$12,000 in damages. The defendant objects on grounds of burden. Write a short analysis under Rule 26(b)(1) of whether the request is within scope and what the court might do.
hint: Apply the proportionality factors.
solution: Rule 26(b)(1) permits discovery that is relevant to a claim or defense and proportional to the needs of the case, considering the importance of the issues, the amount in controversy, the parties' relative access, their resources, the importance of the discovery and whether burden outweighs benefit. The emails may be relevant, but ten years of all emails for a \$12,000 claim is likely disproportionate: the amount in controversy is small, and the burden of searching ten years of ESI is high. The defendant has superior access to the information, which favors discovery, and the issue may be important if there is a safety question. The court is likely to narrow the request, for example by limiting the time period, custodians and search terms, under Rules 26(b)(2)(C) and 26(c), rather than denying discovery outright.
