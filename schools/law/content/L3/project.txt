title: Litigation file: where to sue, the complaint, and the motions to come
overview: From supplied facts about a product injury, you will decide where your client should sue and why, draft a complaint that contains its jurisdictional and venue allegations, and write a memo predicting the defendants' motions to dismiss and proposing a discovery plan. The project uses personal jurisdiction, subject-matter jurisdiction, venue, Erie, pleading, joinder and discovery together, the way a first-year associate would build a case file.
goal: Choose a forum by analyzing personal jurisdiction, subject-matter jurisdiction, venue, the forum-selection clause and limitations law for each defendant.
goal: Draft a complaint that satisfies Rule 8 and the plausibility standard and pleads the facts that support jurisdiction and venue.
goal: Anticipate the defendants' Rule 12 motions, transfer and removal, and prepare a discovery plan that follows Rules 16 and 26(f).
step: Read the materials below and make a fact chart for each party: citizenship (individual domicile, corporate state of incorporation and principal place of business, LLC members), forum contacts, and key dates (injury, today, service).
step: For each defendant, analyze personal jurisdiction in State A courts: general jurisdiction first, then specific jurisdiction (availment, relatedness, reasonableness), and the effect of the forum-selection clause in the website terms.
step: Analyze subject-matter jurisdiction for three options: a federal court in State A against Brightline alone, a federal court against both defendants, and a State A state court against both. Apply complete diversity, the amount in controversy, Rule 19 and § 1367, and the risk of removal.
step: Analyze venue under 28 U.S.C. § 1391(b) for the federal options and explain how a transfer motion under § 1404(a) would be decided. Then explain which state's statute of limitations applies in a federal diversity case and whether the claim is timely under the commencement rule.
step: Write a one-page forum recommendation: the court you will use, the reasons, the best alternative, and the two biggest risks.
step: Draft the complaint: caption, parties, jurisdiction and venue allegations (Rule 8(a)(1)), facts, counts, the relief demanded, and the jury demand. Label which allegations are facts and avoid conclusions without supporting facts.
step: Write the motions memo (about 800 to 1,200 words): the likely Rule 12(b)(2), 12(b)(3) and 12(b)(6) motions, the transfer or removal moves, how you would respond, and what you would add to the complaint now to reduce the risk.
step: Write the discovery plan as for a Rule 26(f) conference: subjects, initial disclosures, ESI custodians, depositions, experts (with Federal Rule of Evidence 702 in mind), privilege log method and timing under the December 2025 amendments, and the summary judgment schedule.
material: **Client facts.** Ana Lind is domiciled in State A. Eighteen months ago, at home in State A, the lid of a pressure cooker failed and sprayed boiling liquid on her. She has \$210,000 in medical bills and \$40,000 in lost wages, and a continuing nerve injury. Today's date is the date you begin the project. Suit has not been filed.
material: **Defendant 1: Brightline Appliances, Inc.** Brightline is incorporated in State B and has its headquarters in State B. It sells appliances through its own website to customers in all states, advertising in State A through paid ads targeted to State A users; about 4 percent of its sales go to State A residents, and it ships from a warehouse in State B. It has no office, employees or property in State A. It has a showroom in State C. Ana bought the cooker from Brightline's website and shipped it to her State A home. The website's terms of service, which Ana accepted by clicking a box, say that disputes "shall be brought exclusively in the state or federal courts located in State B" and that State B law governs. An email from Brightline's customer service to another customer, produced informally to Ana's counsel, says that the valve supplier "has had lid-valve complaints since last year."
material: **Defendant 2: Kestrel Components, LLC.** Kestrel manufactures the lid valve and sells it in bulk to Brightline under a supply contract that does not name any state of delivery. Kestrel is organized in State C and has its only plant and headquarters in State C. Its two members are Ben Okoro, domiciled in State A, and Carla Voss, domiciled in State C. Kestrel has no website sales to consumers and no office or employees in State A. Brightline's State A sales include cookers with Kestrel valves, and Kestrel's catalog lists "nationwide distribution through Brightline."
material: **Law of State A.** State A's long-arm statute reaches as far as the Due Process Clause allows. State A has a two-year limitations period for personal injury claims, and its courts hold that an action is commenced for limitations purposes when the complaint is filed and served within 60 days; service later than that means the action is deemed commenced at service. State A has three federal districts (Northern, Central and Southern). Ana lives in the Central District and was injured there. State A state courts of general jurisdiction hear the claim.
material: **Assignment notes.** State your assumptions where the facts are silent. Treat the case as a negligence and strict product liability action under State A law. All facts are invented, and nothing here is legal advice.
deliverable: A one-page forum recommendation memo.
deliverable: A draft complaint with jurisdiction, venue, facts, counts, relief and a jury demand.
deliverable: A motions memo (about 800 to 1,200 words) and a discovery plan.
rubric: The forum analysis correctly applies personal jurisdiction to each defendant, including the effect of the forum-selection clause, and reaches a justified conclusion.
rubric: Subject-matter jurisdiction is analyzed correctly (Kestrel's citizenship through its members, complete diversity, the amount in controversy, Rule 19, § 1367, and removal).
rubric: Venue and limitations (the Erie analysis and commencement under State A law) are applied correctly and a timing risk is identified.
rubric: The complaint separates facts from conclusions, pleads jurisdiction and venue, and would plausibly survive Rule 12(b)(6).

@review
### Week by week

- **Weeks 1 and 2, personal jurisdiction.** Two questions: a statute (long-arm or Rule 4(k)) and due process. Bases: presence (Burnham), domicile, consent (Mallory), and minimum contacts (International Shoe). General jurisdiction over a corporation: where it is at home (Daimler, BNSF). Specific jurisdiction: purposeful availment, a claim that arises out of or relates to the contacts (Ford; Walden; Bristol-Myers Squibb), then reasonableness. The defense is waived under Rule 12(h)(1) if omitted.
- **Weeks 3 and 4, federal courts.** Federal question (well-pleaded complaint, Grable). Diversity: complete diversity, citizenship rules (corporations, LLCs, domicile), more than \$75,000 exclusive of interest and costs. Supplemental jurisdiction (§ 1367(a), (b), (c)). Removal: defendants only, forum-defendant rule, unanimity, 30 days from service, one year in diversity cases.
- **Weeks 5 and 6, venue and Erie.** Section 1391(b), transfer under § 1404(a) and § 1406(a), Atlantic Marine. Erie, Hanna, the Rules Enabling Act, Walker v. Armco Steel (limitations and commencement), Klaxon.
- **Weeks 7 and 8, pleading and joinder.** Rule 8, Twombly and Iqbal, Rule 9(b), Rule 12(b)(6) and 12(d), Rule 15 (amendment, relation back). Rules 13, 14, 19, 20, 22, 23 and 24.
- **Weeks 9 and 10, discovery and summary judgment.** Scope and proportionality, work product, disclosures, the devices and their limits, Rule 37, the December 2025 changes (Rules 16(b), 26(f), and new Rule 16.1). Rule 56, Celotex, Anderson, Matsushita, Rule 56(d).
- **Weeks 11 and 12, trial, appeal and preclusion.** Seventh Amendment, Rules 50, 59 and 60(b), the final judgment rule and its exceptions, Trump v. CASA (injunction scope), claim and issue preclusion, Parklane, Taylor v. Sturgell, Semtek.

### Common errors

- Treating personal jurisdiction as non-waivable, or subject-matter jurisdiction as waivable.
- Counting an LLC like a corporation for diversity, or using residence for domicile.
- Using the plaintiff's contacts in the specific-jurisdiction analysis.
- Treating FRCP 3 as controlling state limitations periods in diversity cases.
- Raising Rule 50(b) without a prior Rule 50(a) motion.
- Applying claim preclusion only to what was actually litigated.

@exam mc
prompt: Dana, domiciled in State D, sues Corp. in a federal district in State D for \$500,000 in damages from an injury that happened in State E at a Corp. facility there. Corp. is incorporated in Delaware with its headquarters in State E, and it operates a retail store in State D that has no connection to the injury. State D has no consent-by-registration statute. Corp. timely moves to dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction and improper venue. What is the best ruling?
choice: Deny both motions, because Corp. does substantial business in State D and is therefore at home there.
choice*: Grant the personal jurisdiction motion, because Corp. is not at home in State D and the claim does not arise from or relate to its State D contacts; venue is also improper, so the court may dismiss or transfer to State E in the interest of justice.
choice: Deny the personal jurisdiction motion, because Rule 4(k)(1)(A) gives a federal court nationwide reach over corporations.
choice: Grant only the venue motion, because the State D store waives any personal jurisdiction objection.
hint: Check where Corp. is at home, then whether the claim relates to its State D store, then venue under § 1391(b).
solution: Corp. is at home in Delaware and State E; its State D store does not make it at home there (Daimler, BNSF) and the injury is unrelated to that store, so there is no specific jurisdiction. Venue in State D fails because Corp. does not reside there for this action and the events occurred in State E. Rule 4(k)(1)(A) ties federal reach to the state court's reach. The court may transfer to State E under § 1406(a) in the interest of justice.

@exam num
prompt: A plaintiff sues two defendants in State P court on a federal claim. The first defendant is formally served on July 1. Assume that this defendant removes on the last day permitted by 28 U.S.C. § 1446(b)(1) (30 days after service). Under FRCP 81(c)(2), after removal a defendant who has not answered must do so within the longest of 21 days after receiving the initial pleading, 21 days after being served with the summons, or 7 days after the notice of removal is filed. Assume no weekend or holiday issue. On what day of August is the answer due? Enter the day of the month.
answer: 7
check: (1 + 30 + 7) - 31
hint: July has 31 days. Find the removal date first, then compare the three periods.
solution: Thirty days after July 1 is July 31, the removal date. The 21-day periods (from July 1) end July 22. Seven days after July 31 is August 7, which is the longest period, so the answer is due August 7.

@exam self
prompt: Facts: Pru, domiciled in State X, is injured in State Y by a defective ladder sold by Hardware, Inc. (incorporated and headquartered in State Z). She sues Hardware in a State X federal court for \$200,000 under diversity jurisdiction. The complaint alleges that "Hardware acted negligently and defectively" without further facts. Hardware (a) moves to dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction (its only State X contacts are a few online orders), (b) moves to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6), and (c) argues that the claim is time-barred under a State X rule that treats an action as commenced when the defendant is served, which occurred after the two-year period but within two years of the filing. Write a short analysis of each motion and the likely ruling, naming the governing doctrines.
hint: Take each motion in turn: availment and relatedness for (a), Iqbal step one and two for (b), and Walker v. Armco Steel for (c).
solution: (a) Personal jurisdiction: Hardware is at home in State Z, not State X (Daimler). Specific jurisdiction requires purposeful availment and a claim that arises out of or relates to the forum contacts (Ford). The injury occurred in State Y and Pru's residence is not a defendant contact (Walden), so a few online orders would not support jurisdiction unless the ladder was one sold into State X; if Hardware deliberately sold the ladder to a State X buyer, jurisdiction may exist, and the facts need development. The defense is preserved because Hardware raised it in its first Rule 12 motion. (b) Rule 12(b)(6): "acted negligently and defectively" is conclusory and set aside under Iqbal; the complaint pleads no facts about the defect, the injury or causation, so it likely fails under Twombly and Iqbal, but dismissal is likely with leave to amend under Rule 15(a)(2) (or amendment as of course within 21 days of the motion). (c) Limitations: in a diversity case, Erie requires application of the state's limitations period and commencement rule, and under Walker v. Armco Steel, FRCP 3 does not displace a state rule that commencement occurs on service. Service came after the two-year period under State X law, so the claim is likely time-barred. Conclusion: Hardware likely wins the limitations argument and, on the facts as stated, may win personal jurisdiction as well, so Pru should add facts and consider where the claim is still timely.
rubric: Applies general and specific jurisdiction correctly and uses Walden and Ford.
rubric: Separates conclusions from facts under Iqbal and identifies amendment as the remedy.
rubric: Applies Erie and Walker to hold that State X's service-based commencement rule governs.
rubric: Reaches a clear conclusion on each motion.
