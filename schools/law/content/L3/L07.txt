@article
**Pleadings** (FRCP 7(a)) are the complaint, the answer, an answer to a counterclaim or cross-claim, a third-party complaint and any reply the court orders. They frame the dispute. A pleading system answers a basic question: how much must a plaintiff say to get into discovery?

### Notice pleading and its limit: Rule 8

FRCP 8(a) requires a complaint to contain (1) a short and plain statement of the grounds for the court's jurisdiction, (2) a short and plain statement of the claim showing that the pleader is entitled to relief, and (3) a demand for the relief sought. The Federal Rules replaced code pleading's "facts constituting the cause of action" with this simpler "notice pleading." For fifty years, *Conley v. Gibson* (1957) was read to dismiss only if "no set of facts" could support the claim.

### The plausibility standard

*Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly* (2007) retired the "no set of facts" language. An antitrust complaint alleging parallel conduct by competitors, plus a conclusory assertion of agreement, was dismissed: parallel conduct was as consistent with lawful independent action as with conspiracy. A complaint needs "enough facts to state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face." *Ashcroft v. Iqbal* (2009) applied this standard to all civil actions and described a **two-step** approach:

1. **Set aside conclusions.** Legal conclusions and bare recitals of the elements ("Defendant conspired," "acted with discriminatory purpose") are not entitled to the presumption of truth.
2. **Assess plausibility.** Take the remaining well-pleaded **factual** allegations as true and ask whether they permit a reasonable inference that the defendant is liable. This is a context-specific task that draws on judicial experience and common sense. Plausibility is more than possibility but less than probability, and the judge does not weigh evidence or choose between competing inferences on the merits.

*Swierkiewicz v. Sorema N.A.* (2002) still holds that a plaintiff need not plead a prima facie case of employment discrimination as a pleading requirement (it is an evidentiary framework). A plaintiff need not cite the correct legal theory (*Johnson v. City of Shelby*, 2014), provided the facts support some claim. Critics say plausibility invites judges to dismiss before discovery, when plaintiffs lack access to the defendants' information; defenders say it screens costly, meritless suits.

### Particularity: Rule 9(b)

In alleging **fraud or mistake**, a party must state "with particularity the circumstances constituting fraud or mistake." Courts commonly require the who, what, when, where and how. **Malice, intent, knowledge and other conditions of mind** may be alleged generally, though Iqbal says generally does not mean conclusorily. Special damage must be specifically stated (Rule 9(g)).

### Rule 11

By presenting a pleading, motion or paper, an attorney or unrepresented party certifies after reasonable inquiry that it is not presented for an improper purpose, that legal contentions are warranted by existing law or a nonfrivolous argument for changing it, and that factual contentions have (or, if specifically identified, will likely have) evidentiary support (Rule 11(b)). A motion for sanctions must be served on the other side but not filed or presented to the court until **21 days** after service, giving the target the chance to withdraw or correct the paper (the **safe harbor**, Rule 11(c)(2)).

### Responding to the complaint

A defendant answers or moves under Rule 12(b) within 21 days after service. **Rule 12(b)(6)** tests whether the complaint states a claim on which relief can be granted. The court accepts well-pleaded facts as true and considers the complaint, documents attached to it or incorporated by reference, and matters subject to judicial notice. If **matters outside the pleadings** are presented and not excluded, the motion **must be treated as one for summary judgment** under Rule 56, and all parties must have a reasonable opportunity to present pertinent material (Rule 12(d)). Rule 12(e) permits a motion for a more definite statement of a pleading so vague that a response is impossible, and Rule 12(f) permits striking redundant or immaterial matter.

The answer must admit or deny each allegation. Failing to deny an allegation (other than damages) admits it (Rule 8(b)(6)). **Affirmative defenses**, such as statute of limitations, contributory negligence, release and statute of frauds, must be stated in the answer (Rule 8(c)) or are generally waived.

### Amendment: Rule 15

A party may **amend once as a matter of course** within 21 days after serving the pleading, or, if the pleading requires a response, within 21 days after service of the responsive pleading or of a Rule 12(b), (e) or (f) motion, whichever is earlier (Rule 15(a)(1)). Otherwise, amendment requires the opposing party's written consent or leave of court, which is to be "freely given when justice so requires" (Rule 15(a)(2)). Because dismissal for failure to state a claim is often with leave to amend, a complaint's weakness is often a first draft problem.

**Relation back** (Rule 15(c)(1)): an amendment relates back to the date of the original pleading if the law providing the limitations period allows it, or if it asserts a claim or defense that **arose out of the same conduct, transaction or occurrence** as the original. An amendment that **changes the party** relates back only if, within the Rule 4(m) period (90 days after the complaint is filed, unless extended), the new party received notice so that it will not be prejudiced, and knew or should have known that the action would have been brought against it but for a **mistake concerning the proper party's identity**. *Krupski v. Costa Crociere S.p.A.* (2010) held that the focus is on what the prospective defendant knew or should have known, not on the plaintiff's diligence or knowledge.

### Applying it

When you evaluate a complaint, label each allegation as a fact or a conclusion, discard the conclusions, and ask whether the remaining facts support each element of a recognized claim.
@example
title: Evaluating a complaint
problem: A complaint alleges: "Defendant Bank committed fraud and acted with intent to deceive. On June 4, Bank's loan officer, Lee, told Plaintiff in a signed email that the loan carried a fixed rate of 5 percent for ten years. Bank's loan documents, which Lee knew were inconsistent with the email, set a variable rate. Plaintiff relied on the email and signed, and paid \$14,000 more interest than the fixed rate would have required." Bank moves to dismiss under Rules 9(b) and 12(b)(6). How should the court rule?
step: Separate facts from conclusions. "Committed fraud" and "acted with intent to deceive" are conclusions. The date, the speaker, the signed email, the misstatement, the contradictory documents, reliance and the \$14,000 loss are facts.
step: Apply Rule 9(b). The circumstances of the alleged fraud (who, what, when, how) are stated with particularity. Intent and knowledge may be alleged generally, and here Lee's knowledge is supported by facts.
step: Apply Iqbal step two. The facts support a reasonable inference of misrepresentation, reliance and damages, which are the elements of fraud.
step: Conclude that the motion should be denied. The conclusory labels are disregarded, but the facts suffice.
@mistakes
- Believing that a plaintiff must plead evidence or prove the claim at the motion to dismiss stage.
- Treating the "no set of facts" language of Conley as current law.
- Applying Rule 9(b) particularity to intent and knowledge, which may be alleged generally.
- Considering affidavits on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion without converting it to summary judgment under Rule 12(d).
