@article
**Personal jurisdiction** (also called jurisdiction in personam) is a court's power to bind a particular defendant. It is distinct from **subject-matter jurisdiction** (power over the type of case) and from **venue** (the proper place within a system of courts). A judgment entered without personal jurisdiction is void as to that defendant, and the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment is the constitutional limit on state courts. Federal courts borrow that limit through FRCP 4(k)(1)(A).

### The territorial era: Pennoyer v. Neff (1878)

*Pennoyer v. Neff* treated a state as a sovereign with power over persons and property inside its borders and no power outside them. A state could reach a defendant who was **present** in the state and personally served there, or property located in the state (in rem and quasi in rem jurisdiction). A default judgment against a nonresident served only by publication was void, and the Fourteenth Amendment gave the loser a way to attack it later.

The model strained as commerce crossed borders. *Hess v. Pawloski* (1927) accepted a fiction of **implied consent**: a nonresident motorist who drove on a state's roads was deemed to appoint a state official to receive process. *Milliken v. Meyer* (1940) upheld jurisdiction over a **domiciliary** even when he was served outside the state.

### The modern test: International Shoe Co. v. Washington (1945)

*International Shoe* held that a state may exercise jurisdiction over an out-of-state defendant who has "certain minimum contacts" with the forum, such that the suit does not offend "traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice." The case involved a corporation whose salesmen worked in Washington and who was sued for unpaid contributions to the state unemployment fund. The test replaced the question "is the defendant present?" with a question about the relationship among the **defendant, the forum and the litigation**.

Later cases divided the doctrine into **general** (all-purpose) and **specific** (case-linked) jurisdiction, which you will study in the next lesson.

### Traditional bases that survive

- **Presence (tag jurisdiction).** *Burnham v. Superior Court* (1990) unanimously upheld jurisdiction over a nonresident personally served while voluntarily in the state, even though the claim was unrelated to his visit. Justices disagreed on why (a plurality relied on historical tradition; others relied on purposeful availment of the state's benefits). Courts generally refuse tag jurisdiction when the defendant was brought into the state by fraud or force.
- **Domicile.** A person is subject to general jurisdiction where he or she is domiciled.
- **Consent.** A party may consent in advance (forum-selection clause), by appearance, or by failing to object. *Mallory v. Norfolk Southern Railway Co.* (2023) held that a state statute making registration to do business consent to general jurisdiction did not violate due process on those facts.

### Two questions, not one

In every case ask (1) whether a **statute** authorizes jurisdiction (a long-arm statute, or Rule 4(k) in federal court) and (2) whether exercising it satisfies **due process**. Many state long-arm statutes reach as far as the Constitution allows, which collapses the two questions into one; others list specific acts (transacting business, committing a tort in the state), so the statute must be satisfied first. Notice is separate: service must be "reasonably calculated" to inform the defendant (*Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co.*, 1950).

### Raising and waiving the defense

Lack of personal jurisdiction is raised by motion under **FRCP 12(b)(2)** or in the answer. It is **waivable**: under **FRCP 12(h)(1)**, the defense is lost if the defendant omits it from a pre-answer Rule 12 motion (whatever ground that motion raises), or fails to include it in the first responsive pleading. This is a consolidation rule, unlike subject-matter jurisdiction, which can never be waived. The answer is due 21 days after service of the summons and complaint (FRCP 12(a)(1)(A)(i)), or 60 days after the request was sent if service was waived under Rule 4(d).

### Policy

Territorial limits protect defendants from distant, burdensome litigation and respect state sovereignty; the contacts test also lets states protect residents and regulate those who do business there.

### Applying it

State the rule (statute plus due process), identify the basis claimed (presence, domicile, consent, minimum contacts), apply it to the defendant's own conduct and the forum, and conclude. Do not rely on the plaintiff's convenience or residence alone.
@example
title: Served at a trade show
problem: Dana lives in State A. She flies to State B for one day to attend a trade show and is handed a summons while in the hotel lobby. The suit by a State B resident claims breach of a contract made and performed entirely in State A. Dana moves to dismiss under FRCP 12(b)(2). How should the court rule?
step: Identify the basis. The plaintiff relies on personal service in State B, which is tag jurisdiction, not on minimum contacts.
step: Apply Burnham. All nine Justices agreed that personal service on a nonresident voluntarily present in the state satisfies due process, even for an unrelated claim.
step: Check for the exception. Dana came voluntarily, and nothing suggests she was lured by fraud or brought by force.
step: Conclude: the court has personal jurisdiction, and the motion should be denied. (Dana may still seek dismissal on other grounds such as forum non conveniens, but not for lack of personal jurisdiction.)
@mistakes
- Treating personal jurisdiction as non-waivable like subject-matter jurisdiction; it is lost under FRCP 12(h)(1) if omitted from the first Rule 12 motion or responsive pleading.
- Skipping the long-arm statute and analyzing only due process (or the reverse).
- Using the plaintiff's residence or convenience as the contact; the defendant's own contacts with the forum count.
- Applying Pennoyer's strict territoriality as if International Shoe had never been decided.
