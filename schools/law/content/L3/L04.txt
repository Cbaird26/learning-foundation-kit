### Supplemental jurisdiction: 28 U.S.C. § 1367

Once a federal court has a claim within its original jurisdiction, it may hear related claims that lack an independent jurisdictional basis. The statute, which codified and expanded *United Mine Workers v. Gibbs* (1966), has three parts.

**Section 1367(a): the grant.** Supplemental jurisdiction extends to claims "so related" to the original claims that they form part of the same Article III case or controversy, which means they derive from a **common nucleus of operative fact**. It includes claims that involve joining or intervening of additional parties.

**Section 1367(b): limits in diversity cases.** When the original jurisdiction rests only on § 1332, the court may not exercise supplemental jurisdiction over (1) claims **by plaintiffs** against persons made parties under Rules 14, 19, 20 or 24, or (2) claims by persons proposed to be joined as plaintiffs under Rule 19 or seeking to intervene as plaintiffs under Rule 24, when doing so would be inconsistent with § 1332. This codifies the result of *Owen Equipment & Erection Co. v. Kroger* (1978): a plaintiff cannot sue a nondiverse third-party defendant in a diversity case through supplemental jurisdiction. Section 1367(b) does not restrict claims by **defendants**: a defendant's compulsory counterclaim, cross-claim, or Rule 14 impleader claim may proceed.

**Section 1367(c): discretion.** The court may decline jurisdiction if (1) the claim raises a novel or complex state-law issue, (2) the state claim substantially predominates, (3) the court has dismissed all claims within original jurisdiction, or (4) in exceptional circumstances there are other compelling reasons. When the federal claims drop out early, the usual result is dismissal or remand of the state claims. Section 1367(d) tolls the state limitations period while the claim is pending and for 30 days after dismissal.

**Amount in controversy and complete diversity.** *Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Allapattah Services, Inc.* (2005) held that once one plaintiff in a diversity case satisfies the amount in controversy, the court may exercise supplemental jurisdiction over other plaintiffs' claims that fall short (and class members' claims), as long as complete diversity exists. Complete diversity itself is never supplied by § 1367.

**Counterclaims.** A **compulsory** counterclaim (FRCP 13(a): same transaction or occurrence) falls within supplemental jurisdiction. A **permissive** counterclaim generally needs its own jurisdictional basis, though some courts allow it under § 1367 when it shares a common nucleus.

### Removal: 28 U.S.C. §§ 1441, 1446 and 1447

A **defendant** (not a plaintiff) may remove a civil action from state court to the federal district court for the district where the action is pending if the action could have been filed in federal court originally (§ 1441(a)).

**Requirements.**
- **Original jurisdiction.** The case must fall within federal question or diversity jurisdiction (or another basis). Removal on federal question grounds follows the well-pleaded complaint rule. A federal defense or federal counterclaim does not support removal. Only an original defendant may remove: a plaintiff facing a counterclaim cannot (*Shamrock Oil & Gas Corp. v. Sheets*, 1941), a third-party counterclaim defendant cannot (*Home Depot U.S.A., Inc. v. Jackson*, 2019), and most courts hold the same for third-party defendants.
- **Forum-defendant rule.** An action removable solely on diversity grounds may not be removed if any properly joined and served defendant is a citizen of the forum state (§ 1441(b)(2)).
- **Unanimity.** All defendants who have been properly joined and served must join in or consent to removal (§ 1446(b)(2)(A)).
- **Timing.** The notice of removal must be filed within **30 days** after the defendant receives, through formal service, the initial pleading (*Murphy Bros., Inc. v. Michetti Pipe Stringing, Inc.*, 1999). Each defendant has 30 days from its own service, and later-served defendants may remove with earlier-served defendants consenting (§ 1446(b)(2)(B)–(C)). A diversity case may not be removed more than **one year** after commencement unless the plaintiff acted in bad faith to prevent removal (§ 1446(c)(1)).
- **Nonremovable actions.** Some actions cannot be removed, such as certain workers' compensation and Federal Employers' Liability Act claims (§ 1445).
- **Amount in controversy.** The notice need contain only a plausible allegation that the amount exceeds the threshold (*Dart Cherokee Basin Operating Co. v. Owens*, 2014).

**Remand.** A plaintiff may move to remand. A motion based on a defect other than lack of SMJ must be made within **30 days** after the notice of removal (§ 1447(c)); a lack of SMJ can be raised at any time before final judgment, and the court must remand. An order remanding on SMJ or a procedural defect is generally not reviewable on appeal (§ 1447(d)), with narrow exceptions. The court may award costs and attorney's fees if the removal lacked an objectively reasonable basis. Courts also apply the **fraudulent joinder** doctrine: a nondiverse or forum defendant who has no reasonable possibility of liability is disregarded.

### Policy

Supplemental jurisdiction promotes efficiency by letting one court hear the whole dispute. Removal lets a defendant choose the federal forum that a plaintiff could have chosen. Congress keeps the forum-defendant rule and the unanimity rule because the premise of diversity jurisdiction (protection against local bias) is weaker when a defendant is local, and removal is construed narrowly with doubts resolved in favor of remand.

### Applying it

To decide whether a case belongs in federal court, first identify an original basis (federal question or diversity with the amount in controversy). Then test each additional claim under § 1367. If the case started in state court, run the removal checklist: original jurisdiction, defendant who removes, forum-defendant rule, consent, timing.

title: Removal checklist
problem: Pia, domiciled in State P, sues Quik Inc. (incorporated and headquartered in State Q) and Ron (domiciled in State P) in State P court for \$120,000 in negligence damages. Quik is served on March 1 and removes to federal court on March 20 with Ron's consent. Pia moves to remand. Is removal proper?
step: Identify the basis for original jurisdiction: only diversity, since the claim is state negligence law.
step: Test complete diversity. Pia and Ron are both citizens of State P, so diversity is incomplete and the case could not have been filed in federal court originally.
step: Note the forum-defendant rule problem too: Ron is a forum-state citizen. But the more basic defect is lack of original jurisdiction.
step: Conclude: removal is improper and the court must remand under § 1447(c). Timely notice and unanimity cannot cure the lack of SMJ.
@mistakes
- Forgetting that only defendants can remove, and that a federal defense does not make a case removable.
- Applying the forum-defendant rule to federal question cases; it applies only when removal rests solely on diversity.
- Applying § 1367(b) to defendants' claims; it limits plaintiffs' claims against parties joined under Rules 14, 19, 20 and 24.
- Measuring the 30 days from the day the defendant first hears about the suit instead of from formal service.
