@article
When parents live in different states, two questions arise: which state's court may decide **custody**, and which may set or change **child support**. Without uniform rules, parents raced to the courthouse and moved children to find a favorable court. Two uniform acts, adopted in essentially every state, solve this.

### Custody: the UCCJEA

The **Uniform Child Custody Jurisdiction and Enforcement Act (UCCJEA)** (1997) supplies subject-matter jurisdiction rules for custody. The federal **Parental Kidnapping Prevention Act (PKPA)**, 28 U.S.C. § 1738A, requires states to enforce other states' custody orders made consistently with similar rules. The UCCJEA has been adopted in nearly all states and the District of Columbia.

**Initial determination (§ 201).** A state may make an initial custody determination only in this order of priority:

1. **Home state.** The state that is the child's home state on the date of filing, or was the home state within six months before filing and the child is absent from it but a parent or person acting as a parent **continues to live** there. **Home state** means the state where the child lived with a parent or person acting as a parent for at least **six consecutive months immediately before the case began** (for a child under six months old, since birth). Temporary absences count as part of the period.
2. **Significant-connection jurisdiction**, if there is no home state, or the home state has declined jurisdiction because another state is a more appropriate forum: the child and at least one parent have a significant connection with the state beyond mere presence, and substantial evidence about the child is there.
3. **More appropriate forum**, if all courts with priority decline.
4. **Default jurisdiction**, if no state qualifies under the first three.

Personal jurisdiction over the parents is **not required** for a custody determination; the child's status is the basis, though notice and an opportunity to be heard must be given.

**Temporary emergency jurisdiction (§ 204).** A state where the child is present may issue a temporary order if the child has been abandoned or an emergency requires protection from mistreatment or abuse of the child, a sibling or a parent. The order lasts until the state with jurisdiction acts.

**Exclusive continuing jurisdiction (§ 202).** The state that made the initial determination keeps exclusive jurisdiction to modify until (a) a court of that state finds that neither the child, nor the child and one parent, nor the child and a person acting as a parent has a significant connection with the state and that substantial evidence is no longer available there, or (b) a court of any state finds that the child and the parents have all left the state. A different state may **modify** (§ 203) only if the original state has lost or declined exclusive continuing jurisdiction and the new state has jurisdiction under § 201.

**Other provisions.** Courts must stay a case when another state is proceeding with jurisdiction (§ 206, simultaneous proceedings); a court may decline as an **inconvenient forum** (§ 207); and a court may decline jurisdiction obtained by **unjustifiable conduct**, such as abduction (§ 208). The Act provides for registration and **expedited enforcement** of other states' orders.

### International abductions: the Hague Convention

The **Hague Convention on the Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction** (implemented in the United States by the International Child Abduction Remedies Act) requires prompt return of a child wrongfully removed to or retained in a country from the child's **habitual residence**, subject to exceptions (such as grave risk of harm). *Abbott v. Abbott* (2010) held that a ne exeat right (a parent's right to veto the child's removal from the country) is a right of custody under the Convention. *Monasky v. Taglieri* (2020) held that habitual residence is determined by the **totality of the circumstances** rather than a requirement of the parents' actual agreement, and the factual finding is reviewed for clear error. *Golan v. Saada* (2022) held that a court deciding on the grave-risk exception is not required to consider ameliorative measures that could ensure the child's safe return before denying return, though it may.

### Support: UIFSA

The **Uniform Interstate Family Support Act (UIFSA)** (every state must enact it as a condition of federal funding) gives a **one-order system**: only one support order is controlling at a time.

- **Long-arm jurisdiction (§ 201)** over a nonresident for support cases rests on bases such as personal service in the state, consent, residence in the state with the child, the child's residing in the state because of the person's acts or directives, and conduct in the state that may have conceived the child. (Constitutional due process still applies; compare *Kulko v. Superior Court* from Week 3.)
- **Continuing, exclusive jurisdiction (CEJ) (§ 205).** The state that issued the support order keeps exclusive jurisdiction to modify it as long as the obligor, the obligee or the child continues to reside there, or unless the parties file written consent in another state to modification.
- **Modification (§ 611).** If no party and no child resides in the issuing state, a party who lives elsewhere may register the order and ask a state with personal jurisdiction over the respondent to modify it. A petitioner who is a nonresident of the state where the respondent lives must ask the respondent's state, and the respondent's state must have personal jurisdiction over the respondent. A court that modifies applies the issuing state's law on the **duration** of the support (§ 604).
- **Spousal support.** A tribunal of the state that issued a spousal support order keeps continuing, exclusive jurisdiction to modify it; other states may enforce it but not modify it.
- **Enforcement.** An income-withholding order from one state may be sent **directly to an employer** in another state. Federal law (28 U.S.C. § 1738B) requires full faith and credit to child-support orders.

### Applying the rules: IRAC for custody jurisdiction

First ask whether there is already an order, and which state has CEJ. If none, find the home state (six consecutive months immediately before filing, or within six months before filing with a parent still there). If none, test significant connection, then forum issues. Check emergency facts. Then ask whether another state's proceeding was filed first.

@example
title: Finding the home state
problem: Fran and Gus live in State A with their child, who was born there. On March 1 Fran moves with the child to State B. Gus stays in State A. On April 20 Fran files for custody in State B, and on May 10 Gus files in State A. Which state may make the initial custody determination?
step: State B's stay is only seven weeks, so State B is not the home state; the child did not live there for six consecutive months before April 20.
step: State A was the home state within six months before the filing, and a parent (Gus) continues to live there, so State A has home-state jurisdiction (the extended home-state rule).
step: State B has no jurisdiction under § 201 while State A has home-state jurisdiction, so State B must decline (and, if a case is also pending in State A, the simultaneous-proceedings rule of § 206 points the same way).
step: Conclusion: State A has jurisdiction; Fran should litigate custody there.

@mistakes
- Treating the child's presence in a state as enough for jurisdiction; presence alone does not create home-state status.
- Counting six months from the date the child left the state; the period is measured backward from the date of filing.
- Believing the state of the child's current residence may modify an order whenever the child moves; the issuing state keeps exclusive continuing jurisdiction as long as the statutory connection exists.
- Applying UIFSA CEJ to custody, or the UCCJEA to child support; they govern different issues.

@practice self
prompt: Deliverable: decide which state may hear a custody case under the UCCJEA. Harper, Ian and their child Jo (age 5) lived in State X from Jo's birth until June 1, when Harper took Jo to State Y to live with her parents. Ian remained in State X. No custody case has been filed anywhere. On September 15 Harper files for custody in State Y. On October 1 Ian files in State X. Which state has jurisdiction and why, and what should each court do?
solution: Issue: which state may make the initial custody determination under UCCJEA § 201. Rule: home-state jurisdiction is first; the home state is where the child lived with a parent for six consecutive months immediately before filing, or was the home state within six months before filing and a parent continues to live there. Application: Jo lived in State X from birth until June 1, so State X was the home state as of June 1. Jo has lived in State Y only since June 1, which is about three and a half months before September 15, so State Y is not the home state. On September 15, State X was the home state within six months before filing, and Ian still lives there, so State X has extended home-state jurisdiction. Under § 206 the court of State Y, which is not the proper court, must communicate with State X and defer (stay or dismiss); State X's court should proceed (assuming it was the first state with jurisdiction under § 201). Harper's removal is not decisive, though a court may also weigh unjustifiable conduct under § 208. Conclusion: State X may hear the case. Harper can be heard there, and State Y should not decide custody unless State X declines as an inconvenient forum.
rubric: States the home-state definition including the six-month extended rule.
rubric: Counts the time correctly and concludes that State X, not State Y, has jurisdiction.
rubric: Explains what each court should do (State Y defers; State X proceeds) and mentions inconvenient forum or emergency exceptions.
hint: Count back from the filing date and ask where Jo lived with a parent for six consecutive months.
