@article
Many couples live together for years without marrying. Marriage brings default rules on property division, support and inheritance. Cohabitants get none of those defaults, so the question is what they can claim on their own. Couples who do marry can change the defaults by contract in advance. This lesson covers both.

### Rule: cohabitants and Marvin

In *Marvin v. Marvin* (1976), the California Supreme Court held that unmarried cohabitants may enforce **express contracts** about property and earnings, and that courts may also find **implied-in-fact contracts**, **implied agreements**, or award relief in **quantum meruit** or by **constructive or resulting trust**, unless the agreement rests on an explicit exchange for sexual services alone. The court did not create marital rights; it applied ordinary contract and equity principles.

Most states that have addressed the question follow *Marvin* in some form. A minority refuses to enforce agreements between cohabitants in whole or part; the leading example is *Hewitt v. Hewitt* (Illinois, 1979), which reasoned that rights of unmarried partners are for the legislature, and Illinois reaffirmed that view in *Blumenthal v. Brewer* (2016). Some states require the agreement to be in writing (the statute of frauds or a special statute). A handful of states recognize domestic partnerships or registered partnerships with defined rights, and the American Law Institute's *Principles of the Law of Family Dissolution* proposes treating long-term "domestic partners" like spouses; courts have not generally adopted that proposal.

Quantum meruit awards the reasonable value of services rendered with the expectation of payment, less the reasonable value of support received.

### Rule: premarital agreements

A **premarital (prenuptial) agreement** is a contract made in contemplation of marriage and effective on marriage. Under the **Uniform Premarital Agreement Act (UPAA)** (1983), which many states have adopted in some form, the agreement must be in writing and signed by both parties; no consideration is needed. The parties may contract about:

- rights in property, whenever and wherever acquired;
- the disposition of property on separation, divorce or death;
- modification or elimination of spousal support;
- wills, trusts and life insurance beneficiaries;
- the choice of law; and
- any other matter not violating public policy or a criminal statute.

They **may not** adversely affect a child's right to **child support** (UPAA § 3(b)). Custody and parenting terms are likewise not binding on a court, which decides those by the child's best interests.

### When an agreement is unenforceable (UPAA § 6)

The party resisting enforcement must prove one of two things:

1. The agreement was **not executed voluntarily**; or
2. It was **unconscionable when executed** **and**, before signing, that party (a) was not given a fair and reasonable disclosure of the other's property and obligations, (b) did not voluntarily waive disclosure in writing, and (c) did not have, and reasonably could not have had, adequate knowledge of them.

So under the UPAA, an unfair agreement is enforceable if disclosure was adequate. Voluntariness turns on duress, the timing of the presentation (for example, a few days before the wedding), whether the party had independent counsel or the chance to get it, and whether the party understood the terms. Also under § 6, if modifying support would leave a party eligible for public assistance, the court may order support despite the waiver.

**Variations.** Some states test unconscionability at enforcement (not just at signing), or look for "manifest hardship," and some require independent counsel for a spousal support waiver. The **Uniform Premarital and Marital Agreements Act (UPMAA)** (2012), adopted in a few states, adds a requirement of adequate financial disclosure and an opportunity for counsel, and lets a court refuse enforcement of a term causing substantial hardship from circumstances arising after signing.

### The policy

Courts favor private ordering and certainty, especially for second marriages, family businesses and inheritances. They guard against overreaching because the parties are not bargaining at arm's length: they trust each other and are under social pressure to marry.

### Applying the rules: IRAC

Issue: is the agreement enforceable? Rule: the governing statute (UPAA or the state's version). Application: take each element, voluntariness first, then disclosure and unconscionability, then whether the clause at issue is one the parties can control (child support cannot be waived). Conclusion: enforce, enforce except for specific clauses, or refuse.

@example
title: Enforceability under the UPAA
problem: Dana and Eli sign a written premarital agreement six weeks before their wedding. It says that each keeps his or her own property and that neither will seek alimony. Eli attached a complete schedule of his assets, and Dana, who read it, had a lawyer of her choice review the agreement and chose to sign. Years later Dana, who earns far less, claims the alimony waiver is unconscionable and unenforceable. The state follows UPAA § 6. Is the waiver enforceable?
step: Voluntariness. Dana had six weeks, saw the assets schedule and had a lawyer. Nothing suggests duress, so she cannot show involuntariness.
step: The second route requires unconscionability when executed and lack of disclosure. Eli gave full disclosure, so even if the waiver was one-sided, Dana cannot satisfy the disclosure elements.
step: Check the public-assistance exception only if Dana would be eligible for public assistance without support; the facts do not say so.
step: Conclusion: the alimony waiver is enforceable under the UPAA.

@mistakes
- Treating unconscionability alone as enough to void an agreement under the UPAA; the UPAA also requires that the challenger lacked disclosure and knowledge.
- Letting parents bargain away child support or fix custody in a premarital agreement; courts will not be bound.
- Assuming cohabitants have marital-type rights automatically; Marvin enforces contracts and equitable claims, and Illinois and a few other states reject even those.
- Forgetting that a premarital agreement takes effect only on marriage.

@practice self
prompt: Deliverable: decide whether the premarital agreement is enforceable. Fay (a physician with substantial savings) presented a written agreement to Gil (a teacher) two days before the wedding, after invitations were sent and a venue had been paid for. It waived all alimony and gave Gil none of Fay's premarital property or its growth. Fay's lawyer drafted it; Gil was told he could consult a lawyer but said there was no time, and he signed without one. No financial schedule was attached, though Gil knew Fay was well paid and owned a home. Twelve years and two children later, they divorce, and Gil asks the court to disregard the agreement. Assume the state follows UPAA § 6. Write an IRAC analysis.
solution: Issue: whether Gil can show under UPAA § 6 that the agreement is unenforceable. Rule: the challenger must prove either that he did not sign voluntarily, or that the agreement was unconscionable when executed and he had no fair and reasonable disclosure, did not waive disclosure in writing, and lacked adequate knowledge of the other's finances. Application: voluntariness is a factual question. The timing (two days before the wedding, after costs had been incurred), the absence of counsel, and the one-sided terms support a claim of pressure, but Gil was told he could consult a lawyer and chose not to, and there is no express threat to cancel, so a court could find voluntariness. On the second route, the terms are one-sided (no alimony for a lower earner and no share of property), which supports unconscionability at signing, and no schedule was attached or disclosure waived in writing. The remaining question is knowledge: Gil knew generally that Fay was well paid and owned a home, which could show adequate general knowledge, but he did not know the amount of her savings. Conclusion: this is a close case. The best argument for Gil is the second route (unconscionable plus no disclosure, with knowledge only of the general nature of Fay's assets). A court could enforce on the ground that Gil had adequate general knowledge and signed voluntarily, so an answer reaching either result is acceptable if it applies each element. Child support and custody provisions would not be affected either way.
rubric: States the UPAA § 6 test with both routes and the three disclosure-related elements for the unconscionability route.
rubric: Applies each element to the facts (timing, counsel, disclosure, knowledge, terms) and notes which side each fact helps.
rubric: Reaches a reasoned conclusion and recognizes that child support and custody cannot be controlled by the agreement.
hint: Take voluntariness first, then unconscionability, then the three disclosure elements.
