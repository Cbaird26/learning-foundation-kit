@article
On divorce a court must (1) **classify** each asset and debt as separate or marital, (2) **value** the marital property, and (3) **divide** it. States use one of two systems, and the classification step is similar in both.

### Two systems

**Community property.** Nine states (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington and Wisconsin) treat most property acquired by either spouse **during the marriage** as owned by the community, one-half by each spouse, no matter whose name is on the title. Alaska lets couples opt in. On divorce, community property is divided **equally** in most community states; Texas directs a "just and right" division, which may be unequal. Each spouse keeps his or her separate property.

**Equitable distribution.** The other states treat property acquired by either spouse during the marriage as **marital property** that the court divides **equitably** (fairly, not necessarily equally) after considering statutory factors. The Uniform Marriage and Divorce Act (§ 307) lists factors such as the length of the marriage, each spouse's contribution (including as homemaker), economic circumstances, and each spouse's opportunity to acquire assets in the future. Many states also consider fault that affected the marital estate (for example, **dissipation**, the waste or hiding of assets) but disregard ordinary marital fault. A few states presume an equal split; most do not.

**Policy.** Both systems treat marriage as an economic partnership and recognize unpaid homemaking and child care as contributions. Community property gives a firm default; equitable distribution gives discretion.

### Classifying property

**Separate property** usually includes: property owned before marriage; property acquired during marriage by **gift or inheritance** to one spouse; and, in most states, compensation for personal injury (except for lost wages or medical expenses paid from marital funds). Property acquired in exchange for separate property remains separate (**tracing**). Marital property (or community property) includes earnings and everything bought with earnings during the marriage, including retirement benefits earned during the marriage.

- **Commingling.** Mixing separate funds with marital funds may turn the whole into marital property if the separate part cannot be traced.
- **Transmutation.** Spouses may change the character of property by agreement or conduct, for instance by retitling a separate asset in joint names. Some states require a writing (California requires an express written declaration), and some presume a gift to the marriage.
- **Appreciation.** The increase in value of separate property is usually separate if **passive** (the market did it), and marital in whole or part if **active** (the effort of a spouse during the marriage caused it).

### Special assets

- **Pensions.** The portion earned during the marriage is marital. The **time rule** (coverture fraction) is: months of service during the marriage divided by months of total service when the pension starts, times each payment, then divided according to the state's rule. Courts divide ERISA-governed plans with a **qualified domestic relations order** (QDRO).
- **Professional degrees.** Most states hold that a degree or license is not property, because its value is too speculative, but they give the supporting spouse **reimbursement** or rehabilitative support. *O'Brien v. O'Brien* (New York, 1985) is the best-known contrary case, and later New York legislation limited its reach.
- **Business goodwill.** **Enterprise goodwill** (tied to the business) is marital property in most states; **personal goodwill** (tied to the owner's skill and reputation) is excluded in many states.
- **Military retired pay.** Under the Uniformed Services Former Spouses' Protection Act, states may treat **disposable retired pay** as divisible property (*Mansell v. Mansell*, 1989, held that pay a retiree waives to receive VA disability benefits is not divisible). *Howell v. Howell* (2017) held that a state court may not order a retiree to indemnify a former spouse for the reduction caused by a later waiver, though a court may account for the possibility of waiver when it sets the division or support.

### Debts

Debts incurred during marriage for family purposes are marital and divided with the assets. Debts for one spouse's separate purpose (gambling, an affair) may be assigned to that spouse.

### Applying the rules

Step 1: classify each item (when acquired, how, and any commingling). Step 2: value at the date the state uses (often date of separation, filing or trial). Step 3: divide the marital estate under the state system, then add each spouse's separate property to find totals. In a community state, assume half unless told otherwise.

@example
title: Equal division in a community property state
problem: In a community property state that divides equally, Ana and Ben have a house with \$300,000 of equity, a retirement account of \$180,000 earned entirely during the marriage, and \$30,000 of credit-card debt for household expenses. Ana also has \$50,000 that she inherited and kept in an account in her name alone. What is each spouse's total after division?
step: Classify. The house equity, the retirement account and the debt are community property or community debt. The inheritance is Ana's separate property.
step: Value the community estate: \$300,000 + \$180,000 - \$30,000 = \$450,000.
step: Divide equally: each spouse receives \$225,000.
step: Add separate property. Ana's total is \$225,000 + \$50,000 = \$275,000, and Ben's total is \$225,000.

@mistakes
- Treating all property acquired during the marriage as marital, even a gift or inheritance to one spouse.
- Assuming equitable distribution means an equal split; it means a fair division in light of the factors.
- Forgetting to subtract marital debts before dividing the net estate.
- Dividing a pension without applying the time rule, so that years of service before the marriage are wrongly shared.

@practice self
prompt: Explain the main differences between a community property system and an equitable distribution system for dividing property on divorce, covering what counts as the shared estate, the default division rule, and how a court's discretion differs.
solution: In a community property state, most property acquired by either spouse during the marriage belongs to the community, with each spouse owning half, and the court on divorce divides it equally in most states (Texas uses a just and right division). Separate property is kept by its owner. In an equitable distribution state, property acquired during marriage is marital property that the court divides equitably after weighing statutory factors such as the length of marriage, contributions including homemaking, economic circumstances and future prospects, and in many states dissipation. The division need not be equal. Both systems classify gifts, inheritances and premarital property as separate. The main difference is the starting point: equal and firm in community property, and discretionary in equitable distribution, which gives the court flexibility but less predictability.
