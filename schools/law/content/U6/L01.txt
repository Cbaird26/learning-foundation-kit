@article
Family law is **state law** almost everywhere. Each state decides who may marry, how a marriage ends, who is a parent, and who decides custody. Federal law and the Constitution set outer limits, and a few federal statutes matter (the Respect for Marriage Act, the Parental Kidnapping Prevention Act, and federal child-support enforcement law). Rules, ages and percentages in this course are therefore stated as the common or majority approach, and you must check the statute of the jurisdiction in any real matter. This course is for study, not legal advice.

### The rule: what makes a marriage valid

Marriage is both a **civil contract** and a **status** that the state controls. A valid ceremonial marriage generally requires:

- **Capacity**: the parties meet the minimum age (18 in most states, with exceptions in some for younger parties with parental or court consent, and a growing number of states setting 18 with no exception) and have the mental capacity to understand the nature of marriage.
- **Consent**: free and voluntary agreement. Duress, fraud going to the essence of the marriage and a lack of understanding can make a marriage voidable.
- **No legal impediment**: no existing undissolved marriage (a bigamous marriage is void) and no prohibited degree of kinship (a close-relative marriage is void).
- **Formalities**: a license, solemnization by an authorized officiant and often witnesses and recording. Most states treat a failure of the formalities as an irregularity that does not defeat a marriage entered in good faith.

A **void** marriage is invalid from the start and may be attacked by anyone, even after a party's death. A **voidable** marriage is valid until a court annuls it, and usually only a party may challenge it, during the parties' lifetimes (Week 4). Bigamy and close kinship make a marriage void; youth, lack of capacity, fraud and duress usually make it voidable.

### Variations

**Common-law marriage** is recognized by a small minority of states and the District of Columbia. It requires (1) a present-tense agreement to be married now, (2) cohabitation and (3) holding out to the community as spouses. No license or ceremony is needed, and no minimum period of cohabitation. Most states do not allow a common-law marriage to be created in the state, but they recognize one validly created in a state that does.

**Place of celebration.** A marriage valid where it was celebrated is valid everywhere, unless it violates a strong public policy of the state with the most significant relationship to the parties (the approach of Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws § 283). The **Respect for Marriage Act** (2022) now requires every state to recognize a marriage valid where entered, whatever the sex, race or ethnicity of the spouses, and requires federal recognition.

The **putative spouse doctrine** protects a person who goes through a marriage ceremony in good faith believing it valid when it is not (for example, because the other party was still married). Many states, following the Uniform Marriage and Divorce Act, treat that person as a spouse for property and support purposes until the defect is discovered.

### Constitutional limits

The Supreme Court treats marriage as a fundamental right under the Due Process and Equal Protection Clauses.

- *Loving v. Virginia* (1967) struck down Virginia's ban on interracial marriage under both clauses.
- *Zablocki v. Redhail* (1978) held that a statute that directly and substantially interferes with the right to marry (there, by requiring court permission for a parent behind on child support) is reviewed with critical examination and must be closely tailored to important state interests. Reasonable regulations that do not significantly interfere, such as license and age rules, are upheld.
- *Turner v. Safley* (1987) held that prisoners keep the right to marry; a rule allowing marriage only for "compelling reasons" was not reasonably related to legitimate penological interests.
- *Obergefell v. Hodges* (2015) held that the Fourteenth Amendment requires states to license marriages between two people of the same sex and to recognize those validly entered elsewhere. The majority relied on the fundamental right to marry and equal protection. The dissenting opinions argued that the question belongs to the democratic process rather than the courts. Do not confuse this with the statute: the Respect for Marriage Act supplies a statutory layer of recognition that does not depend on the holding.

### Applying the rules

Ask in order: Does a prohibited impediment exist (void)? Is there a defect that makes the marriage voidable? Were the formalities met, or is this a common-law or putative marriage? Where was it celebrated, and is it recognized elsewhere? Is a state restriction one that significantly interferes with the right to marry, so that *Zablocki* applies?

@example
title: A marriage celebrated in one state and challenged in another
problem: Ana (age 17) and Ben (age 19) marry in State A with a court order and parental consent that State A's statute allows. They move to State B, where the minimum marriage age is 18 with no exception. Years later, Ben argues in a State B divorce case that the marriage was never valid. Is the marriage valid?
step: Identify the rule. A marriage valid where celebrated is valid elsewhere unless it violates a strong public policy of the state with the most significant relationship.
step: Apply it. The marriage met State A's capacity rules, so it was valid when celebrated. State B's higher age is a regulation of its own ceremonies, not an impediment of the kind (like bigamy) that offends a strong public policy.
step: Add the passage of time. Ana is now an adult and the parties cohabited, so any age defect is cured by ratification even if it had mattered.
step: Conclusion: the marriage is valid and State B should proceed to dissolve it under its divorce law.

@example
title: A void marriage and a putative spouse
problem: Cal is married to Dot and has not divorced her. Without telling Eve, Cal goes through a ceremony with Eve, who believes in good faith that Cal is single. What is the status of the second marriage, and what can Eve claim?
step: Cal has an existing undissolved marriage. A bigamous second marriage is void from the start, so Eve and Cal are not married, despite the ceremony and license.
step: Eve acted in good faith, so in a state with the putative spouse doctrine she is treated as a spouse for property division and support while her good faith lasts.
step: Dot remains Cal's lawful spouse, and Cal also faces possible criminal liability for bigamy.

@mistakes
- Treating every defective marriage as void; most defects (age, fraud, duress, lack of capacity) make a marriage only voidable, and the marriage stays valid until a court annuls it.
- Believing a license or ceremony is always required; a minority of states recognize common-law marriage, and failure of formalities usually does not invalidate a good-faith marriage.
- Applying strict scrutiny to every marriage regulation; only rules that significantly interfere with the right to marry get critical examination under Zablocki, while reasonable licensing and age rules are upheld.
- Mixing up the holding of Obergefell (a constitutional requirement) with the Respect for Marriage Act (a federal statute that would continue to require recognition if the case were ever overruled).

solution: A valid ceremonial marriage requires capacity (minimum age and mental capacity), consent that is free and voluntary, no legal impediment (no existing marriage and no prohibited kinship), and compliance with formalities (license and solemnization by an authorized officiant), although a good-faith failure of formalities usually does not defeat the marriage.
