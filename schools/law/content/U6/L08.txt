@article
Custody has two parts. **Legal custody** is the authority to make major decisions (education, health care, religion). **Physical custody** is where the child lives. Either can be **sole** or **joint**. **Parenting time** (visitation) is the schedule of time with each parent. Many states now require a **parenting plan** setting out both.

### The standard

The central rule is the **best interests of the child**. The court decides on the evidence about what serves the child, with no presumption in favor of either parent based on sex. The **tender years presumption** (mother preferred for young children) has been abandoned. The UMDA § 402 lists factors:

- the wishes of the child's parents and of the child;
- the child's interaction with parents, siblings and others;
- the child's adjustment to home, school and community;
- the mental and physical health of everyone involved.

Many state statutes add: each parent's history as caregiver; willingness to support the child's relationship with the other parent; **domestic violence** (many states presume against custody for a parent who has committed it); stability; and the child's preference, weighted by age and maturity. Courts may interview the child in chambers and appoint a **guardian ad litem** or custody evaluator.

Approaches vary. Some states presume **joint custody** or encourage shared parenting; some use a **primary caretaker** presumption; the American Law Institute proposes an **approximation rule**, under which the court allocates time to match each parent's past caretaking. Some states treat a parent's relocation as presumptively allowed if in good faith; others make the relocating parent prove the move is in the child's best interests. The standards for relocation vary widely.

### Constitutional limits

- *Palmore v. Sidoti* (1984): a court may not change custody because the custodial parent's new spouse is of a different race. Private biases cannot be given legal effect.
- *Stanley v. Illinois* (1972) held that an unwed father is entitled to a hearing on fitness before the state takes his children; later cases require a relationship and not only biology (Week 9).
- *Santosky v. Kramer* (1982): the state must prove grounds for **terminating parental rights** by at least **clear and convincing evidence**.
- *Troxel v. Granville* (2000) (plurality): Washington's statute allowing any person to petition for visitation at any time, and the court to grant it on a best-interests basis, was unconstitutional as applied to a fit mother's decision. The Due Process Clause protects a parent's fundamental right to make decisions concerning the care, custody and control of children, and a court must give **special weight** to a fit parent's determination. The Court did not decide whether the Constitution always requires proof of harm. Most states allow grandparent visitation only under statutes that presume that fit parents' decisions are in the child's interests.

Parents' constitutional claims also appear in other areas (for example, *Mahmoud v. Taylor* (2025) on parents' free-exercise claim to opt their children out of certain classroom instruction), but the central custody framework remains the best interests of the child.

### Non-parents

A third party such as a grandparent, stepparent or **de facto parent** (someone who has lived with the child and functioned as a parent with the legal parent's consent) may seek custody or visitation under statutes that vary. Against a fit legal parent, the non-parent generally must overcome the presumption in favor of the parent, for example by showing unfitness or extraordinary circumstances.

### Modification

A custody order is modifiable on a showing of a **substantial change in circumstances** and that modification serves the child's best interests. Many states bar modification motions for a period (such as one or two years) after the decree unless the child is endangered. Courts value stability and are reluctant to change a long-standing arrangement.

### The percentage-of-income child-support model

Under a **percentage-of-obligor-income** model, support equals a set percentage of the paying parent's income, which increases with the number of children and is limited by an income cap. Because states differ in the percentages and caps, a problem must give them as assumptions. For example, assume 20% for one child, 25% for two and 30% for three, applied to net resources up to a cap.

### Applying the rules

State the best-interests factors that matter on the facts, check for constitutional limits (race, a fit parent's choices, a hearing for an unwed father), then identify what the court can order and whether any modification standard applies.

problem: Assume a state uses these percentages of the obligor's net resources: 20% for one child, 25% for two and 30% for three, and the guideline applies only to the first \$9,200 of net resources per month. The obligor's net resources are \$11,000 per month and there are two children. What is the guideline support?
step: Apply the cap. The guideline applies only to the first \$9,200, so use \$9,200, not \$11,000.
step: Choose the percentage for two children: 25%.
step: Multiply: 0.25 x \$9,200 = \$2,300 per month.
step: Income above the cap does not enter the guideline; the court may consider more for the child's needs, but the presumptive amount is \$2,300.

@mistakes
- Applying a presumption that mothers should have custody of young children; the tender years presumption is gone.
- Using a parent's race, or a new spouse's race, as a factor in custody; Palmore forbids it.
- Treating Troxel as holding that grandparent visitation statutes are always unconstitutional; the plurality decided only that the statute was unconstitutional as applied, because it gave no special weight to a fit parent.
- Applying the percentage to income above the guideline cap.

@practice self
prompt: Pia and Quin, never married, share a six-year-old child. For five years Pia has been the primary caregiver, working part time, while Quin works long and variable hours and sees the child on weekends. Quin recently reduced his hours and wants equal time. Pia wants to keep primary custody and objects to equal time. A court must set a parenting plan. Analyze the best-interests factors on these facts and say what arrangement a court is likely to order.
solution: The central issue is what arrangement serves the child's best interests. Factors favoring Pia: she has been the primary caregiver for five years, so continuity and the child's adjustment to the home, school and routine support keeping the child primarily with her. Factors favoring more time for Quin: his reduced hours increase his availability, a child generally benefits from a relationship with both parents, and a parent's willingness to foster the other's relationship is a factor (there is no sign either parent interferes). The child's age (six) limits the weight of any preference. There is no domestic violence or safety concern indicated. A court is likely to keep primary physical custody with Pia and expand Quin's time beyond weekends (for example, additional weekday time that grows as his schedule shows reliability), possibly with joint legal custody. An equal split is possible in a state favoring shared parenting if the parents can cooperate and live close together, but the history of caregiving makes it less likely immediately.
rubric: Identifies the best-interests factors that matter: caregiving history, continuity, availability, support of the other parent's relationship, and safety.
rubric: Notes the limited weight of a six-year-old's preference and the absence of safety concerns.
rubric: Reaches a reasoned arrangement and recognizes that it may vary by state presumption.
hint: Weigh continuity of care against the change in Quin's availability.
