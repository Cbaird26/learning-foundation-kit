@article
Both parents have a legal duty to support their minor child. The duty belongs to the child, so it is enforced by the state and **cannot be bargained away by the parents**. Support is set by a formula (a **guideline**) that courts apply unless the result would be unjust or inappropriate on the facts.

### The federal frame

As a condition of receiving federal child-support enforcement funding, every state must have **presumptive guidelines** (42 U.S.C. § 667), and a court that departs from the guideline amount must make a written or specific finding that applying it would be unjust or inappropriate. States must offer a review of support orders at least every three years on request. The details (the formula, the schedule, the dollar amounts) are state law. **Because dollar amounts and schedules vary and change, every calculation in this course gives them as assumptions.**

### Three models

1. **Income shares** (most states). The guideline estimates what an intact two-parent family with the combined income would spend on the child. A **schedule** gives the **basic obligation** for the combined income and the number of children. Each parent pays the share of that amount equal to his or her **percentage of combined income**. The noncustodial parent pays his or her share to the custodial parent.
2. **Percentage of obligor income** (for example Texas and Wisconsin). Support is a fixed percentage of the paying parent's income that rises with the number of children (for example, 20% for one child and 25% for two in some states), usually with an income cap.
3. **Melson formula** (a minority of states). It first protects each parent's basic needs, then allocates the remaining income by a percentage that gives the child a share of the higher standard of living.

### Income shares step by step

1. Determine each parent's **gross income** (or net income in some states), including wages, bonuses, self-employment income and often alimony received. Courts may **impute income** to a parent who is voluntarily unemployed or underemployed, based on earning capacity.
2. Add the parents' incomes to get **combined income**; find each parent's **percentage share**.
3. Use the **schedule** for the basic obligation.
4. Add **add-ons**: work-related child care, health insurance premiums for the child, and extraordinary medical or educational expenses, in the proportion the state directs.
5. Multiply the total by the noncustodial parent's share.
6. Consider adjustments (shared parenting time, a **self-support reserve** for low-income obligors) and **deviations** with findings.

### Duration and scope

Support usually lasts until age 18 or high school graduation (up to 19 in many states). A few states allow courts to order support for college. At common law a stepparent owes no duty of support, though some states impose one while the child lives in the stepparent's household. Income withholding, license suspension, passport denial and contempt are the main enforcement tools.

### Modification and arrears

A support order may be modified on a **substantial change in circumstances** (or a guideline change by an amount set by statute). Under the federal **Bradley Amendment** (42 U.S.C. § 666(a)(9)), each installment is a **judgment** when due, and a court cannot modify support **retroactively** to a date before the other party received notice of the motion. A parent who loses a job must therefore file promptly, because the court can only reduce support from the filing date forward.

### Parents' agreements

A private agreement that one parent will not seek support, or that support is waived in exchange for giving up visitation, does not bind the court. Support and parenting time are independent: failure to pay does not justify denial of visits, and interference with visits does not excuse nonpayment.

### Due process in enforcement

In *Turner v. Rogers* (2011), a parent faced up to a year of jail for civil contempt for not paying support, and neither parent had a lawyer. The Court held that the Due Process Clause does **not automatically require counsel** at a civil contempt hearing where the opposing party is unrepresented and the state provides **substitute procedural safeguards**: notice that ability to pay is the critical issue, a form to elicit financial information, an opportunity to respond to statements about finances, and an express finding by the court that the defendant has the ability to pay. Without such safeguards, incarceration violates due process.

### Applying the rules

Identify the guideline model (stated in the problem), compute the numbers, add add-ons, assign the obligation, and then check any need for deviation or modification.

@example
title: An income-shares calculation with child care
problem: Assume an income-shares state. The parents' combined monthly net income is \$8,000 and the schedule gives a basic obligation of \$1,200 for one child at that income. Parent A, the noncustodial parent, earns \$3,000 and Parent B, the custodial parent, earns \$5,000. Work-related child care costs \$480 per month, and the state prorates child care in the same ratio as the basic obligation. What does Parent A pay monthly?
step: Find A's share of combined income: \$3,000 / \$8,000 = 37.5%.
step: Add the add-on to the basic obligation: \$1,200 + \$480 = \$1,680.
step: Multiply by A's share: 0.375 x \$1,680 = \$630.
step: Parent A pays \$630 per month to Parent B, who bears the remaining 62.5% directly.

@mistakes
- Assigning the whole basic obligation to the noncustodial parent instead of prorating by income shares.
- Treating a parents' agreement to waive support as binding; the child's right belongs to the child.
- Expecting a court to reduce support retroactively to the date of job loss; modification reaches back only to the date of notice of the motion.
- Thinking Turner v. Rogers guarantees counsel in every contempt case; it requires adequate procedural safeguards.

@practice self
prompt: A court is setting child support in an income-shares state. The father quit a job paying \$80,000 and took a part-time job paying \$20,000 so that he could pursue a hobby business that loses money. The mother has primary custody. Explain whether and how the court may impute income to the father and what the court will consider.
solution: A court may impute income to a parent who is voluntarily unemployed or underemployed, as a way to base support on earning capacity rather than actual income, so that a parent cannot avoid the obligation by choosing less income. The court will ask whether the reduction was voluntary and in good faith (not to avoid support), and consider the parent's work history, qualifications, the job market and the availability of comparable work, and the reason for the change. Here the father left an \$80,000 job for a part-time job and an unprofitable hobby, apparently by choice, so the court is likely to impute income at or near \$80,000 (or what his qualifications can earn), unless he shows a legitimate reason such as health, a good-faith career change or a necessary caretaking responsibility. The court then runs the guideline using the imputed income and may consider whether any deviation is warranted.
rubric: States that income may be imputed to a parent who is voluntarily underemployed, and why.
rubric: Lists factors such as good faith, earning capacity, work history and the job market.
rubric: Applies the factors to the facts and reaches a conclusion.
hint: Ask whether the income reduction was voluntary and whether he could earn more.
