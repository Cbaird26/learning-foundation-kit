@article
**Adoption** is a statutory proceeding that creates a legal parent-child relationship between people who are not biologically related and ends the relationship with the former parents. There is no common-law adoption, so the statute must be followed.

### Types

- **Agency adoption**, in which a licensed agency places the child.
- **Independent (private) adoption**, arranged directly between birth and adoptive parents, often through a lawyer; some states limit it.
- **Stepparent adoption**, which is the most common; the stepparent's spouse keeps parental rights, and the other legal parent must consent or have rights terminated.
- **Relative and foster-parent adoption**, often from the child welfare system.
- **Adult adoption**, allowed in many states, and **second-parent adoption**, which lets an unmarried partner of a legal parent become a second legal parent.

An adoption requires a petition, a **home study**, **consent or termination** of the existing parents' rights, a finding that adoption serves the **child's best interests**, and a decree. The **Interstate Compact on the Placement of Children (ICPC)** governs placements across state lines.

### Consent

Parents with legal rights must consent unless their rights have been terminated. Consent may not be given before birth in many states (a waiting period of days is common), and it must be voluntary and informed. The **revocability** of consent varies widely: in some states it is irrevocable once signed or after a short period, absent fraud or duress; in others it can be withdrawn until the court enters an order. The unwed father's consent depends on the Constitution and on state registry and acknowledgment rules (Week 9); after *Lehr v. Robertson* a father who has not developed a relationship or registered may be bypassed.

### Involuntary termination of parental rights

If a parent does not consent, the state must prove a statutory **ground** for **termination**, such as abandonment, severe or chronic abuse or neglect, long-term failure to support or contact, or unfitness, and that termination serves the child's best interests. The standard of proof is at least **clear and convincing evidence** (*Santosky v. Kramer*, 1982). Poverty alone is not a ground. Under the **Adoption and Safe Families Act of 1997 (ASFA)**, a state must generally file a petition to terminate rights once a child has been in foster care for **15 of the most recent 22 months**, subject to exceptions (such as placement with a relative), and must make reasonable efforts toward reunification except in aggravated circumstances.

### Effects of a final decree

The adoptee becomes the child of the adoptive parents **for all purposes**, including inheritance, support and custody, and the adoptee's legal relationship with the birth parents ends (except in a stepparent adoption, where the spouse's relationship continues). The birth parents ordinarily have no right to visitation. Some states enforce **open adoption** agreements that allow contact, with the court retaining authority over best interests. Original birth records are sealed in many states, though a growing number allow adult adoptees to obtain them. Once final, a decree is attacked only for fraud or similar grounds within a short statutory period. A person who was promised adoption and raised as a child but never formally adopted may, in some states, inherit as an **equitably adopted** child.

### The Indian Child Welfare Act (ICWA)

ICWA (1978) sets federal standards for child-custody proceedings involving an **Indian child** (a member of, or eligible for membership in, a federally recognized tribe and the child of a member). It gives tribal courts exclusive jurisdiction over children resident or domiciled on a reservation, provides for transfer to tribal court, requires notice to the tribe, requires active efforts to prevent the breakup of the family and heightened proof, including testimony of qualified expert witnesses (clear and convincing evidence for foster-care placement and proof beyond a reasonable doubt for termination of parental rights), and sets **placement preferences**.

- *Adoptive Couple v. Baby Girl* (2013): the provisions barring involuntary termination without a showing that continued custody by the parent is likely to cause serious harm (§ 1912(f)), and the requirement of active efforts (§ 1912(d)), do not apply when the Indian parent never had legal or physical custody of the child. The placement preferences of § 1915(a) do not apply when no other eligible party seeks to adopt.
- *Haaland v. Brackeen* (2023) rejected the challenges to ICWA: Congress acted within its constitutional power over Indian affairs, ICWA does not violate the anticommandeering doctrine, and the challengers lacked standing on the equal protection and nondelegation claims.

### Parentage in several arrangements

When asked who the legal parents are, ask: who gave birth; whether a spouse is presumed; whether anyone signed a VAP or was adjudicated; whether there was donor conception or surrogacy and who consented; and whether there has been an adoption or termination.

### Applying the rules

Check for consent or grounds for termination, the standard of proof, any ICWA overlay, and the effect of the final decree on inheritance and support.

@example
title: Termination of rights in a stepparent adoption
problem: Rae (mother) is married to Sy. Rae's former partner Tom is the legal father of her child, but for four years he has not seen or supported the child and has made no effort to. Sy petitions to adopt. Tom objects. What must Sy show?
step: Because Tom is a legal parent who has not consented, his rights must be terminated before the adoption can proceed.
step: Sy must show a statutory ground, such as abandonment or failure to support or maintain contact, and the court must find that termination and adoption serve the child's best interests.
step: The state's proof standard must be at least clear and convincing evidence under Santosky.
step: If the court terminates Tom's rights and grants the adoption, Sy becomes a legal parent with Rae, and Tom's rights and duties end.

@mistakes
- Assuming that a birth parent may revoke consent whenever she wishes; revocation rules differ and are often strict.
- Terminating parental rights on a preponderance of the evidence or because of poverty alone.
- Assuming that ICWA's protections always apply; Adoptive Couple held that some provisions do not apply when the parent never had custody.
- Forgetting that the final decree ends the birth parents' rights except in a stepparent or similar adoption.

@practice self
prompt: Deliverable: determine legal parentage in three family arrangements, under the UPA (2017) and the rules in this course. (1) Ana and her husband Ben have a child conceived by donor insemination with an anonymous donor; Ben consented in writing. (2) Cy and Dee, unmarried, have a baby; Cy signs a voluntary acknowledgment of parentage at the hospital and does not rescind it within 60 days, and a later DNA test shows he is not the biological father. (3) Eli and Fran, a married couple, contract with Gia, a gestational surrogate with no genetic link, to carry their embryo in a state with a valid gestational surrogacy statute that they followed. Identify the legal parents in each and explain why.
solution: (1) Ana is the legal mother because she gave birth. Ben is a legal father because he consented to the assisted reproduction (and, as the husband, is also presumed to be the father). The donor is not a parent under the UPA. (2) The birth mother, Dee, is the legal mother. Cy is the legal father because a VAP not rescinded within 60 days has the force of a judgment, and it can be set aside only for fraud, duress or material mistake of fact; a later DNA test alone does not undo it if none of those is shown. The biological father is not a legal parent unless adjudicated or the VAP is set aside. (3) Eli and Fran are the legal parents. In a state with a valid gestational surrogacy statute that the parties complied with, parentage is established in the intended parents (often by pre-birth or post-birth order), and Gia, who has no genetic link and agreed to the arrangement, is not a parent. If the statute were not followed or not recognized, the intent test of Johnson v. Calvert would likely point to the same result in states that follow it.
rubric: Identifies the legal parents correctly in all three arrangements.
rubric: Explains each result with the correct rule: consent and the donor rule, VAP finality, and gestational surrogacy.
rubric: Notes the conditions: the VAP challenge grounds and compliance with the surrogacy statute.
