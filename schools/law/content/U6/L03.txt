@article
**Divorce** (also called dissolution of marriage) ends a valid marriage by court decree. The decree ends marital status and usually resolves property, support and children in the same case. Because divorce is purely statutory, each state's divorce statute controls.

### Grounds: from fault to no-fault

At common law divorce required proof of **fault** by one spouse. Traditional fault grounds are adultery, cruelty (physical or mental), desertion or abandonment for a statutory period, and (in some states) felony conviction, habitual drunkenness or drug use, and impotence. The innocent spouse sued, and the defendant could raise defenses: **recrimination** (the plaintiff is also at fault), **condonation** (forgiveness), **connivance** (plaintiff arranged the misconduct), and **collusion** (agreement to fabricate grounds). These defenses led to perjury and sham proceedings.

California adopted the first pure no-fault statute in 1969, and by 2010, when New York added irretrievable breakdown as a ground, every state offered some form of no-fault divorce. The usual **no-fault grounds** are **irreconcilable differences**, **irretrievable breakdown** and **living separate and apart** for a statutory period (commonly six months to two years). The Uniform Marriage and Divorce Act uses "irretrievable breakdown." A number of states allow only no-fault divorce; others keep fault grounds as an alternative. Where fault grounds remain, fault may still matter to property division or alimony in some states, and it matters little in others. A few states also offer **covenant marriage** (Louisiana, Arizona and Arkansas), in which spouses agree beforehand to limited grounds and counseling.

### Jurisdiction and residency

Divorce is a **status** proceeding, so a state has power to grant one if at least one spouse is **domiciled** there. This is so even if the other spouse is absent and was served only by publication or out of state (an **ex parte** divorce): *Williams v. North Carolina* (1942) required full faith and credit to the divorce decree of a state where one spouse was truly domiciled. In *Williams II* (1945) the Court held that another state may examine whether the divorcing spouse actually was domiciled in the granting state, so a decree obtained on a sham domicile can be challenged.

Statutes add **durational residency** requirements, usually between six weeks and one year, which the Supreme Court upheld in *Sosna v. Iowa* (1975) (a one-year requirement) as a reasonable way to prevent forum shopping and protect the state's decree. Courts treat the requirement as a prerequisite to the petition, not a mere formality.

Ending the marriage is different from deciding **financial claims**. Under the **divisible divorce** doctrine, a court with only status jurisdiction can end the marriage, but it needs **personal jurisdiction** over the absent spouse to cut off that spouse's right to support or to divide property. *Estin v. Estin* (1948) held that a Nevada ex parte divorce ended the marriage but did not extinguish the wife's New York separation decree for support. *Vanderbilt v. Vanderbilt* (1957) held that a court without personal jurisdiction over the wife could not terminate her support rights. In *Kulko v. Superior Court* (1978) the Court held that a father's agreeing that his child live in another state did not give that state personal jurisdiction over him on a child-support claim as a matter of due process; the Uniform Interstate Family Support Act now supplies a long-arm statute for such cases (Week 12).

### Access to court

*Boddie v. Connecticut* (1971) held that due process forbids a state from denying access to the divorce court to an indigent person who cannot pay filing and service fees, because the state has a monopoly on ending marriage.

### The stages of a case

1. **Petition or complaint** filed in a court with jurisdiction and venue; **service** on the other spouse.
2. **Temporary (pendente lite) orders**: temporary support, custody and parenting time, exclusive use of the home, and orders freezing assets.
3. **Disclosure and discovery**: financial affidavits, document requests, depositions, valuation of assets.
4. **Alternative dispute resolution**: mediation is required in many places, especially for custody.
5. **Settlement or trial**: most cases settle. A settlement agreement is submitted to the court and is usually **incorporated** into the decree; the court reviews terms about children and, in most states, may reject unconscionable terms.
6. **Decree** after any statutory waiting period; **post-decree** enforcement and modification of support and custody.

If a spouse dies before the decree is final, the action abates in most states and the surviving spouse is treated as a spouse.

### Applying the rules

Ask first whether the court can end the marriage (domicile and durational residency), second whether it can decide money and children (personal jurisdiction over the other spouse, and the UCCJEA for custody), third which ground applies, and fourth what procedure the statute requires.

@example
title: Ex parte divorce and an existing support order
problem: Hal and Wynn are domiciled in State A. A State A court enters a decree of legal separation ordering Hal to pay Wynn \$1,500 monthly. Hal moves to Nevada, is domiciled there in good faith, and obtains a divorce after serving Wynn only by publication. Wynn, who stayed in State A and did not appear, learns of the divorce. Is Hal free of the marriage and of the support order?
step: Marriage. Hal is domiciled in Nevada, so Nevada has jurisdiction over the status. Under Williams, other states must give full faith and credit, so the marriage has ended.
step: Support. Nevada had no personal jurisdiction over Wynn, so under Estin and Vanderbilt its decree cannot terminate her right to support under the State A order.
step: Conclusion: Hal is divorced, but the \$1,500 support order survives and remains enforceable in State A.

@mistakes
- Treating a no-fault state as one in which fault is never relevant; some no-fault states still consider fault for property or support, and fault grounds survive as alternatives in others.
- Confusing a court's power to end the marriage (domicile) with its power to cut off support (personal jurisdiction over the other spouse).
- Counting a statute's waiting period from the wrong date; read whether it runs from filing, service or the hearing and exclude the first day.
- Believing a spouse's own misconduct (recrimination) still bars a divorce in a modern no-fault state.

@practice self
prompt: Outline the stages of a contested divorce case from filing through decree, and identify at least three kinds of temporary orders a court may enter while the case is pending.
solution: The case begins with a petition filed in a court with jurisdiction and venue, followed by service on the other spouse. The court may enter temporary (pendente lite) orders: temporary spousal and child support, temporary custody and a parenting schedule, exclusive possession of the family home, and orders restraining the transfer or dissipation of assets. The court enters the final decree after any waiting period, and later enforcement and modification of support and custody remain available.
