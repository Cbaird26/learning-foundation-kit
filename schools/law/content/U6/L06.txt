@article
**Spousal support** (alimony or maintenance) is a payment from one former spouse to the other after separation or divorce. It is different from property division: property division splits what the spouses have, while support addresses income going forward. Support rules vary widely by state, and many states now have guidelines or caps.

### Types of support

- **Temporary (pendente lite)** support during the case.
- **Rehabilitative** support for a limited period while a spouse gets education or training to become self-supporting.
- **Durational or bridge-the-gap** support for a fixed term, often tied to the length of the marriage.
- **Permanent or indefinite** support, usually reserved for long marriages in which a spouse cannot become self-supporting because of age or health. Some states have limited or abolished it; for example, Florida eliminated permanent alimony in 2023.
- **Reimbursement** support to repay a spouse who financed the other's education or career.
- **Lump-sum** or property-based alimony, in which a fixed amount replaces periodic payments.

### The standard: need and ability to pay

The usual statute, modeled on UMDA § 308, allows support if the spouse seeking it lacks sufficient property to provide for reasonable needs and cannot support himself or herself through appropriate employment (or is the custodian of a child whose condition makes outside work inappropriate). Courts then fix the amount and duration after considering:

- each spouse's financial resources, including the property awarded;
- the time needed for education or training;
- the standard of living during the marriage;
- the duration of the marriage;
- age and physical and emotional condition; and
- the paying spouse's ability to meet his or her own needs while paying.

In many states **fault** is a factor, in others it is relevant only for egregious conduct, and some states exclude it. Some states use **formulas**: for example, a percentage of the income difference, with duration tied to marriage length. Because formulas differ, exam problems must state the formula as an assumption.

### Gender neutrality

*Orr v. Orr* (1979) held that a state statute allowing alimony to be awarded against husbands only violates the Equal Protection Clause, because the state's interest in providing for needy spouses is served by a need-based, gender-neutral rule.

### Modification and termination

Support is **modifiable** on a showing of a **substantial change in circumstances** (job loss, disability, a large raise), unless the parties agreed it is non-modifiable and the state allows that. Support ends on the death of either party and, under most statutes, on the **remarriage** of the recipient. Many states also allow termination or reduction on the recipient's **cohabitation** with a new partner in a marriage-like relationship. Retirement of the payor at a normal age can be grounds for modification.

### Taxation

For divorce or separation instruments executed after December 31, 2018, federal law (as changed by the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017) makes alimony **non-deductible** by the payor and **not income** to the recipient. Earlier instruments keep the old rules unless modified to adopt the new ones.

### Applying the rules, and the property deliverable

In a case you usually do both: first divide the property, then ask whether the recipient can meet needs with his or her share and income. A generous property award may reduce support, and a small estate may increase it. To compare the two property systems, classify first, then divide the marital estate (equally in community property, or as the court finds equitable under the facts), then add separate property.

@example
title: Applying a support guideline
problem: Assume a state guideline sets monthly alimony at 30% of the difference between the spouses' gross monthly incomes, for a period equal to one-half the length of the marriage. The marriage lasted twelve years. The payor earns \$9,000 per month and the recipient earns \$3,000 per month. How much will be paid in total?
step: Find the income difference: \$9,000 - \$3,000 = \$6,000.
step: Apply the percentage: 30% of \$6,000 = \$1,800 per month.
step: Find the duration: one-half of twelve years is six years, or 72 months.
step: Multiply: \$1,800 x 72 = \$129,600 over the term, subject to modification on changed circumstances.

@mistakes
- Treating alimony as a matter of right after every divorce; it depends on need, ability to pay and the statute.
- Assuming alimony is still deductible for the payor; for instruments executed after 2018, it is not.
- Forgetting to account for the property awarded when deciding need.
- Treating support as fixed forever; it is modifiable on a substantial change in circumstances unless the parties validly agreed otherwise.

prompt: Deliverable: divide a supplied marital estate under both systems. A couple's property is: a house bought during the marriage with \$400,000 of equity, a retirement account of \$150,000 earned during the marriage, and \$40,000 of marital debt. The husband also has \$50,000 in a premarital account never commingled, and the wife has a \$20,000 inheritance kept separate. Assume that under community property each spouse receives half of the community estate, and that under equitable distribution the court awards the wife 60% of the marital estate because she was the primary caregiver. Give, in order, (1) the husband's total under community property, (2) the wife's total under community property, and (3) the wife's total under equitable distribution, in dollars.
answer: (305000, 275000, 326000)
check: ((400000 + 150000 - 40000) / 2 + 50000, (400000 + 150000 - 40000) / 2 + 20000, 0.6 * (400000 + 150000 - 40000) + 20000)
hint: The marital estate is the house equity plus the retirement account minus the debt.
hint: The marital estate is \$510,000. Add each spouse's separate property after dividing.
solution: The marital estate is \$400,000 + \$150,000 - \$40,000 = \$510,000. Community property: each gets \$255,000, so the husband's total is \$255,000 + \$50,000 = \$305,000, and the wife's total is \$255,000 + \$20,000 = \$275,000. Equitable distribution: the wife gets 60% of \$510,000, which is \$306,000, plus her \$20,000, for \$326,000.

@practice self
prompt: Maya (45) and Noel (47) have been married twenty years. Noel earns \$140,000 a year. Maya left a nursing career fifteen years ago to raise their children and now has an outdated license and no current income. They have a modest marital estate that will be divided about equally. Maya asks for spousal support. Applying the usual factors (as in UMDA § 308), explain what type and duration of support a court is likely to order and why.
solution: Maya's need is established: after an equal division of a modest estate she cannot meet her reasonable needs without income, and she is out of the workforce for reasons connected to the marriage. Noel has a high income and the ability to pay. The marriage was long (twenty years), and Maya's absence from nursing was a joint family decision that Noel benefited from. Her age (45) and the time needed to renew her nursing credentials suggest that she can eventually return to work. A court is therefore likely to order rehabilitative or durational support: enough to cover the time to renew her license and re-enter the workforce, probably for several years, with a review. Because she is in her mid-forties and could become self-supporting, permanent alimony is less likely, although a court in a state with long-marriage guidelines could order a longer term. Support remains modifiable on a substantial change in circumstances and ends on either party's death or Maya's remarriage.

rubric: Chooses a type and duration (rehabilitative or durational) and explains why permanent support is less likely.
rubric: Notes modification and termination rules.
hint: Ask first whether Maya can become self-supporting and how long it would take.
