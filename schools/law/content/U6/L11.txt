@article
A **civil protection order** (also called a restraining order or order of protection) is a court order that prohibits a person (the **respondent**) from abusing, threatening, harassing or contacting another (the **petitioner**). Every state authorizes protection orders in cases of domestic violence, and most also allow orders for stalking and sexual assault. Who may petition varies: spouses, former spouses, intimate partners, people who share a child, household members and, in some states, dating partners and other family members.

### Process

1. **Petition and ex parte (temporary) order.** The petitioner files a sworn petition, often with court help. If the petition shows **immediate and present danger** or a credible threat of abuse, the judge may issue a **temporary order without notice** to the respondent. Due process allows this because the order is short and a prompt hearing follows.
2. **Service and hearing.** The respondent must be served. A **hearing**, commonly within ten to twenty-one days (the statute sets the period), gives the respondent notice and an opportunity to be heard. The standard of proof is a **preponderance of the evidence** in most states.
3. **Final order.** If the court finds abuse or a credible threat, it issues a final order lasting a set term (often one to five years, renewable) or, in some states, permanently.

### Relief

Typical relief: no abuse or harassment; **no-contact** or **stay-away** provisions; **exclusive possession** of a shared home; temporary **custody** and parenting-time arrangements and child support; support for the petitioner in some states; return of property; surrender of **firearms**; counseling or intervention programs for the respondent; and in some states protection of pets. Courts may not use a civil protection order to impose punishment without a separate contempt or criminal process.

### Enforcement

A violation can be punished as **criminal contempt** and often as a separate crime; many states encourage or require **arrest** when police have probable cause. The order binds the **respondent**. The petitioner cannot waive or excuse a violation by inviting contact, because only the court can modify its own order. Courts avoid **mutual orders** unless each party filed and the court made findings as to both.

*Town of Castle Rock v. Gonzales* (2005) held that a person protected by a restraining order has no constitutionally protected **property interest** in police enforcement of the order, so failure to enforce does not give rise to a procedural due process claim under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, even where state law uses mandatory language about arrest. (Compare *DeShaney v. Winnebago County* (1989), holding that the Due Process Clause generally does not impose a duty on the state to protect a person from private violence.) Remedies lie in state law and in the criminal process.

### Firearms and the Second Amendment

Federal law, 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(8), prohibits possession of firearms by a person subject to a qualifying domestic-violence protection order (issued after notice and a hearing, and either including a finding that the person poses a credible threat to the physical safety of an intimate partner or child, or explicitly prohibiting the use of force). In *United States v. Rahimi* (2024), the Court upheld the provision against a facial challenge under the Second Amendment, relying on the historical tradition, shown by surety and "going armed" laws, of disarming individuals who threaten the physical safety of others. The Court clarified that *New York State Rifle & Pistol Ass'n v. Bruen* (2022) requires that a modern regulation be consistent with the principles underlying the nation's historical tradition, not a "historical twin." Eight Justices joined the holding; one dissented. Federal law also bars possession by persons convicted of a misdemeanor crime of domestic violence (§ 922(g)(9)).

### Interstate enforcement: the Violence Against Women Act

Under 18 U.S.C. § 2265, a protection order issued by one state (or tribe) must be given **full faith and credit** and enforced in every other state as if it were its own, if the issuing court had jurisdiction over the parties and the subject matter and the respondent had **reasonable notice and an opportunity to be heard** (for an ex parte order, notice and hearing within a reasonable time). The protected person need not register the order in the new state for it to be enforceable. Orders that are mutual are entitled to full faith and credit only if the court made findings as to each party, or each filed a petition. Crossing a state line to violate an order is also a federal crime (18 U.S.C. § 2262).

Separately, *Counterman v. Colorado* (2023) requires proof of at least recklessness for a criminal prosecution for true threats, which affects stalking and threat prosecutions that accompany protection-order violations.

### Policy

The system balances quick protection for people in danger against the respondent's liberty, property and parental interests, which is why the ex parte stage is short and the final order requires a hearing.

### Applying the rules

Identify the stage (ex parte or final), whether notice and a hearing were provided, what relief the statute allows, whether the violator knew of the order, and, where the case crosses state lines, whether the VAWA conditions are met.

@example
title: Enforcing an out-of-state order
problem: Ana obtains a one-year final protection order against Ben in State A after a hearing at which Ben appeared. She then moves to State B and shows the order to State B police when Ben appears at her new workplace. State B has no registration process. May State B police enforce it?
step: Under 18 U.S.C. § 2265, an order of another state must be enforced if the issuing court had jurisdiction and Ben had notice and an opportunity to be heard.
step: State A had jurisdiction over the parties, and Ben appeared at the hearing, so notice and an opportunity to be heard are satisfied.
step: Registration is not a precondition to enforcement, so the absence of a registration process does not matter.
step: Conclusion: State B police must enforce the order as if it were a State B order.

@mistakes
- Believing that a petitioner can excuse a violation by consenting to contact; only the court can modify the order.
- Assuming that a failure by police to enforce an order gives the petitioner a federal due process claim; Castle Rock held otherwise.
- Overreading Rahimi to uphold every firearm restriction; it upheld the federal ban for a person under an order with a credible-threat finding.
- Thinking that an out-of-state order must be registered before it is enforceable; full faith and credit does not require it.

@practice self
prompt: Describe the process by which a person obtains a civil protection order, from petition to final order, and list at least four kinds of relief a court may include.
solution: The petitioner files a sworn petition describing the abuse. If it shows immediate and present danger, the court may issue a temporary ex parte order without prior notice to the respondent. The respondent is then served and a hearing is held within a statutory period (commonly ten to twenty-one days) at which both sides may testify and present evidence. The court decides under a preponderance of the evidence standard in most states whether abuse or a credible threat occurred, and if so issues a final order for a fixed term (renewable) or in some states permanently. Relief commonly includes a prohibition on abuse and harassment, no-contact and stay-away provisions, exclusive possession of the home, temporary custody and parenting-time terms, child support, surrender of firearms and counseling. Violation is punishable by contempt and often as a crime.
