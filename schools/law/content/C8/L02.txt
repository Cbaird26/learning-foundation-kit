@article
**Legal positivism** is the view that what counts as law depends on social facts, not on moral merit. It does not say law is good, and it does not say you should obey it. It says only that you identify law by asking *what was enacted, decided or practised*, not *whether it is just*.

### Two core theses

- **The separability thesis.** There is no *necessary* connection between law and morality. A rule can be law although it is unjust, and a moral principle is not law merely because it is moral.
- **The social sources thesis.** Whether a norm is legally valid is a matter of social fact: for example, that it was enacted by a legislature, announced in a precedent or accepted in official practice.

Positivists give reasons for this separation. It keeps law identifiable and predictable, since lawyers can find the law without settling moral disputes. It keeps moral criticism available, because if every unjust statute were automatically "not law," we could not say in clear words that a valid law is unjust. Bentham and Austin also pressed an ethical point: confusing law with morality either gives bad laws undeserved prestige or makes moral reform look like lawbreaking.

### Austin's command theory

In Austin's classical version, law is the **command of a sovereign** backed by a threat of sanction. The sovereign is a person or body that is habitually obeyed by most of the population and that does not itself habitually obey anyone. A statute, on this view, is a general command: "do X, or suffer harm."

This theory is clear and tied to observable facts, but it faces serious objections, developed by Hart in the next lesson:

1. **Power-conferring rules.** Rules for making wills, contracts or marriages do not order anyone to do anything; they give people powers.
2. **Law binds lawmakers.** Constitutions limit sovereigns, which sits awkwardly with a sovereign above law.
3. **Continuity.** When a ruler dies, the successor's orders are law at once, though no habit of obedience to the new ruler exists yet.
4. **The gunman.** Being obliged (forced) is not the same as being obligated (having a duty). A threat explains the first but not the second.

### Later positivism

Kelsen's pure theory treats legal norms as a hierarchy, each valid because authorized by a higher norm, up to a presupposed basic norm (*Grundnorm*). Hart replaced commands with social rules (next lesson). Contemporary positivists divide on one point:

- **Inclusive (soft) positivism** says that a legal system *may* make moral criteria part of the test for legal validity; for example, a constitution that invalidates statutes that are "unjust" would make morality a condition of law. Even then, the status of the moral test is a social fact.
- **Exclusive (hard) positivism**, associated with Raz, says that legal validity *never* depends on moral argument, because law claims authority, and authority must be identifiable without reconsidering the very moral issues it was meant to settle.

### Objections to positivism

- Fuller and Finnis say it ignores law's moral purpose and leaves no way to explain why legal systems merit respect.
- Dworkin argues that judges apply legal principles whose status depends on moral argument, which positivism, as a theory of pedigree, cannot capture (Lesson 4).
- Some critics say that positivism cannot explain legal obligation: knowing that a rule is valid does not tell you why you should follow it. Positivists reply that this is the question of the *moral* duty to obey law, which is a separate question from what law is.

### Unjust statutes

Faced with an unjust statute, the positivist says: it is law, and it should be criticized or repealed. The natural lawyer says: its injustice undermines its legal force, fully or to some degree. Both positions can agree about the statute's evil and about whether to resist it. They differ about whether *non-law* or *law-that-should-not-be-obeyed* is the right description.

@example
title: Comparing two views of an unjust statute
problem: A state enacts, through its proper legislative process, a statute fining any person who gives food to a neighbor who has been convicted of a political offense. Compare how a positivist and a natural lawyer would describe the statute.
step: Both start by identifying the facts: the statute was enacted by the competent legislature in the required way, and it is clearly unjust.
step: The positivist applies the separability and social sources theses. The statute meets the system's criteria of validity, so it is law. The positivist then adds an independent moral judgment: the law is unjust and should be repealed, and individuals may have a moral duty to disobey.
step: A natural lawyer (on a strong reading) says that a rule that so plainly defeats the common good lacks the full force of law. A moderate natural lawyer (Finnis, Radbruch) calls it a defective instance of law, or invalid because the injustice is intolerable.
step: State the disagreement exactly: it concerns how to describe the statute (valid but unjust versus invalid or defective), not whether the statute is morally bad or whether someone should defy it.

@example
title: Spotting a weakness in the command theory
problem: A statute says a will is valid only if signed by the testator and two witnesses. Why is this hard to fit into the command theory?
step: The command theory treats law as an order backed by a threat.
step: The statute orders nobody to make a will and threatens no one. It gives individuals a legal power to create valid wills.
step: A defective will is "void," not punished. So not every law is a command with a sanction, which is Hart's objection about power-conferring rules.

@mistakes
- Saying positivists believe unjust laws must be obeyed; positivism is silent on the moral duty to obey.
- Equating the social sources thesis with majority rule; the social facts may be enactment, precedent or custom.
- Treating Austin's command theory as the only positivism, when it is the oldest version and most positivists reject it.
- Saying inclusive positivists agree with natural lawyers; they still hold that a moral test is law only because a social rule so provides.
