@article
Hart's *The Concept of Law* (1961) is the most influential statement of modern positivism. He begins with Austin's failure: law is not the gunman's order writ large. A gunman orders you to hand over your wallet and you comply, but you are *obliged*, not *under an obligation*. Law is different: it presents itself as giving reasons to act, not only threats.

### Rules, not habits

Hart distinguishes a **habit** from a **social rule**. If most of us habitually go to the cinema on Friday, we simply do so; deviation draws no criticism. A social rule exists when a group regularly follows a pattern *and* treats it as a standard: deviation is criticized, and the standard is cited as justification. A participant who takes this attitude has the **internal point of view**; an observer who only notes regularities and predicts sanctions has the **external point of view**. The internal point of view explains how rules can be reasons and not just predictions.

### Primary and secondary rules

A simple community might have only **primary rules**: rules that impose duties, such as bans on violence and theft. Hart says such a regime has three defects, and each is cured by a **secondary rule**:

| Defect | Remedy | What the rule does |
|---|---|---|
| Uncertainty about what the rules are | **Rule of recognition** | Specifies criteria for identifying valid rules |
| Rigidity: no way to change rules | **Rules of change** | Empower people to enact and repeal rules |
| Inefficiency in resolving disputes | **Rules of adjudication** | Empower officials to decide breaches |

The union of primary and secondary rules is, for Hart, the heart of a legal system.

### The rule of recognition

The **rule of recognition** is the system's ultimate criterion of validity. In the United States, one would say that a norm is federal law if it comes from the Constitution, an enactment of Congress made under the Constitution or a rule of federal common law, with the Supremacy Clause settling conflicts with state law. Three features matter:

- It is a **social rule**, existing in the practice of officials (especially judges) who treat its criteria as binding standards. It is not itself enacted, and it is not assumed (as Kelsen assumed a basic norm); it is a fact about what officials actually do.
- It is **ultimate**: other rules are valid because they satisfy it, while it is "valid" only in the sense of existing as a practice.
- Ordinary citizens need only obey. The system exists when private citizens generally comply and officials accept the secondary rules from the internal point of view.

### Open texture

Language has a core of settled meaning and a **penumbra** of uncertain meaning. A rule "No vehicles in the park" plainly bans cars (the core), but whether it bans bicycles, a motorized wheelchair or a war memorial tank is unclear. Hart calls this the **open texture** of law. In penumbral cases, the rule alone does not give a determinate answer, and an official must exercise **discretion**, guided by the rule's purposes and relevant considerations. Hart therefore says that judges in hard cases make law *interstitially*, in the gaps, though within limits.

### Objections

- **Dworkin** argues that Hart's theory cannot account for legal principles or for the way judges argue that some outcome is already required by law (next lesson).
- **The existence of one ultimate rule.** Some doubt that real legal systems have a single rule of recognition with agreed criteria, since judges may disagree about the criteria themselves. Hart replies that disagreement at the margins is compatible with convergence on a core of criteria.
- **Normativity.** Critics ask whether the practice of officials can explain why the law gives *genuine* reasons. Hart suggests that an account of what law is does not require a justification of obedience.

@example
title: Core and penumbra
problem: An ordinance reads, "No vehicle may be driven in Riverside Park." Classify the following under Hart's account: (a) a delivery truck, (b) an ambulance answering an emergency, (c) a child's electric toy car.
step: Start with the paradigm cases of the rule's ordinary meaning. A delivery truck is a clear vehicle driven in the park: it lies in the core, and the rule applies.
step: The ambulance is a vehicle in the core, but a hard question arises about whether the rule's purpose (safety and quiet in the park) supports an exception. This is a question about justification or exceptions, not about the word "vehicle."
step: A child's electric toy car is a penumbral case. It is a vehicle in some senses, but it is far from the paradigm.
step: In the penumbra, the language of the rule does not decide. An official must exercise discretion, considering the purposes, the harm and the costs of enforcement. Hart says the judge is then making law in a limited way.

@example
title: Sorting primary and secondary rules
problem: Classify each as primary or secondary and say which secondary rule it is, if any: (1) "No person may steal." (2) "Congress may enact statutes by majority vote of both houses and presidential signature." (3) "A norm is valid federal law if it appears in the Constitution or a statute made under it." (4) "The courts shall decide whether a defendant has violated the rule against theft."
step: A primary rule imposes duties. (1) forbids stealing: primary.
step: (2) empowers an institution to make and change rules: a rule of change.
step: (3) states criteria for what counts as valid law: the rule of recognition.
step: (4) empowers officials to decide breaches: a rule of adjudication.

@mistakes
- Treating the rule of recognition as an enacted rule, like a statute; it exists as a social practice of officials.
- Saying that citizens must accept the rule of recognition; Hart requires only general obedience from citizens and acceptance by officials.
- Describing the penumbra as "no law"; Hart claims the rule gives a clear answer in the core and runs out only at the margins.
- Mixing up "obliged" (forced by threat) with "obligated" (under a rule that is a standard).

hint: Check your draft against each point a good answer must cover.
solution: The rule of recognition is the secondary rule that sets out criteria for identifying valid law in a legal system. In the United States, its criteria plausibly include conformity to the Constitution, enactment by Congress or a state legislature in the constitutionally required way, and the precedents of courts, with federal law prevailing over inconsistent state law. It is a social rule because it exists in the practice of officials, especially judges, who treat these criteria as standards for deciding what the law is and who criticize deviations. It is not enacted by any body, because enactment itself depends on criteria of validity. Its existence is a social fact, not a legal norm authorized by a higher norm, which distinguishes Hart from Kelsen's presupposed basic norm.
