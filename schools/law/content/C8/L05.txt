@article
In the early twentieth century, American courts were often described by their critics as practicing **formalism**: judges were said to deduce results from rules and concepts, as in a mechanical process. **Legal realism**, a loose movement among scholars in the 1920s and 1930s, argued that this picture was incomplete. Realists asked what judges *actually do*, and what social facts influence the results.

### Holmes and the prediction theory

Holmes's essay "The Path of the Law" (1897) is a forerunner. He wrote that "the prophecies of what the courts will do in fact, and nothing more pretentious, are what I mean by the law." This is the **prediction theory** of law. It views law from the perspective of the **bad man**, someone who cares about law only for the material consequences of breaking it. The bad man asks: what will a court make me pay, and how likely is that? For him, a legal duty is a prediction that if he acts in a certain way, he will suffer a sanction.

### Two kinds of skepticism

Realists differed among themselves, but two themes stand out:

- **Rule skepticism.** Legal rules are less determinate than they appear. Precedents can be read broadly or narrowly, and statutes bend under interpretation. Llewellyn, in a well-known essay, listed paired **canons of construction** that point in opposite directions ("thrust and parry"): for example, one canon says that statutes in derogation of the common law are to be strictly construed, while another says that remedial statutes are to be liberally construed. If either canon can be chosen, the canons do not decide.
- **Fact skepticism.** Even if the rules were clear, the facts are found by trial courts and juries through fallible processes of testimony, credibility and inference. The outcome depends on what happens at the trial.

Realists drew several lessons: study judicial behavior empirically, see law in its social context (the "law in action" versus "law in the books"), and evaluate legal rules by their real effects. This functional approach influenced the Uniform Commercial Code, drafted with a focus on commercial practice, and shaped later movements, including the empirical study of courts. Modern political-science models, such as the **attitudinal model**, claim that judges' political preferences help predict their votes, especially in ideologically divisive cases; **strategic models** add that judges anticipate other actors' reactions.

### Objections and replies

1. **Most cases are easy.** Realists focus on appellate disputes, which are a selected sample. Most legal questions never reach a court because the answer is clear. *Reply:* realists mostly aim at the hard cases and at the general lesson that the rules do not determine outcomes in those.
2. **Hart's critique.** The prediction theory takes only the external point of view. Judges and citizens treat rules as reasons, not as predictions, and a judge cannot treat the law merely as a prediction of her own decision. *Reply:* realists can say that their account explains how the system works behind the rhetoric of rule-following.
3. **Overstatement.** Unanimity is common among appellate judges, and many outcomes cannot be explained by politics. Critics say the legal model (law constrains) has explanatory power. *Reply:* modern realists accept constraint but say that its extent should be measured, not assumed.
4. **Skepticism and legitimacy.** If judging is political, is judicial authority illegitimate? Some realists, and critics, draw that conclusion; others emphasize that recognizing discretion lets us make it more responsible.

### The upshot

Realism is not the claim that "judges decide as they like." It is the claim that rules, standing alone, underdetermine outcomes in disputed cases, and that a full account of law must include other influences. Positivists, natural lawyers and Dworkin each respond to this challenge in different ways: Hart through the core and penumbra, Dworkin through principles and integrity.

@example
title: The bad man's calculation
problem: A business owner is deciding whether to break a supply contract. If she breaks it, she estimates a 50% chance of being sued and an 80% chance of losing if sued, with damages of \$50,000. Using Holmes's bad man perspective, what is her expected cost of breaching from legal liability, ignoring legal fees?
step: The bad man cares about material consequences, so convert the legal risk into an expected cost.
step: The probability of paying is the probability of being sued times the probability of losing: $0.5 \times 0.8 = 0.4$.
step: Multiply by the damages: $0.4 \times \$50{,}000 = \$20{,}000$.
step: The "law" for the bad man is this prediction, and he compares it with the gain from breaching. Note Hart's objection: this treats law only from the external point of view.

@example
title: Thrust and parry
problem: A statute changes a common-law rule about landlord liability and says nothing about its scope. Explain how the canons of construction can support opposite results.
step: One canon says that statutes in derogation of the common law should be read narrowly: a court could use it to confine the statute.
step: Another says that remedial statutes should be read broadly to achieve their purpose: a court could use it to expand the statute.
step: If both canons are available, the choice between them is not made by the canons. The realist concludes that something else, such as policy views or the judge's sense of fairness, may be doing the work. The opponent replies that canons are only guides, and that text, structure and precedent constrain the choice.

@mistakes
- Saying realists think judges decide arbitrarily or by whim; their claim is that rules underdetermine results in contested cases.
- Treating Holmes's prediction theory as a description of how judges themselves should reason.
- Confusing rule skepticism (rules are indeterminate) with fact skepticism (fact-finding is uncertain).
- Assuming realism and positivism are the same; realists make claims about judicial behavior, while positivists make claims about what counts as law.

solution: Hart objected that the prediction theory describes law only from the external point of view. It treats a legal rule as a prediction that a sanction will follow, but officials and many citizens treat rules as standards that give reasons for action and criticism. A judge deciding a case cannot treat the rule as merely a prediction of her own decision, and a person who pays taxes because it is the law does not merely predict punishment. The prediction theory therefore leaves out the normative character of law. The realist can reply that Holmes's bad man perspective was never meant to be a complete theory of rules, but a way of seeing what matters about law for those facing it, and that modern realists accept the internal viewpoint but add that the real influences on decisions (policy, ideology, institutional roles) must also be studied.
