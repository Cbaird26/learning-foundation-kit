@article
Lawyers and politicians speak constantly of rights, but the word covers several things. **Theories of rights** ask what a right is, what grounds it, and how strong it is.

### Hohfeld's analysis

Hohfeld observed that the single word "right" hides four distinct legal positions. Each has a **correlative** (the position it imposes on someone else) and an **opposite**:

| Position | Correlative | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Claim-right** | Duty | You are owed something: if Ana has a right that Ben not trespass, Ben has a duty not to |
| **Privilege (liberty)** | No-right | Ana may enter her own yard, and no one has a claim that she not |
| **Power** | Liability | Ana can change legal relations: she may sell her land, and the buyer becomes liable to have title pass |
| **Immunity** | Disability | Ben cannot change Ana's legal position: a city has no power to take her land without lawful process |

Hohfeld's scheme lets lawyers separate, for example, a **liberty** to do something from a **claim** that others not interfere. A person may have a liberty to speak without a claim that the government subsidize her speech, which is the contrast between negative and positive rights.

### What grounds a right?

- **The will (choice) theory** says a right gives its holder control over another's duty: she may waive it, enforce it or leave it alone. Its strength is in explaining contract rights. Its difficulty is with rights held by those who cannot exercise choice, such as infants, and with rights that cannot be waived.
- **The interest theory** says a right exists when an individual's interest is a sufficient reason to impose a duty on others. It explains the rights of infants, but critics ask whether it makes everyone who benefits from a duty a right holder.

### Natural rights and legal rights

A **natural (moral) right** is claimed to exist independently of any legal system, as in the Declaration of Independence's reference to "unalienable Rights." A **legal right** exists because a legal system grants it. Positivists keep these apart; natural lawyers connect them. Constitutions often give a legal form to moral rights. In U.S. due process doctrine, unenumerated rights have been recognized when they are "deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition," the test of *Washington v. Glucksberg* (1997), which the Court in *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization* (2022) applied in overruling *Roe* and *Casey*; the dissent argued for a different reading of liberty. Dworkin described rights as "trumps": they defeat ordinary collective goals, such as increased social welfare.

### Conflicts and limits

Rights can conflict (speech and privacy), and most are not absolute. Courts use frameworks such as tiers of scrutiny, balancing and categorical exceptions to resolve conflicts. A theory of rights should say how rights relate to the common good, not just state their existence.

### Three critiques and replies

1. **The utilitarian critique.** Bentham called natural rights "nonsense upon stilts": only the law creates rights, and rules should be justified by their consequences. *Reply:* defenders of rights argue that consequences alone would permit sacrificing individuals for aggregate gain; rights set limits (Dworkin's trumps; Kantian respect for persons). Rule utilitarians add that a practice of rights has good consequences.
2. **The communitarian critique.** Rights-talk treats persons as isolated individuals and neglects responsibilities, community and the common good. *Reply:* defenders say rights protect the conditions of community life, such as association and speech, and that rights can be framed to include duties and relations.
3. **The critical critique.** Rights are indeterminate and can be used to defend the status quo; they may give formal protection while leaving social power untouched. *Reply:* defenders say that rights have been powerful tools for the powerless and that indeterminacy is a matter of degree; some critical scholars (and many others) agree that rights remain useful for strategic reasons.

@example
title: Hohfeld applied
problem: Ana owns a field. She gives Ben permission to cross it, and a statute provides that no town may take her land without paying compensation. Identify one Hohfeldian relation in each sentence.
step: Ana's permission to Ben is an exercise of a **power**: she changed Ben's legal position from trespasser to licensee. Ben is under a **liability** to have his position changed.
step: Once she has granted the permission, Ben has a **privilege** to cross, and Ana has a **no-right** that he not do so.
step: The statute makes Ana **immune** from a taking without compensation; the town has a **disability**.
step: Name the correlative of each position every time. Duty and claim-right are correlatives; privilege and no-right; power and liability; immunity and disability.

@mistakes
- Saying a "right" always means a claim-right; Hohfeld shows it may mean a liberty, power or immunity.
- Mixing up the correlative of a privilege (a no-right) with its opposite (a duty).
- Treating the will theory and the interest theory as claims about which rights exist, not about what rights are.
- Reporting that Dobbs "ended" all unenumerated rights; the decision concerned the right recognized in Roe and Casey, and the majority stated that its reasoning did not cast doubt on precedents on other rights.
