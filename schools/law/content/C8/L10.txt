@article
Law claims to bind. **Political authority** is the claimed right to rule, with a correlative duty to obey. **Power** is only the ability to get people to comply. A gunman has power; a legitimate government is supposed to have authority. Two questions follow. **Legitimacy**: when does a government have the right to rule? **Political obligation**: do citizens have a moral duty to obey its laws, and why?

### Types of authority

Weber distinguished three types of legitimate authority, in terms of what subjects believe: **traditional** (grounded in established custom), **charismatic** (grounded in personal qualities) and **legal-rational** (grounded in enacted rules and offices). Modern constitutional states claim the third. Weber's types describe why people accept authority, not whether they should.

### Why obey? Four accounts

1. **Consent.** In social contract theory, such as Locke's, legitimate government rests on the agreement of the governed. *Objection:* most people never consent; "tacit consent" (living here, using roads) is weak, since leaving is costly. *Reply:* hypothetical or constitutional consent, such as the idea that rational people would agree, though critics say hypothetical consent is not consent.
2. **Fair play.** Those who benefit from a cooperative scheme owe a fair share of the burdens. *Objection:* citizens often receive benefits they did not choose. *Reply:* limit the argument to benefits that are accepted or are public goods people actively use.
3. **Natural duty.** Rawls argued that persons have a natural duty to support just institutions. *Objection:* it may not explain why obligation attaches to *this* state rather than another. *Reply:* it applies to the institutions that govern us.
4. **Raz's service conception.** Authority is legitimate when subjects better comply with the reasons that apply to them by following the authority's directives than by acting on their own judgment (the **normal justification thesis**). *Objection:* it makes authority depend on the content and success of each directive, perhaps leaving less than a general duty to obey. *Reply:* Raz accepts that the duty is limited.

**Philosophical anarchism** holds that no state has a general moral right to be obeyed, though one may often have good reason to comply, such as avoiding harm. The debate is among scholars who agree that most laws deserve compliance in most cases.

### Civil disobedience

Rawls defined **civil disobedience** as a public, non-violent, conscientious yet political act contrary to law, usually done to bring about change in laws or policies. Features include:

- **Publicity**: it is done openly, not secretly.
- **Non-violence**: it appeals to the community's sense of justice, not force.
- **Conscience**: it is motivated by moral conviction.
- **Willingness to accept legal consequences**: many (not all) theorists add this, as a mark of fidelity to law, though others reject it as a requirement.

It differs from **conscientious refusal** (declining personally to obey a law, for example refusing an order, without aiming to change policy) and from **revolutionary action**, which seeks to overthrow the legal order, and from ordinary crime. Whether it is justified depends on the injustice, the failure of legal channels and the risk of damaging respect for law.

### Legal treatment

Courts do not treat civil disobedience as a defense. Defendants typically raise **necessity**, the choice-of-evils defense. At common law it requires that the harm sought to be avoided be imminent and greater than the harm of the offense, and that there be no reasonable lawful alternative. MPC § 3.02 drops the imminence requirement: it requires a belief that the conduct is necessary to avoid a greater harm, and no plain legislative purpose to exclude the justification. In most cases of political protest, courts hold that the harm was not imminent, that lawful alternatives existed, or that the legislature has already weighed the issue, so the defense fails. Judges and juries retain discretion in sentencing and charging, where the moral character of an act may matter.

### Applying two theories of justice

The block's deliverable asks you to compare two theories of justice on a distribution question. For instance, on whether a country should adopt a wealth tax, a Rawlsian asks how it affects the least advantaged under fair institutions, while an entitlement theorist asks whether it violates just holdings.

@example
title: Necessity and a protest
problem: Protesters trespass at a facility they believe is harming the environment and are charged with trespass. They raise necessity. Why is the defense unlikely to succeed under the usual rule?
step: Recall the common-law elements of necessity: the actor reasonably believed the conduct was necessary to avoid a greater harm, the harm was imminent, and no reasonable legal alternative existed. (MPC § 3.02 omits imminence but asks whether the legislature has already weighed the issue.)
step: Apply the elements: the harm alleged is long-term and political, not imminent. Lawful alternatives (lobbying, litigation, public protest) were available.
step: The legislature or regulators have usually weighed the issue, so courts decline to let a jury re-weigh it.
step: Conclusion: the defense likely fails, although the protesters' conscientious motives may matter for charging and sentencing.

@example
title: Comparing two theories on a wealth tax
problem: Compare a Rawlsian and an entitlement theorist on a proposed annual tax on large fortunes used to fund education.
step: The Rawlsian asks whether the tax and the education spending improve the position of the least advantaged and promote fair equality of opportunity, without violating basic liberties. If so, it is permissible within a just basic structure.
step: The entitlement theorist asks whether the fortunes were acquired and transferred justly. If so, taxing them beyond what is needed to protect rights violates self-ownership and holdings.
step: The disagreement is about the starting point: fair cooperative terms versus historical entitlements. Describe both without choosing.

@mistakes
- Treating authority and power as the same; authority is a claim to a right to rule.
- Treating Rawls's definition of civil disobedience as the only one; some theorists do not require acceptance of punishment.
- Saying courts recognize civil disobedience as a defense; defendants invoke necessity or other defenses, which usually fail.
- Describing philosophical anarchism as a call to disobey; it denies a general duty to obey, not that one often has reasons to comply.
