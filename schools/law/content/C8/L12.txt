@article
Constitutional interpretation raises the same questions as statutory interpretation, with higher stakes: the text is old, general and hard to amend, and courts can set aside statutes. Main methods follow. You are not asked to choose, only to understand each at its best.

### Originalism

**Originalism** holds that the Constitution's meaning is fixed when it is adopted, and that judges are bound by it until it is changed by amendment. Versions differ:

- **Original intent** looks to the intentions of the framers or ratifiers. This version is now less common because of the difficulty of finding a collective intent.
- **Original public meaning** asks how the words would have been understood by a reasonable member of the public at adoption. This is the leading version.
- Many originalists distinguish **interpretation** (discovering meaning) from **construction** (applying meaning to new facts where the text is general).

Arguments for originalism: it constrains judges, it respects the democratic authority of those who adopted the text, and it keeps law stable. *Objections:* the "dead hand" problem (why should people long dead govern us?); historical evidence can be unclear and contested, so history can be selective; the general language ("equal protection," "unreasonable searches") invites questions about the level of generality at which to read it. *Replies:* originalists say that the people can amend the text, that history is a constraint even if imperfect, and that other methods give judges more discretion.

### Living constitutionalism

**Living constitutionalism** holds that the Constitution's meaning evolves with changing circumstances and understandings, sometimes through a **common-law method** in which precedent develops incrementally. The Eighth Amendment is the standard example: *Trop v. Dulles* (1958) said it draws meaning from "the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society," and *Roper v. Simmons* (2005) used this idea in holding that the death penalty cannot be imposed on offenders who were under eighteen when the crime was committed. *Objections:* it gives judges wide discretion, risks replacing the text with judges' values, and takes decisions from voters. *Replies:* defenders say that general text calls for judgment, that precedent and tradition discipline it, and that the original text permitted such growth.

### Text, history and tradition in recent cases

Both camps use history. *District of Columbia v. Heller* (2008) held, over dissent, that the Second Amendment protects an individual right to keep and bear arms; the majority relied on the original meaning of the text; Justice Stevens's dissent read the original meaning as protecting a right tied to militia service, and Justice Breyer's dissent would have weighed the law's benefits against its burdens. *New York State Rifle & Pistol Ass'n v. Bruen* (2022) required that a firearm regulation be consistent with the Nation's historical tradition of firearm regulation. *United States v. Rahimi* (2024) upheld a ban on possession by persons under domestic-violence restraining orders, stating that the question is whether the law fits the *principles* of the historical tradition, not whether there is a historical twin. *Wolford v. Lopez* (2026) and *United States v. Hemani* (2026) were two further Second Amendment decisions, the first striking Hawaii's default ban on licensed carry on private property open to the public without the owner's express permission, and the second holding the federal ban on gun possession by drug users unconstitutional as applied without a showing of dangerousness or impairment. *Kennedy v. Bremerton School District* (2022) read the Establishment Clause by reference to historical practices and understandings and abandoned the *Lemon* test. *Dobbs* (2022) asked whether the asserted right was deeply rooted in history and tradition. In *Trump v. Barbara* (2026), the Court held that children born in the United States to parents who are unlawfully or temporarily present are citizens at birth under the Fourteenth Amendment. In several of these cases, the dissenters disputed the majority's history or its level of generality.

### Other sources of argument

- **Precedent (stare decisis)** gives weight to prior decisions, though the Court has overruled some, such as *Humphrey's Executor*, in *Trump v. Slaughter* (2026).
- **Structure and purpose** infer rules from the relationships among institutions, such as separation of powers.
- **Prudential and consequential arguments** weigh practical effects.

### Amendment and interpretation

Article V provides that an amendment is proposed by two-thirds of both houses (or a convention called on application of two-thirds of the states) and ratified by three-fourths of the states. Because that process is demanding, a central question is how much change should come through interpretation, which originalists and living constitutionalists answer differently.

@example
title: Applying two methods to location data
problem: Police obtain a person's location history from a technology company using a geofence warrant. Describe how an originalist and a living constitutionalist might each approach the Fourth Amendment question, noting the holding in Chatrie v. United States (2026).
step: Holding: in Chatrie, the Court held that obtaining a person's location data from Google with a geofence warrant is a Fourth Amendment search.
step: An originalist asks what "unreasonable searches" and "persons, houses, papers, and effects" meant at the founding, and what principle (protection against arbitrary intrusions into private spheres) should govern new technology.
step: A living constitutionalist asks how the amendment's purposes, such as privacy against government surveillance, apply under present conditions, using precedent to develop the doctrine incrementally.
step: Both can reach the same conclusion. Note the shared issue: how general the principle should be. Take no side on which method is better.

@example
title: Reading a decision for its method
problem: A majority opinion canvasses laws from 1791 to 1868 and concludes that a modern regulation is consistent with a long tradition. A dissent says the majority described the historical practice at too specific a level. Which issue divides them?
step: Both use history, so the dispute is not originalism versus living constitutionalism.
step: The dispute concerns the level of generality: how broadly or narrowly to describe the historical tradition.
step: Rahimi emphasizes principles, not historical twins, which shows that courts treat this as a central question.

@mistakes
- Saying originalists ignore the Constitution's general language; they distinguish interpretation from construction.
- Treating living constitutionalism as free judicial policymaking; its defenders emphasize precedent and the common-law method.
- Assuming that the use of history implies a particular political outcome; both sides cite history, and results vary.
- Confusing formal amendment under Article V with interpretive change.

prompt: A statute says: "No person shall leave a vehicle unattended with the engine running." An owner leaves an electric car powered on, with no combustion engine, while she steps into a shop. In 150 to 200 words, apply textualism and purposivism to the provision and say whether the two methods diverge.
hint: Consider the ordinary meaning of "engine" at the time of enactment.
hint: Identify at least two plausible purposes (theft, fire, pollution) and say how each affects the result.
solution: A textualist asks what "engine" meant in ordinary usage when the statute was enacted, in the context of a rule about vehicles left running. If the ordinary meaning was an internal combustion engine, an electric car has no engine and the provision does not apply. If "engine" ordinarily included any motor that propels a vehicle, it does apply; dictionaries and usage in other statutes would be evidence. A purposivist identifies the aim: preventing theft of cars left ready to drive, preventing fires, or reducing idling and pollution. Theft prevention would support applying the provision to an electric car that is powered on and ready to drive; the pollution rationale would not. The methods may diverge: a textualist might not apply the rule if the ordinary meaning of "engine" is narrow, while a purposivist focused on theft might apply it. They would converge if the evidence of meaning and purpose points in the same direction.
