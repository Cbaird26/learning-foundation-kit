@article
Most of the law you will apply as a lawyer is statutory, and most statutory disputes turn on what a provision means. A statute is enacted by a process the Constitution prescribes, **bicameralism and presentment**: passage by both houses of Congress and presentment to the President, with a veto overridden only by two-thirds of each house. The theories of interpretation differ about what, among the materials produced by that process, a court should treat as authoritative.

### Textualism

**Textualism** holds that a court should look to the **ordinary meaning of the enacted text**, read in context, as understood when it was enacted. Its arguments: only the text went through bicameralism and presentment; committee reports and floor statements reflect the views of some members; and a text-focused approach constrains judges and gives fair notice. Textualists use dictionaries, grammar, the **whole-text** canon (read provisions together) and **semantic canons**; they generally avoid legislative history, except sometimes to show how a term was used. *Objection:* words have no meaning outside purposes, a literal reading can produce results no legislature would accept, and "ordinary meaning" is often indeterminate. *Reply:* textualists accept that context matters, use the **absurdity doctrine** for results that are plainly irrational, and argue that purposive judges have even more discretion. *Bostock v. Clayton County* (2020) shows that both sides can claim the text: the majority read Title VII's "because of sex" to cover discrimination based on sexual orientation and transgender status, while the dissents read the same words according to their ordinary meaning in 1964 and reached the opposite result.

### Purposivism and intentionalism

**Purposivism**, associated with the legal process school, asks what purpose a reasonable legislature would have had in enacting the statute, and reads the words to achieve it, using legislative history and the statute's structure as evidence. *Holy Trinity Church v. United States* (1892) is the classic example: a statute barring contracts to import foreigners "to perform labor or service of any kind" was held not to cover a church's hiring of a pastor, because it was aimed at imported manual labor, though the words seemed to fit. **Intentionalism** looks for the actual intent of the enacting legislators. *Objections:* Congress is a collective body with no single purpose; purposes can be described at many levels of generality; legislative history can be selective. *Reply:* purposivists say that a statute is a purposive act and that ignoring its evident aim distorts it; they treat purpose as an objective inference from the text and context.

### Canons of construction

| Canon | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ***Noscitur a sociis*** | A word takes meaning from its neighbors |
| ***Ejusdem generis*** | General words after a list cover items of the same kind |
| ***Expressio unius*** | Listing some items implies excluding others |
| **Rule of lenity** | Ambiguity in a criminal statute is resolved for the defendant |
| **Constitutional avoidance** | Prefer a reasonable reading that avoids serious constitutional doubts |
| **Major questions doctrine** | Congress must speak clearly to give an agency power over a question of vast economic and political significance (*West Virginia v. EPA*, 2022; *Biden v. Nebraska*, 2023; a plurality applied it again in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump*, 2026) |

Realists note that canons can point in different directions, and defenders reply that they are guides to be weighed with text, structure and purpose.

### Agencies after Loper Bright

*Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo* (2024) overruled *Chevron*. Courts now decide for themselves the best reading of a statute and may give an agency's view *Skidmore* respect, depending on its thoroughness and persuasiveness. Where Congress expressly delegates discretion, the court enforces the limits of the delegation. Earlier decisions that rested on *Chevron* remain good law under statutory stare decisis.

### Applying the methods

For any provision, state the text and context, the textualist reading, the purposivist reading, and what each would need to be persuasive. Often they converge; when they diverge, the reasons are instructive.

@example
title: One provision, two methods
problem: A statute states: "It is unlawful to play any musical instrument in a public cemetery so as to disturb the peace of the cemetery." A family arranges for a piper to play at a graveside funeral service, with the cemetery's knowledge. Apply textualism and purposivism.
step: Text: "any musical instrument" covers a bagpipe, and the provision is limited by the phrase "so as to disturb the peace."
step: Textualist: ask whether the piping, in its context, disturbs the peace. A funeral tune at a service the cemetery permitted may not disturb the peace in the ordinary sense; the limiting clause is part of the text. The word "any" does not remove it.
step: Purposivist: the evident purpose is to protect the dignity and quiet of a place of mourning. A funeral performance requested by the family supports rather than defeats that purpose.
step: Both methods may reach the same result here, but by different routes. A purposivist might reach it even if the text had no limiting clause, which is where the methods would diverge.

@example
title: Ejusdem generis
problem: A park rule bans selling "food, drinks, flowers or other perishable goods." A vendor sells printed T-shirts. Does the rule apply?
step: The list names food, drinks and flowers: each is perishable.
step: Under ejusdem generis, "other perishable goods" covers items like those listed. T-shirts are durable.
step: Conclusion: the rule likely does not reach T-shirts. A purposivist might ask whether the aim was to limit commercial activity generally, and then read the rule differently.

@mistakes
- Saying that textualists ignore context; they read the text in its linguistic and legal context.
- Treating legislative history as the same thing as the text: only the text was enacted.
- Describing Chevron as still governing; Loper Bright overruled it, though earlier holdings stand.
- Assuming that canons decide cases by themselves; they are guides that can conflict.

prompt: A statute makes it unlawful to "play any musical instrument in a public cemetery so as to disturb the peace of the cemetery." A family arranges for a piper to play at a graveside funeral service with the cemetery's knowledge, and a neighbor complains. In 200 to 250 words, apply textualism and purposivism to the provision, and say whether they diverge.
hint: Start with the text, including the limiting phrase.
hint: State the purpose a purposivist would identify and ask whether it supports an exception.
solution: A textualist begins with the words. "Any musical instrument" covers a bagpipe, but the provision applies only when it is played "so as to disturb the peace of the cemetery," and that phrase is part of the enacted text. Whether the piping disturbs the peace depends on the ordinary meaning of that phrase in context; music at a funeral that the cemetery permitted does not obviously disturb the peace of a place of mourning, though a neighbor's complaint is relevant evidence. A purposivist identifies the aim as protecting the quiet and dignity of a place of mourning. A family-arranged funeral performance is consistent with, and may even serve, that purpose, so the purposivist would not apply the provision. The two methods probably converge here because the text itself contains a limiting phrase. They would diverge if the statute banned "any musical instrument" without that phrase: a strict textualist would then apply the words, subject to the absurdity doctrine, whereas a purposivist might read in an exception for funerals.
