@article
Every legal system raises two different questions. One is **what the law is**: what a judge, lawyer or citizen can identify as binding. The other is **what the law ought to be**: whether a rule is fair, wise or just. Jurisprudence asks whether those questions are connected. **Natural law theory** says that they are: there is some necessary connection between law and morality, so that a norm's moral defects can affect whether, or how far, it counts as law.

### The classical view

Aquinas defined law as an ordinance of reason, directed to the common good, made by whoever has care of the community, and promulgated. On this definition, law has a moral purpose built into it. He distinguished four levels: **eternal law** (the order of the universe), **natural law** (the part of that order human reason can grasp), **divine law** (revealed), and **human law** (made by people). Human law gets its force from natural law in two ways:

- **Conclusion.** Some rules follow from natural law almost directly: a ban on murder follows from the principle that innocent life should be protected.
- **Determination.** Where natural law leaves room, people must choose among reasonable options: driving on the right rather than the left is a determination, because either side would serve safety.

Aquinas wrote that an unjust law "seems to be no law at all." Read strongly, this means an unjust enactment is not valid law. Read weakly, it means such an enactment is a defective or "perverted" case of law, which may lose its moral claim on you (though he thought it might still bind in conscience to avoid scandal or disorder). Learners should know that interpreters disagree about which reading he meant.

### Modern versions

- **Fuller's inner morality of law.** Fuller argued that law is a purposive activity that works only if it meets eight principles: general rules, promulgation, non-retroactivity, clarity, non-contradiction, possibility of compliance, constancy over time, and congruence between the rules as announced and as applied. A system that wholly fails these is not law at all.
- **Finnis's new natural law.** Finnis holds that there are basic goods (life, knowledge, play, aesthetic experience, friendship, practical reasonableness, religion) that give reasons to act, and that law's *central case* is a system that serves the common good by securing the conditions for pursuing them. Defective legal systems are still law, but they are not law in the central, full sense.
- **The Radbruch formula.** After the Second World War, Radbruch proposed that positive law prevails even when unjust, unless its conflict with justice reaches an intolerable degree, in which case it must yield. The formula was invoked in German courts dealing with Nazi-era informers and later with border guards in the divided Germany.

The natural law tradition also shaped political rhetoric: the Declaration of Independence appeals to "the Laws of Nature and of Nature's God," and the Nuremberg tribunal rejected the defense that the accused had followed domestic law.

### Objections and replies

1. **The positivist objection.** Hart argued that law and morality should be kept conceptually apart, so that we can say plainly "this is law, but it is too wicked to obey." Calling a wicked rule "not law" confuses the question of validity with that of obligation. *Reply:* the natural lawyer answers that the concept of law is not morally neutral, and that treating wicked orders as full law lends them an authority they do not deserve.
2. **The fact of evil legal systems.** Officials in unjust regimes apply rules, keep records and hear cases. If that is not law, ordinary speech about such regimes becomes strained. *Reply:* Finnis's central-case method lets us call it law while saying it is a deficient instance.
3. **Moral disagreement.** If law depends on morality, and people disagree about morality, how can anyone identify the law? *Reply:* natural lawyers say that law's basic moral test is only about *extreme* or *systematic* failure, not everyday disagreement.
4. **Procedural versus substantive morality.** Hart said that Fuller's eight principles are compatible with evil ends, as a poisoner can be efficient. *Reply:* Fuller held that a system committed to legality finds it hard to sustain openly evil aims.

Your task is not to pick a winner but to state each position in the form its best defenders would accept.

@example
title: One unjust statute, two descriptions
problem: A former regime's statute provided that anyone who criticized the head of state in private conversation could be imprisoned without trial. After the regime fell, a court must decide whether a person who denounced a neighbor under this statute committed a crime. How would a strong natural lawyer, a Radbruch-style lawyer and a positivist analyze the statute's status?
step: Identify the question: is the statute valid law, so that the denouncer acted lawfully at the time?
step: A strong reading of Aquinas says a statute that is unjust by its content lacks the force of law, so the denouncer had no legal justification.
step: The Radbruch formula is narrower: the statute is law unless its injustice is intolerable. Detention without trial for private speech is extreme, so it would yield.
step: A positivist says the statute was valid law under the regime's own criteria, but adds that it was morally monstrous, and that a new legislature or court may adopt a retroactive rule on moral grounds, which raises its own fairness concerns about non-retroactivity.
step: Both sides can reach the same result (punishing the denouncer) by different routes, and they disagree about how to describe the statute rather than about how bad it was.

@example
title: Conclusion or determination?
problem: A legislature enacts (1) a ban on killing the innocent and (2) a rule that contracts must be signed within thirty days. Using Aquinas's categories, how do you classify each?
step: Ask whether natural law leaves room for choice. A ban on killing the innocent follows almost directly from the basic good of life, so it is a conclusion.
step: The thirty-day period could reasonably be twenty or sixty, so natural law does not fix it. It is a determination.
step: Both are legitimate human law; the difference is how closely each is tied to moral principle. Determinations still need to serve the common good.

@mistakes
- Saying natural law theorists claim all unjust rules are invalid; the strong reading is controversial, and Radbruch and Finnis state narrower views.
- Confusing "natural law" with the laws of nature in physics, or with "natural rights" alone.
- Treating the positivist objection as a claim that law is always morally good, instead of a claim about how to describe law.
- Assuming the Declaration of Independence settles the jurisprudential debate; it is political rhetoric that draws on natural law ideas.

@practice self
prompt: In 100 to 150 words, state the positivist objection to the claim that an unjust rule is "not law," and give the natural lawyer's best reply.
hint: Outline each position in one sentence first, then compare them.
hint: Check your draft against each point a good answer must cover.
solution: The positivist objection is that the existence of law and its moral merit are separate questions. If we say a wicked statute is not law, we blur the distinction between validity and obligation and lose the plain way of saying "this is law, but it ought not to be obeyed or should be repealed." Positivists add that clear language helps citizens and officials resist injustice, since it keeps moral judgment explicit. The natural lawyer replies that the concept of law is itself purposive, so that a rule which flagrantly defeats the point of law (the common good) is a defective case, or not law in the full sense. Calling it fully valid law may give it an unearned aura of authority and weaken moral resistance. Finnis softens the claim by treating unjust systems as law in a secondary, deficient sense.
