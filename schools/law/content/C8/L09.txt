@article
A theory of justice tells us how the benefits and burdens of social cooperation should be shared, and which institutions are fair. Law constantly makes distributive choices: tax rates, tort liability, property rules, welfare. This lesson compares the main theories. As with every contested theory, our aim is to state each at its strongest, with its best objection, and not to choose among them.

### Utilitarianism

Bentham and Mill held that the right act or rule is the one that maximizes overall welfare (happiness or preference satisfaction). Its strengths are impartiality (each person counts for one) and a clear standard. *Objection:* it seems to permit sacrificing a minority for aggregate gain, and it ignores the separateness of persons. *Reply:* sophisticated versions use rules or institutions, and argue that respecting rights maximizes welfare in the long run; diminishing marginal utility also supports some redistribution.

### Kantian approaches

Kant's theory begins with respect for persons as ends in themselves, never merely as means. Justice requires rules that all rational agents could accept, protecting a sphere of equal freedom. *Objection:* the principle can be abstract and may give little guidance on distribution or on conflicts between duties. *Reply:* defenders say that it supplies limits (rights against exploitation) and that other principles can fill in policy.

### Rawls: justice as fairness

Rawls asks what principles free and equal persons would choose from an **original position**, behind a **veil of ignorance**, not knowing their class, talents, race, sex or conception of the good. He argues that they would choose two principles:

1. Each person has an equal claim to a fully adequate scheme of equal **basic liberties**.
2. Social and economic inequalities are acceptable only if (a) attached to positions open to all under conditions of **fair equality of opportunity**, and (b) to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged (the **difference principle**).

The difference principle is often illustrated by **maximin**: choose the arrangement in which the worst-off position is best. Rawls applies the principles to the **basic structure** of society, and his index of advantage is "primary goods" (rights, income, opportunities, the social bases of self-respect). *Objections:* (i) maximin is too cautious about risk, since parties might prefer a higher average with a minimum floor; (ii) the theory ignores desert and responsibility. *Replies:* Rawls argues that parties choosing principles for a permanent constitution have reason to be cautious, and that people do not deserve their natural talents.

### Nozick: entitlement theory

Nozick holds that a distribution is just if it arose by just steps: **justice in acquisition** of unowned things, **justice in transfer** (voluntary exchange and gift), and **rectification** of past injustice. Patterned theories (equal shares, shares by need) require continuing interference with free choices, so they violate **self-ownership**. *Objections:* the theory's account of initial acquisition is thin; it may allow great inequality that shapes opportunity, and rectification of history is hard. *Reply:* defenders say that voluntary exchange respects persons and that redistribution by taxation is forced labor in part.

### The capabilities approach

Sen and Nussbaum say that justice should be measured by what people are actually able to do and be (their **capabilities**: health, education, political participation, affiliation), not just by income or primary goods. People with the same resources may have different abilities to convert them. *Objection:* it needs a list of capabilities, which may impose a particular view of the good; measurement is hard. *Reply:* defenders say the list is a threshold for dignity that people can endorse from many viewpoints.

### Law and justice

These theories appear in doctrine as arguments, not as rules: progressive taxation, strict liability, the social safety net, takings and compensation. A lawyer who can say which theory an argument assumes can evaluate it.

@example
title: Two theories on a choice of distribution
problem: A small society of three people chooses among three arrangements of annual income, listed in thousands: Plan X gives (20, 20, 20), Plan Y gives (12, 30, 50), Plan Z gives (18, 25, 28). Which plan does maximin choose, and which does a utilitarian who sums income choose?
step: Compute the totals: X is 60, Y is 92, Z is 71.
step: A utilitarian who maximizes total income chooses Plan Y, with a total of 92.
step: Maximin compares the worst-off position in each plan: X has 20, Y has 12, Z has 18. The highest minimum is 20, so maximin chooses Plan X.
step: The plans give different answers because the theories value different things. Note that Rawls's difference principle allows inequality that benefits the least advantaged, and applies to the basic structure, not to isolated plans.

@example
title: A tax and the entitlement theory
problem: A legislature funds a health program by taxing wages. How would Nozick and Rawls each evaluate the tax in principle?
step: Nozick starts with entitlement. If wages were justly acquired by voluntary exchange, a tax to fund a program beyond protection against force and fraud interferes with self-ownership, and is hard to justify.
step: Rawls starts with the basic structure. If the program raises the position of the least advantaged and does not violate basic liberties, it is a legitimate part of a just system.
step: The disagreement lies in the starting point, since one begins from historical entitlement and the other from fair terms of cooperation. State both without choosing.

@mistakes
- Saying the difference principle requires equal incomes; it permits inequalities that benefit the least advantaged.
- Treating the veil of ignorance as a description of how people actually reason, instead of a device to ensure fairness.
- Describing utilitarianism as majority rule; it counts welfare, not votes.
- Attributing to Nozick the view that the poor deserve nothing; he argues that justice concerns the process, not that outcomes do not matter morally.

@practice self
prompt: A city has a fixed budget and must choose between funding a new transit line that would raise average incomes across the city and funding job training for its poorest residents. In 150 to 200 words, explain how a utilitarian and a Rawlsian might each approach the choice.
hint: State what each theory counts as the measure of a good outcome.
hint: Note the information each would need to decide.
solution: A utilitarian would ask which option produces the greater total welfare, counting each person's gain equally. If the transit line increases overall welfare more, even if most of the gain goes to higher-income residents, the utilitarian would favor it; diminishing marginal utility might favor the training program if it helps people with very low incomes. A Rawlsian would ask how the options affect the least advantaged and would apply the difference principle as part of a just basic structure. If job training improves the position of the worst off more than transit does, and inequalities elsewhere are justified by their benefit to the worst off, the Rawlsian would favor training. If the transit line eventually improves the position of the least advantaged through jobs and lower costs, the Rawlsian could favor it. Both theories require empirical information about effects, and they differ in what they treat as the measure of a good result.
rubric: States the utilitarian measure (total or average welfare).
rubric: States the Rawlsian measure (position of the least advantaged, with basic liberties and fair opportunity).
rubric: Notes the empirical information each would need, and does not declare a winner.
