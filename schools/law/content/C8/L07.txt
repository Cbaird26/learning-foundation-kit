@article
**Critical approaches** question whether law is neutral, and ask who benefits from legal rules and from the way they are justified. They come from different traditions and disagree among themselves, and their critics disagree with them in turn. Your job is to state each claim as its supporters would, and then to state the replies as their critics would.

### Critical legal studies (CLS)

CLS emerged in the 1970s and 1980s among law professors who drew on realism and on social theory. Its recurring claims:

- **Indeterminacy.** Legal doctrine is sufficiently contradictory (for example, between freedom of contract and fairness, or individualism and altruism) that it can justify opposite outcomes. Judges choose; doctrine does not.
- **Law is politics.** Because doctrine does not compel results, legal outcomes reflect political and ideological choices, often favoring existing hierarchies of wealth and power.
- **Legitimation.** Legal reasoning makes contingent arrangements seem natural and neutral, and so makes them harder to challenge.

*Replies.* Hart's core-and-penumbra view says most cases are determinate and that indeterminacy is local. Critics add that CLS's claim of indeterminacy, if true everywhere, would undermine its own arguments. Dworkin replies that law is constrained by fit and by principle. Defenders of the rule of law say that even imperfect legal constraint protects against arbitrary power.

### Critical race theory (CRT)

CRT arose from scholars who asked why racial inequality persisted after formal legal equality. Common themes:

- **Race and structure.** Racial disadvantage can be sustained by facially neutral rules and institutions, not only by individual prejudice.
- **Interest convergence.** Advances in racial justice happen when they also serve the interests of dominant groups.
- **Intersectionality.** The concept holds that people's experience is shaped by overlapping categories (such as race and gender), so single-axis analysis misses some harms.
- **Narrative and experience.** Personal narrative offers evidence about how law works that standard doctrinal analysis can miss.

A doctrinal illustration is *Washington v. Davis* (1976): the Supreme Court held that a law with a racially disproportionate impact violates the Equal Protection Clause only if adopted with a discriminatory purpose, though impact may be evidence of purpose. CRT scholars argue that this requirement leaves much structural inequality without a remedy; the Court's reasoning stressed that disparate-impact liability would call into question many ordinary laws and that statutes may provide impact-based remedies (as Title VII does for employment).

*Replies.* Critics argue that universal, race-neutral principles protect everyone better than group-based analysis; that interest convergence is hard to test empirically; that narrative evidence is hard to evaluate; and that legal progress, including the civil rights statutes, shows the system can correct itself. Proponents respond that these points do not meet the structural claim, and that empirical work on disparities is the evidence for it.

### Feminist legal theory

Feminist legal theory is a family of views, not a single theory:

- **Liberal feminism** seeks equal rights and the removal of sex-based classifications that restrict opportunity.
- **Difference (or cultural) feminism** says that law, built on male experience, undervalues women's distinctive experience, such as caregiving; it asks that law accommodate or value that difference.
- **Dominance feminism** treats sex inequality as a matter of power: the key question is whether a practice subordinates women, not whether it treats men and women alike or differently.
- **Anti-essentialist and intersectional approaches** warn that speaking of "women" as a single group hides differences of race, class and other positions.

*Replies.* Critics say formal equality is the best protection; that "difference" arguments risk reinforcing stereotypes; that dominance theory's claims about power are hard to test; and that generalizing about what women experience is itself essentialist. Proponents reply that neutrality may reproduce existing disadvantage, and that these debates occur within feminism as well.

### Fair summary

A fair summary names the claim, gives the best reason offered for it, and states the strongest reply without caricature. Neither the critics nor their opponents should be described in the other's words.

title: Summarizing a critique with its reply
problem: Summarize the CLS claim that law is indeterminate, and give one reply, in a form that a supporter and a critic would each accept.
step: State the claim as a supporter would: legal doctrine contains opposing principles (for example, freedom of contract and protection of the weak), so judges can justify more than one result.
step: Give the supporter's best reason: pairs of precedents and canons that point in opposite directions, and contested appellate cases.
step: State the best reply as a critic would: most legal questions are determinate, so the indeterminacy claim holds, at most, in a minority of hard cases; and law is constrained by text, precedent and legal craft, which narrow the range of acceptable answers.
step: Add the supporter's rejoinder to remain fair: the hard cases are those that matter for power, and the appearance of determinacy hides choices.

@example
title: Washington v. Davis and a structural critique
problem: A city's police-hiring exam is passed at a much lower rate by applicants of one race, and no evidence shows that the city adopted the exam for a discriminatory purpose. Under the Equal Protection Clause, what follows, and how would a CRT critic respond?
step: Apply the rule: under Washington v. Davis, disparate impact alone does not violate the Equal Protection Clause; the plaintiff must prove discriminatory purpose, and impact may be evidence of purpose.
step: Conclusion: with no evidence of purpose, the equal protection claim likely fails (a statutory claim, such as one under Title VII for an employer, may still be available).
step: A CRT critic would say that this requirement leaves structural inequality unaddressed, since neutral rules can reproduce racial hierarchy.
step: The reply the Court's reasoning suggests: ordinary laws often have uneven effects, and legislatures and Congress can address impact through statutes. Present both without taking a side.

@mistakes
- Treating CLS, CRT and feminist theory as one position; each has internal debates.
- Presenting a critique as if the critics had no replies, or presenting a reply as a refutation.
- Describing Washington v. Davis as holding that impact is irrelevant; impact is evidence of purpose but is not sufficient alone.
- Using the labels as insults rather than as descriptions of a body of argument.
