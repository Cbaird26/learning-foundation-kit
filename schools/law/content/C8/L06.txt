@article
**Law and economics** applies the tools of economics, especially the analysis of incentives and efficiency, to legal rules. It has two modes. The **positive** project explains how legal rules affect behavior and why the common law has the content it has. The **normative** project asks which rules *should* exist, often using efficiency as a standard.

### Efficiency concepts

- **Pareto efficiency.** An outcome is Pareto efficient when no change can make someone better off without making someone worse off. A change is a **Pareto improvement** if it makes at least one person better off and nobody worse off.
- **Kaldor-Hicks efficiency.** A change is Kaldor-Hicks efficient if the winners' gains exceed the losers' losses, so that the winners *could* compensate the losers, whether or not they do. Most legal changes create losers, so this is the standard that economic analysis commonly uses. Posner's wealth maximization is a related idea.

### The Coase theorem

Coase argued that if **transaction costs** are zero and rights are clearly defined, parties will bargain to the efficient outcome whatever the initial assignment of the legal right. If a factory's smoke harms a neighboring laundry, the efficient result (the factory cuts smoke, or the laundry moves, whichever is cheaper) occurs whether or not the law gives the laundry a right to clean air. The assignment of the right affects who is richer, not the outcome. Real transaction costs are not zero, so law's allocation matters. This leads to the idea that the law should assign rights to the party who can use them most efficiently, or that it should lower transaction costs. Calabresi and Melamed's framework distinguishes **property rules** (entitlements protected by injunction), **liability rules** (protected by damages) and **inalienability rules**.

### The Hand formula

In *United States v. Carroll Towing Co.* (1947), a barge broke loose, and the court analyzed negligence in terms of the burden of precautions against the probability and gravity of the harm. In the usual formulation, a party is negligent if

$$B < P \times L,$$

where $B$ is the burden (cost) of the precaution, $P$ the probability of the harm and $L$ the magnitude of the loss. The idea is that precautions should be taken only when they cost less than the expected harm they prevent. Courts do not usually apply the formula numerically, but they use its logic when weighing reasonableness.

### Efficient breach

A contract party may find that breaching and paying expectation damages leaves everyone as well off as performance would, and leaves the breaching party better off. If the seller's profit from selling to a third party exceeds the damages owed to the buyer, the breach is efficient, because the goods go to the party who values them most. Critics say the theory ignores reliance, promises as moral commitments and the cost of litigation.

### Objections

1. **Wealth and distribution.** Efficiency depends on willingness to pay, which depends on wealth. A rule may be efficient because the rich value something more, so efficiency does not settle fairness. Dworkin's well-known objection is that wealth is not itself a value that a legal system should aim to maximize.
2. **Behavioral findings.** Experiments suggest that people often depart from the rational-actor model, for example by valuing what they own more than what they could buy (the endowment effect). Economists respond by adjusting models to include such findings.
3. **Measurement.** Costs and benefits, such as the value of life or dignity, are hard to quantify.
4. **Descriptive claims about the common law.** The claim that common-law rules tend toward efficiency is contested.

Law and economists respond that efficiency is one value among others, that the framework is useful even if distribution is left to tax and transfer systems, and that making costs explicit improves legal reasoning.

@example
title: The Hand formula in practice
problem: A warehouse could prevent forklift collisions at a cost of \$9,000 per year by installing a warning system. Without it, the chance of a serious collision in a year is 2%, and the loss from such a collision would be \$500,000. Under the Hand formula, is failing to install it negligent?
step: Identify $B = \$9{,}000$, $P = 0.02$, $L = \$500{,}000$.
step: Compute the expected loss: $P \times L = 0.02 \times 500{,}000 = \$10{,}000$.
step: Compare: $B = 9{,}000 < 10{,}000 = PL$. The precaution costs less than the harm it prevents.
step: Conclusion: under the Hand formula, failing to install the system is negligent. If $B$ were above \$10,000, the formula would find no negligence.

@example
title: Analyzing a decision from two perspectives
problem: A court holds a grocery store liable when a customer slips on a puddle that had been on the floor for an hour. How might a law-and-economics analyst and a realist describe the decision?
step: The economic analyst asks who could prevent the accident most cheaply. Regular floor inspections cost little compared with the expected injuries, so liability gives the store an incentive to inspect.
step: The realist asks what else may be influencing the decision. The rule ("reasonable care") is flexible, so the judge's view of fairness to injured customers and of store practices, or the jury's sympathies, may fill the gap.
step: The two views are not exclusive: economics offers a normative rationale, and realism offers a description of how indeterminate standards are applied.

@mistakes
- Treating Kaldor-Hicks efficiency as if the losers were actually compensated; it requires only that winners could compensate.
- Stating the Coase theorem without its conditions (low transaction costs, well-defined rights).
- Applying the Hand formula with the wrong inequality; negligence requires $B < PL$.
- Presenting efficient breach as lawless; it assumes that expectation damages are paid.
