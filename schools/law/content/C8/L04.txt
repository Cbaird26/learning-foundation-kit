@article
Dworkin argued, against Hart, that law includes more than rules, and that judges in hard cases are not free to legislate. Instead, they are bound to find the best answer that the law, properly understood, already supplies.

### Rules and principles

Dworkin draws on *Riggs v. Palmer* (1889). A grandson who murdered his grandfather stood to inherit under a valid will, and the statute of wills had no exception for killers. The New York Court of Appeals held that he could not inherit, citing the principle that no one should profit from his own wrong. Dworkin says that the principle was part of the law, though it was not enacted and does not come from any single precedent. He distinguishes:

- **Rules** apply in an all-or-nothing way: if the facts they stipulate are given, then either the rule is valid and the result follows, or it is not valid.
- **Principles** state a reason pointing in one direction, with a dimension of **weight**. When principles conflict (freedom of contract versus protection against unconscionable terms), the judge weighs them, and neither is invalidated.

Dworkin's attack was that Hart's rule of recognition identifies law by **pedigree** (how a norm was enacted or decided). Principles, he argues, are law because of their fit with the legal materials and their moral soundness, not their pedigree, so positivism cannot explain them. And if principles are law, judges in hard cases are applying law, not exercising discretion.

### Law as integrity

Dworkin's mature theory treats law as an **interpretive** practice. Law as **integrity** asks judges to decide as if the community spoke with one coherent voice, by principles of justice, fairness and procedural due process. A judge must find the principles that satisfy two tests:

1. **Fit.** The principles must fit the existing legal materials (statutes, precedent, constitutional text) well enough to count as an interpretation of them, not an invention.
2. **Justification.** Among the interpretations that fit adequately, the judge chooses the one that shows the law in its best moral light.

Dworkin's image is the **chain novel**: each judge, like a novelist adding a chapter, must read what earlier authors wrote and continue the story coherently. His ideal judge, **Hercules**, has superhuman ability and time to work out the best interpretation. Dworkin also holds a **rights thesis**: judges should decide hard cases on arguments of **principle** (about individual rights) rather than **policy** (about collective goals such as economic growth), since the latter are for legislators. And he defends the **right answer thesis**: even in hard cases, one answer is usually best, though people disagree about which.

### Replies from positivists

- **Inclusive positivism.** The rule of recognition may itself direct judges to apply moral principles. If so, principles are law because a social rule says so, and positivism survives.
- **Hart's postscript.** Hart answered that he had always allowed that legal standards could be morally based, and that in cases where all sources fail, judges still have discretion.
- **Raz's exclusive positivism.** Where judges apply moral principles that the law does not contain, they are not applying law but exercising moral judgment that law directs them to.

### Objections to Dworkin

- **Hercules is idealized.** Real judges cannot do what Hercules does, so the theory seems to give little guidance.
- **Indeterminacy and the right answer.** In a society with reasonable moral disagreement, the idea that a single answer is "right" seems implausible, or it merely reflects the judge's own morality.
- **Hidden politics.** Fit and justification may permit judges to choose results they prefer; critics argue this hides judicial discretion under another name.
- **Description or prescription?** Critics ask whether Dworkin describes the law or recommends how judges should decide.

Treat the debate as a question about how best to describe what judges do in hard cases, a question where both sides have serious arguments.

@example
title: Hart and Dworkin on a gap
problem: A statute says a life-insurance beneficiary is entitled to the policy's proceeds. It says nothing about a beneficiary who intentionally kills the insured. A court must decide a claim by such a beneficiary. How would Hart and Dworkin describe the court's decision if it denies the claim?
step: Identify the gap: the statute's text clearly grants proceeds to beneficiaries, but a killer is a penumbral case the legislature probably did not consider.
step: Hart: the rule runs out. The judge has discretion, and denying the claim is a legitimate choice, informed by the purpose of insurance law and by widely held values. In doing so, the judge makes law in a limited way.
step: Dworkin: the judge is not free to choose. The principle that no one should profit from his own wrong is already part of the law and has weight. It fits the law of wills, contracts and crimes, and it justifies the practice. The judge applies the law.
step: Note the limits of the disagreement. Both approaches can lead to the same result. They differ about whether the outcome was already required by law and so about how to describe the judge's authority.

@example
title: Principle or policy?
problem: A judge writes two possible grounds for protecting a newspaper's right to publish: (a) "Each person has a right to speak and to hear without government censorship," and (b) "Allowing the paper to publish will increase advertising revenue and boost the local economy." Which is an argument of principle and which of policy?
step: An argument of principle appeals to an individual or group right. Ground (a) states a right to speak and to hear.
step: An argument of policy appeals to a collective goal. Ground (b) points to economic benefits.
step: Dworkin's rights thesis says that courts should decide hard cases on grounds like (a), because they involve rights, and leave policy to legislatures.

@mistakes
- Saying that Dworkin denies that rules exist; he claims that law also contains principles, which function differently.
- Treating principles as merely moral opinions; Dworkin says they must fit the legal materials to count as law.
- Assuming that Dworkin says judges can always impose their own morality; fit is a real constraint.
- Presenting Hart as denying that judges rely on morality; he says they may, but he calls the result discretion.

prompt: A statute allows a landlord to evict a tenant for "nuisance." A tenant plays quiet music at 9 p.m., and the statute and precedent give no clear answer. In 150 to 200 words, explain how Hart and Dworkin would each describe how the judge should decide.
hint: Outline each position in one sentence first, then compare them.
hint: Check your draft against each point a good answer must cover.
solution: Hart would say that "nuisance" has a core (loud, disruptive conduct) and a penumbra, and that quiet music at 9 p.m. lies in the penumbra, so the statute does not settle the case. The judge has discretion, guided by the statute's purpose of protecting neighbors' use of their homes and by relevant considerations, and in exercising it the judge makes law in the gap. Dworkin would say that the judge should seek the principles that best fit and justify the law of nuisance, tenancy and neighborly use, such as reasonable use of property and protection of quiet enjoyment, and apply them, so that the law already supplies an answer which the judge must find. They may reach the same result, but they differ on whether the judge is making law or finding it.
