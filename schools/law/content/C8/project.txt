title: A jurisprudence essay on a hard case
overview: You will write a 2,000-word essay on a supplied hypothetical hard case. First explain how a positivist, a natural lawyer, a realist and a textualist or originalist would each approach it, then defend your own view. The aim is accurate, charitable presentation of each theory followed by a reasoned conclusion of your own.
goal: Present each of four jurisprudential approaches accurately and in a form its defenders would accept.
goal: Identify the strongest objection to each approach as applied to the case.
goal: Defend a considered view of how the case should be decided, and say what you would give up to hold it.
step: Read the case materials below twice. List the facts, the statutory text and the question the court must answer.
step: For each of the four approaches, write one paragraph on how it identifies the governing law and what it concludes. For the positivist, say whether the answer comes from the sources of law or from discretion (Hart), and note Dworkin's reply. For the natural lawyer, address whether any moral limit on law is engaged. For the realist, identify what besides the rules may influence the decision. For the textualist or originalist, apply the text and the canons, noting what the text and context settle.
step: Write one paragraph on the strongest objection to each approach in this case, drawing on Weeks 1 to 12.
step: Choose your own view. State the result you would reach, the theory or combination of theories that best supports it, and the reasons. Say what the best opposing argument is and why you reject it.
step: Check your essay for charity: give each approach in terms its defenders would accept, and do not write the opposing views in words chosen to make them look weak.
step: Edit to about 2,000 words. Include a short introduction, one section per approach, a section stating and defending your view, and a brief conclusion.
material: **The case: Ana v. Brightline Inc.** Ana was the bookkeeper for Brightline Inc., a private company. She noticed ledger entries that she believed concealed false statements to investors. She reported her concerns to her supervisor, who told her to stop asking questions. Ana then copied 200 pages of the ledgers without the company's permission and delivered them to the State Securities Office, which opened an investigation. Brightline discharged her the next day. Ana sues, claiming that her discharge violated the state's Fraud Reporting Act. Brightline argues that she broke the Confidential Records Act and that the Fraud Reporting Act does not protect her. The question is whether Ana's discharge was unlawful.
material: **Statutory text (invented for this exercise).** Fraud Reporting Act § 2: "An employer shall not discharge, demote or otherwise penalize an employee because the employee reported to a public authority a suspected violation of law." Fraud Reporting Act § 3: "Nothing in this Act authorizes an employee to take or disclose records of an employer that are protected by the Confidential Records Act." Confidential Records Act § 1: "No employee shall copy or remove confidential records of an employer without the employer's written consent." Confidential Records Act § 4: "An employer may discharge an employee who violates this Act." The committee report on the Fraud Reporting Act says that its purpose is "to encourage employees to bring fraud to the attention of the authorities." The Confidential Records Act was enacted twenty years earlier to protect trade secrets and client data.
material: **Further facts.** The ledgers contained no trade secrets, but they did include some client names. Ana's supervisor had been told of her concerns before the copying. The state's highest court has never decided how the two statutes relate, and the trial court's reasoning is not in the record.
deliverable: A 2,000-word essay (plus or minus 10 percent) with a title and section headings.
deliverable: A one-paragraph note, after the essay, listing the two weakest points in your own argument.
rubric: Accuracy: each theory is described correctly (for example, Hart's rule of recognition and discretion, Dworkin's principles, the realist focus on what influences decisions, textualist use of text and canons).
rubric: Charity: each approach is stated in terms its defenders would accept, with its best objection and the best reply.
rubric: Application: each approach is applied to the specific statutory text and facts, not stated in the abstract.
rubric: Argument: your own view is stated clearly, supported by reasons, and tested against the strongest opposing argument.
rubric: Organization and writing: clear structure, no padding, within 10 percent of 2,000 words.
tutor: Which provision of the two statutes controls if they conflict, and what canons might help me decide?
tutor: How would Hart describe the court's task if the text of both statutes seems to apply?
tutor: What is the best objection to my own view, and how would I answer it?

### Week by week

- **Week 1, natural law.** Law and morality are connected. Know Aquinas (conclusion and determination, "unjust law seems to be no law"), Fuller's eight principles, Finnis's basic goods, the Radbruch formula and the objections.
- **Week 2, positivism.** Separability and social sources; Austin's command theory and its failures; Kelsen; inclusive and exclusive positivism.
- **Week 3, Hart.** Primary and secondary rules; rule of recognition as a social practice of officials; internal and external points of view; core, penumbra and discretion.
- **Week 4, Dworkin.** Rules and principles, *Riggs v. Palmer*, law as integrity (fit and justification), principle versus policy, the right answer thesis, and the replies and objections.
- **Week 5, realism.** Prediction theory, the bad man, rule and fact skepticism, canons "thrust and parry," and the objections (easy cases, Hart's point about the internal view).
- **Week 6, law and economics.** Pareto and Kaldor-Hicks efficiency, Coase, the Hand formula $B < PL$, efficient breach, and objections about wealth, behavior and measurement.
- **Week 7, critical and feminist theory.** CLS indeterminacy, CRT (structure, interest convergence, intersectionality, *Washington v. Davis*), feminist strands, and fair summaries with replies.
- **Week 8, rights.** Hohfeld's four pairs, will and interest theories, natural and legal rights, and three critiques with replies.
- **Week 9, justice.** Utilitarian, Kantian, Rawlsian, entitlement and capabilities approaches; maximin versus sum.
- **Week 10, authority and disobedience.** Weber's types; consent, fair play, natural duty and Raz; civil disobedience and necessity.
- **Week 11, statutory interpretation.** Textualism, purposivism, intentionalism, canons, major questions, *Loper Bright* (overruling *Chevron*).
- **Week 12, constitutional interpretation.** Original intent and public meaning, living constitutionalism, text-and-history cases, Article V.

### Self-check

- Can you state each theory so that its defenders would agree with your statement?
- Can you give the strongest objection to each, and the best reply?
- Can you apply two theories to one fact pattern and say where they diverge?
- Can you explain the difference between a claim about what law is and a claim about what law should be?

### Common errors

- Equating positivism with the claim that unjust laws must be obeyed.
- Attributing to Hart a view that judges never use morality, or to Dworkin the view that judges may impose any morality.
- Treating realism as "judges decide as they like."
- Stating efficiency as if it settled fairness.
- Presenting a critique without its replies, or the reverse.
- Choosing a side on a contested constitutional or political question in an exam answer that asks for neutral presentation.

Which description of the three approaches is accurate?
choice*: A Dworkinian would say the court applied a legal principle that is already part of the law, Hart would call it a penumbral gap filled by discretion, and a strict textualist would ask whether the text read in context supports an exception.
choice: A Dworkinian would say the court exercised discretion, Hart would say the court applied a principle that is already part of the law, and a textualist would approve of the decision because purposes matter most.
choice: All three would agree that the court exercised unconstrained discretion.
choice: A Dworkinian would say the statute is not law because it is unjust, and Hart would agree.
hint: Recall which theorist says hard cases are decided by law and which says they are decided by discretion.
hint: A textualist begins with the enacted text, not with the maxim.
solution: Dworkin: principles are law, so the court applied law. Hart: in the penumbra, the rule runs out and the judge has discretion. A textualist asks whether the statute's text in context allows an exception. The other choices reverse the positions or misstate them.

prompt: A utility can install a safety system for \$40,000 per year. Without it, the chance of a fatal accident in a year is 4%; with it, 1%. The loss from an accident is \$2,000,000. Using the Hand formula's logic, what is the net benefit in dollars of installing the system, defined as the reduction in expected loss minus the cost of the system?
answer: 20000
check: round((0.04 - 0.01) * 2000000 - 40000)
unit: dollars
hint: First compute the expected loss with and without the system.
hint: The reduction in probability is 3 percentage points of \$2,000,000.
solution: The reduction in expected loss is $(0.04 - 0.01) \times \$2{,}000{,}000 = \$60{,}000$. Subtracting the cost of \$40,000 gives a net benefit of \$20,000. Because $B < \Delta P \times L$, the Hand formula points toward negligence if the precaution was not taken. A critic might add that this analysis ignores who bears the burden and the value of life, which are objections from Week 6.

@exam self
prompt: A court must decide whether a statute that is clearly written but morally troubling is valid and how it should be applied. In 300 to 400 words, state how a positivist, a natural lawyer and a textualist each approach the question; then name one objection to each approach; and finish by identifying one point on which they agree.
hint: Separate the question of validity from the question of application.
hint: Give each theory one sentence and one objection, then look for agreement (for example, that the statute is morally troubling and may be criticized).
solution: A positivist identifies the statute as valid law if it satisfies the system's criteria of validity, such as proper enactment, and treats its moral defects as grounds for criticism and repeal; the objection is that this may leave too little room for the moral limits on law that many people believe exist. A natural lawyer holds that law is connected to morality, so that an extreme injustice may deprive a rule of legal force (the Radbruch formula) or make it a defective instance of law; the objection is that moral disagreement makes legal validity hard to identify. A textualist asks what the enacted words mean in context and applies them unless the text itself, read with canons such as the absurdity doctrine, leads elsewhere; the objection is that ordinary meaning is often indeterminate, and literal readings may produce results no legislature would have accepted. All three can agree that the statute's moral troubles are a legitimate subject of criticism and that legislatures and citizens may seek reform; they disagree on how far those troubles affect validity and on the proper role of judges.
rubric: States each of the three approaches accurately.
rubric: Gives one real objection to each.
rubric: Identifies a genuine point of agreement and stays neutral.
