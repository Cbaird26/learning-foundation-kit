@article
The federal income tax is a tax on **taxable income**, a number the Internal Revenue Code builds in stages. Almost every problem in this course is one stage of the same computation, so learn the skeleton first.

### The formula

1. **Gross income** (§ 61): "all income from whatever source derived," unless a provision excludes it (Weeks 2 and 3).
2. Subtract the **above-the-line deductions** listed in § 62 (business expenses of a sole proprietor, rental expenses, losses on property sales, and similar items). The result is **adjusted gross income (AGI)**.
3. Subtract the greater of the **standard deduction** or **itemized deductions** (§ 63(b), (c), (d)). Under § 63(a) the result is **taxable income**. A separate deduction for qualified business income (§ 199A) can also reduce taxable income, and Congress made it permanent in the 2025 budget reconciliation law.
4. Apply the **rate schedule** in § 1 (which depends on filing status) to get the tax.
5. Subtract **credits** to get the net liability, then compare it with withholding and estimated payments to see what is owed or refunded.

AGI matters beyond the formula, because many phase-outs and floors are keyed to it (for example the medical-expense floor in Week 6).

### Deduction versus credit

A **deduction** reduces the income to which rates apply, so its value is the deduction times the taxpayer's marginal rate. A **credit** reduces the tax itself, dollar for dollar. A \$1,000 deduction saves \$220 for someone whose marginal rate is 22%; a \$1,000 credit saves \$1,000. An **exclusion** keeps an item out of gross income altogether; a **deferral** postpones the year of inclusion; **nonrecognition** postpones gain (Week 9).

### Progressive rates: marginal and average

Section 1 is **progressive**: each bracket's rate applies only to the slice of taxable income inside that bracket. The **marginal rate** is the rate on the next dollar; the **average (effective) rate** is total tax divided by taxable income. A raise never lowers take-home pay merely by pushing the taxpayer into a higher bracket, because only the excess is taxed at the higher rate. The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 lowered most rates and suspended personal exemptions, and the 2025 budget reconciliation law made those rate structures permanent. Dollar thresholds are adjusted for inflation, so in this course every bracket, deduction amount and credit amount is given to you as an assumption in the problem.

### Sources of authority

The hierarchy runs from the **Constitution** (the Sixteenth Amendment authorizes taxes on incomes without apportionment) to the **Internal Revenue Code**, then **Treasury regulations** (issued under § 7805 and specific grants), then **revenue rulings and revenue procedures** (the IRS's published positions), and finally **private letter rulings**, which bind only the taxpayer who received them and may not be cited as precedent by others (§ 6110(k)(3)). Courts decide cases in the Tax Court, district courts, and the Court of Federal Claims, with appeals to the circuits and the Supreme Court.

After *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo* (2024), which overruled *Chevron*, a court decides for itself the best reading of the Code. An agency's interpretation receives only *Skidmore* weight, the persuasiveness that its reasoning earns. Where Congress has expressly delegated authority to prescribe the rules (a "legislative" regulation), courts ask whether the agency stayed within that delegation. Earlier decisions that applied *Chevron* to tax regulations remain good law by statutory stare decisis.

### Why the structure exists

Economists often define income as consumption plus the change in net worth (the Haig-Simons definition). The Code departs from it in many ways: it taxes gains only when **realized**, it excludes some benefits, and it allows deductions for costs of earning income but not personal consumption (§ 262). Learning those departures is the course.

@example
title: From wages to tax liability
problem: Single taxpayer Ana earns \$90,000 in wages and has a \$3,000 above-the-line deduction. Assume the standard deduction is \$15,000, she does not itemize, and the rate schedule is 10% on taxable income up to \$12,000, 12% on income from \$12,000 to \$48,000, and 22% on income above \$48,000. She has a \$1,000 credit. Find her net tax and her marginal rate.
step: AGI is gross income minus the § 62 deduction: \$90,000 - \$3,000 = \$87,000.
step: Taxable income is AGI minus the standard deduction: \$87,000 - \$15,000 = \$72,000.
step: Tax on the first \$12,000 is \$1,200; on the next \$36,000 is 12% of \$36,000 = \$4,320; on the last \$24,000 is 22% of \$24,000 = \$5,280. Total tax is \$1,200 + \$4,320 + \$5,280 = \$10,800.
step: Subtract the credit: \$10,800 - \$1,000 = \$9,800. Her marginal rate is 22% and her average rate on taxable income is \$10,800 / \$72,000 = 15%.

@mistakes
- Applying the top bracket's rate to all taxable income instead of only the slice above the threshold.
- Subtracting a credit from income, or a deduction from tax; credits come off the tax, deductions come off income.
- Treating the standard deduction as an above-the-line item; it is subtracted after AGI is found.
- Treating a Treasury regulation or revenue ruling as binding on a court that disagrees with it after Loper Bright.
