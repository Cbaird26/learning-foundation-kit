@article
Section 262 states the default: no deduction for personal, living or family expenses. The Code then makes a few exceptions, collected as **itemized deductions**, and offers a flat **standard deduction** as an alternative (§ 63(c)). A taxpayer takes whichever is larger (§ 63(b), (d)). Because Congress raised the standard deduction in 2017 and again in 2025, most taxpayers now do not itemize. The amounts are adjusted for inflation, so in this course they are assumptions.

### The main itemized deductions

- **Medical expenses (§ 213):** deductible to the extent they exceed a percentage of AGI (7.5% is the statutory floor; problems give it as an assumption).
- **State and local taxes, "SALT" (§ 164):** property taxes and income (or sales) taxes, subject to a **cap** (§ 164(b)(6)). The 2017 act set a \$10,000 cap; the 2025 reconciliation law temporarily raised it and phases it down for higher incomes, so use the cap given in each problem.
- **Home mortgage interest (§ 163(h)):** interest on acquisition indebtedness up to a dollar limit (the 2025 law made the lower limit adopted in 2017 permanent) and mortgage-insurance premiums treated as interest under the 2025 law from 2026.
- **Charitable contributions (§ 170):** gifts to qualified organizations, limited by percentages of AGI (cash gifts to public charities are limited to 60% of AGI under current law), with substantiation requirements. Beginning in 2026 the 2025 law adds a floor for itemizers (the first part of contributions is not deductible) and a limited deduction for non-itemizers.
- **Casualty and theft losses (§ 165(h)):** personal casualty losses are deductible only if attributable to a federally declared disaster (the 2025 law extends this to certain state-declared disasters), after a per-event reduction and a 10%-of-AGI floor.
- **Interest on investment indebtedness and others** (§ 163(d)) in narrow cases.

### Suspended and new items

**Miscellaneous itemized deductions** (those subject to the 2%-of-AGI floor of § 67), including unreimbursed employee expenses, tax preparation fees and investment fees, are suspended by § 67(g); the 2025 law made the suspension permanent. The deduction for personal exemptions was reduced to zero in 2017 and that is also permanent. The 2025 law adds temporary deductions (2025 through 2028) for qualified tips, qualified overtime compensation and interest on certain new vehicle loans, and a deduction for older taxpayers, each with limits and income phase-outs given by the statute; also, beginning in 2026, a new limit reduces the tax value of itemized deductions for taxpayers in the top bracket. Check the Code for the amounts, which this course will supply.

### Policy

Deductions for medical costs, taxes, mortgage interest and charity are tax expenditures: Congress allows them to reduce the cost of socially favored spending or to reflect reduced ability to pay. Because deductions are worth more to taxpayers in higher brackets, critics call them regressive; defenders say they reflect ability to pay. A larger standard deduction simplifies filing but reduces the incentive effect.

### Applying it

Compute each item with its floor or cap, add them to total itemized deductions, compare with the standard deduction, and use the larger. The choice is made year by year.

@example
title: Itemizing or taking the standard deduction
problem: Assume the standard deduction is \$15,000, the SALT cap is \$10,000 and the medical floor is 7.5% of AGI. Tara has AGI of \$80,000, medical expenses of \$9,000, state income and property taxes of \$13,000, home mortgage interest of \$6,500 and charitable gifts of \$3,000. Which should she take?
step: Medical: the floor is 7.5% x \$80,000 = \$6,000, so the deduction is \$9,000 - \$6,000 = \$3,000.
step: SALT: \$13,000 of taxes, limited by the \$10,000 cap, gives \$10,000.
step: Total itemized: \$3,000 + \$10,000 + \$6,500 + \$3,000 = \$22,500, which exceeds the \$15,000 standard deduction.
step: Tara itemizes and her deduction is \$22,500.

@mistakes
- Deducting all medical expenses instead of only the excess over the AGI floor.
- Ignoring the SALT cap.
- Claiming unreimbursed employee expenses as itemized deductions even though they are suspended.
- Adding the standard deduction to itemized deductions instead of choosing the larger.
