@article
**Section 61(a)** defines gross income as "all income from whatever source derived," and lists examples: compensation for services, business income, gains from dealings in property, interest, rents, royalties, dividends, and income from discharge of indebtedness. The list is illustrative. Congress used the full extent of its taxing power, so the working assumption is that a receipt is income unless a specific provision excludes it.

### The definition of income

*Commissioner v. Glenshaw Glass Co.* (1955) held that punitive damages are income and described income as "undeniable accessions to wealth, clearly realized, and over which the taxpayers have complete dominion." That gives three elements: (1) an **accession to wealth**, (2) that is **clearly realized**, and (3) over which the taxpayer has **dominion**. Applications:

- *Cesarini v. United States* (1969, Ohio federal district court): cash found in a piano bought years earlier was income when discovered. The Treasury regulations adopt the same rule for treasure trove: income in the year it is reduced to undisputed possession.
- *James v. United States* (1961): embezzled money is income to the embezzler. Illegality does not exempt a receipt.
- *Old Colony Trust Co. v. Commissioner* (1929): when an employer pays an employee's income tax, the payment is additional compensation.

### What is not income

- **Loan proceeds.** Borrowing is not an accession to wealth because the borrower has an obligation to repay.
- **Return of capital.** Recovering what you invested (basis) is not income; only the excess is gain (Week 7).
- **Unrealized appreciation.** *Eisner v. Macomber* (1920) and the realization requirement mean a stock that rises in value produces no income until a sale or other realization event.
- **Imputed income.** The value of one's own services or the rental value of one's own home is not taxed, though a cash payment for the same services would be.

### Barter

If you receive property or services in exchange for services, you have income equal to the **fair market value** of what you receive (Treas. Reg. § 1.61-2(d)). Two people trading services each have income, even though no cash moves.

### Discharge of indebtedness

When a creditor forgives a debt, the borrower is relieved of an obligation previously treated as wealth, so § 61(a)(11) makes the cancelled amount gross income. Section 108 excludes it in several cases. The two most important: discharge in a **title 11 bankruptcy case**, and discharge while the taxpayer is **insolvent**, but only to the extent of the insolvency (liabilities minus the fair market value of assets immediately before the discharge). An excluded amount is not free; the taxpayer reduces tax attributes (such as loss carryovers and basis) under § 108(b). A gift from the creditor out of generosity is rare; creditors usually forgive for business reasons, so the discharge is income, not a gift.

### Policy and application

Broad inclusion serves fairness (equal incomes taxed equally) and prevents taxpayers from converting wages into untaxed in-kind benefits. When you analyze a receipt, ask: Is it an accession to wealth? Realized? Does the taxpayer have dominion? Is there an obligation to repay? Then check whether an exclusion (Week 3) applies. Conclude with the amount and the year.

@example
title: Barter between professionals
problem: A plumber repairs a lawyer's pipes (the plumber's usual price is \$800). In exchange, the lawyer drafts a will for the plumber (the lawyer's usual price is \$800). Neither pays cash. What is each person's gross income?
step: Under § 61(a)(1) and Reg. § 1.61-2(d), each receives services in exchange for services, an accession to wealth that is realized when the services are provided.
step: Each person includes the fair market value of what he or she received. The plumber received a will worth \$800 and the lawyer received repairs worth \$800.
step: Each has \$800 of gross income, in addition to any other income. The absence of cash does not matter.

@example
title: Forgiven debt and insolvency
problem: Cara owes a bank \$10,000 on an unsecured loan. The bank accepts \$4,000 in full settlement and forgives the other \$6,000. Cara is not in bankruptcy. What is her income if (a) she is solvent, and (b) just before the discharge her liabilities exceed her assets by \$4,000?
step: The cancelled amount is \$10,000 - \$4,000 = \$6,000, which is income under § 61(a)(11) unless § 108 applies.
step: (a) Solvent: no § 108 exclusion applies, so Cara has \$6,000 of income.
step: (b) Insolvent by \$4,000: the exclusion under § 108(a)(1)(B) is limited to the amount of insolvency, \$4,000. The remaining \$6,000 - \$4,000 = \$2,000 is income.

@mistakes
- Treating forgiven debt as a nontaxable gift or as "not income because no cash was received."
- Treating loan proceeds as income, or treating unsold appreciation as income.
- Excluding all discharged debt for an insolvent taxpayer instead of only the amount of insolvency.
- Concluding that illegal receipts are not income.
