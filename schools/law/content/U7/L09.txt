@article
Realized gain is generally recognized (§ 1001(c)), but Congress defers or excludes gain in situations where the taxpayer's investment continues in a similar form or where the property is a home. Nonrecognition is usually **deferral**, preserved by a **substituted basis**.

### Like-kind exchanges: § 1031

Under § 1031(a), no gain or loss is recognized on the exchange of **real property** held for **productive use in a trade or business or for investment** for like-kind real property that will be held for those purposes. Since the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017, § 1031 applies **only to real property**. Exchanges of equipment, vehicles, artwork and other personal property no longer qualify, and they are taxable (with depreciation recapture for equipment). "Like kind" refers to the nature or character of the property, not its grade or quality, so an apartment building can be exchanged for farmland or raw land, but real property in the United States is not like-kind to real property outside the United States. Property held primarily for sale (inventory), and a personal residence, do not qualify.

**Timing (§ 1031(a)(3)):** the taxpayer must **identify** the replacement property within **45 days** after transferring the relinquished property and **receive** it within **180 days** (or the due date of the return, if earlier). Most exchanges are **deferred**, using a **qualified intermediary** who holds the proceeds; if the taxpayer actually or constructively receives the cash, the exchange fails.

**Boot** is money or other property that is not like-kind received in the exchange. Under § 1031(b) gain is recognized to the extent of the boot received, but never more than the realized gain. Relief from a liability counts as money received, and a liability the taxpayer takes on offsets liabilities relieved (netting), and cash the taxpayer pays also offsets liability relief, but liabilities the taxpayer takes on do not offset cash or other boot the taxpayer receives.

**Loss** is never recognized in a like-kind exchange (§ 1031(c)).

**Basis (§ 1031(d)):** the basis of the new property equals the adjusted basis of the old property, **decreased** by money (including liability relief) received and **increased** by gain recognized. Equivalently, new basis equals the fair market value of the new property minus the deferred gain. The holding period includes that of the old property (§ 1223(1)).

### Sale of a principal residence: § 121

Section 121(a) lets a taxpayer **exclude up to \$250,000** of gain from the sale of a principal residence (\$500,000 for married taxpayers filing jointly, if either spouse meets the ownership test and both meet the use test). The taxpayer must have **owned** and **used** it as a principal residence for **at least two of the five years** before the sale; the two years need not be consecutive. Use of the exclusion is limited to once every two years. These amounts are fixed in the statute and are not indexed for inflation.

If the taxpayer fails the two-year tests because of a change in place of employment, health, or unforeseen circumstances, the exclusion is reduced in proportion to the fraction of 24 months satisfied (for example, 18 months qualifies for 18/24 of the maximum). Gain attributable to **depreciation** taken after May 6, 1997 (for example for a home office) is not excludable (§ 121(d)(6)), and gain allocated to periods of **nonqualified use** after 2008 (such as renting the house before moving in) is also not excludable. A **loss** on a residence is a personal loss and not deductible.

### Involuntary conversions: § 1033

If property is destroyed or condemned and the taxpayer reinvests the proceeds in similar property within the statutory period, gain is deferred in a similar way.

### Policy

The like-kind rule rests on continuity of investment and difficulty of valuation; critics say it is a subsidy for real estate investors, and Congress narrowed it in 2017. The home sale exclusion reflects mobility and relieves the effect of inflation on home values.

### Applying it

For § 1031: confirm real property, investment or business use, like kind, timing; then compute realized gain, boot, recognized gain (the lesser), and new basis. For § 121: check ownership, use and frequency; then compute gain, subtract the exclusion, and add back any depreciation.

@example
title: A like-kind exchange with cash boot
problem: Ana exchanges a rental building with an adjusted basis of \$300,000 and a fair market value of \$500,000 (no mortgage) for another rental building worth \$450,000 plus \$50,000 in cash. Find her realized gain, recognized gain, and basis in the new building.
step: Amount realized is \$450,000 + \$50,000 = \$500,000, so realized gain is \$500,000 - \$300,000 = \$200,000.
step: Boot received is the \$50,000 of cash. Recognized gain is the lesser of the boot (\$50,000) and the realized gain (\$200,000): \$50,000.
step: New basis = old basis \$300,000 - boot received \$50,000 + gain recognized \$50,000 = \$300,000. Check: fair market value \$450,000 minus deferred gain \$150,000 equals \$300,000.

@example
title: The home sale exclusion
problem: Brooke, who is single, bought her home years ago for \$200,000 and has lived in it as her principal residence for six years. She sells it for \$520,000 and pays \$20,000 in selling costs. What gain is recognized?
step: Amount realized is \$520,000 - \$20,000 = \$500,000; realized gain is \$500,000 - \$200,000 = \$300,000.
step: She meets the ownership and use tests, so she may exclude up to \$250,000.
step: Recognized gain is \$300,000 - \$250,000 = \$50,000.

@mistakes
- Applying § 1031 to equipment, vehicles or other personal property after 2017.
- Recognizing the whole realized gain instead of the lesser of boot and realized gain.
- Forgetting that liability relief is treated as boot, or forgetting the 45-day identification rule.
- Giving the new property a fair market value basis instead of a substituted basis.
