@article
Progressive rates give families a reason to shift income to members in lower brackets. The courts responded with the **assignment of income doctrine**: **income is taxed to the person who earns it or who owns the property that produces it**, whatever private arrangement redirects the payment.

### The cases

- *Lucas v. Earl* (1930): a husband and wife agreed that each would own half of all earnings. The Court held the husband was taxable on all his salary: anticipatory arrangements and contracts, however skillfully devised, could not stop the salary from being his income when earned. The image is that the fruit is attributed to the tree on which it grew.
- *Helvering v. Horst* (1940): a father detached interest coupons from a bond and gave them to his son shortly before they were due. The son collected. The father was taxed: he had the power to dispose of the income and he enjoyed the satisfaction of the gift, so it was as if he had received and given it.
- *Blair v. Commissioner* (1937): a beneficiary assigned part of her interest in a trust's income to her children. Because she gave away a **property interest** and not merely income from it, the children were taxed on the income they received.

The distinction: a gift of the **income-producing property itself** (the bond, the stock, the interest) shifts future income to the donee; a gift of **income already earned or about to be earned** from retained property does not. Income from personal services always stays with the person who performs them (*Lucas v. Earl*), so a lawyer cannot direct fees to her child. Gifts of appreciated property carry over the donor's basis (§ 1015), so the donee is taxed on the gain on sale. Related rules: § 73 taxes a child's earnings to the child, and the **grantor trust** rules (§§ 671-679) tax the creator of a trust on income if she keeps certain powers or interests.

### The kiddie tax: § 1(g)

To limit shifting of investment income to children, § 1(g) taxes a child's **unearned income** above a threshold at the parents' marginal rate. It applies to children under 18, 18-year-olds whose earned income does not exceed half their support, and certain older students. The threshold is an assumption in each problem.

### Filing status and credits

The rate schedules depend on **filing status**: married filing jointly, married filing separately, head of household and single. Marital status is determined at the end of the year (§ 7703). The joint return schedules can produce a **marriage penalty** for some two-earner couples at high incomes and a **bonus** for one-earner couples. A **dependent** is a qualifying child or qualifying relative (§ 152). Since 2018 the personal exemption amount is zero, and the 2025 reconciliation law made that permanent, so dependents matter chiefly for filing status and credits, such as the **child tax credit** (§ 24), whose amount and phase-out are assumptions in problems.

### Divorce

- **Transfers between spouses and former spouses incident to divorce (§ 1041):** no gain or loss is recognized and the recipient takes the transferor's **carryover basis**.
- **Alimony:** for divorce or separation instruments executed after 2018, alimony is **not deductible** to the payor and **not income** to the recipient; Congress repealed §§ 71 and 215 in 2017. Older instruments keep the old rule unless modified to adopt the new one.
- **Child support** is never deductible or income.

### Policy

The doctrine protects the progressive structure. Its reach is limited by the need to leave ordinary gift giving untouched; the courts draw the line at the control of the income-producing source. The kiddie tax and the grantor trust rules are statutory responses that block specific shifting devices.

### Applying it

Ask: who earned the income or owns the property producing it? Did the transfer pass the income-producing property itself? Then compute with the correct taxpayer's basis and rates.

@example
title: Fruit and tree
problem: A lawyer who earns a \$100,000 fee agrees in a contract with her spouse that half of all fees belong to the spouse. Who is taxed?
step: The fee is income from personal services, earned by the lawyer.
step: Under *Lucas v. Earl* an anticipatory assignment of earnings does not shift the tax.
step: The lawyer is taxed on the full \$100,000, whatever the contract says about ownership.

@example
title: Gift of appreciated property
problem: A parent gives stock with basis \$10,000 and fair market value \$25,000 to an adult child, who sells it a month later for \$25,000. Who is taxed, and on how much?
step: A gift of the income-producing property is respected, and the child is the owner when the sale occurs.
step: The child's basis is the parent's \$10,000 (§ 1015(a)).
step: The child is taxed on gain of \$25,000 - \$10,000 = \$15,000, at the child's rate (or the parents' rate under the kiddie tax if the child is a minor).

@mistakes
- Assuming a contract or gift of earned income can move the tax to another family member.
- Treating a gift of appreciated property as a sale by the donor or giving the donee a stepped-up basis.
- Treating alimony under a post-2018 instrument as deductible or taxable.
- Confusing the donation of the income-producing property (respected) with a gift of only the income (not respected).
