@article
Substantive tax law is only half of the subject. A taxpayer files a return, the IRS may audit it, and a dispute can end in Tax Court, a refund suit or a criminal case. A tax lawyer must know the deadlines, the forums and the ethical limits.

### Filing, assessment and limitations

A taxpayer files a return (§ 6012) by the due date (generally April 15 for individuals); an extension of time to file (§ 6081) is **not** an extension of time to pay. The IRS must assess a deficiency within **three years** after the return is filed (§ 6501(a)); a return filed early is treated as filed on the due date (§ 6501(b)(1)). The period is **six years** if the taxpayer omitted more than 25% of the gross income stated in the return (§ 6501(e)), and **unlimited** if no return was filed or the return was fraudulent (§ 6501(c)).

### Disputes

After an audit the IRS may send a **notice of deficiency** (the "90-day letter," § 6212). The taxpayer may petition the **U.S. Tax Court** within **90 days** of mailing (§ 6213(a)), which is the one forum where the taxpayer may sue **without paying first**. The deadline is strict, and a late petition leaves the taxpayer to pay and sue for a refund. In the **refund route** the taxpayer pays the tax, files a claim for refund within the later of three years of filing the return or two years of payment (§ 6511), and then sues in a district court or the Court of Federal Claims (§ 7422). *Flora v. United States* (1960) requires full payment of the assessment before a refund suit (with limited exceptions for divisible taxes). Interest runs on underpayments (§ 6601).

*Boechler, P.C. v. Commissioner* (2022) held that the 30-day deadline to petition the Tax Court for review of a collection due process determination is not jurisdictional and is subject to equitable tolling. Courts have disagreed about whether the § 6213(a) deadline is jurisdictional, so treat the 90 days as strict.

### Civil penalties

- **Failure to file (§ 6651(a)(1))** and **failure to pay (§ 6651(a)(2))**: monthly percentages of the unpaid tax, with caps (the rates are given as assumptions in problems).
- **Accuracy-related penalty (§ 6662):** 20% of the underpayment attributable to negligence or a substantial understatement. It is avoided by reasonable cause and good faith (§ 6664(c)), which can include reliance on competent professional advice, and for a position supported by substantial authority or adequately disclosed with a reasonable basis.
- **Civil fraud (§ 6663):** 75% of the portion of the underpayment attributable to fraud.

### Criminal liability

Tax evasion (§ 7201), willful failure to file (§ 7203) and filing a false return (§ 7206(1)) require **willfulness**: a voluntary, intentional violation of a known legal duty. *Cheek v. United States* (1991) held that a good-faith belief, even if unreasonable, that the law does not impose a duty negates willfulness; but a belief that the law is unconstitutional does not.

### Regulations and rulings

Treasury regulations are issued under § 7805(a) and specific delegations. After *Loper Bright* (2024), courts do not defer to an agency's reasonable reading of an ambiguous statute. They decide the best reading, giving *Skidmore* weight to a persuasive interpretation, and where the statute expressly delegates authority to the agency, they ensure the agency acts within the delegation and engages in reasoned decision-making. Earlier cases that upheld regulations under *Chevron* (such as *Mayo Foundation*, 2011) are not automatically overturned; statutory stare decisis keeps their holdings. Challenges to a regulation under the APA accrue when the plaintiff is injured (*Corner Post*, 2024), and *CIC Services, LLC v. IRS* (2021) held that the Anti-Injunction Act did not bar a pre-enforcement challenge to a reporting requirement.

### Ethics

- **Circular 230** (31 C.F.R. Part 10) governs practice before the IRS. Practitioners must act with due diligence (§ 10.22) and may not sign a return, or advise a client to take a position on one, that lacks a reasonable basis or is otherwise barred by § 10.34. A practitioner who knows that a client has not complied with the tax laws, or has made an error in or omission from a return, must promptly advise the client of it and its consequences (§ 10.21).
- **Preparer penalty (§ 6694):** a return preparer is penalized for an understatement due to an unreasonable position (substantial authority or, if disclosed, a reasonable basis) or willful or reckless conduct.
- **Privilege (§ 7525):** communications with a federally authorized tax practitioner about tax advice receive the attorney-client privilege only in noncriminal tax matters before the IRS or in federal court; it does not cover tax shelters or return preparation.
- **ABA Model Rule 1.2(d):** a lawyer may not counsel or assist a client in conduct the lawyer knows is criminal or fraudulent, but may discuss the legal consequences and help the client make a good-faith effort to determine the meaning of the law. Rule 1.6 protects confidentiality, with limited exceptions.

### Applying it

For a procedure question, fix the key date, determine the limitation period and the forum. For an ethics question, ask what the lawyer knows, whether the position has a reasonable basis, and what the duties and penalties are.

title: Counting to the Tax Court deadline
problem: The IRS mails a notice of deficiency on March 3. Ignoring weekends and holidays, what is the last day to petition the Tax Court?
step: Section 6213(a) gives 90 days from the mailing of the notice.
step: From March 3, 28 more days reach March 31; April adds 30 for 58; May adds 31 for 89.
step: Day 90 is June 1, so the petition must be filed by June 1.

@mistakes
- Treating an extension to file as an extension to pay.
- Believing that the Tax Court requires prepayment, or that a refund suit does not.
- Applying Chevron deference to a regulation after Loper Bright.
- Assuming that the § 7525 privilege covers return preparation or criminal cases.
