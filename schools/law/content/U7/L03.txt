@article
Section 61 is broad, but Congress has carved out many **exclusions**. Each has its own elements, so for every receipt ask: is there a specific provision that removes it from gross income?

### Gifts and inheritances: § 102

Section 102(a) excludes the value of property acquired by **gift, bequest, devise or inheritance**. In *Commissioner v. Duberstein* (1960) the Court held that a gift proceeds from "a detached and disinterested generosity," out of "affection, respect, admiration, charity or like impulses." The test looks at the transferor's dominant intent, applied by the trier of fact; the absence of a legal obligation is not enough, and a payment made in return for services is not a gift. **Section 102(c)** adds that amounts transferred by or for an employer to or for the benefit of an employee are not excluded (its cross-references point to the separate exclusions for certain employee achievement awards under § 74(c) and de minimis fringe benefits under § 132(e)).

The exclusion covers the property itself. **Income** the property later produces (rent, dividends) is taxable to the recipient (§ 102(b)).

### Basis of gifts and inheritances

Basis keeps the excluded value from escaping tax forever.

- **Gift (§ 1015(a)):** the donee takes the donor's basis (**carryover basis**). For computing a loss, if the donor's basis exceeds fair market value at the time of the gift, the donee's basis for loss is that lower fair market value. A sale between the two figures produces neither gain nor loss.
- **Inheritance (§ 1014):** property acquired from a decedent generally takes a basis equal to fair market value at death (the **step-up**, or step-down), and the holding period is treated as long-term.

### Other statutory exclusions

- **Life insurance (§ 101(a)):** death benefits are excluded, subject to a transfer-for-value exception (§ 101(a)(2)); interest earned after death is taxable.
- **State and local bond interest (§ 103).**
- **Employer health insurance (§§ 105, 106):** premiums paid by the employer and benefits are excluded for employees.
- **Meals and lodging (§ 119):** furnished on the employer's business premises for the employer's convenience (and for lodging, as a condition of employment). A cash meal allowance is not excluded (*Commissioner v. Kowalski*, 1977).
- **Fringe benefits (§ 132):** working-condition fringes, de minimis fringes, qualified employee discounts, and qualified transportation fringes within statutory limits. Employee moving-expense reimbursements have been taxable since 2018 and the 2025 reconciliation law left that rule in place for most employees.
- **Scholarships (§ 117):** amounts for tuition and required fees of a degree candidate; amounts paid for services or room and board are taxable.

### Damages: § 104(a)(2)

Section 104(a)(2) excludes damages received "on account of personal physical injuries or physical sickness." Congress added "physical" in 1996 after *Commissioner v. Schleier* (1995), which held that age-discrimination back pay is not excludable. Consequences:

- Compensatory damages for physical injury (medical costs, lost wages, pain and suffering flowing from the physical injury) are excluded.
- Emotional distress damages are taxable unless they flow from a physical injury, except for the cost of medical care for the distress.
- **Punitive damages** are taxable even in a physical-injury case (§ 104(a)(2), "other than punitive damages"; *O'Gilvie v. United States*, 1996).
- Interest on a judgment is taxable.
- Settlements are allocated according to the claim, in light of the settlement agreement and the intent of the payor. Since 2018, § 162(q) denies a deduction for sexual harassment or abuse settlements subject to a nondisclosure agreement.

### Policy

Gift and inheritance exclusions reflect that the donor, not the donee, earned the wealth, and the carryover basis taxes the donor's untaxed appreciation to the donee when sold. The step-up at death is a deliberate departure that lets accrued gains on inherited property escape income tax. Damages are excluded when they restore a physical loss rather than add to wealth.

@example
title: Allocating a personal injury settlement
problem: Gus is injured in a car accident. He settles his claim for \$100,000, allocated as \$60,000 for medical bills, lost wages and pain from the physical injuries, \$30,000 for punitive damages and \$10,000 of interest on the delayed payment. How much is included in gross income?
step: The \$60,000 is compensatory damages on account of a personal physical injury, excluded by § 104(a)(2).
step: Punitive damages are not excluded (*O'Gilvie*), so \$30,000 is included.
step: Interest is income under § 61(a)(4), so \$10,000 is included.
step: Gus includes \$30,000 + \$10,000 = \$40,000 and excludes \$60,000.

@example
title: Carryover basis
problem: Mom gives Sam stock with an adjusted basis of \$10,000 and a fair market value of \$15,000. Sam sells it for \$18,000. What is Sam's gain?
step: The gift is excluded by § 102. Under § 1015(a) Sam takes Mom's \$10,000 basis (the fair market value is higher, so the loss rule does not matter).
step: Amount realized is \$18,000, so gain under § 1001 is \$18,000 - \$10,000 = \$8,000.
step: Of the gain, \$5,000 accrued while Mom owned it and \$3,000 after the gift; Sam is taxed on all \$8,000.

@mistakes
- Treating an employer's "gift" to an employee as excluded under § 102 despite § 102(c).
- Forgetting that income from gifted or inherited property is taxable to the recipient.
- Giving the donee a stepped-up basis on a gift, or a carryover basis on an inheritance.
- Excluding punitive damages or interest because the underlying claim was a physical injury.
