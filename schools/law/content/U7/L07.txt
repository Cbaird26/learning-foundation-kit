@article
Income from property is taxed when a gain is **realized** and **recognized**. *Eisner v. Macomber* (1920) held that a stock dividend did not give the shareholder income because nothing had been realized, and the realization requirement still means that appreciation is not taxed until a sale or other disposition. *Cottage Savings Ass'n v. Commissioner* (1991) held that a swap of financial interests that are materially different, meaning they embody legally distinct entitlements, is a realization event, so a loss can be realized and claimed.

### The formula: § 1001

- **Gain** realized = **amount realized** minus **adjusted basis** (§ 1001(a)).
- **Loss** realized = adjusted basis minus amount realized.
- Under § 1001(c) all realized gain or loss is **recognized** (taxed or deducted) unless a nonrecognition rule such as § 1031 or § 121 applies (Week 9). A recognized loss may still be limited by other rules, such as § 165 (personal losses) and the capital loss limits (Week 8).

### Amount realized: § 1001(b)

The amount realized is the sum of **money received** and the **fair market value of other property received**. It is reduced by selling expenses (commissions and similar costs). It also includes **liabilities** from which the seller is relieved.

### Basis

- **Cost basis (§ 1012):** the cost of the property, including cash paid, the fair market value of property given, and debts assumed or taken subject to, plus capitalized acquisition costs such as title fees and commissions.
- **Adjusted basis (§§ 1011, 1016):** basis increased by capital improvements and decreased by depreciation and certain other items.
- **Gift basis (§ 1015)** and **inheritance basis (§ 1014)** were explained in Week 3.

### Mortgages: Crane and Tufts

*Crane v. Commissioner* (1947): a buyer's basis includes the amount of a nonrecourse mortgage on the property, because the buyer is treated as having borrowed and bought; on sale, the seller's amount realized includes the outstanding mortgage the buyer assumes or takes subject to, even when the seller has no personal liability. *Commissioner v. Tufts* (1983) extended this: when a taxpayer transfers property subject to a **nonrecourse** liability that exceeds the property's fair market value, the amount realized includes the full liability. The Court reasoned that the borrowed money was never taxed because of the obligation to repay, so the relief from that obligation must be counted at the full amount when the property is disposed of.

For **recourse** debt, Treas. Reg. § 1.1001-2 splits the transaction: the amount realized from the property is the lesser of the debt or the property's fair market value, and any excess of debt over value forgiven is cancellation of indebtedness income under § 61(a)(11) (subject to § 108). For **nonrecourse** debt the debt is entirely treated as amount realized, and there is no cancellation income.

### Policy

Basis ensures the taxpayer is taxed only on gain, not return of capital. Counting debt as part of both basis and amount realized prevents a leveraged owner from taking depreciation based on borrowed money and then avoiding gain by walking away from the debt.

### Applying it

List the amount realized: cash + FMV of property + debt relief − selling costs. Determine the adjusted basis: cost + improvements − depreciation. Subtract. Then ask what recognition rules, and what character rules, apply.

@example
title: Sale subject to a mortgage
problem: A buyer purchased a building for \$1,000,000 using a \$200,000 down payment and an \$800,000 mortgage. She claimed \$150,000 of depreciation. She sells it for \$100,000 in cash, the buyer taking subject to the remaining mortgage balance of \$780,000. Find her realized gain.
step: Her cost basis was \$1,000,000 (the mortgage is included, *Crane*). Adjusted basis is \$1,000,000 - \$150,000 = \$850,000.
step: Amount realized is cash plus liability relief: \$100,000 + \$780,000 = \$880,000.
step: Gain realized under § 1001(a): \$880,000 - \$850,000 = \$30,000.

@example
title: Tufts: debt exceeds value
problem: An owner's adjusted basis is \$600,000. The property is subject to a \$780,000 nonrecourse mortgage, and its fair market value is \$700,000. The owner transfers it to the lender in satisfaction of the debt and receives no cash. What is the gain?
step: In a nonrecourse transaction the amount realized includes the full debt: \$780,000, even though the property is worth \$700,000 (*Tufts*).
step: Gain is \$780,000 - \$600,000 = \$180,000.
step: There is no cancellation-of-indebtedness income because the debt is nonrecourse; the whole \$180,000 is gain from the disposition of property.

@mistakes
- Treating only the cash received as the amount realized and ignoring relieved debt.
- Using fair market value instead of the full nonrecourse balance after Tufts.
- Forgetting to reduce basis for depreciation before computing gain.
- Taxing unrealized appreciation, or forgetting that selling expenses reduce the amount realized.

@practice self
prompt: Explain in a short paragraph why Crane includes a nonrecourse mortgage in both basis and amount realized, and why Tufts holds that the amount realized includes the debt even when the property is worth less than the debt.
solution: Borrowed money is not income because the borrower must repay it. When the buyer uses a mortgage, Crane treats the buyer as having invested the borrowed funds, so basis includes the debt and depreciation can be claimed on it. When the property is sold subject to the debt, the seller is relieved of the obligation, a benefit equal to the full debt. If it were not counted, the seller would have deducted depreciation on borrowed money without ever being taxed on the corresponding relief. Tufts holds that the same logic applies when the debt exceeds value, because the borrower still received the loan proceeds tax-free.
