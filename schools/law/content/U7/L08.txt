@article
A gain on the sale of a **capital asset** is a **capital gain**, and long-term capital gain is taxed at lower rates than ordinary income. The rules reward long-term investing and, in part, relieve the "bunching" of gains that accrue over many years. They also complicate the Code by making the *character* of income matter.

### Capital assets: § 1221

Section 1221(a) defines a capital asset as **any property** held by the taxpayer, then lists exclusions. The main exclusions are:

1. **Inventory** and property held primarily for sale to customers in the ordinary course of business;
2. **Depreciable property or real property used in the trade or business** (governed instead by § 1231);
3. **Self-created** copyrights, literary and artistic compositions and similar property in the creator's hands;
4. **Accounts or notes receivable** acquired in the ordinary course of business for services or sales.

*Arkansas Best Corp. v. Commissioner* (1988) held that stock is a capital asset even if the taxpayer bought it for a business motive, because it does not fit any listed exclusion; the statute's list of exceptions is read as exclusive. Stocks, bonds, a personal residence and a personal car are capital assets.

### Holding period: §§ 1222, 1223

A gain or loss is **long-term** if the asset was held for **more than one year** and **short-term** if held for one year or less. The holding period starts the day after acquisition, so an asset bought on March 15 must be sold after March 15 of the next year to be long-term. Inherited property is treated as held long-term (§ 1223(9)); gifted property takes the donor's holding period when it takes the donor's basis (§ 1223(2)).

### Netting: § 1222

1. Net short-term gains and losses; net long-term gains and losses.
2. If the net long-term gain exceeds the net short-term loss, the excess is **net capital gain**, taxed at the preferential rates of § 1(h) (a 0%, 15% or 20% structure for most gain, with higher maximum rates for collectibles and unrecaptured § 1250 gain; the thresholds and rates are assumptions in this course).
3. Net short-term gain is taxed as ordinary income.

### Capital losses: §§ 1211(b), 1212(b)

For an individual, capital losses are deductible against capital gains plus up to **\$3,000** of ordinary income each year (\$1,500 for a married person filing separately). The unused loss carries over **indefinitely** to later years, keeping its short-term or long-term character. The limit prevents taxpayers from choosing when to realize losses against ordinary income while deferring gains.

### Section 1231

Depreciable and real property used in a business and held more than one year gets "heads I win, tails I lose" treatment: if the year's § 1231 gains exceed § 1231 losses, the net is treated as long-term capital gain; if losses exceed gains, the net is an ordinary loss. A five-year lookback (§ 1231(c)) recharacterizes net gain as ordinary to the extent of recent net § 1231 losses, and depreciation is first recaptured as ordinary income (Week 5).

### Wash sales: § 1091

If a taxpayer sells stock or securities at a loss and, within the period beginning 30 days before and ending 30 days after the sale, buys substantially identical securities, the **loss is disallowed**. The disallowed loss is added to the basis of the replacement shares, and the holding period of the old shares carries over. The rule stops a taxpayer from harvesting a loss while keeping the position.

### Net investment income tax

Separately, § 1411 imposes an additional tax on net investment income of higher-income taxpayers; the rate and thresholds are assumptions, and gain on capital assets is part of the base.

### Applying it

Classify each asset (§ 1221), find the holding period, net by class, apply the loss limit, then apply the rate. Property subject to a mortgage is handled with the Week 7 method; this week you also need the character of the gain.

@example
title: Netting gains and losses
problem: A taxpayer has short-term capital gain of \$5,000, short-term capital loss of \$8,000, long-term capital gain of \$12,000 and long-term capital loss of \$3,000. Assume net capital gain is taxed at 15%. Find the net capital gain and the tax on it.
step: Net short-term: \$5,000 - \$8,000 = a \$3,000 net short-term loss.
step: Net long-term: \$12,000 - \$3,000 = a \$9,000 net long-term gain.
step: Net long-term gain exceeds net short-term loss by \$9,000 - \$3,000 = \$6,000, which is the net capital gain.
step: Tax at the assumed 15% rate: \$6,000 x 0.15 = \$900.

@example
title: A net capital loss
problem: A taxpayer has a short-term capital loss of \$10,000 and a long-term capital gain of \$2,000. How much can she deduct this year, and what carries over?
step: Net: \$2,000 - \$10,000 = a \$8,000 net capital loss, which is short-term in character.
step: Under § 1211(b) she may deduct up to \$3,000 against ordinary income.
step: The remaining \$8,000 - \$3,000 = \$5,000 carries over as a short-term loss (§ 1212(b)).

@mistakes
- Counting a sale on the one-year anniversary as long-term; the holding period must exceed one year.
- Applying the \$3,000 limit separately to short-term and long-term losses.
- Forgetting that unused capital losses carry over indefinitely.
- Ignoring the 30-day window before the sale when applying the wash sale rule.
