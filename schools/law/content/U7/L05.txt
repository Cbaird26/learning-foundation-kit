@article
A capital expenditure buys a benefit that lasts several years, so matching cost to benefit suggests spreading the deduction. **Depreciation** is that spreading: § 167(a) allows a deduction for the exhaustion, wear and tear of property used in a trade or business or held for the production of income.

### What is depreciable

Only property with a **determinable useful life** that wears out or becomes obsolete is depreciable: equipment, vehicles, buildings and improvements. **Land is not depreciable**, since it does not wear out, and neither is property held for personal use (a family car or a home). When a taxpayer buys land and a building together, the price is allocated between them and only the building's share is depreciated.

### MACRS: § 168

Most tangible property placed in service after 1986 is depreciated under the **Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System**. The taxpayer finds the **recovery period** for the property class (examples: 5 years for computers and automobiles, 7 years for office furniture, 27.5 years for residential rental real property and 39 years for nonresidential real property) and applies the statutory method and convention:

- Personal property uses the declining-balance method switching to straight line, with the **half-year convention** (a half-year of depreciation in the first year, no matter when during the year the property was placed in service, unless the mid-quarter convention applies because more than 40% of the year's personal-property basis was placed in service in the last quarter).
- Real property uses straight line with the **mid-month convention**.

In practice the taxpayer reads a percentage from the IRS tables. In this course tables are always given in the problem, for example for 5-year property with the half-year convention: Year 1, 20%; Year 2, 32%; Year 3, 19.2%; Year 4, 11.52%; Year 5, 11.52%; Year 6, 5.76%. The percentages apply to the original **unadjusted basis** and not to the declining adjusted basis.

### Expensing and bonus depreciation

- **Section 179** lets the taxpayer **elect** to deduct the cost of qualifying property in the year it is placed in service, up to a dollar limit that phases out when total purchases exceed a threshold, and not above the taxable income from the active conduct of the business. The 2025 reconciliation law raised these limits; the amounts are assumptions in problems. Any § 179 deduction is taken first and **reduces basis** before MACRS percentages are applied to the remainder.
- **Bonus depreciation (§ 168(k))** is an additional first-year deduction for qualified property. Under the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act it was 100% for property acquired after September 27, 2017, then began phasing down after 2022; the 2025 reconciliation law restored 100% bonus depreciation permanently for qualified property acquired after January 19, 2025. Unless the taxpayer elects out, bonus applies automatically, so the regular MACRS deduction is computed on what remains.

### Adjusted basis and recapture

Under § 1016(a)(2), basis is reduced by depreciation **allowed or allowable**, so a taxpayer who forgets to claim depreciation still reduces basis. Adjusted basis is cost minus all depreciation (including § 179 and bonus). When the property is sold, gain is amount realized minus adjusted basis (Week 7). Because the deductions were ordinary, the Code **recaptures** them:

- **§ 1245** (personal property such as equipment): gain is ordinary income up to the total depreciation taken; only gain above that can be § 1231 or capital gain.
- **§ 1250** (real property): since most real property is depreciated straight line, ordinary recapture is rare, but gain attributable to depreciation (**unrecaptured § 1250 gain**) is taxed at a special maximum rate for individuals.

### Policy

Accelerated cost recovery is an incentive: faster deductions reduce the present value of tax and encourage investment. Recapture prevents a taxpayer from deducting depreciation against ordinary income and then reporting the corresponding gain as capital gain.

@example
title: MACRS on five-year equipment
problem: A business places in service equipment costing \$50,000 that is 5-year property. Use the table: Year 1, 20%; Year 2, 32%. Assume no § 179 election and no bonus depreciation. Find the depreciation for each of the first two years and the adjusted basis after Year 2.
step: Year 1 depreciation is 20% of \$50,000 = \$10,000.
step: Year 2 depreciation is 32% of \$50,000 = \$16,000.
step: Under § 1016(a)(2) adjusted basis is cost minus depreciation: \$50,000 - \$10,000 - \$16,000 = \$24,000.

@example
title: Section 179 first, MACRS on the rest
problem: The same \$50,000 equipment is bought, and the taxpayer elects to expense \$20,000 under § 179. What is the total Year 1 deduction and the adjusted basis at the end of Year 1? Use 20% for Year 1 and assume no bonus depreciation.
step: The § 179 deduction is taken first: \$20,000. The remaining depreciable basis is \$50,000 - \$20,000 = \$30,000.
step: Year 1 MACRS is 20% of \$30,000 = \$6,000.
step: Total Year 1 deduction: \$20,000 + \$6,000 = \$26,000. Adjusted basis: \$50,000 - \$26,000 = \$24,000.

@mistakes
- Depreciating land, or depreciating the full price of a land-and-building purchase.
- Applying the MACRS percentage to the declining adjusted basis rather than the original basis.
- Applying the MACRS percentage to the full cost after a § 179 election instead of to the remaining basis.
- Failing to reduce basis for depreciation that was allowable but not claimed.

@practice self
prompt: Explain in four or five sentences why basis is reduced by depreciation and what recapture does. Use a short numerical illustration with a machine purchased for \$10,000 and sold later.
solution: A depreciation deduction returns part of the cost to the taxpayer through a tax saving, so basis must fall by the same amount or the taxpayer would recover the cost twice (§ 1016(a)(2)). For example, if a \$10,000 machine is depreciated by \$7,000, adjusted basis is \$3,000. If sold for \$8,000, gain is \$5,000. The deductions reduced ordinary income, so under § 1245 the gain is ordinary income up to the \$7,000 depreciation taken. Recapture prevents converting ordinary deductions into capital gain.

rubric: Computes adjusted basis (\$3,000) and gain (\$5,000) correctly.
rubric: States that § 1245 treats the gain as ordinary income to the extent of depreciation.
hint: Ask what happens if basis is not reduced.
hint: Compare the gain with the depreciation taken.
