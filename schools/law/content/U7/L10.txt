@article
Taxable income is computed for an annual period (§ 441), so the **year** in which an item is included or deducted matters: deferral of income and acceleration of deductions are valuable because of the time value of money and because rates and personal circumstances change.

### Accounting methods: § 446

A taxpayer computes income under the method regularly used in keeping books, if it clearly reflects income (§ 446(a), (b)). The two principal methods:

- **Cash method:** income is included when actually or constructively received, and deductions are taken when paid. Receipt includes cash, checks and **cash equivalents**; the **economic benefit** doctrine can also tax unfunded promises that are secured or funded.
- **Accrual method:** income is included when the **all events test** is met (the right to the income is fixed and the amount can be determined with reasonable accuracy); a deduction when the all-events test and **economic performance** (§ 461(h)) have occurred. For services the economic performance usually occurs as the services are provided. A **recurring item** exception can allow a deduction if performance occurs within 8.5 months after year-end, under conditions in the regulations.

Limits: C corporations and tax shelters generally cannot use the cash method (§ 448), except small businesses under a gross receipts threshold, and businesses with inventories generally use the accrual method for purchases and sales unless they meet the small-business exception of § 471(c); the threshold is inflation adjusted and is given as an assumption when needed. Individuals and most small businesses use the cash method.

### Constructive receipt

Under Treas. Reg. § 1.451-2 income is **constructively received** when it is credited to the taxpayer's account, set apart, or otherwise made available so the taxpayer can draw on it, unless notice of an actual restriction exists. A taxpayer cannot defer income by turning her back on a check delivered in December. A deferred-compensation arrangement made **before** services are performed is not constructive receipt if the right is only an unsecured promise.

### Prepayments

Under § 451(c) an accrual-method taxpayer may defer advance payments for goods or services to the following year in part, and a cash-method taxpayer's prepaid expenses (for example rent paid in advance) are generally deductible in the year paid only if the benefit does not extend beyond the earlier of 12 months or the end of the next year (the 12-month rule of the capitalization regulations).

### Installment method: § 453

If a taxpayer sells property and receives at least one payment after the year of sale, the gain is reported **proportionately as payments are received**. The **gross profit ratio** is gross profit divided by the contract price; each payment's gain equals the payment times that ratio. The method does not apply to inventory or dealer sales, or to publicly traded stock, and it is mandatory unless the taxpayer elects out. Depreciation recapture is recognized in the year of the sale regardless (§ 453(i)).

### Annual accounting and claim of right

*Burnet v. Sanford & Brooks Co.* (1931) holds that income is determined year by year without carrying back later losses. Under the **claim of right** doctrine (*North American Oil Consolidated v. Burnet*, 1932; *United States v. Lewis*, 1951) a taxpayer who receives income under a claim of right and without restriction on its use must include it in that year, even if he must repay it later. If a taxpayer repays in a later year, he generally deducts the repayment in that year. **Section 1341** gives relief when the repayment exceeds \$3,000: the taxpayer takes the more favorable of a deduction or a credit for the tax attributable to the earlier inclusion. The **tax benefit rule** (§ 111) is the converse: a recovery of an amount deducted earlier is income to the extent the deduction produced a tax benefit.

### Applying it

Identify the method, then the event that triggers inclusion or deduction (receipt, accrual, economic performance, installment payment) and the year in which it occurs.

@example
title: Cash method versus accrual method
problem: A consulting firm finishes a project on December 20 and sends a \$10,000 invoice. The client pays on January 10 of the next year. In which year is the \$10,000 income to a cash-method firm and to an accrual-method firm?
step: Accrual method: the firm's right to payment became fixed and determinable on completion in December, so the \$10,000 is income in the earlier year.
step: Cash method: the firm received nothing in December and is not in constructive receipt (the invoice is not an amount available to it), so the income is included in the next year.
step: The same facts produce income in different years; the method must be one that clearly reflects income and be used consistently.

@example
title: Installment sale
problem: Sam sells land with an adjusted basis of \$60,000 for \$200,000. He receives \$40,000 at closing and four annual payments of \$40,000. How much gain does he report in the year of sale, and what is the total gain?
step: Gross profit is \$200,000 - \$60,000 = \$140,000, and the contract price is \$200,000, so the gross profit ratio is \$140,000 / \$200,000 = 70%.
step: Gain in each year in which he receives a \$40,000 payment is 70% x \$40,000 = \$28,000.
step: Over five payments the total is 5 x \$28,000 = \$140,000, which equals the total gain. (Interest on the deferred payments is separate ordinary income.)

@mistakes
- Treating an accrual-method deduction as available when the contract is signed, before economic performance.
- Overlooking constructive receipt when a check is delivered but not cashed.
- Reporting all installment gain in the year of sale, or forgetting that recapture is recognized immediately.
- Amending the earlier year for a repayment under the claim of right rather than deducting the repayment in the year paid.
