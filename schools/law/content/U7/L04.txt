@article
Gross income is reduced by the costs of earning it, because income is the net gain, not the receipts. But the Code is stingy about what counts as a cost, and the central question is always the same: **is this expense deductible now, deductible over time, or not at all?**

### The rule: § 162(a)

Section 162(a) allows a deduction for **ordinary and necessary expenses paid or incurred during the taxable year in carrying on any trade or business**. The elements are:

1. **Ordinary**: common and accepted in the taxpayer's field (*Welch v. Helvering*, 1933, which denied a deduction for payments to a former employer's creditors made to rebuild goodwill, calling them capital in nature and not ordinary).
2. **Necessary**: appropriate and helpful to the business; courts rarely second-guess necessity.
3. **Paid or incurred during the year**, subject to the taxpayer's accounting method (Week 10).
4. **In carrying on a trade or business**: a profit motive and regular, continuous activity; start-up costs are treated separately (§ 195: a limited amount may be deducted immediately and the rest amortized over 180 months).
5. **Reasonable in amount** (compensation, for instance, must be reasonable).

Section 212 gives a parallel deduction for expenses to produce income or manage income-producing property (for example, investment advisory fees). For individuals, § 212 expenses other than those connected with rents and royalties are miscellaneous itemized deductions, which § 67(g) has suspended since 2018; the 2025 reconciliation law made that suspension permanent.

### Capitalization: § 263

Section 263(a) bars a current deduction for amounts paid for new buildings or for permanent improvements or betterments that increase value, and the cost is recovered through depreciation (Week 5) or on sale. *INDOPCO, Inc. v. Commissioner* (1992) held that an expense producing significant long-term benefits can be capital even if it does not create a separate asset. Rule of thumb: a repair keeps property in ordinary operating condition and is deductible; an improvement, restoration or adaptation is capitalized.

### Personal expenses: § 262

Section 262 denies a deduction for personal, living or family expenses, subject to explicit exceptions. Commuting is personal. When an expense serves both purposes, the **origin of the claim** controls (*United States v. Gilmore*, 1963): legal fees to defend a suit arising from business activity are business expenses; fees from a divorce are personal even if the outcome protects business assets.

### Specific limits

- **Meals (§ 274(k), (n)):** business meals are generally 50% deductible when substantiated.
- **Entertainment (§ 274(a)):** since 2018 no deduction for entertainment expenses, even if business-related.
- **Substantiation (§ 274(d)):** travel, gifts and certain property need records of amount, time, place and purpose.
- **Fines and penalties (§ 162(f)):** no deduction for amounts paid to a government for violation of law (with an exception for restitution identified in the order).
- **Illegal bribes, lobbying** (§ 162(c), (e)).
- **Hobbies (§ 183):** an activity not engaged in for profit produces included income but its expenses are deductible only as miscellaneous itemized deductions, which are not currently allowed.
- **Home office (§ 280A):** deductible only for space used regularly and exclusively as the principal place of business (or to meet clients, or as a separate structure).
- **Research expenditures:** § 174A, added in 2025, restores current deduction for domestic research and experimental costs for tax years beginning after 2024, replacing the 2022-2024 required amortization.

### Self-employed taxpayers

A sole proprietor reports receipts minus deductions as **net business income** (Schedule C) and deducts business expenses above the line (§ 62(a)(1)). Net earnings are also subject to self-employment tax, and the taxpayer deducts one-half of it above the line (§ 164(f)); a separate qualified business income deduction under § 199A may apply. Dollar amounts and rates for these items are assumptions in each problem.

### Applying it

For each expense: (1) is it business or investment related? (2) ordinary and necessary and reasonable? (3) is it a capital expenditure? (4) does a special limit apply? Then compute.

@example
title: Net income of a sole proprietor
problem: A freelance writer has receipts of \$90,000. She paid \$4,000 for office supplies, \$6,000 for software subscriptions, \$2,000 for substantiated meals with clients, \$1,200 for tickets to a ball game for a client, and \$900 for her own commuting. What is her net business income?
step: Supplies (\$4,000) and software (\$6,000) are ordinary and necessary, deductible in full under § 162.
step: Business meals are 50% deductible: \$2,000 x 0.50 = \$1,000.
step: The ball game tickets are entertainment (§ 274(a)) and commuting is personal (§ 262): both nondeductible.
step: Deductions are \$4,000 + \$6,000 + \$1,000 = \$11,000. Net business income is \$90,000 - \$11,000 = \$79,000.

@mistakes
- Deducting 100% of meals or deducting client entertainment.
- Deducting an improvement to property in the year paid instead of capitalizing it.
- Deducting commuting or home expenses that fail the exclusive-use rule.
- Using the type of lawyer's fee, not the origin of the claim, to decide deductibility.
