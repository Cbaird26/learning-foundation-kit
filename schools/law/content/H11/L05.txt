@article
An **argument** in academic writing is a claim supported by reasons and evidence, offered to a reasonable audience who might disagree. It is not a quarrel. A good argument makes its reasoning visible so that readers can evaluate it, and it earns trust by taking opposing views seriously.

### What makes a claim arguable

A **fact** can be checked ("The clinic opened in 2019"). A **preference** cannot be argued ("I like quiet libraries"). A **topic** is not a claim ("School start times"). An arguable claim takes a position that a reasonable person could reject: "Public high schools should start no earlier than 8:30 a.m." Test it by asking, "Who would disagree, and why?" If nobody could, you have a fact or a platitude.

### Toulmin's model

Stephen Toulmin's model shows how arguments are built:

- **Claim**: the position you defend.
- **Reasons and evidence (grounds)**: why the claim is true and the data or examples that show it.
- **Warrant**: the often unstated principle that links evidence to claim.
- **Backing**: support for the warrant.
- **Qualifier**: words like *usually* or *in most cases* that match the claim to the evidence.
- **Rebuttal or reservation**: the conditions under which the claim might fail.

Example: Claim: schools should start later. Evidence: teenagers' sleep patterns shift later in adolescence. Warrant: schools should schedule around the biology of the students they serve. A reader who disputes the warrant (maybe other costs matter more) is disagreeing about values, not about the evidence, and your essay should say so.

### The strongest objection

Readers distrust one-sided essays. Introduce a **counterargument**, the best case against your claim, and respond. Choose the strongest objection, not a **straw man**, a weakened version that is easy to knock down. State it in terms its supporters would accept. Then respond in one of three ways:

1. **Concede** a point that is true and show it does not defeat your claim.
2. **Qualify** your claim so it survives the objection ("in most districts").
3. **Rebut** by showing the objection rests on weak evidence, a faulty premise or a trade-off that favors your side.

A common pattern is: "Critics argue that... This is a serious concern, because... However, ..." Placing the counterargument near the end of the body, just before the conclusion, often leaves the reader with your response.

### Common fallacies

- **Ad hominem**: attacks the person instead of the argument.
- **Straw man**: misrepresents the opposing view.
- **False dilemma**: presents only two options when more exist.
- **Slippery slope**: claims one step will inevitably lead to an extreme without evidence.
- **Hasty generalization**: draws a broad conclusion from too little evidence.
- **Post hoc**: assumes that because B followed A, A caused B.
- **Appeal to popularity**: treats "many people believe it" as proof.

Naming a fallacy is not a rebuttal in itself. Explain how the reasoning fails.

@example
title: Answering the strongest objection
problem: Claim: "Cities should replace some downtown parking spaces with bike lanes." Strongest objection: "Small businesses depend on customers who arrive by car." Write a short response that concedes, then rebuts.
step: State the objection fairly. Business owners worry that fewer parking spaces will reduce customers who drive, and that worry is reasonable.
step: Concede what is true. Some customers do drive, and losing convenient parking could hurt a few stores, especially those with bulky purchases.
step: Rebut with a reason and a type of evidence. Studies of shopper behavior in several cities have found that people who walk or bike often visit more frequently, so total customers may not fall. (In an essay, cite a specific study.)
step: Qualify the claim. The plan should keep short-term loading spaces near stores that sell bulky goods.
step: Combined answer: "Business owners rightly worry about losing drivers. That risk is real for stores selling bulky goods, so the plan should keep loading spaces. Still, because walkers and cyclists often shop more frequently, the loss of some parking is unlikely to reduce overall customers."

@mistakes
- Choosing a claim that is a fact or a preference, so no one can disagree.
- Attacking a straw man instead of the strongest objection.
- Naming a fallacy without explaining why the reasoning fails.
- Claiming certainty that the evidence does not support, with no qualifier.

solution: Claim: Our school should let students keep one free period each day for supervised study. With supervision, the time gives students a place to finish work that would otherwise cut into sleep. The policy can be reviewed after a semester, which limits the cost of being wrong.
rubric: The claim takes a position a reasonable person could reject.
rubric: The objection is stated fairly and is not a straw man.
rubric: The response concedes, qualifies or rebuts with a reason.
