material: Essay 1 (hypothetical, 30 minutes). On November 1, Tenant signed a one-year lease of a residential apartment from Landlord at \$1,200 per month. In January the building's boiler failed, and Tenant's apartment had no heat, with indoor temperatures around 50 degrees. Tenant gave Landlord written notice of the problem on January 5. Landlord replied that he would "get to it" but did nothing. After three weeks without heat, on January 26, Tenant moved out and stopped paying rent. Landlord sued Tenant for the rent for the remaining months of the lease and has made no effort to re-let the apartment. The jurisdiction follows the majority rules on residential leases. What claims and defenses can the parties assert, and who is likely to prevail?
material: Essay 2 (hypothetical, 30 minutes). Supplier and Buyer, both merchants, signed a written contract for Supplier to deliver 1,000 widgets to Buyer in 60 days at \$10 each. Thirty days later, a supply disruption raised Supplier's documented costs by \$1.80 per widget. Supplier told Buyer it would deliver only if the price became \$11.50 per widget. Buyer needed the widgets to fill its own orders, and no other supplier could deliver in time. Buyer signed a written modification at \$11.50. After delivery, Buyer paid \$10 per widget and refused to pay more, claiming the modification is not binding. Supplier sues for the \$1,500 difference. Who is likely to prevail, and why?
material: Performance test, task memo (hypothetical; State of Alder; 90 minutes). To: Associate. From: Supervising Attorney. Re: Nia Ortiz. Our client, Nia Ortiz, was fired by Harbor Cafe. She says she was fired because she told her manager she was about to file a wage complaint. Please write an objective memorandum (no more than about 900 words) addressing (1) whether Ms. Ortiz engaged in protected activity under the Alder Fair Pay Act, (2) whether she can show that the firing was because of that activity, and (3) what remedies she could recover. Use only the file and the library. Cite the file by document name and the library by authority name. Address facts and authority that hurt our case. Use headings for each question and a short conclusion. You do not need a statement of facts.
material: Performance test, file (hypothetical). **Document 1: Interview notes.** Nia Ortiz worked as a server at Harbor Cafe for 14 months at \$18 per hour, 40 hours per week. She often worked 50 hours and was paid straight time for all hours. On March 3, she complained to her manager, Sam, that she was owed overtime. Sam said, "Don't make trouble." On March 4, she scheduled an intake appointment with the Alder Labor Department for March 10 to file a complaint about unpaid overtime. **Document 2: E-mail, March 4, from Nia to Sam.** "I have an appointment with the Alder Labor Department on March 10 to file a complaint about unpaid overtime. I wanted you to know." **Document 3: Termination letter, March 5, from Harbor Cafe.** "Your employment is terminated effective today because of attitude and attendance problems." **Document 4: Written warnings.** A warning dated January 8 for arriving 12 minutes late once, and a warning dated February 2 for arriving 8 minutes late once. No other discipline appears in Ms. Ortiz's file. **Document 5: Payroll summary.** Hours worked per week in the 12 weeks before termination ranged from 46 to 52. **Document 6: Interview notes (continued).** Ms. Ortiz found a comparable job at the same wage starting April 2. Between March 5 and April 1, she had no income. Harbor Cafe's menu includes a seasonal soup (irrelevant).
material: Performance test, library (hypothetical; State of Alder). **Alder Fair Pay Act § 8(a):** No employer shall discharge or discriminate against any employee because the employee has filed any complaint or instituted or caused to be instituted any proceeding under this Act, or has testified or is about to testify in any such proceeding. **Alder Fair Pay Act § 9:** An employee discharged in violation of § 8 may recover legal or equitable relief, including reinstatement, back pay, and an additional equal amount as liquidated damages. **Brandt v. Keel (Alder Supreme Court, 2018):** An employee who complained only to a supervisor, and took no step to start a proceeding before the Department, did not "file" a complaint and was not protected by § 8(a). The court read "filed" to require a submission to the Department or a court. **Orr v. Sable Foods (Alder Court of Appeals, 2021):** An employee who had scheduled an intake appointment with the Department and told her employer so had taken a concrete step to start a proceeding and had "caused to be instituted" a proceeding under § 8(a). The court distinguished *Brandt* because there the employee had taken no step beyond an internal complaint. **Marin v. Tile Co. (Alder Supreme Court, 2019):** A retaliation plaintiff must prove that the protected activity was a but-for cause of the discharge. Close timing between the protected activity and the discharge may support an inference of causation. The employer may then offer a legitimate reason, and the employee may show that the reason is a pretext, for example because the stated reason was minor, inconsistent with the employer's past practice or newly asserted. **Alder Employment Practices Manual (secondary source, not binding):** Employers should document performance problems when they occur.
material: Point sheets and model answers, Essay 1. Point sheet (12 points): implied warranty of habitability recognized for residential leases and heat as an essential service (2); breach after notice and failure to repair within a reasonable time (2); constructive eviction elements: substantial interference, landlord's fault, notice, vacating within a reasonable time (3); application to the facts (3 weeks, written notice, no repair, move-out on January 26) (3); conclusion that Tenant's rent obligation ended and Landlord cannot recover future rent (1); mitigation point as a fallback (1). Model answer: **Implied warranty of habitability.** In most states a residential landlord impliedly warrants that the premises are habitable throughout the lease, and a lack of heat in winter is a serious defect. Tenant gave written notice on January 5 and Landlord did nothing for three weeks, which is not a reasonable time to repair a failed boiler in January, so Landlord breached the warranty. Tenant's remedies include terminating the lease and vacating, withholding rent and damages. **Constructive eviction.** Constructive eviction requires a substantial interference with the tenant's use and enjoyment that is attributable to the landlord, notice and a reasonable time to cure, and the tenant's vacating within a reasonable time. Heat is essential to use of a residence, the boiler is the landlord's responsibility, Tenant gave notice and waited three weeks, and then left on January 26. Those facts satisfy each element, so Tenant was constructively evicted and her obligation to pay later rent ended. **Landlord's claim.** Landlord's suit for the remaining rent therefore fails. If a court found Tenant had abandoned without justification, the majority rule requires a landlord to make reasonable efforts to re-let and to credit rent received, and Landlord made none, so his recovery would be limited. **Conclusion.** Tenant likely prevails. Landlord cannot recover future rent, and Tenant may be able to recover any security deposit and damages for the lack of heat.
material: Point sheets and model answers, Essay 2. Point sheet (12 points): sale of goods between merchants is governed by UCC Article 2 and § 2-209(1) permits a modification without consideration (2); the modification must meet the good-faith standard (honesty in fact and observance of reasonable commercial standards) (2); application of good faith: documented \$1.80 per-widget cost increase is a legitimate commercial reason (2); writing satisfied (the modification is signed and any statute of frauds problem is met) (1); economic duress: improper threat and no reasonable alternative (2); application of duress: threat to withhold delivery, no alternative supplier, but modest, cost-based increase and good faith (2); conclusion (1). Model answer: **Governing law and modification.** A contract for widgets is a sale of goods, so UCC Article 2 governs. Under § 2-209(1), an agreement modifying a contract needs no consideration to be binding, so the usual objection that Supplier already owed delivery at \$10 is not available. A modification must meet the UCC's good-faith requirement, which for merchants means honesty in fact and observance of reasonable commercial standards. A seller's demand for more money with no legitimate commercial reason would be bad faith, but a demand based on a real increase in costs is a legitimate reason. Supplier's costs rose a documented \$1.80 per widget, and the price increase of \$1.50 per widget is smaller than the cost increase. Supplier acted in good faith. **Writing.** The parties signed the modification, so the signed-writing requirements are met. **Economic duress.** Buyer may argue duress, which requires an improper threat that leaves no reasonable alternative. A threat to breach a contract can be improper, and Buyer had no other supplier that could deliver in time. But a demand tied to documented costs and made in good faith is not improper, and the price increase was modest. Buyer could also have signed under protest and sued rather than accepting the modification. **Conclusion.** The modification is likely enforceable, and Supplier recovers the \$1,500 difference, though a court that found the threat improper could reach the opposite result.
material: Point sheet and model answer, performance test. Point sheet (20 points): follows the task memo (objective memo, three headed sections, a short conclusion, no statement of facts, roughly 900 words) (3); Question 1: statute text, Brandt (not protected because no step beyond internal complaint), Orr (concrete step, appointment and notice to employer), application to Document 2 and the March 4 appointment (5); Question 2: Marin but-for test, timing (one day after the e-mail), the stated reason versus two minor warnings, pretext, honest discussion of the warnings as an adverse fact (5); Question 3: § 9 remedies, back pay for 4 weeks at \$720 per week (40 hours at \$18) = \$2,880 at a minimum, with credit for noting that her usual 46 to 52 hours would raise it, an equal amount as liquidated damages for \$5,760 total at the minimum, reinstatement as an option (4); honest assessment of weaknesses and gaps (for example, the manager's reasons and any further documentation) (2); cites file and library by name (1). Model answer: **1. Protected activity.** Section 8(a) protects an employee who has "filed" a complaint or "caused to be instituted" a proceeding. Brandt v. Keel held that an internal complaint to a supervisor is not enough. Ms. Ortiz's March 3 complaint to Sam is an internal complaint, so by itself it is unprotected under Brandt. But on March 4 she scheduled an intake appointment with the Department and told Sam in writing (E-mail, March 4). In Orr v. Sable Foods, scheduling an intake appointment and telling the employer was a concrete step that "caused to be instituted" a proceeding. Her facts match Orr, and Brandt is distinguishable because the employee there took no step beyond an internal complaint. Ms. Ortiz likely engaged in protected activity. **2. Causation.** Under Marin v. Tile Co., she must show that her protected activity was a but-for cause of the firing. She was terminated on March 5, one day after the e-mail (Termination Letter), which supports an inference of causation. Harbor Cafe will rely on its stated reason, "attitude and attendance problems." That is a legitimate reason on its face, and the file shows two warnings for lateness (Written Warnings, January 8 and February 2). These hurt us. But each warning was for one short lateness (12 and 8 minutes), there was no other discipline, the termination letter came one day after the e-mail, and "attitude" is vague and may refer to her complaints. Under Marin, those facts support pretext. The result is likely favorable but not certain, and we should find out whether Harbor Cafe fired others for similar lateness. **3. Remedies.** Section 9 allows reinstatement, back pay and an equal amount as liquidated damages. Ms. Ortiz earned \$18 per hour for 40 hours, or \$720 per week, and was without income from March 5 to April 1, four weeks, so back pay is at least \$2,880 on a 40-hour week, and more if her usual 46 to 52 hours are counted. With liquidated damages, the total is at least \$5,760, and reinstatement is possible, although she has started a new job. Her overtime claim is a separate wage claim. **Conclusion.** Ms. Ortiz likely has a viable retaliation claim, with the main risk being the lateness warnings.

### Key ideas by week

1. **Grading.** Point sheets reward issue, rule, application and conclusion issue by issue. The legacy UBE written section is six 30-minute MEE essays (30%) and two 90-minute MPT tasks (20%) through February 2028; the NextGen UBE was first given in July 2026 and spreads by July 2028.
2. **IRAC under time.** Read the call, outline in 4 minutes, write headed IRAC units, review for 3.
3. **Contracts, torts and property.** UCC or common law first; § 2-207 and § 2-201; strict products liability; recording acts and adverse possession.
4. **Business, estates, family.** Apparent authority, loyalty and the business judgment rule; will formalities, anti-lapse and omitted spouse; UCCJEA home state and property division.
5. **Performance test reading.** Task memo controls; library is self-contained; rank authority and extract rules.
6. **Performance test writing.** Objective memo versus brief; point headings that state conclusion and reason; disclose adverse authority.
7. **NextGen integrated sets.** Read the role and the questions first; cross-subject issues; best next step for the client; confirm formats with NCBE.
8. **NextGen performance tasks.** Hierarchy and currency of authority; *Loper Bright*; relief limited after *Trump v. CASA*.
9. **Exam-day strategy.** Checkpoints, stamina, logistics, accommodations deadlines.
10. **Character and fitness and licensing.** Candor, accurate disclosure, rehabilitation evidence, MPRE, deadlines.
11. **Full session.** Realistic conditions, grading and the error log; evidence and civil procedure rules.
12. **Final repair.** Prioritize, match the method to the error, retest.

### Most common errors

- Missing issues and writing rule dumps with no facts.
- Starting to write before reading the call or the task memo.
- Using memorized law on a performance test, or omitting adverse authority.
- Omitting information on a character and fitness application.
- Studying new material in the last days instead of rehearsing.

solution: Under the pre-existing duty rule, a promise to do what one is already bound to do is not consideration for a new promise.
