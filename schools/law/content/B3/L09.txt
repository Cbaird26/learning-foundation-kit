@article
### Know the schedule you will actually sit

Under the usual legacy UBE schedule, the written components fall on one day and the MBE on the other: the six MEE essays in a three-hour morning session and the two MPT tasks in a three-hour afternoon session, then the 200-question MBE in two three-hour sessions of 100 questions each on the other day. That is roughly 12 hours of testing across two days. **Confirm the schedule, the order of the days and the rules for your own sitting** with your jurisdiction, and, if you will take the NextGen exam, check NCBE's current schedule and format materials, because they differ from the legacy UBE and may change.

### Timing is a skill

Because points accumulate issue by issue, a rigid, practiced timing plan is worth more than inspired writing.

- **Essays.** Six essays in 180 minutes means 30 minutes each. Write the start time of each essay on your scratch paper or note. Stop when the clock says stop, even mid-sentence, and move on. A thin answer to the last issue earns more than a missing essay.
- **Performance tests.** Ninety minutes each. Set checkpoints: reading and outline done at about 35 minutes; first section done by about 60; last section started by about 75.
- **MBE.** 100 questions in 180 minutes is 1.8 minutes per question. Pace yourself in blocks (for example, 25 questions by the 45-minute mark), mark hard questions and return only if time remains. Never leave an answer blank.

### Handling the bad moments

- **A bad essay.** Each essay is graded independently and is worth about 5% of the legacy UBE. Do not replay it. Take a breath and begin the next one.
- **Running out of time.** Switch to short, complete IRAC units for the remaining issues. Do not polish.
- **A blank mind.** Write the call of the question, list the parties, and walk the elements of the most likely claim. The rule often comes once you write the elements.
- **A technical problem.** Raise your hand and follow the proctor's instructions. Do not try to fix it yourself. Know your jurisdiction's backup procedures for laptop or software failure before the day.

### Stamina

Endurance is built the way strength is: gradually and under realistic conditions. Take at least two full practice sessions of the real length (a full three-hour essay session and a full three-hour MBE session), at the real start time, with the real breaks. Practice writing by hand or on the keyboard the same way you will on exam day. In the last week, shift from learning new material to sharpening what you know: review checklists, redo missed questions, and keep your sleep schedule on the exam's clock.

### Logistics

Handle logistics early, because errors there can cost a sitting.

- Read the examinee rules: identification, permitted items, electronics, clothing, food and break rules. Do not bring a prohibited item, such as a phone, into the room.
- Install and test any exam software on your laptop weeks ahead, and know the deadlines for downloads and mock exams.
- Travel: visit the site if possible, plan the route and leave extra time. Book lodging near the site in a busy city.
- **Accommodations.** If you need testing accommodations (for example for a disability), apply by your jurisdiction's deadline, which is usually well before the exam, and expect to submit documentation. Do not wait.

### Rest and food

Plan meals, hydration and sleep like study blocks. Eat a normal breakfast, bring allowed snacks for breaks, avoid new foods or heavy caffeine you have not tried. Sleep is more valuable the night before than one more hour of cramming.

### Policy

The exam is long because licensing authorities want to see that a lawyer can think clearly under pressure. A plan converts anxiety into procedures, and procedures work when stress is high.

### Planning exam week

Write a schedule for the final 10 days: practice sessions, review, rest, logistics checklist, travel, the night-before routine and what you will do after each session (nothing involving the exam, other than rest). Keep a short list of triggers and responses: "If I freeze, I will write the elements," "If I run late, I will write short IRACs."

@example
title: A checkpoint plan for a three-hour essay session
problem: Hypothetical: your morning essay session starts at 9:00 a.m. with six 30-minute essays. Create checkpoints and decide what to do if you are two minutes behind at the third checkpoint.
step: Compute the end time of each essay: essay 1 ends 9:30, essay 2 ends 10:00, essay 3 ends 10:30, essay 4 ends 11:00, essay 5 ends 11:30, essay 6 ends 12:00. Write these on your paper.
step: At each end time, check progress. At 10:30 you are two minutes behind the plan, so you are still on essay 3.
step: Decide: stop writing essay 3 within a few seconds, with a conclusion. Do not borrow time from later essays, because each essay is worth the same and a rushed start in one costs more than a slightly shorter finish in another.
step: Move to essay 4 and spend the first four minutes reading and outlining as planned.

@mistakes
- Using exam week to learn new law instead of rehearsing timing and checklists.
- Borrowing time from the next essay to finish the previous one.
- Leaving the accommodations request, software installation or hotel booking to the last month.
- Assuming the schedule and rules of the legacy UBE apply to a NextGen sitting without checking.
