### What the legacy UBE written section contains

The legacy Uniform Bar Exam (UBE) has three parts: the Multistate Bar Examination (MBE, 200 multiple-choice questions), the Multistate Essay Examination (MEE, **six 30-minute essays**) and the Multistate Performance Test (MPT, **two 90-minute tasks**). The legacy UBE runs through February 2028. The **NextGen UBE** was first given in July 2026 in ten jurisdictions; more jurisdictions join in July 2027 and nearly all UBE jurisdictions are scheduled to switch by July 2028. Which version you sit depends on your jurisdiction and your sitting, so confirm both with your bar admission authority.

Under the legacy UBE the components are weighted MBE 50%, MEE 30% and MPT 20% of a 400-point total score. Each MEE essay is therefore worth 5% and each MPT task 10%. A strong MPT is worth two essays. Jurisdictions set their own passing scores, so the same total can pass in one and fail in another.

### Who grades and how

In UBE jurisdictions, local graders score the essays and performance tests. NCBE supplies the questions, grading materials and training, but each jurisdiction runs the grading. Graders typically read hundreds of answers against a **point sheet** (a grading guideline listing the issues, the governing rules and the facts that matter), and many use a scale of roughly 0 to 6 per answer. Raw written scores are then **scaled to the MBE** so that written scores are comparable across sittings. Consult your jurisdiction for its scale.

Because graders are working fast, they look for visible, labeled credit. A point sheet usually rewards four things for each issue:

1. **Issue**: you noticed it and named it.
2. **Rule**: you stated the governing rule, with its elements, accurately.
3. **Application**: you tied specific facts to each element.
4. **Conclusion**: you reached a conclusion that follows from your analysis.

Application usually carries the most weight, and it is where most candidates lose points. A correct rule followed by "therefore the contract is enforceable" earns the rule point and almost nothing else.

### What costs points

- **Skipped issues.** Points are earned issue by issue. A brilliant discussion of the main issue does not make up for omitting three others.
- **Rule dumps.** Reciting everything you know about a subject, including rules that no fact calls for, earns little and burns time.
- **Facts left unused.** Bar essay facts are chosen on purpose. If a fact seems odd, ask what element it satisfies or defeats.
- **No conclusion, or a conclusion without a reason.** Say what you conclude and why, even when the issue is close. On a close issue, show both sides briefly and then commit.
- **Answering the wrong question.** The call of the question (for example "Can Buyer recover damages?") tells you which issues matter. Read it first.

### The performance test is graded differently

The MPT grading guideline rewards following the task memo: the right document in the right format, accurate use of the file and library, and a persuasive or objective analysis as assigned. You need no outside knowledge of the law, because the library supplies the authority. Lesson 5 and lesson 6 cover the MPT in depth.

### The NextGen difference

NCBE describes NextGen as testing foundational legal concepts and principles together with lawyering skills, using integrated question sets and performance tasks. Its formats are delivered differently from the MEE. Lessons 7 and 8 explain what is publicly known; check NCBE's current content scope and sample materials before relying on any format detail, and check your jurisdiction's rules on which exam you will take.

### Policy behind point sheets

Graders must score thousands of answers consistently. A point sheet turns judgment into a checklist, so an answer that is easy to check against the list earns more credit than an equally smart answer that hides its reasoning. Write for a tired grader.

@example
title: Scoring a short answer against a point sheet
problem: Hypothetical point sheet for one issue: 1 point for identifying that a contract for the sale of goods is governed by UCC Article 2; 1 point for stating that a sale of goods for \$500 or more needs a signed writing; 2 points for applying the facts; 1 point for the conclusion. Facts: Buyer and Seller orally agreed that Seller would sell Buyer 200 widgets for \$4,000. Neither has signed anything. A candidate writes: "The UCC applies because widgets are goods. A contract for \$500 or more must be in writing. Here the deal was oral for \$4,000, and Buyer signed nothing, so the statute of frauds is not satisfied and the contract is unenforceable, unless an exception applies." How many of the 5 points does the answer earn?
step: Issue and governing law: the candidate says the UCC applies because widgets are goods. That earns the 1 issue point.
step: Rule: the candidate states that a contract of \$500 or more must be in writing. That is the rule point, so the total is 2.
step: Application: the candidate ties the oral agreement, the \$4,000 price and the absence of a signed writing to the rule. That earns the 2 application points, so the total is 4.
step: Conclusion: the candidate concludes the contract is unenforceable under the statute of frauds, which is a supported conclusion. That is 1 more point, for 5 of 5. The phrase "unless an exception applies" flags an exception without analyzing it, which would help only if the point sheet had a point for exceptions.

@mistakes
- Spending the whole 30 minutes on the issue you know best and skipping the others.
- Writing the rule and then jumping to a conclusion without tying any fact to an element.
- Treating the MPT as a test of what you already know instead of what the library says.
- Assuming NextGen is graded or formatted like the MEE without checking NCBE's published materials.

@practice self
prompt: Hypothetical point sheet for one issue: (a) names negligence as the claim; (b) states the four elements of negligence (duty, breach, actual and proximate cause, damages); (c) applies the facts: a store employee left a wet floor unmarked for two hours and a customer who slipped broke her wrist; (d) concludes. Below is a candidate's entire answer: "Negligence requires duty, breach, causation and damages. The store is liable because the floor was wet." Score the answer against the point sheet and rewrite it in 4 to 6 sentences so it would earn full credit.
solution: Score: it names negligence implicitly and lists the elements, so about 1 to 2 of the first two points; it applies no facts and gives only a one-line conclusion, so it earns little of (c) and (d). A full-credit rewrite: "Customer can sue the store for negligence, which requires duty, breach, actual and proximate cause, and damages. A store owes its customers, as business invitees, a duty of reasonable care to keep the premises safe and to warn of dangers it knows or should know of. The employee left the floor wet and unmarked for two hours, which is long enough that the store should have discovered and addressed it, so the store breached its duty. The wet floor was the actual cause of the fall (but for the wet floor, she would not have slipped) and a proximate cause, because a fall is a foreseeable result of a wet floor. Customer's broken wrist is damage. The store is therefore likely liable, subject to any comparative-fault defense."
rubric: States the claim and all four elements.
rubric: Uses at least three specific facts (two hours, unmarked, customer fell, broken wrist).
rubric: Addresses causation and damages, not just breach.
rubric: Ends with a conclusion supported by the analysis.
hint: Count points by category: issue, rule, application, conclusion.
