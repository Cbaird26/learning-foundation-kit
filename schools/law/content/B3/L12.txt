@article
Practice does not improve scores by itself. Repair does: finding what is costing you points, fixing it with a method that matches the problem and retesting. This lesson turns your records from lessons 1 through 11 into a plan.

### Step one: aggregate the error log

Collect every practice essay, performance test and integrated set you graded. Count lost points two ways:

- **By error type**: missed issue, rule error, thin application, conclusion or reasoning error, time or format.
- **By subject or task**: contracts, torts, property, business associations, estates, family law, performance tests, integrated sets.

Add the tallies. A single subject can hide an error type that appears everywhere (for example, thin application across five subjects), and an error type can hide a subject gap (for example, missing every future-interests issue).

### Step two: prioritize

Rank problems by **points at stake** and **speed of repair**. A rule you can fix in one hour and that appears on many essays (the hearsay framework, the elements of negligence) ranks above an obscure rule you may never see. A format error that cost 6 points (writing the wrong document type on a performance test) is fast to fix and ranks high. Choose the top **three to five** items and leave the rest.

### Step three: match the repair to the error

- **Missed issues.** Make an **issue checklist** for each subject (the recurring issues, one line each). Practice spotting by outlining, without writing, six to eight past fact patterns a day. Time yourself at four minutes each.
- **Rule errors.** Make short **rule cards** with the elements. Say the rule from memory, then check. Test the cards at 1 day, 3 days and 7 days (spaced review).
- **Thin application.** Rewrite application paragraphs: for each element, a fact and a "because". Compare with a model. Do not study more rules for this problem.
- **Conclusion and reasoning errors.** For close issues, practice writing both sides in two sentences and then choosing.
- **Time and format.** Rehearse the checkpoint plan from lesson 9. Run mini-sessions of two essays in 60 minutes. On performance tests, spend the first minute writing the document type and instructions at the top of your page.

For NextGen, add skill-specific practice: client-counseling next steps, library hierarchy and currency checks, and short written responses, using NCBE's current sample materials and rules.

### Step four: schedule

Time-box the plan:

- **Days 1 to 3**: repair work in the top three areas, about two to three hours a day, in short blocks with breaks.
- **Day 4**: one timed session (three essays or one performance test) to test the repairs; grade and update the log.
- **Days 5 to 7**: repair the new top error and refresh older material with spaced review.
- **Final days**: taper. Light review, rest and logistics (lesson 9).

### Step five: retest

Repair works only if you can show it. Take an unseen essay or task under time and grade with the same criteria. If application improves from "thin" to "complete", the repair worked. If not, change the method, not just the amount of study.

### Calibrating your confidence

A practice score rarely matches the real result exactly: graders differ, and scales differ by jurisdiction. Use your own trend, from earlier to later practice answers graded the same way, as the evidence of progress. Treat a flat trend as a signal to change method.

### Mindset

Fixing five specific things is more reliable than trying to know everything. Remember that a bar passes candidates who are competent across the board, not those who are perfect in a few areas.

### The oral explanation

Part of completing this course is explaining one major idea aloud without notes, for example the IRAC unit for an issue, the rule for a recurring doctrine or how to attack a performance test. If you cannot explain it aloud in two minutes, you do not yet own it. Add that idea to your repair list.

### Policy

A licensing exam is a sampling device. Targeted repair raises performance across the sample more than broad review, because most lost points come from a small number of repeated patterns.

title: Building a three-item repair plan
problem: Hypothetical error-log totals over eight graded essays: thin application, 24 lost points; missed issues in property and estates, 12 points; rule errors in evidence, 8 points; time or format, 4 points. You have 20 study hours. Choose a plan.
step: Rank by points at stake: thin application (24), missed issues (12), evidence rules (8), time (4).
step: Assign hours in proportion to points but adjust for speed: thin application, 10 hours of application rewrites; missed issues, 5 hours with issue checklists for property and estates; evidence rules, 3 hours with rule cards and spaced review; time, 2 hours on checkpoint practice.
step: Add a retest: a timed unseen essay after about 10 hours.
step: Define success: application earns full marks on at least half of the elements, and the same issues are caught on the retest.

@mistakes
- Reviewing every subject equally instead of targeting the repeated errors.
- Studying more rules to fix thin application.
- Skipping the retest and relying on the feeling that you improved.
- Using the last days for new material rather than rest and rehearsal.

answer: 1/2
check: 24 / (24 + 12 + 8 + 4)
hint: Add the lost points to find the total.
solution: The total is $24 + 12 + 8 + 4 = 48$, and $24/48 = 1/2$.
