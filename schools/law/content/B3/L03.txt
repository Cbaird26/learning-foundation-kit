@article
Contracts, torts and property are the subjects that come up on essays most reliably. Each has a short list of recurring issues. Learn the recurring issue and the first sentence you will write about it.

### Contracts: pick the governing law first

The first sentence of nearly every contracts essay is the choice of law between the **common law** and **UCC Article 2**. Article 2 governs contracts for the sale of goods (movable things). For mixed contracts for goods and services, most courts apply the **predominant purpose** test: if the main object of the deal is goods, Article 2 governs the whole contract. Name the source: "This is a sale of goods, so UCC Article 2 governs."

Recurring contracts issues:

- **Formation and the battle of the forms (UCC § 2-207).** A definite and seasonable expression of acceptance operates as an acceptance even though it states additional or different terms, unless acceptance is expressly made conditional on the offeror's assent to them. Between merchants, additional terms become part of the contract unless the offer expressly limits acceptance to its terms, the new terms materially alter the deal, or the offeror objects in advance or within a reasonable time. Terms that cause surprise or hardship (arbitration clauses, warranty disclaimers) are the usual examples of material alterations. For conflicting (different) terms, most courts apply the knockout rule: the conflicting terms cancel and the UCC's gap-fillers supply the term.
- **Statute of frauds (UCC § 2-201).** A contract for goods of \$500 or more needs a writing signed by the party to be charged, with a quantity term. Exceptions include specially manufactured goods, admissions, partial performance and the merchant's confirmation, which binds the recipient who does not object in writing within 10 days.
- **Remedies.** The default is expectation damages. A buyer who covers in good faith recovers the difference between the cover price and the contract price (UCC § 2-712), plus incidental and any foreseeable consequential damages and minus expenses saved. Remember *Hadley v. Baxendale* (1854) for foreseeability of consequential damages and the duty to mitigate.

### Torts: a short menu

Torts essays reward discipline. For a negligence claim, give the four elements, then the defenses. Name the duty, then the breach (reasonable care; negligence per se when a statute defines the standard), then actual and proximate causation, then damages. Then add the defenses: comparative fault (pure in some states, which reduces recovery by the plaintiff's percentage; modified in others, which bars a plaintiff at or above 50% or 51%) and assumption of the risk.

For products, **strict liability** applies to a commercial seller (manufacturer, distributor or retailer) of a product with a **manufacturing defect, design defect or warning defect** that makes it unreasonably dangerous and that causes injury to a foreseeable user. The plaintiff need not prove fault. Comparative fault principles may reduce the recovery for unreasonable misuse of the product.

Vicarious liability, covered in lesson 2, requires an employee acting within the scope of employment. An employer is not generally liable for an independent contractor's torts.

### Property: recording and possession

Two property issues come up again and again. **Recording acts** (pure race, pure notice, race-notice) decide priority between successive grantees. Always check whether the later buyer gave value, had notice (actual, record or inquiry notice, including open possession by someone else), and recorded first. **Adverse possession** requires possession that is actual, open and notorious, exclusive, hostile (without the owner's permission) and continuous for the statutory period, which varies by state (commonly 10 to 20 years). Title vests at the end of the period.

Other recurring property topics are landlord-tenant (constructive eviction; the implied warranty of habitability in residential leases; duty to mitigate on the tenant's abandonment in most states), easements (creation by express grant, implication, necessity or prescription) and future interests with the Rule Against Perpetuities. Know the names and the first rule of each.

### Applying the rule to the facts

For each issue ask three questions in order: what is the rule, which element is in dispute, and which facts decide it. A reader should be able to find the dispute in one glance, so put the contested element in your heading ("Is the arbitration clause a material alteration?") and decide it in one paragraph.

### Why these rules look the way they do

The UCC loosens common-law formality because merchants deal quickly. Strict products liability places the cost of defects on the seller who can spread it. Recording acts reward the buyer who checks the record, and adverse possession rewards use of land and discourages stale claims.

@example
title: Does the UCC or the common law govern a mixed contract?
problem: Hypothetical: Owner hires Window Co. to supply and install new windows in her house for \$10,000. Of the price, \$7,000 is for the windows and \$3,000 is for installation labor. Owner refuses to pay, claiming the windows were defective. Does UCC Article 2 govern?
step: Identify the issue: a contract for both goods (windows) and services (installation) raises the question of which body of law governs.
step: State the majority test: courts apply the predominant purpose test, asking whether the main object of the contract is the sale of goods or the provision of services. Some courts apply the gravamen test, asking what the dispute is about.
step: Apply it: Owner's main purpose is to obtain windows. The windows account for most of the price, and installation is incidental to getting the goods in place. Under the majority test the contract is predominantly for goods.
step: Conclude: Article 2 governs, so implied warranties of merchantability apply if Window Co. is a merchant of windows. Under a minority gravamen approach, the result could differ if the dispute were about installation workmanship.

@mistakes
- Applying the common-law mirror-image rule to a sale of goods between merchants without mentioning UCC § 2-207.
- Treating an arbitration clause in an acceptance as automatically part of the contract.
- Stating "strict liability" for a manufacturing defect without naming the defendant's commercial-seller status or causation.
- Counting adverse possession time from the date of the deed rather than from the start of hostile possession.

@practice self
prompt: Timed essay 3 (30 minutes; hypothetical facts). Seller, a merchant, sent Buyer, also a merchant, a signed written quote: "1,000 units at \$5 each, delivery in 30 days. Order by return mail." Buyer returned a signed purchase order: "Accepted: 1,000 units at \$5 each. Any dispute will be resolved by binding arbitration." The quote did not mention arbitration and did not say acceptance was limited to its terms. Seller shipped the units on time. Later a dispute arose over late payment, and Seller sued in court. Buyer moved to compel arbitration. Is there a contract, and must the dispute be arbitrated? Write your answer in 30 minutes, then grade it against the model answer.
solution: Model answer: **Governing law.** The deal is a sale of goods between merchants, so UCC Article 2 governs. **Formation.** Seller's signed quote is an offer. Under UCC § 2-207(1), a definite and seasonable expression of acceptance operates as an acceptance even if it states additional terms, unless acceptance is expressly conditional on the offeror's assent to those terms. Buyer's signed purchase order matches the goods, quantity and price and does not say it is conditional on Seller's agreement to arbitration, so it is an acceptance and a contract formed. **Statute of frauds.** The price is over \$500, but both sides signed writings stating the quantity, so § 2-201 is satisfied. **The arbitration clause.** Because both parties are merchants, under § 2-207(2) the additional term becomes part of the contract unless the offer expressly limited acceptance to its terms, the term materially alters the contract, or Seller objected in time. The quote did not limit acceptance and Seller did not object. The remaining question is whether the clause materially alters the contract. Most courts treat an arbitration clause as a material alteration because it waives the right to a court and a jury and would surprise the offeror. Seller was surprised by the clause (the quote said nothing about it), so the clause is likely excluded. **Conclusion.** A contract was formed on Seller's terms, the arbitration clause did not become part of it, and Buyer's motion to compel arbitration should be denied. The dispute can proceed in court. A contrary view that treats arbitration as non-material would compel arbitration, but the majority treats it as material.
rubric: Applies UCC § 2-207(1) to find an acceptance and a formed contract.
rubric: Applies § 2-207(2) and the material alteration exception, naming surprise or hardship.
rubric: Concludes the arbitration clause is likely excluded and the case may proceed in court.
rubric: Keeps rule statements short and uses headings by issue.
hint: Decide whether there is an acceptance first; then decide what happens to the additional term.

@quiz self
prompt: Timed essay 4 (30 minutes; hypothetical facts). Maker manufactures stepladders. A batch left the factory with a rung that was not welded correctly. Hardware Store, a retailer, sold one of the ladders to Pat. Pat placed the ladder on soft, uneven ground in her yard, ignoring a warning label that said "Use only on firm, level ground," and climbed it. The faulty rung broke and Pat fell, breaking her arm. Pat sues Maker and Hardware Store for strict products liability and negligence in a jurisdiction applying pure comparative fault. What claims can Pat bring and what defenses can the defendants raise? Write your answer in 30 minutes, then grade it against the model answer.
solution: Model answer: **Strict liability against Maker.** A commercial seller of a defective product that causes injury is strictly liable. Maker is the manufacturer. The improperly welded rung is a manufacturing defect, because it departs from Maker's intended design. The defect made the ladder unreasonably dangerous, it existed when the ladder left Maker's control, and it caused the rung to break and Pat to fall, so actual cause is met. A fall from a broken ladder is a foreseeable consequence, so proximate cause is met. Pat suffered a broken arm. **Strict liability against Hardware Store.** A retailer is a commercial seller in the chain of distribution and is strictly liable for a defective product even without fault, so Pat can recover from either defendant. Hardware Store may seek indemnity from Maker. **Negligence.** A negligence claim requires fault. Maker owed a duty of reasonable care in manufacturing and likely breached it if its quality control was inadequate, and res ipsa loquitur may help. Hardware Store is negligent only if it knew or should have known of the defect, and nothing suggests it did. **Defenses.** Pat placed the ladder on soft, uneven ground against a warning. This may be unreasonable misuse or assumption of the risk, but under pure comparative fault her recovery is reduced by her percentage of fault rather than barred. A jury could find she was partly at fault, but the defective rung is an actual cause of the fall, and whether the soft ground also contributed is a question of fact. **Conclusion.** Pat can likely recover from both defendants in strict liability for the manufacturing defect, with her damages reduced by her share of fault. Negligence is a weaker claim against Hardware Store.
rubric: Identifies manufacturing defect and strict liability for both Maker and Hardware Store.
rubric: Addresses causation and damages, not only defect.
rubric: Applies pure comparative fault to the soft-ground misuse and the ignored warning.
rubric: Distinguishes the negligence claim and notes it is weaker against Hardware Store.
hint: Walk through defect, causation, damages and then defenses for each defendant.
