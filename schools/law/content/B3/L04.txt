@article
These three subjects are statute-heavy and vary by state. On the exam, write the majority or uniform-code rule and say so when states differ.

### Business associations

**Agency.** A principal is bound by an agent's acts within **actual authority** (express or implied) and **apparent authority**, which arises when the principal's manifestations lead a third party to reasonably believe the agent is authorized. An agent also may bind the principal through ratification. A principal is vicariously liable for an employee agent's torts within the scope of employment.

**Partnerships.** Under the Revised Uniform Partnership Act, a general partnership is an entity, each partner is an agent of the partnership for apparently ordinary business, and general partners are **jointly and severally** liable for partnership obligations. **Limited liability companies and limited partnerships** shield limited partners and members from personal liability for entity debts, subject to the limits of each statute.

**Corporations.** Shareholders are not personally liable for corporate debts unless a court **pierces the corporate veil**, which courts do reluctantly when the owners ignored corporate formalities, commingled funds, undercapitalized the entity or used it to commit fraud or injustice. Directors and officers owe fiduciary **duties of care and loyalty**. The **business judgment rule** presumes directors acted on an informed basis, in good faith and in the honest belief the action served the corporation, and a court will not second-guess a disinterested decision. The rule does not protect a self-dealing transaction. An **interested-director transaction** is generally not voidable if (1) the material facts were disclosed and a majority of disinterested directors approved it, (2) disinterested shareholders approved it after disclosure, or (3) it was fair to the corporation. Remember the **corporate opportunity doctrine**: a fiduciary cannot take an opportunity the corporation could pursue without first offering it. A shareholder may sue on the corporation's behalf in a **derivative suit**, usually after making a demand on the board or showing demand would be futile.

### Trusts and estates

**Will formalities.** The Uniform Probate Code (UPC § 2-502) requires a writing signed by the testator (or another in the testator's name and presence) and signed by at least two witnesses who witnessed the signing or the testator's acknowledgment. Some states allow handwritten (holographic) wills. A will can be revoked by physical act or a later writing.

**Lapse and anti-lapse.** If a devisee dies before the testator, the gift lapses and falls into the residue, unless an **anti-lapse statute** applies. The UPC version (§ 2-603) substitutes the devisee's descendants when the devisee was a grandparent, or a descendant of a grandparent, of the testator; many states are narrower and cover only the testator's descendants. A will that expresses a contrary intent controls.

**Omitted spouse.** Under UPC § 2-301, a spouse who married the testator after the will was executed receives an intestate-type share unless the will was made in contemplation of the marriage or the testator provided for the spouse in other ways. Most states have comparable statutes, and the elective share separately protects a surviving spouse in nearly every common-law state.

**Trusts.** A valid express trust requires intent, a trustee, a beneficiary, trust property and a lawful purpose. A trust is revocable only if the settlor reserved that power (the UPC presumes revocability, many states presume irrevocability). A trustee owes duties of loyalty and prudence.

### Family law

- **Divorce.** Every state permits no-fault divorce.
- **Property division.** Most states follow **equitable distribution** of marital property, a fair division that need not be equal. Community property states (such as California) treat property acquired during marriage as community property, which many of them divide equally (others divide it in a "just and right" or equitable way). Separate property (pre-marital or inherited) generally stays with its owner.
- **Custody.** The standard is the **best interests of the child**. Jurisdiction over an initial custody decision rests with the child's **home state** under the Uniform Child Custody Jurisdiction and Enforcement Act (UCCJEA): the state where the child lived with a parent for at least six consecutive months immediately before the case began, or that was the home state within six months before the case began if a parent still lives there.
- **Support.** Child support follows state guidelines and may be modified on a substantial change in circumstances. Parents cannot waive a child's right to support. Alimony varies by state and is generally modifiable on changed circumstances.
- **Premarital agreements.** Under the Uniform Premarital Agreement Act, an agreement is unenforceable if the party resisting it did not sign voluntarily, or if it was unconscionable when signed and that party had no fair disclosure of the other's finances and no waiver of disclosure.

### Why the rules look like this

Agency doctrine protects third parties who rely on appearances. Fiduciary duties exist because directors control other people's money, and the safe harbors for interested transactions let honest deals proceed. Will formalities protect the testator's intent. The UCCJEA prevents custody forum fights.

@example
title: Apparent authority
problem: Hypothetical: Principal Corp. told Vendor that its purchasing manager, Mia, handles all supply orders. Principal Corp. then privately told Mia not to buy more than \$5,000 of goods. Mia ordered \$20,000 of goods from Vendor, who knew nothing of the limit. Is Principal Corp. bound?
step: State the rule: a principal is bound by an agent's acts within actual or apparent authority. Apparent authority exists when the principal's manifestations to a third party make it reasonable to believe the agent is authorized.
step: Actual authority: Mia's actual authority is limited to \$5,000, so a \$20,000 order exceeds it.
step: Apparent authority: Principal Corp. told Vendor that Mia handles all supply orders, a manifestation by the principal. Vendor reasonably believed that Mia could place the order, because the limit was never communicated to Vendor.
step: Conclude: Principal Corp. is bound by apparent authority and may seek reimbursement from Mia for exceeding her actual authority.

@mistakes
- Applying the business judgment rule to a transaction in which a director has a personal interest.
- Treating anti-lapse as automatic: check whether the devisee qualifies under the state statute and whether the will shows contrary intent.
- Saying a divorce court divides property "equally" in every state, rather than distinguishing equitable distribution from community property.
- Forgetting that agency authority is judged from the principal's manifestations to the third party, not the agent's private belief.

@practice self
prompt: Timed essay 5 (30 minutes; hypothetical facts). Gadget Corp. has five directors. Dan, a director, owns Dan Supplies LLC. At a board meeting, Dan said nothing about his ownership. The board voted 4 to 1, with Dan voting yes, to buy \$900,000 of components from Dan Supplies LLC. The components were worth \$600,000 on the market. A shareholder, Sue, learned of this a year later. What claims can Sue bring and what defenses might Dan raise? Write your answer in 30 minutes, then grade it against the model answer.
solution: Model answer: **Sue's claim.** Sue sues derivatively on behalf of Gadget Corp. for breach of the duty of loyalty, after making a demand on the board or showing that demand would be futile (the board approved the deal, and the interested director sat on it). **Self-dealing.** A director breaches the duty of loyalty in a transaction in which the director has a personal interest unless a safe harbor applies. Dan owns Dan Supplies LLC, so the contract is an interested-director transaction. **Safe harbors.** First, disinterested director approval: it must follow disclosure of the material facts, and Dan said nothing about his interest, so the vote was not informed. Dan's own vote cannot count, and even without it the vote was 3 to 1, but the approval is ineffective because of the lack of disclosure. Second, shareholder approval after disclosure: none occurred. Third, fairness: the corporation paid \$900,000 for goods worth \$600,000, so the deal was not fair as to price. **Business judgment rule.** It does not protect interested transactions, and the board was uninformed about Dan's conflict. **Defenses.** Dan may argue that the other directors would have approved anyway, that the price reflected special terms or quality, or that Sue's suit is untimely. None is strong on these facts. **Remedies.** The court may rescind the contract if feasible, or order Dan to disgorge profit or pay the \$300,000 difference to Gadget Corp. **Conclusion.** Dan likely breached the duty of loyalty and Gadget Corp. may recover through Sue's derivative suit.
rubric: Identifies a derivative suit and the duty of loyalty for self-dealing.
rubric: Tests all three safe harbors (disinterested approval after disclosure, shareholder approval, fairness).
rubric: Explains why the business judgment rule does not protect Dan.
rubric: States the remedy: rescission or recovery of the \$300,000 difference.
hint: Start with the claim, then the safe harbors, then remedies.

@quiz self
prompt: Timed essay 6 (30 minutes; hypothetical facts). Testator signed a will in 2019, in front of two witnesses who also signed. It leaves her house to her sister Ana and the residue to her friend Ben. In 2022, Testator married Cora. She never changed the will. Ana died in 2023, leaving a daughter, Dee. Testator died in 2024. She is survived by Cora, Dee and Ben. The state follows the Uniform Probate Code. Discuss who is likely to receive the house and what Cora may claim. Write your answer in 30 minutes, then grade it against the model answer.
solution: Model answer: **Validity.** The will was in writing, signed by Testator and by two witnesses, so it satisfies UPC § 2-502 and is valid. **The house.** The gift to Ana lapsed because Ana died before Testator. Without anti-lapse, the house would fall into the residue and pass to Ben. Under the UPC anti-lapse statute (§ 2-603), when a devisee who was a descendant of the testator's grandparent predeceases the testator, the devisee's descendants substitute unless the will shows a contrary intent. Ana was Testator's sister, so she was a descendant of the testator's grandparents, and the will does not say what happens if Ana predeceases. Dee, Ana's daughter, therefore takes the house. **Cora's claims.** Cora married Testator after the will was signed, so under the omitted-spouse statute (UPC § 2-301) she receives an intestate-type share of the estate unless the will was made in contemplation of the marriage, the will shows an intent that it should apply despite the marriage, or Testator provided for her through other transfers. The will predates the marriage and does not mention Cora, and nothing suggests other provision for her, so Cora is likely entitled to a share, which comes out of the devised property. In addition, Cora may elect against the estate and take an elective share under the state's elective-share statute, if that gives her more. **Conclusion.** Dee takes the house by anti-lapse, subject to Cora's omitted-spouse share, which reduces the gifts to Dee and Ben, and Cora may choose the elective share if it is larger.
rubric: Finds the will valid and explains lapse and the UPC anti-lapse substitution for Dee.
rubric: Identifies Cora as an omitted spouse and applies the exceptions.
rubric: Mentions the elective share as an alternative claim.
rubric: States a clear conclusion on the house and on Cora.
hint: Take the house first, then Cora.
