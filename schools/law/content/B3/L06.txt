@article
Reading is half of the MPT. The other half is writing a document that a supervising lawyer could use. This lesson covers the two documents that appear most often: an **objective memorandum** and a **persuasive brief**. Other tasks (a client letter, a contract clause, a closing argument outline) follow the same principle: do what the task memo says, for that reader.

### The objective memorandum

An objective memo tells a supervisor how the law applies to a client's facts, whether the news is good or bad. A common structure, unless the task memo directs otherwise:

1. **Heading** (To, From, Date, Re).
2. **Question presented and brief answer** (when asked for).
3. **Statement of facts**: only the facts that matter, accurately, with cites to the file.
4. **Discussion**: one heading per issue, each with rule, application using facts, counterarguments and a conclusion.
5. **Conclusion**: a short overall assessment, and next steps if the memo asks.

Tone is neutral. Weigh both sides, say which is stronger and why, and flag uncertainty. Do not hide a bad fact or a bad case.

### The persuasive brief

A brief argues for the client to a court. Typical parts: **statement of facts** (accurate but organized to favor the client), **argument** with persuasive **point headings**, and a short **conclusion** asking for specific relief. A good point heading is a full sentence that states a conclusion and gives the reason, using facts:

- Weak: "The covenant."
- Better: "The covenant is unenforceable."
- Strongest: "The covenant is unenforceable because a five-year, nationwide ban is far broader than a single-location bakery needs to protect its customers."

The weakest heading names a topic, the middle one asserts without a reason, and the strongest ties the rule to the facts.

### Applying the rule inside each section

Use the same IRAC discipline as in the essays, but with citations to the library and file. State the rule from the library authority, with the case name and the key holding. Then apply the **facts** with a record cite, compare or distinguish **similar cases**, and conclude. If the library gives a multi-part test, address each part under its own subheading.

### Adverse authority and harmful facts

An objective memo discusses them. A brief must also confront them. Under Model Rule 3.3(a)(2), a lawyer must disclose to the tribunal legal authority in the controlling jurisdiction known to be directly adverse to the client's position and not disclosed by opposing counsel. Rule 4.1 forbids false statements of material fact or law to others. In practice, in a brief you state the adverse case honestly and then distinguish it: "Unlike the covenant in *Dune*, which covered one county, this covenant covers the entire country." Never misstate the holding or invent a fact. Graders penalize both.

### Formatting and citation

Follow the task memo's instructions on length and format. Citing the library by case name and file by document (for example, "Interview, p. 2") is usually enough; a formal citation system is rarely required, but the task memo will say. Use short paragraphs and headings so a grader can find each point.

### Time in the writing phase

With 45 to 55 minutes to write, a workable plan: 5 minutes for the heading, question and facts, then equal time per issue, with about 5 minutes held for the conclusion and a skim for typos. Write the strongest issue first only if the memo does not require a particular order; otherwise follow the memo.

### Other document types

A **client letter** should be in plain language, answer the client's question, explain the reasons briefly and state next steps; avoid jargon, and take care with confidentiality. A **contract drafting** task should track the supervisor's instructions and use clear, unambiguous terms. A **closing argument outline** follows the facts and the law.

### Why graders like this approach

A supervising lawyer wants to find the answer, the law and the facts quickly. Headings, short rules and cited facts make an answer easy to score.

@example
title: Strengthening a point heading
problem: Hypothetical: a brief argues that a former employee should not be bound by a three-year nationwide covenant. The draft heading is "Reasonableness." Rewrite it.
step: Identify the rule from the library: a covenant is enforceable only if reasonable in duration and geographic scope.
step: Identify the facts: the employer has one shop with customers within five miles; the covenant lasts three years and covers the entire country.
step: Combine them in one sentence that states the conclusion and the reason: "The covenant is unreasonable in scope because it bars Reed from working anywhere in the country when Bakery's customers all live within five miles of its single shop."
step: Check it: it asserts a conclusion, gives a reason, and uses a fact from the file, so a grader can see the issue before reading the section.

@mistakes
- Writing a heading that names a topic ("Duration") instead of stating a conclusion and reason.
- Stating the library rule without using any file facts to apply it.
- Ignoring or misdescribing a library case that cuts against the client.
- Using the whole time on the first issue and leaving the last section as a single sentence.

@practice self
prompt: Timed mini performance test (about 25 minutes; hypothetical materials). Task memo: "Write an objective memo on whether Employer can enforce a covenant against Reed." Library: Alder Code § 12 (hypothetical): a covenant not to compete is enforceable only to the extent reasonably necessary to protect a legitimate business interest and reasonable in duration and geographic scope. Hale v. Orr (Alder Ct. App., hypothetical): a covenant barring work for one year within 10 miles of a shop was reasonable. Kemp v. Voss (Alder Ct. App., hypothetical): a covenant barring work for five years anywhere in the state was unreasonable for a one-location employer. File: (1) Reed worked at Bakery's only shop for two years. (2) Bakery's customers are walk-ins within three miles. (3) Reed's covenant bars work at any bakery in the country for three years. (4) Reed knows Bakery's secret recipes. (5) Reed now works at a bakery two blocks from Bakery's shop. Write the Discussion section (about 250 words) with point headings, rules, application, adverse facts and a conclusion.
solution: Model answer: **A. Bakery likely has a legitimate business interest.** Under § 12 an employer may protect a legitimate business interest. Reed knows Bakery's secret recipes (File 4) and works two blocks away (File 5), so Reed could use the recipes against Bakery in the same market. **B. The covenant is probably unreasonable in geographic scope and duration as written.** Section 12 requires reasonableness in duration and area. In Hale, one year and 10 miles was reasonable for a shop; here the covenant covers the whole country for three years (File 3), but Bakery's customers are walk-ins within three miles (File 2). Like the covenant in Kemp, which covered the entire state for five years and was struck for a one-location employer, this covenant far exceeds what a single shop needs. Bakery will argue that recipes can be used anywhere, so a broad bar is necessary, but its business is local, so the argument is weak. The three-year duration falls between Hale's one year and Kemp's five and is a closer call, but the national scope is the larger problem. **C. Enforcement may be limited.** Because § 12 makes a covenant enforceable "to the extent" reasonably necessary, a court may enforce a narrowed covenant, for example a one-year ban within a few miles, and Reed's current job two blocks away would fall within it. **Conclusion.** The covenant is likely unenforceable as written, but Bakery has a real chance of enforcing a narrowed version against Reed.
rubric: Applies the statute's elements (interest, duration, area) in separate headed sections.
rubric: Uses and distinguishes both Hale and Kemp with their holdings.
rubric: Cites file facts and addresses the facts adverse to Reed (recipes, nearby new job).
rubric: Notes the "to the extent" language and the possibility of narrowing; reaches a clear conclusion.
hint: Make each heading a conclusion plus a reason, then support it with a library case and a file fact.
