@article
The Multistate Performance Test (MPT) tests whether you can do a lawyer's task with the materials given: read a file, find the law in a library and write a document. Under the legacy UBE you complete **two 90-minute tasks** in a three-hour session. Each task is worth twice an MEE essay, and every point comes from using the materials in front of you.

### The three parts

1. **The task memo.** A supervising attorney tells you what to write (an objective memo, a persuasive brief, a client letter, a contract provision, a proposal) and any instructions on format, length or content. **It controls.** If the memo specifies headings or says "do not include a statement of facts", follow it exactly. Many lost points come from writing the wrong document.
2. **The file.** Facts, in forms like client interviews, emails, contracts, police reports, transcripts and notes. The file may contain irrelevant or inconsistent facts, and the supervisor's memo may warn you to look for gaps or conflicts. Facts are your evidence. Cite them.
3. **The library.** Statutes, cases, rules and sometimes secondary sources, much of it fictional. The library is **self-contained**: you need no outside law, and memorized law outside the library is neither required nor rewarded. Some library items are irrelevant, and some are relevant to only one issue. You are to use the library's rules, even if they differ from real-world law.

### A reading strategy for 90 minutes

A common split is **30 to 40 minutes** to read and outline and **45 to 55 minutes** to write, leaving a few minutes for review. Adjust it to your pace, but do not start writing until you have a plan.

1. **Read the task memo first, twice.** Underline the document type, the audience, the questions and the format instructions.
2. **Skim the library.** Learn what authorities exist and how they relate: which is the controlling statute, which cases interpret it, and which cases fit which issue. Write the rule from each case in one line, with the case's name and what the court held.
3. **Read the file with the issues in mind.** Mark facts that satisfy or defeat the elements. Note inconsistencies and missing facts.
4. **Build an outline.** List the headings you will use, the rule under each, and the key facts. Assign a time to each part.

### Extracting rules from the library

Each case supplies a rule, an application and a result. Capture all three. If two cases appear to conflict, ask whether the facts differ, whether one court interprets a different statute, or whether one is from a higher court. Use the controlling authority first. Where the library gives a multi-factor test, list the factors as a checklist and walk each one in the discussion. If a case reached the same result on facts like yours, say so; if it reached the opposite result on facts that differ, explain the difference.

### Sorting the facts

The file has helpful facts, harmful facts and noise. In an **objective** memo you must address harmful facts honestly. In a **persuasive** document you address them in a way that favors your client but never misstate them. In both, cite the file by document and page (for example, "Interview, p. 2").

### Professional responsibility in the file

Some tasks hide an ethics problem: a client who wants you to conceal a document, a conflict of interest, or a confidentiality limit. If the memo or file raises it, address it. The ABA Model Rules of Professional Conduct, such as Rule 1.6 (confidentiality), Rule 3.3 (candor toward the tribunal) and Rule 4.1 (truthfulness in statements to others), may inform the answer, but the library will usually supply the governing rule.

### What graders look for

The MPT point sheet usually checks: right format, right audience, accurate statement of the library rules, use of the key facts under each rule, a conclusion that follows, organization and clarity. It also checks that you did not invent facts or law.

### Policy behind the MPT

The MPT mirrors real practice: a lawyer is handed a file and authority, not a subject outline. Graders reward the habits that make a lawyer useful: follow the instruction, read closely, use authority honestly and write clearly.

@example
title: Extracting a rule and sorting facts
problem: Hypothetical library for the State of Alder. Alder Code (hypothetical) § 12: "A covenant not to compete in an employment contract is enforceable only to the extent it is reasonably necessary to protect the employer's legitimate business interest and is reasonable in duration and geographic scope." Task memo: write an objective memo on whether Bakery can enforce a covenant against Reed, a baker who worked at one bakery location for two years and now works for a competitor. File facts: (1) the covenant bars Reed from working for any bakery anywhere in the United States for five years; (2) Bakery has one location and sells only to walk-in customers within three miles; (3) Reed learned Bakery's recipes; (4) Reed's new job is a bakery in the same city; (5) Reed's favorite color is blue. Which facts matter and how?
step: Read the task memo: an objective memo, so you will analyze both sides honestly. The question is whether the covenant is enforceable.
step: Turn the statute into a checklist: legitimate business interest; reasonable duration; reasonable geographic scope; the covenant enforced only to the extent reasonably necessary.
step: Sort the facts. Fact (3) supports a legitimate interest in protecting recipes. Fact (4) shows direct competition in the same market. Facts (1) and (2) bear on scope: five years and nationwide far exceed a single-location business with a three-mile customer base. Fact (5) is noise.
step: Plan the outline: Issue 1, legitimate interest (recipes, local competition): likely yes; Issue 2, duration and geography: likely unreasonable as written; Issue 3, partial enforcement: because § 12 says enforceable "to the extent" necessary, ask whether a court may narrow the covenant. Assign times accordingly.

@mistakes
- Writing a persuasive brief when the memo asks for an objective memo, or the reverse.
- Using memorized real-world law instead of the library's rules.
- Quoting facts from the file without citing where they appear.
- Spending the whole reading period reading the library and starting to write with 20 minutes left.

@practice self
prompt: Hypothetical task (reading and planning, 15 minutes). Task memo: "Write an objective memo on whether Client may rescind a purchase of a used boat because Seller said it had 'no hull damage.'" Library: Alder Code § 31 (hypothetical): "A buyer may rescind a sale if the seller made a material misrepresentation of fact on which the buyer reasonably relied." Case: Pruitt v. Dune (Alder Sup. Ct., hypothetical): "A statement is a misrepresentation of fact if it asserts something that can be proved true or false; statements of opinion do not count unless the speaker has superior knowledge." File: (1) Seller told Client "no hull damage" in an email dated June 2. (2) Seller had a repair invoice for a hull patch dated last year. (3) Client had the boat inspected by a marine surveyor who did not check below the waterline. (4) Client bought the boat on June 10. List the issues, the rule for each, the key facts you will use, and a time plan for a 90-minute task.
solution: Model answer: Issues and rules: (1) Was "no hull damage" a misrepresentation of fact? Rule: Pruitt v. Dune: a statement is a factual misrepresentation if it asserts something provable true or false. Facts: the email of June 2 and the repair invoice show the statement was false and provable. (2) Was it material? Facts: a hull is central to a boat's value and safety. (3) Did Client reasonably rely? Facts: Client bought eight days after the email and hired a surveyor; the surveyor did not check below the waterline, which Seller will use to argue lack of reliance or lack of diligence. Conclusion to plan: rescission is likely available. Time plan: 35 minutes reading and outlining, 5 minutes for the heading structure, 45 minutes writing (about 13 minutes for each of three sections and about 6 for a short facts statement), 5 minutes review, for 90 in all.
rubric: Identifies all three issues from the statute: misrepresentation of fact, materiality and reasonable reliance.
rubric: Matches each issue to the correct library authority and file facts, including the invoice and the surveyor.
rubric: Notes the adverse fact (limited inspection) that the memo must address honestly.
rubric: Gives a realistic time plan totaling 90 minutes or less.
hint: Break the statute into elements and match a fact to each.

@quiz num
prompt: A performance test is 90 minutes. You reserve 5 minutes for review. You want your writing time to be exactly 1.5 times your reading time. How many minutes should you spend reading?
answer: 34
check: (90 - 5) / (1 + 1.5)
hint: Let $r$ be reading time. Then $r + 1.5r$ equals the time left after review.
solution: $r + 1.5r = 85$, so $2.5r = 85$ and $r = 34$ minutes.
