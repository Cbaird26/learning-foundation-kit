@article
An essay is a short document with a job: show a grader, in about 30 minutes, that you can find the issues, state the law and use the facts. The structure that does this fastest is **IRAC**, repeated once per issue.

### Budget the 30 minutes

A workable split for a legacy MEE essay is about **4 minutes** to read the call and facts and outline, **22 to 23 minutes** to write, and **3 minutes** to review. If you finish early, add application, not extra rules. Under the legacy UBE you have six essays in three hours, so there is no time to carry over. Practice the split until it is automatic. These are suggestions to adapt, not rules from NCBE.

### Step one: read the call first

The call of the question is the sentence at the end ("What claims can Pat bring against Dee?"). It tells you which parties, which claims and which defenses matter. Read it first, then read the facts with a pen, marking facts that match elements. Many bar facts are planted for an element: a time, a price, a relationship, a statement.

### Step two: outline by issue

List each issue in the order a legal analysis would follow. For a claim, begin with the claim and then walk elements, then defenses. For several parties, group by party. A tight outline is a heading and three or four fact-words, for example: *Respondeat superior: employee? scope? detour*. Do not outline the rule in full; you know it.

### Step three: write each issue as an IRAC unit

1. **Heading.** One line naming the issue and, ideally, the parties ("Cal's negligence claim against Ben").
2. **Rule.** Two sentences or fewer: the rule and its elements, with the source if the doctrine has a name or governing code ("Under UCC Article 2, ..."). Mention a majority or minority variation only when it matters to the facts.
3. **Application.** The longest part. For each element, quote or paraphrase the fact and explain why it satisfies or fails the element: "because ...", "so ...". Argue both sides briefly when the issue is close.
4. **Conclusion.** One sentence that follows from your application.

### Make it easy to score

Use short paragraphs, one per issue, with the heading in bold or underlined. Use the vocabulary of the rule ("duty", "offer", "bona fide purchaser"). Never leave an issue's conclusion off. If you run short on time, write a brief but complete IRAC for each remaining issue instead of a long one for one issue.

### Common structure for multi-party facts

Name every party the call asks about, in order, and say who has a claim against whom. Then separate **liability** from **defenses** and **remedies**. A cluttered answer that mixes them hides points.

### Why the structure helps

IRAC forces the connection between rule and fact that point sheets reward. It also protects you from a common failure under time: reciting the law you know best at the expense of the issues the facts raise.

### Self-grading with a model answer

After a timed practice essay, compare your answer to a model answer. Do not ask whether it looks like yours. Instead, list the issues the model covers and check which you missed, then check rules, then check whether your application used the same facts. Record the pattern (missed issue, vague rule, thin application, ran out of time) because lesson 12 builds a repair plan from it.

@example
title: From call and facts to an outline
problem: Hypothetical facts: Ana owns a bakery and employs Ben as a delivery driver. On a Tuesday route, Ben ran a red light and hit Cal's car, injuring Cal. Ben was driving the bakery van and carrying Ana's cakes. The call: "What claims can Cal bring, and against whom?" Prepare a four-line outline.
step: Read the call: Cal's claims and the parties are Ben and Ana. That tells you the claims are negligence against Ben and a vicarious-liability claim against Ana.
step: Outline issue one: negligence against Ben. Duty (driver's duty of reasonable care), breach (ran a red light, which is also negligence per se in most states if the statute protects this class of person against this harm), causation, damages (injuries).
step: Outline issue two: respondeat superior against Ana. Ben was an employee (hired as a driver); the tort was within the scope of employment (on a delivery route with Ana's cakes). An employer is vicariously liable for torts an employee commits within the scope of employment.
step: Allocate time: after about 4 minutes spent reading and outlining, give about 10 minutes to each issue, then 3 minutes for a short note on remedies (compensatory damages) and 3 for the review pass, which uses all 30 minutes. The outline is four lines: Ben negligence (duty, breach, cause, damages); Ana respondeat superior (employee, scope); damages; conclusion.

@mistakes
- Starting to write before reading the call of the question.
- Writing a full paragraph of rules for every issue and leaving too little time for application.
- Writing one long paragraph so the grader cannot find your issue headings.
- Finishing with a "winner" and no reasoning for close issues.

@practice self
prompt: Timed essay 1 (30 minutes; hypothetical facts). On Monday, Ana emailed Ben: "I will sell you my used delivery van for \$12,000. This offer stays open until Friday at 5 p.m." Ana is not a merchant in vans. On Wednesday, Ana sold the van to Cal for \$12,500 and told Dee, a mutual friend of Ana and Ben, who immediately told Ben. On Thursday morning, Ben emailed Ana: "I accept." Ana refuses to deliver the van to Ben. Can Ben enforce a contract against Ana? Write your answer in 30 minutes, then grade it against the model answer.
solution: Model answer: The sale of a used van is a sale of goods governed by UCC Article 2, but the UCC does not change the basic rule for revoking this offer. **Was Ana's offer irrevocable?** Under the common law, an offer is revocable at any time before acceptance unless supported by consideration as an option contract. The UCC's firm-offer rule (UCC § 2-205) makes an offer irrevocable without consideration, but only if made by a merchant in a signed writing that assures it will be held open. Ana is not a merchant in vans, so the firm-offer rule does not apply, and Ben gave nothing for her promise to hold the offer open. The offer was therefore revocable despite the Friday deadline. **Was the offer revoked?** Revocation ordinarily must be communicated to the offeree before acceptance. Ana did not tell Ben herself. But under the common-law rule of *Dickinson v. Dodds* (1876), an offeree who learns from a reliable source that the offeror has sold the subject matter to another person has received notice, which operates as revocation. Dee, a mutual friend, told Ben on Wednesday that Ana had sold the van to Cal. That is a reliable source, and the sale is inconsistent with continuing the offer, so the offer was revoked on Wednesday. **Was there an effective acceptance?** Ben's Thursday email came after revocation, so there was no offer left to accept. Conclusion: no contract exists, and Ben cannot enforce one against Ana. If Ben had accepted before learning of the sale, there would have been no revocation, because a revocation takes effect only when the offeree receives it or learns of it from a reliable source, and a contract would have formed.
rubric: Identifies that the offer was revocable because Ana is not a merchant and Ben gave no consideration (no option, no UCC firm offer).
rubric: Applies indirect revocation through a reliable source (Dee) and the sale to Cal.
rubric: Concludes that the Thursday acceptance came too late and no contract exists.
rubric: Uses issue headings and keeps the rules to one or two sentences each.
hint: First ask whether the offer could be revoked; then whether it was revoked; then whether acceptance was effective.

@quiz self
prompt: Timed essay 2 (30 minutes; hypothetical facts). Owner conveyed Blackacre to Ana by a deed that Ana did not record. Ana moved onto Blackacre and lives there. Later, Owner conveyed Blackacre to Ben, who paid fair value, had never heard of Ana, and recorded his deed the next day. Ana then recorded her deed. Ben never visited Blackacre before buying. Who has title to Blackacre? Discuss under each of the three types of recording act (pure race, pure notice, and race-notice). Write your answer in 30 minutes, then grade it against the model answer.
solution: Model answer: **Framework.** Owner conveyed to Ana first and then to Ben, so the issue is which grantee prevails under the recording act. A prior unrecorded deed is valid between the parties, but a later purchaser may take priority under the recording act. **Pure race jurisdiction.** The first deed to be recorded wins regardless of notice. Ben recorded first, so Ben has title. **Pure notice jurisdiction.** A subsequent purchaser for value without notice prevails over an earlier unrecorded conveyance, whether or not the purchaser records first. Ben paid fair value, so he gave value. The question is notice. He had no actual notice of Ana's deed, and Ana's deed was not recorded when Ben bought, so he had no record notice. But Ana was living on Blackacre, and a purchaser is charged with inquiry notice of what a reasonable inspection of the land would reveal. Ben never visited. Possession by Ana would have put a reasonable buyer on inquiry, so Ben is charged with notice and is not a bona fide purchaser. Ana prevails. **Race-notice jurisdiction.** The subsequent purchaser must be a purchaser for value without notice and must record first. Ben recorded first and paid value, but the notice analysis is the same as in a pure notice state: Ana's open possession gave him inquiry notice. Ana prevails. **Conclusion.** Ben wins in a pure race state; Ana wins in pure notice and race-notice states because Ben is charged with inquiry notice from her possession.
rubric: States the three types of recording act accurately.
rubric: Recognizes Ana's possession as a source of inquiry notice for Ben.
rubric: Reaches the correct result under each type (Ben in pure race; Ana in notice and race-notice).
rubric: Uses a heading and a conclusion for each of the three jurisdictional approaches.
hint: Recall what each type of act requires of a subsequent purchaser.
