### What is known about NextGen

The **NextGen Uniform Bar Examination** was first given in July 2026 in ten jurisdictions. More jurisdictions join in July 2027 and nearly all UBE jurisdictions are scheduled to switch by July 2028. The legacy UBE (MBE, MEE and MPT) runs through February 2028. Your jurisdiction decides which exam you take and when, so confirm that first.

NCBE describes NextGen as testing **foundational legal concepts and principles** together with **lawyering skills**, assessed through **integrated question sets** (a scenario followed by a series of related questions) and **performance tasks**. Skills NCBE has identified include legal research, legal writing, issue spotting and analysis, investigation and evaluation, client counseling and advising, and client relationship and management. **Treat any format detail you read here, in a prep course or online as provisional.** Check NCBE's current content scope, item descriptions, sample questions, the exam schedule and scoring information, and your jurisdiction's own rules, before you finalize any study plan. The exercises in this lesson are original practice designed to build the underlying skills. They are not NCBE questions and do not predict NCBE's exact format.

### Why integrated sets feel different

On the legacy exam, a subject-specific question tests one rule. In an integrated set the same facts can raise issues from several subjects and ask you to act as a lawyer would: analyze, advise, draft or decide what to do next. That mirrors practice. Clients bring facts, not subject labels.

### A method for any integrated set

1. **Identify your role and the task.** Are you advising the client, evaluating a case, reviewing a document or preparing for a hearing? Read the instructions on what the questions ask before the facts.
2. **Skim the questions first, then read the scenario.** Know what to look for. Note the subjects each question touches.
3. **Mark the legally significant facts**: dates, parties, relationships, what was said and in writing, amounts, procedure.
4. **For each question, state the governing rule in your head** before looking at the answer choices or writing a response. Then test the choices against the rule.
5. **Distinguish a question about the law from a question about the next step.** For "What should you advise?", consider the client's goals, risks and costs, ethical limits (confidentiality, candor, conflicts) and what the law allows. The best choice is accurate, responsive and protective of the client.
6. **Do not import facts.** Use what the scenario gives. If a fact you want is missing, the answer that depends on it is probably wrong or at least not the best.

### Short answers

If a question asks for a short written response, give a conclusion, the rule in one sentence and the controlling fact, in a few sentences (check NCBE's sample questions for the expected length). That is IRAC compressed, and the conclusion should come first.

### Cross-subject issues that recur

- A contract dispute that raises the statute of frauds, parol evidence and remedies.
- An injury with agency (respondeat superior), negligence and comparative fault.
- A criminal stop that raises Fourth Amendment questions, evidence admissibility and the lawyer's next step.
- A business owner with partnership or corporate liability questions and a civil procedure question about where to sue.

Keep your rule statements ready across these overlaps. Core rules matter more than obscure ones.

### Client-counseling questions

When asked what to do, avoid options that are unethical (concealing evidence, advising fraud), that exceed the lawyer's role (deciding the client's objectives for her), or that ignore a deadline. Usually the strongest answer collects missing information, preserves rights (such as meeting a limitation period) and explains risks.

### Why integration

Licensing boards want evidence that a new lawyer can combine knowledge with skills. Integrated sets compress that into an exam that tests reading, reasoning and judgment at once.

@example
title: One scenario, several issues
problem: Hypothetical set. Seller, a merchant, orally agreed to sell Buyer, also a merchant, equipment for \$20,000. Seller emailed Buyer a signed message confirming the terms and quantity. Buyer did not reply. Two weeks later, Buyer said he never agreed and refused to pay. You represent Seller. Question: Can Seller overcome a statute-of-frauds defense?
step: Identify the role and the call: you represent Seller, and the question is about the statute of frauds, which governs a sale of goods of \$500 or more under UCC § 2-201.
step: State the rule: a sale of goods for \$500 or more needs a writing signed by the party to be charged. Between merchants, a written confirmation signed by the sender satisfies the statute against the recipient unless the recipient gives written objection within 10 days of receipt.
step: Apply it: Seller's signed confirmation was sent to Buyer, a merchant, and Buyer did not object in writing within 10 days (he waited two weeks and objected orally).
step: Conclude: the statute of frauds defense is removed. But the merchant's confirmation rule does not prove that the oral contract was made, so Seller still has to prove an agreement exists. Advise Seller to gather evidence of the oral deal.

@mistakes
- Reading the scenario in full before reading the question, and then not knowing which facts mattered.
- Choosing an answer because it states a true rule that does not answer the question asked.
- Treating a client-counseling question as a pure law question and missing the ethics or deadline point.
- Relying on a study guide's description of NextGen format instead of NCBE's current materials.

@practice mc
prompt: A delivery driver for Cafe Co. took a short detour of two blocks to buy lunch while making a delivery in the company van. While making the detour, the driver ran a stop sign and injured Pat. Pat asks whom she should sue. Which advice is best?
choice: Sue only the driver, because an employer is never liable for an employee's torts.
choice: Sue only Cafe Co., because an employee is immune when working.
choice*: Sue both: the driver for negligence, and Cafe Co. on respondeat superior, because a minor detour while on a delivery is likely within the scope of employment.
choice: Sue neither, because a detour for lunch is a frolic that ends the employment.
hint: Distinguish a minor detour from a substantial departure.
solution: The driver is liable for negligent driving. An employer is vicariously liable for torts within the scope of employment, and a minor detour is generally treated as within the scope, unlike a substantial frolic. Suing both is proper.

@practice self
prompt: Mini integrated set (hypothetical practice item; about 10 minutes). Scenario: You represent Maya, who owns a food truck. On May 1, Maya orally agreed with Lot Co. that she could park on its lot for \$900 per month starting June 1. Lot Co. later e-mailed her a signed lease summary stating the monthly rent and a one-year term. Maya did not reply. On June 15, Lot Co. told her she would have to leave. She asks you (1) whether the lease is enforceable, and (2) what she should do now. Questions: (a) Name the law issue the facts raise for an oral agreement for a one-year lease starting a month later. (b) Answer in three sentences. (c) State the best next step for Maya.
solution: Model answer: (a) The issue is the statute of frauds. (b) A contract that cannot be performed within one year of its making must be in writing signed by the party to be charged, and many states also require a writing for a lease of land longer than one year. The agreement was made on May 1 for a one-year term starting June 1, so performance cannot be completed within one year of May 1, and the statute applies. Lot Co. is the party Maya would charge, and its signed e-mail states the rent and the term, so it likely satisfies the writing requirement; Maya's failure to reply does not matter because only the signature of the party to be charged is required. (c) Next step: preserve the e-mail, confirm the governing statute in the jurisdiction, and send Lot Co. a written demand that it honor the lease; if Maya will lose her business without the lot, consider seeking injunctive relief, and check any deadlines. Do not advise her to ignore the notice.
rubric: Identifies the statute of frauds as the issue and states the rule for leases.
rubric: Applies Lot Co.'s signed e-mail as the writing signed by the party to be charged.
rubric: Gives a practical next step (preserve the e-mail, demand performance) without importing facts.
rubric: Notes that the governing statute should be confirmed for the jurisdiction.
hint: First decide whether a writing is needed, then whether the facts supply one.

@quiz mc
prompt: In an integrated set, you represent a client who asks whether to sign a settlement that releases all claims for \$10,000. You notice that the statute of limitations on her strongest claim expires in two weeks and the other side has not mentioned it. Which next step is best?
choice: Tell the client to sign immediately, because a deadline favors settling.
choice: Say nothing about the deadline, because the other side did not raise it.
choice*: Advise the client of the deadline and her options, including preserving the claim by filing or negotiating a tolling agreement before deciding whether to settle.
choice: File suit without telling the client, to protect her.
hint: A lawyer must keep the client informed and protect her rights.
solution: A lawyer must communicate material information and let the client decide the objectives. Preserving the claim (by filing or tolling) protects her rights while she decides. Hiding the deadline or filing without consulting her violates duties to the client.
