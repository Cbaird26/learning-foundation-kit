@article
A full session teaches things no single essay can: how your pace changes after two hours, how one bad answer affects the next and whether your plan survives fatigue. This lesson is about running a session and learning from it. The project in weeks 13 and 14 is a session of this kind.

### Set up the session

Under the legacy UBE, a written day is a **three-hour essay session** (six 30-minute essays) and a **three-hour performance-test session** (two 90-minute tasks). A full written practice can be one of the sessions (three hours) or both, with a break between them as your jurisdiction allows. Choose your scenario:

- **Condition.** Same start time as the real exam, same device (keyboard or hand), no notes, no phone, no pausing the clock.
- **Materials.** Use original or officially released practice items you have not seen. (This course's items are original and are not NCBE material.)
- **Timing.** Write the end time of each essay or task before you begin and follow the checkpoint plan from lesson 9.
- **If you are preparing for NextGen,** run the same session design with integrated sets and tasks, using NCBE's current sample materials and the timing and formats that NCBE publishes.

### Grade the session properly

Wait until you have finished all items, then grade the next day with a clear head. For each answer:

1. Compare your **issues** to the model answer's. Mark each missed issue.
2. Compare your **rules**. Mark rules that were missing, inaccurate or incomplete (for example, missing an element).
3. Check **application**: did you tie facts to each element, or only state conclusions?
4. Check the **conclusion** and its reasoning.
5. Check **organization and time**: headings, order, whether the last issues were rushed.

Then assign a score on the scale your jurisdiction uses, or a simple 0 to 6, and compare with how you felt. Many candidates underrate answers that were well organized and overrate answers where they wrote down a lot of law.

### The error log

For each lost point, record: subject, issue, **error type** and cause. Use categories like these:

- **Missed issue** (did not see it).
- **Rule error** (misstated or incomplete).
- **Thin application** (rule without facts).
- **Conclusion or reasoning error.**
- **Time or format** (ran out of time, wrong document, wrong structure).

Patterns matter more than any one miss. Ten missed evidence issues, for example, tell you to review the hearsay framework; a spread of thin applications tells you to practice applying facts and not to study more rules.

### A worked example of the rules in an evidence essay

Evidence essays often bundle several recurring issues. Be ready with the Federal Rules of Evidence framework: **hearsay** is an out-of-court statement offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted (FRE 801(c)) and is inadmissible (FRE 802) unless an exclusion or exception applies. A statement by a party's employee on a matter within the scope of the employment relationship, made while it existed, is excluded from hearsay when offered against the party (FRE 801(d)(2)(D)). An **excited utterance**, a statement relating to a startling event made while under its stress, is an exception regardless of availability (FRE 803(2)). **Subsequent remedial measures**, steps taken after an injury that would have made the injury less likely, are not admissible to prove negligence or culpable conduct (FRE 407), but may be admitted for other purposes such as impeachment or, if disputed, ownership, control or feasibility.

### A civil procedure refresher for timed essays

A federal court has **diversity jurisdiction** under 28 U.S.C. § 1332 when the amount in controversy exceeds \$75,000 and the parties are completely diverse (no plaintiff shares citizenship with any defendant). A corporation is a citizen of every state where it is incorporated and the state of its principal place of business. A court without subject-matter jurisdiction must dismiss, but a nondiverse party who is not indispensable may be dropped under FRCP 21 to preserve jurisdiction. Joint tortfeasors are not required parties under FRCP 19 (*Temple v. Synthes* (1990)).

### After the session

Do not review the model answers during the session. Afterward, redo the one or two weakest answers within 48 hours without looking at the model, then compare again. A repair that works shows up as a better answer, not a better feeling.

### Policy

The licensing exam samples a candidate's ability across subjects under pressure. A session mirrors that sampling, and the error log turns it into a study plan.

@practice self
prompt: Timed essay (30 minutes; hypothetical facts). Pat slipped on a wet floor in Store's aisle and sued Store for negligence. At trial, Pat offers (1) testimony by Cy, a shopper, that immediately after Pat fell, Cy shouted at the scene, "I told the manager an hour ago this floor was wet!" and (2) testimony from Pat that the manager later said, "We should have mopped that sooner." The manager's job includes aisle safety. Store also mopped the aisle the next day. Pat offers evidence of the mopping to prove negligence. Discuss the admissibility of each item under the Federal Rules of Evidence. Write your answer in 30 minutes, then grade it against the model answer.
solution: Model answer: **(1) Cy's shout.** The shout is an out-of-court statement offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted (that the floor was wet an hour earlier and the manager was told), so it is hearsay under FRE 801(c) and inadmissible under FRE 802 unless an exception applies. The excited-utterance exception, FRE 803(2), covers a statement relating to a startling event made while under the stress of excitement. Cy shouted immediately after seeing Pat fall, which suggests the stress of the event. The statement partly describes the event and partly reports an earlier warning, but courts generally allow such a statement if it relates to the startling event and was made under its stress. It is likely admissible. Cy must have personal knowledge, and Cy appears to have it as the speaker. **(2) The manager's statement.** It is offered for its truth against Store. The manager is Store's employee, the statement concerns aisle safety, within the scope of employment, and was made while the employment existed, so it is not hearsay under FRE 801(d)(2)(D). It is admissible against Store, although Store may argue it is an opinion about what should have been done; the rule permits it as an opposing party's statement. **(3) The mopping.** Mopping after the injury is a subsequent remedial measure. FRE 407 bars it to prove negligence or culpable conduct. It may be admissible for another purpose, such as impeachment or, if Store disputes it, control or feasibility, but Pat offers it to prove negligence, so it is inadmissible. **Conclusion.** Cy's shout and the manager's statement are likely admissible; the mopping is not, for the purpose offered.
rubric: Identifies hearsay and applies the excited-utterance exception to Cy's shout.
rubric: Applies FRE 801(d)(2)(D) to the manager's statement.
rubric: Applies FRE 407 to the mopping and notes the limited other purposes.
rubric: States a conclusion for each of the three items with a reason.
hint: Take the items in order and ask first whether each is hearsay.

@quiz self
prompt: Timed essay (30 minutes; hypothetical facts). Pat, a citizen of State A, sued Store Corp. and Manager in federal court for \$200,000 for negligence under state law. Store Corp. is incorporated in State B with its principal place of business in State C. Manager is a citizen of State A. Does the federal court have subject-matter jurisdiction, and what can Pat do? Write your answer in 30 minutes, then grade it against the model answer.
solution: Model answer: **Subject-matter jurisdiction.** The claim is a state-law negligence claim, so there is no federal question. The only basis is diversity under 28 U.S.C. § 1332, which requires an amount in controversy over \$75,000 and complete diversity. The amount is \$200,000, so that requirement is met. Store Corp. is a citizen of State B (incorporation) and State C (principal place of business), both different from State A, so it is diverse from Pat. But Manager is a citizen of State A, as is Pat, so there is no complete diversity and the court lacks jurisdiction over the case as filed. **Options.** Pat may drop Manager. Under FRCP 21, a court may dismiss a dispensable nondiverse party to preserve jurisdiction. Joint tortfeasors are not required parties under FRCP 19 (*Temple v. Synthes* (1990)), and Store Corp. can be sued alone, with Store Corp. possibly vicariously liable for Manager's negligence under respondeat superior. Dismissing Manager means Pat cannot recover from Manager in this suit. **Alternatively,** Pat may sue both in State A's state court, where state subject-matter jurisdiction exists. Store Corp. could not remove the case based on diversity because Manager defeats complete diversity. **Conclusion.** The court lacks diversity jurisdiction as filed. Pat should either drop Manager and proceed against Store Corp. in federal court, or sue both in state court.
rubric: Applies diversity jurisdiction: amount in controversy and complete diversity, including corporate citizenship.
rubric: Identifies that Manager destroys complete diversity.
rubric: Gives the options: drop Manager under FRCP 21 or sue in state court, with a reason.
rubric: States a clear conclusion.
hint: Test each party's citizenship against Pat's.
