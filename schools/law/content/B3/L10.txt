@article
### The steps to a license

A bar license requires more than a passing score. Requirements vary by jurisdiction, but the usual sequence is:

1. **Legal education.** In most jurisdictions, a J.D. from an ABA-accredited law school, certified by the dean or registrar.
2. **Application.** Filed with the jurisdiction's board of bar examiners (or equivalent) by a deadline that usually falls months before the exam, with fees and documents. Late fees apply and the final deadline is often strict.
3. **The MPRE.** The Multistate Professional Responsibility Examination is a separate ethics exam of 60 multiple-choice questions in two hours: 50 are scored and 10 are unscored pretest questions. Scores are scaled from 50 to 150, and each jurisdiction sets its own passing score (generally from 75 to 86). Nearly all jurisdictions require it. Confirm whether you must pass it before or after the bar exam.
4. **The bar exam.** The legacy UBE or NextGen UBE, or a jurisdiction's own exam. For the UBE, each jurisdiction sets its minimum score and its rules on transferring scores to other jurisdictions.
5. **Character and fitness review.** Discussed below.
6. **Oath and admission.** After passing the exam and being cleared on character and fitness, you take an oath and are admitted, often at a swearing-in ceremony.

Lawyers licensed elsewhere may be admitted in another jurisdiction by UBE score transfer or by admission on motion, which usually requires years of practice and character review. Check each jurisdiction's own rules.

### Character and fitness

Every jurisdiction examines whether an applicant has the **character and fitness** to practice law, because lawyers hold clients' money, secrets and trust. The applicant usually carries the burden of showing good character. A jurisdiction may investigate with its own staff or use investigation services, including those NCBE offers to some jurisdictions.

Applications commonly ask about:

- **Criminal history**: arrests, citations, charges and convictions, often including dismissed, sealed or expunged matters. Read each question exactly; many ask even about records that were expunged.
- **Academic and professional discipline**: honor-code violations in college or law school, discipline in any other profession.
- **Financial responsibility**: bankruptcy, unpaid judgments, defaulted debts, unpaid taxes and child support.
- **Employment history**: jobs held and reasons for leaving, including terminations.
- **Civil litigation and prior applications** to other bars.
- **Residence and references.**
- **Substance use and health**: Many jurisdictions have narrowed these questions in recent years to focus on conduct and fitness to practice, rather than diagnosis or treatment, in line with ABA guidance. Check your jurisdiction's current form.

### Candor above all

Boards treat **failure to disclose** more seriously than most underlying conduct. A youthful mistake that you report fully rarely ends a career; an omission or a false statement shows the quality the board fears most. Model Rule 8.1 forbids knowingly making a false statement of material fact, or failing to disclose a fact necessary to correct a known misapprehension, in a bar admission application. Practical rules:

- **Disclose when in doubt**, or ask the board in writing how to answer.
- **Be accurate.** Check dates, names and docket numbers against records. Request court records of old matters.
- **Be complete and consistent** across applications to different jurisdictions.
- **Update** the application if facts change before admission.
- **Explain briefly and honestly** in an addendum without minimizing or blaming others.

### Evidence of rehabilitation

When there is a record, boards often weigh: the seriousness and recency of the conduct, your age at the time, whether it was repeated, your candor with the board, acceptance of responsibility, restitution, and positive changes since: steady employment, community service, treatment or compliance where relevant, and letters from people who know the facts. Time without further problems is persuasive.

### Deadlines are part of the process

Count backward from the exam date and write each deadline down: application, fingerprints, dean's certification, accommodations, software, MPRE registration. Missing one can delay licensure a full sitting.

### Policy

Licensing is a protection for clients and courts. The process is intrusive because the profession needs to trust lawyers with confidences from the first day, and honesty with the board is the first test of that trust.

### Your own checklist

Build a personalized checklist: each requirement, its source in your jurisdiction's rules, its deadline, its status and the documents needed. Revisit it weekly.

@example
title: Working back from the exam date
problem: Hypothetical: a jurisdiction requires the application at least 90 days before the first day of the exam, which is July 28. The dean's certification must be filed 30 days after the application. What is the last day to apply, and the last day for the certification?
step: Count back 90 days from July 28. Back 28 days reaches June 30 with 62 days left. Back 30 more days (June) reaches May 31 with 32 left. Back 31 more days (May) reaches April 30 with 1 left, so one more day gives April 29.
step: State the application deadline: April 29 (90 days before July 28). Treat the date as a hard deadline and aim to file earlier.
step: The certification is due 30 days after the application. If you apply on April 29, the certification is due 30 days later: one day reaches April 30 and 29 more reach May 29.
step: Add the dates to the checklist and schedule your request to the registrar well before May 29.

@mistakes
- Omitting a dismissed or expunged matter because it feels irrelevant, when the question asks about it.
- Filling in dates from memory instead of court records.
- Treating the application deadline as the day the exam registration opens, rather than a fixed date months earlier.
- Assuming the MPRE passing score or the UBE minimum score is the same in every jurisdiction.
