### What NextGen performance tasks are

NCBE describes the NextGen exam as including **performance tasks** that assess lawyering skills, such as legal research, legal writing, investigation and evaluation, and client counseling, using materials supplied with the task. Like the MPT, they give you a scenario, a file and authorities, and you produce a work product. **Confirm in NCBE's current materials** how many tasks there are, their length and weight in scoring, the kinds of documents requested and how research materials are presented. Do not rely on this course or any prep provider for those details. The practice tasks here are original and are designed to build the skills, not to predict NCBE's format.

### Research inside a closed library

On a performance task the library is closed: the authority is in front of you. The research skill is not finding books but **selecting and ranking** what you have. Work in this order:

1. **Frame the question.** What must you decide, for whom and in what form? Write the question in one sentence.
2. **Identify the jurisdiction and the hierarchy.** The Constitution controls statutes, statutes control regulations and common law, and a higher court's decision binds lower courts in the same jurisdiction. Cases from other jurisdictions are persuasive only. Where a statute and a case conflict, the statute controls unless it is unconstitutional.
3. **Start with the governing statute or rule.** Break it into elements or factors. A case usually interprets a statute, so reading the statute first tells you what the case is for.
4. **Read the interpreting cases for the holding and the reasoning.** Extract the rule, the facts and the result. Ask which facts the court treated as decisive.
5. **Check currency.** A library may include subsequent history, amendments with effective dates or a note that a case was overruled or superseded. A case decided before an amendment that changed the rule is no longer good on that point. A case that has been reversed or overruled is not controlling. Never rely on an authority without checking its date and history.
6. **Synthesize.** Combine authorities into a single statement of the rule, then apply it to the file facts. If authorities conflict, explain which controls and why.
7. **Identify gaps.** Say what you could not determine from the library, and what facts you would need.

### Agencies and statutes after Loper Bright

If a library includes an agency's interpretation of a statute, remember the current rule: in *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo* (2024), the Supreme Court overruled *Chevron* and held that courts must exercise independent judgment about the best reading of a statute. An agency's view may receive respect under *Skidmore v. Swift & Co.* (1944) in proportion to its thoroughness and persuasiveness, but it does not bind the court. Earlier *Chevron*-based holdings remain in force under statutory stare decisis.

### Remedies and recent changes

When a task asks you to draft relief, tailor it. In *Trump v. CASA, Inc.* (2025), the Supreme Court held that universal injunctions likely exceed the equitable authority Congress gave federal courts, so relief should be limited to what is needed to give the plaintiffs complete relief. A request for relief that sweeps wider than the plaintiffs' injury invites an objection.

### Writing for the reader

A performance task may ask for a client letter, a short memo, a draft clause or a plan. Use the format and length the task states. In a **client letter**, answer the question in the first paragraph, explain the reasoning in plain language, identify the key facts and risks, and give next steps and deadlines. Avoid case citations unless the task asks, and do not overpromise. In a **memo to a supervisor**, state the answer, the authority and the weaknesses.

### Timing

A reasonable practice approach is to spend roughly one-third of the time reading and outlining and the rest writing, with a short check for dates, names and the question asked. Adjust the proportions to the task length that NCBE specifies.

### Why this skill is tested

Lawyers rarely recall the exact rule; they find it, verify that it is still good and explain it to someone who needs a decision. The performance task measures that sequence.

@example
title: Ranking a library by hierarchy and date
problem: Hypothetical library for the State of Alder. (1) Alder Code § 5 (amended effective January 1, 2020): a tenant who gives written notice and 14 days to repair may withhold rent. (2) Dole v. Pike (Alder Ct. App., 2015): the tenant's oral notice suffices. (3) Ames v. Rowe (Alder Sup. Ct., 2021): written notice is required, citing the 2020 amendment. (4) An out-of-state case holding that oral notice suffices. A client gave only oral notice. What controls?
step: Frame the question: whether oral notice allows the client to withhold rent in Alder.
step: Rank by hierarchy: the statute controls, and the Alder Supreme Court's decision binds the lower courts. The out-of-state case is persuasive only.
step: Check currency: Dole (2015) predates the 2020 amendment and Ames (2021) read the statute to require written notice, so Dole is no longer good on that point.
step: Conclude: under § 5 as amended and Ames, written notice is required, so the client's oral notice is likely insufficient, and the client should send written notice now.

@mistakes
- Treating an older case as controlling after a statute was amended or a later case changed the rule.
- Citing an out-of-state or lower-level case as if it binds the court.
- Writing a client letter in dense legal jargon or burying the answer on page two.
- Asking for relief broader than the plaintiffs' injury.

@practice self
prompt: Hypothetical performance task (about 20 minutes). Task: Write a short client letter (150 to 200 words) to Tia, your client, answering whether she can withhold November rent. Library: Alder Code § 44 (hypothetical): a residential tenant may withhold rent that comes due after the landlord has had 14 days, following written notice, to repair a defect that makes the unit uninhabitable. Oral notice does not start the 14 days. File: (1) The heat failed on October 1. (2) Tia phoned the landlord on October 2. (3) She sent written notice on October 20. (4) November rent was due on November 1 and the heat is still broken. (5) Today is November 5. Write the letter.
solution: Model answer: Dear Tia, Thank you for your question. I do not think you can safely withhold the November rent. Under the Alder statute, the 14 days a landlord has to repair begin only after written notice, and your phone call on October 2 does not count. Your written notice went out on October 20, so the 14 days ended on November 3. The November rent was due on November 1, before the 14 days had run, and the law lets you withhold only rent that comes due after that period. If you withhold November rent, the landlord could treat you as late. My advice: pay November rent now, keep a copy of your October 20 notice, and take dated photos and notes of the broken heat. If the heat is still not fixed, you may be able to withhold December rent, because it will come due after the 14 days. Please call me before you do so, so we can confirm the steps. Sincerely, Your attorney.
rubric: States the answer in the first lines and uses plain language.
rubric: Applies the written-notice rule and counts 14 days from October 20 (to November 3).
rubric: Explains that November rent was due before the period ended and gives practical next steps.
rubric: Stays within the library and does not promise an outcome.
hint: Count the 14 days from the written notice, then compare to the due date.

@quiz num
prompt: A client must send written notice, and the statute gives the landlord 14 days to repair after the notice. Written notice was sent on October 24. October has 31 days. On what day of November does the 14-day period end? Give the day number.
answer: 7
check: 24 + 14 - 31
