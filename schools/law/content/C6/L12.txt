@article
Statistical evidence reaches a court through an expert witness, and the rules of evidence decide whether the fact finder hears it.

### Admissibility: FRE 702

Under Federal Rule of Evidence 702, a qualified expert may testify in the form of an opinion if the proponent demonstrates that it is **more likely than not** that (a) the expert's specialized knowledge will help the trier of fact, (b) the testimony is based on sufficient facts or data, (c) it is the product of reliable principles and methods, and (d) the expert's opinion reflects a reliable application of those methods to the facts. The standard builds on *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals* (1993), which made the judge a gatekeeper of reliability and listed factors such as testing, peer review, known error rate and general acceptance; *General Electric Co. v. Joiner* (1997), which allows a court to find too great an analytical gap between data and opinion; and *Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael* (1999), which extended gatekeeping to all expert testimony. A 2023 amendment to Rule 702 made explicit that the proponent bears the burden of showing it is more likely than not that each requirement is met, and that the opinion must reflect a reliable application of the method. Courts also apply FRE 403 to exclude evidence whose probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice or confusion, which is the rule behind objections to statistical testimony that invites the prosecutor's fallacy. New FRE 107 (December 2024) governs illustrative aids, such as charts that summarize data, which are not themselves evidence.

### Forensic statistics and confrontation

The Confrontation Clause limits how an expert may relay what an absent analyst found. In *Smith v. Arizona* (2024) the Supreme Court held that when a testifying expert conveys an absent analyst's statements as the basis of an opinion, and the statements support the opinion only if true, they are offered for their truth, so the Clause applies to them if they are testimonial. Separately, national scientific reviews (the National Research Council's 2009 report and the President's Council of Advisors on Science and Technology's 2016 report) criticized the validation and error-rate evidence behind several forensic pattern-comparison methods, which is why error rates and the reporting of match statistics matter.

### Disparities in juries, hiring and classes

*Castaneda v. Partida* (1977) and *Hazelwood School District v. United States* (1977) treated a disparity of more than two or three standard deviations as a sign that selection was not random, provided the comparison was to the proper pool. In *McCleskey v. Kemp* (1987), the Court assumed that a large statistical study of death-sentencing was valid and held that it was not enough to establish an equal-protection violation or a violation of the Eighth Amendment in the defendant's own case; the opinion shows that proving the existence of a disparity differs from proving a legal violation. In *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes* (2011) the Court held that the plaintiffs' statistical and anecdotal evidence did not establish the common question required for class certification under Rule 23(a)(2), because it did not show a common decision or policy affecting all class members.

### A checklist for critiquing a statistical claim

1. **Transposed conditional:** is a probability of data given a hypothesis presented as a probability of the hypothesis?
2. **Population and sample:** who is in the data, how were they chosen, and is the comparison pool right?
3. **Size and significance:** is the effect large, and is the p-value or interval reported along with the sample size and the number of comparisons?
4. **Confounding and controls:** which variables are omitted, and is any control tainted?
5. **Assumptions:** independence, model form, discount rate, growth rate.
6. **Error rates and reproducibility:** can the calculation be recomputed from the data?

@example
title: Critiquing a statement and valuing a claim
problem: An expert says, "The probability that this pattern in the hiring data arose by chance is 1%, so there is a 99% probability the employer discriminated." Separately, a plaintiff's lost-earnings claim is worth \$40,000 per year at the end of each of $3$ years at a $5\%$ discount rate, and liability has probability $0.75$. Evaluate the statement and compute the expected present value.
step: The statement transposes the conditional. A p-value of $0.01$ means the data would be this extreme only 1% of the time if hiring were neutral. It is $P(\text{data}\mid H_0)$, not $P(\text{discrimination}\mid\text{data})$, and it says nothing about intent or the cause of the pattern.
step: A defensible statement would be: "If hiring probabilities were equal, a gap this large would occur about 1% of the time," followed by the size of the gap, the pool used, and any confounders.
step: Present value of the lost earnings: $40{,}000 \times \frac{1 - 1.05^{-3}}{0.05} = 40{,}000 \times 2.7232 \approx 108{,}930$ dollars.
step: Expected present value at $0.75$: $0.75 \times 108{,}930 \approx 81{,}697$ dollars.
step: Both parts turn on assumptions the other side may challenge: the discount rate and work-life period in the damages model, and the comparison pool and control variables in the disparity analysis. A good report states them and shows how the answer changes.

@mistakes
- Treating a p-value as the probability that the defendant is innocent or guilty.
- Assuming that a large statistical disparity automatically establishes a legal violation: the legal standard and the proper comparison pool also matter.
- Overstating what the leading cases hold: the rules require reliability and a proper comparison, not a fixed number of standard deviations in every case.
- Presenting a model's output without the assumptions that would let the other side recompute it.

@practice self
prompt: A prosecution expert states: "The probability that this DNA profile would match an innocent person is one in a million. Therefore the probability that the defendant is innocent is one in a million." In a short paragraph (a) name the error, (b) explain what additional information is needed to compute the probability that the defendant is the source, and (c) give a more accurate way to state the evidence.
hint: Write the expert's figure as a conditional probability $P(\cdot\mid\cdot)$ and compare it with the probability the expert reports.
solution: (a) This is the prosecutor's fallacy, a transposed conditional: one in a million is $P(\text{match}\mid\text{not the source})$, but the statement reports it as $P(\text{not the source}\mid\text{match})$. (b) By Bayes' theorem, the probability that the defendant is the source also depends on the prior probability, such as the number of people who could have been the source and the other evidence against the defendant, and on how the defendant was identified (for example, a database search). With a pool of about a million and one equally likely sources and no other evidence, the posterior would be about one half. (c) A more accurate statement: "A person who is not the source would match about one time in a million; the match is about a million times more likely if the defendant is the source than if an unrelated person is," leaving the fact finder to combine this with the other evidence.
rubric: Identifies the prosecutor's fallacy or transposed conditional.
rubric: Explains that the posterior depends on the prior, the pool size or other evidence, and the search method.
rubric: Offers an accurate restatement, such as a likelihood ratio or a statement that only the match probability is one in a million.
rubric: Does not claim the random match probability is meaningless or that the defendant is necessarily guilty.
