@article
Courts and agencies often receive survey evidence: consumers surveyed about whether two brands are confusingly similar, residents surveyed about a change of venue, employees surveyed about workplace conditions. A survey speaks about a **population** (everyone of interest) through a **sample** (those actually asked). Whether it can be trusted depends almost entirely on how the sample was chosen.

### Probability samples

In a **probability sample** every member of the population has a known, nonzero chance of selection, which lets us measure sampling error.

- **Simple random sample (SRS):** every group of $n$ members is equally likely to be chosen, as if drawn from a hat.
- **Systematic sample:** choose a random start and take every $k$th member, where $k = N/n$.
- **Stratified sample:** split the population into homogeneous groups (**strata**), then sample within each. In **proportional allocation** the sample from stratum $h$ is $n \times N_h/N$. Stratifying guarantees representation and often improves precision.
- **Cluster sample:** randomly choose whole groups (courtrooms, city blocks) and survey everyone in them. It is cheap but usually less precise than an SRS of the same size.

### Non-probability samples

A **convenience sample** (whoever is easy to reach) and a **voluntary-response sample** (people choose to respond, such as call-in polls and online ratings) have no known selection probabilities. They overrepresent people with strong opinions. Their margins of error are meaningless, because the main error is bias, not chance.

### Sources of bias

- **Selection (coverage) bias:** the list from which you sample omits part of the population, such as a telephone survey that skips people without listed numbers.
- **Nonresponse bias:** people who respond differ from those who do not. A large sample does not fix it.
- **Response bias:** answers are inaccurate, because of leading or loaded questions, a poor order of questions, or social pressure to give the "right" answer.

A big sample size reduces chance variation but never removes bias. A huge biased sample is a precise estimate of the wrong thing.

### Survey evidence in court

Under the Federal Rules of Evidence, a survey is usually attacked on weight more than admissibility: courts look at whether the population was properly defined, the sample drawn by accepted methods, the questions neutral and the analysis transparent. A well-designed survey carries more weight, and a flawed one may be excluded under FRE 702 if the expert cannot show it rests on reliable methods applied reliably.

@example
title: Proportional allocation and the nonresponse trap
problem: A company has $10{,}000$ employees: $6{,}000$ in the plant, $3{,}000$ in offices and $1{,}000$ in management. An investigator wants a stratified sample of $200$ with proportional allocation. Separately, a mailed survey sent to $1{,}000$ people drew $400$ replies. Why can the replies mislead even if $75\%$ of repliers agree?
step: Proportional allocation: sample size for each stratum $= 200 \times N_h/10{,}000$.
step: Plant: $200 \times 0.6 = 120$. Offices: $200 \times 0.3 = 60$. Management: $200 \times 0.1 = 20$. Check: $120 + 60 + 20 = 200$.
step: Response rate: $400/1000 = 40\%$. The $600$ nonrespondents are the problem: if only $25\%$ of them agree, the whole group's agreement is $(300 + 150)/1000 = 45\%$, not $75\%$.
step: The $75\%$ describes the repliers, not the population. When the nonresponse rate is high, the estimate can be wrong by a lot, however many replies arrive.

@mistakes
- Believing a larger sample always cures bias: it only reduces chance error.
- Calling a call-in or online-click poll a random sample because many people responded.
- Treating a cluster sample like an SRS when computing precision.
- Using the stratum's share of the sample rather than its share of the population when allocating proportionally.

@practice num
prompt: A school has $2400$ students, of whom $1500$ are undergraduates. A stratified sample of $160$ students uses proportional allocation. How many undergraduates should be sampled?
answer: 100
check: 160 * 1500 / 2400
hint: The undergraduate share of the population is $1500/2400$.
hint: Multiply that share by $160$.
solution: $160 \times \frac{1500}{2400} = 160 \times 0.625 = 100$.

answer: 20
check: 1200 / 60

@practice mc
prompt: A local news site asks readers to click a link to vote on whether a proposed ordinance is fair. Of $4000$ voters, $82\%$ say it is unfair. An advocate says the poll proves that 82% of the town opposes the ordinance. What is the best criticism?
choice*: It is a voluntary-response sample; people with strong feelings choose to respond, so it cannot support a claim about the town.
choice: The sample size of 4000 is too small to say anything about any town.
choice: The poll is a stratified sample, and stratified samples are never reliable.
choice: The poll is biased only because the news site should have used a larger percentage.
hint: Who decides whether a person is in the sample?
solution: Because respondents select themselves, the sample has no known selection probabilities and overrepresents motivated people. Size cannot fix that.

@practice num
prompt: A mailed survey of $1000$ tenants receives $400$ replies, $300$ of which say the landlord is responsive. Suppose that among the $600$ tenants who did not reply, exactly $25\%$ would have said the landlord is responsive. What fraction of all $1000$ tenants would say the landlord is responsive? Give an exact fraction.
answer: 9/20
check: (300 + 0.25 * 600) / 1000
hint: Count the responsive tenants among the nonrespondents.
hint: $25\%$ of $600$ is $150$. Add the replies from the responders.
solution: $(300 + 150)/1000 = 450/1000 = 9/20$. The repliers' $75\%$ greatly overstates the whole group's $45\%$.

@practice ms
prompt: Select all methods that produce a probability sample.
choice*: A simple random sample drawn from a complete list of the population.
choice*: A stratified sample that draws a random sample within each stratum.
choice*: A cluster sample that randomly selects courtrooms and surveys every juror in them.
choice: A convenience sample of the first 100 people who walk into the courthouse.
choice: An online poll that anyone may answer.
hint: A probability sample needs known, nonzero chances of selection for each member.
solution: Random selection from a list, within strata, or of clusters gives known selection probabilities. Whoever happens by, or whoever chooses to answer, does not.

@quiz num
prompt: A researcher randomly chooses $5$ of $40$ courtrooms in a county and surveys all $30$ jurors who served in each chosen courtroom. How many jurors are in the sample?
answer: 150
check: 5 * 30
hint: A cluster sample surveys everyone in each chosen cluster.
hint: Multiply the number of chosen courtrooms by the jurors per courtroom.
solution: $5 \times 30 = 150$ jurors. The sampling unit is the courtroom, not the juror.

@quiz mc
prompt: A trademark survey asks respondents, "Don't you agree that the defendant's logo is clearly a copy of the plaintiff's famous mark?" Which flaw is most directly present?
choice*: Response bias from a leading question that pushes respondents toward agreement.
choice: Coverage bias because the survey uses a stratified design.
choice: Nonresponse bias because the question has too few words.
choice: No flaw, because a leading question reduces sampling error.
hint: Look at how the question's wording steers the answer.
solution: A leading question builds the desired answer into the wording, which produces response bias. A neutral question would ask what the respondent thinks of the logo without suggesting a reply.
