@article
Week 7 asked, "Is the disparity unlikely under chance?" Week 8 asks, "How big is it, and does it matter?" These are different questions.

### Statistical significance depends on sample size

For a fixed difference in rates, the standard error shrinks as the samples grow, so $z$ grows. With enormous samples, a tiny difference becomes "statistically significant" ($p < 0.05$) even if no one would care. With tiny samples, a large and important difference may not reach significance. **Statistical significance** answers whether an effect is distinguishable from sampling noise. **Practical significance** (importance) is a judgment about magnitude in context.

### Effect sizes

Report the size of the effect directly:

- the **difference** in rates, in percentage points;
- the **impact ratio** (also called the adverse impact ratio), the lower group's selection rate divided by the higher group's;
- for averages, a **standardized effect size** such as $d = (\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2)/s$, the difference measured in standard deviations.

Under the federal Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures, enforcement agencies generally regard a selection rate for a group below four-fifths (80%) of the rate for the highest group as evidence of adverse impact. This **four-fifths rule** is a rule of thumb for enforcement, not a legal definition: it is not a safe harbor, and courts often look at statistical significance as well. In disparate-impact cases under Title VII, following *Griggs v. Duke Power Co.* (1971), the plaintiff must show that a particular practice causes a significant disparity; the employer may then show that the practice is job related and consistent with business necessity.

### The multiple-comparisons problem

If you run $m$ independent tests at $\alpha = 0.05$ and every null hypothesis is true, the chance of at least one false positive is $1-(0.95)^m$, which is $64\%$ for $m=20$. Searching many subgroups, years or job titles until something is significant manufactures findings. A **Bonferroni correction** tests each hypothesis at $\alpha/m$ to control the overall error rate. Honest reports say how many comparisons were made.

### Confidence intervals help

An interval for the difference, such as $(0.5, 14.0)$ percentage points, shows both whether zero is plausible and how large the gap could be. It tells the reader more than a p-value alone.

### In court

Statistical evidence is weighed along with the rest of the case. A court may find a statistically significant disparity too small to matter, or a large disparity not significant for lack of data. Experts should report both.

@example
title: Significant but tiny
problem: An employer hires $50{,}000$ of $100{,}000$ applicants from Group A ($50.0\%$) and $49{,}500$ of $100{,}000$ from Group B ($49.5\%$). Is the difference statistically significant at $\alpha = 0.05$? Is it practically significant?
step: Pooled proportion: $(50{,}000 + 49{,}500)/200{,}000 = 0.4975$. Standard error: $\sqrt{0.4975(0.5025)(2/100{,}000)} \approx 0.002236$.
step: Test statistic: $z = (0.500 - 0.495)/0.002236 \approx 2.24$, giving a two-sided p-value of about $0.025$. The difference is statistically significant.
step: Effect size: the gap is half a percentage point. Impact ratio: $0.495/0.500 = 0.99$, far above the four-fifths threshold of $0.80$.
step: Conclusion: the difference is detectable only because the samples are huge, and it is small by any practical measure. A report that shouted "statistically significant" and omitted the impact ratio would mislead.

@mistakes
- Equating "statistically significant" with "important" or "large".
- Treating a non-significant result from a small sample as proof of no effect.
- Treating the four-fifths rule as a legal safe harbor or as the legal definition of disparate impact.
- Running many subgroup tests and reporting only the significant ones without correction.
