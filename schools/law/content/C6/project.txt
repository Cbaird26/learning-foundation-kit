You will recompute its key numbers from the data supplied below and write a memo of about 1,200 words on the report's strengths and weaknesses. The report is original to this course and the case, parties and numbers are invented.
goal: Recompute selection rates, an impact ratio, a two-proportion z statistic and a p-value from raw counts.
goal: Identify errors of interpretation (a transposed conditional), design (the comparison pool) and inference (confounding, aggregation across departments with different rates, causal language).
goal: Write a clear, balanced memo that a supervising attorney could use to prepare for deposing or cross-examining the expert.
step: Read the report excerpt, the data tables and the regression table in the material below. List every numerical claim the report makes.
step: Recompute the overall selection rates for men and women, the difference in percentage points, the impact ratio and the pooled two-proportion z statistic. Compare each with the report and note any discrepancy.
step: Compute the p-value for your z statistic and a 95% confidence interval for the difference in selection rates. Say what each does and does not show.
step: Recompute the selection rates within each department, the within-department impact ratios and the z statistic for the competitive department. Explain how the departmental picture differs from the overall picture and what legal question determines which picture matters.
step: Interpret the regression table: state the Female coefficient in words, compute its t statistic and an approximate 95% interval, and name at least two confounders or tainted controls that could change the estimate.
step: Identify the report's interpretive errors, including the sentence that transposes a conditional probability, and rewrite that sentence accurately.
step: Draft the memo of about 1,200 words with these parts: summary of the report; recomputation table; strengths; weaknesses; questions for the expert; bottom-line assessment. Keep each numerical claim traceable to the material.
material: **Excerpt from the Expert Report (Reyes v. Acme Corp., invented for this course).** "I examined all applications for the position of warehouse supervisor received by Acme Corp. in 2025. All applicants met the stated minimum qualifications. Of 240 male applicants, 72 were hired (30.0%). Of 160 female applicants, 28 were hired (17.5%). Using a two-proportion z-test I obtained z = 2.83 and p = 0.0047. The probability that this disparity arose by chance is therefore 0.47%, which means there is a 99.53% probability that Acme discriminated against women. In a regression of starting salary on years of experience, department and sex, the coefficient on Female shows that discrimination reduced women's pay. I conclude that Acme's hiring practices caused the disparity."
material: **Table 1: Applicants and hires by department, 2025.** Department P (competitive, higher pay): men 40 applicants, 8 hired; women 140 applicants, 21 hired. Department Q (less competitive): men 200 applicants, 64 hired; women 20 applicants, 7 hired. Totals: men 240 applicants, 72 hired; women 160 applicants, 28 hired. The report did not present this table. The record does not say how applicants came to apply to a particular department.
material: **Table 2: Regression of starting salary (thousands of dollars) for the 100 people hired.** Intercept: estimate 48.0, standard error 3.0. Years of experience: estimate 1.8, standard error 0.5. Department P (1 = yes): estimate 4.0, standard error 1.4. Female (1 = yes): estimate -3.0, standard error 1.0. The model does not include education, prior job title or performance ratings. R squared is 0.31.

deliverable: A memo of about 1,200 words assessing the report's strengths and weaknesses and listing questions for the expert.

@review
This guide runs through the course week by week.

- **Week 1, describing data.** Mean, median, mode, range, sample standard deviation ($n-1$), z-scores; skew pulls the mean toward the tail.
- **Week 2, probability rules.** $P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)$; $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A\cap B)$; $P(A\mid B) = P(A\cap B)/P(B)$; independence means $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$.
- **Week 3, Bayes' theorem.** $P(H\mid E)$ needs the prior, sensitivity and false-positive rate. Use natural frequencies or odds times likelihood ratio.
- **Week 4, prosecutor's fallacy.** Random match probability is $P(\text{match}\mid\text{not source})$, not $P(\text{not source}\mid\text{match})$. Expected coincidental matches equal pool size times RMP. Remember the defense attorney's fallacy and *People v. Collins* (1968).
- **Week 5, sampling.** SRS, systematic, stratified, cluster; bias (selection, nonresponse, response, voluntary response) is not cured by a bigger sample.
- **Week 6, confidence intervals.** $\hat{p} \pm z^*\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}$; the level describes the method; round the required $n$ up.
- **Week 7, hypothesis tests.** $z$ statistics, p-values, Type I and II errors; *Castaneda* and *Hazelwood* (two or three standard deviations, proper pool).
- **Week 8, significance.** Statistical versus practical significance; impact ratio and the four-fifths guideline; multiple comparisons, $1-0.95^m$.
- **Week 9, regression.** Slope $= r\,s_y/s_x$, $R^2 = r^2$, $t = $ estimate / standard error, "holding other variables fixed".
- **Week 10, confounding.** Reverse causation, confounders, Simpson's paradox, tainted controls, randomization.
- **Week 11, damages.** Expected value; $PV = F/(1+r)^t$; annuity factor $[1-(1+r)^{-n}]/r$.
- **Week 12, evidence.** FRE 702 (more likely than not, amended 2023), FRE 403, FRE 107, *Daubert*, *Smith v. Arizona* (2024), *McCleskey*, *Wal-Mart v. Dukes*.

### Self-check

- Can you compute a conditional probability from a table and name the denominator?
- Can you do a Bayes problem with natural frequencies and with odds?
- Can you say in one sentence what a p-value is and what it is not?
- Can you build and interpret a 95% interval for a proportion?
- Can you discount a stream of payments and then weight it by a probability of liability?

### Most common errors

1. Swapping $P(A\mid B)$ and $P(B\mid A)$.
2. Reading a p-value or a confidence level as the probability that a hypothesis or an interval is correct.
3. Treating statistical significance as importance, or non-significance as proof of no effect.
4. Ignoring the comparison pool, confounders or the mix of subgroups.
5. Forgetting to discount future damages, or discounting for the wrong number of years.

@exam num
prompt: An agency audits job advertisements. About $10\%$ of ads contain discriminatory wording. A screening tool flags $90\%$ of discriminatory ads and also flags $5\%$ of lawful ads. An ad is flagged. What is the probability that it is discriminatory? Give an exact fraction.
answer: 2/3
check: (0.9 * 0.10) / (0.9 * 0.10 + 0.05 * 0.90)
hint: Take $1000$ ads: $100$ are discriminatory and $900$ are lawful.
hint: Flagged discriminatory ads: $90$. Flagged lawful ads: $0.05 \times 900 = 45$.
solution: $P = 90/(90+45) = 90/135 = 2/3$. A flag from a tool with a 90% detection rate is correct only two-thirds of the time because lawful ads are so numerous.

@exam num
prompt: A plaintiff has already lost \$30,000 in past earnings (no discounting). In addition, the plaintiff will lose \$45,000 at the end of each of the next $4$ years. The discount rate is $5\%$ per year and liability has probability $0.6$. Find the expected value of the claim (past plus discounted future losses, weighted by the probability of liability), rounded to the nearest dollar.
answer: 113741
tol: 0.5
unit: dollars
check: 0.6 * (30000 + 45000 * (1 - 1.05 ** -4) / 0.05)
hint: Past losses are not discounted; future losses are.
hint: The four-year annuity factor at $5\%$ is about $3.54595$.
solution: Future PV $\approx 45{,}000 \times 3.54595 = 159{,}567.8$. Add past losses: $189{,}567.8$. Times $0.6$: about \$113,741.

@exam self
prompt: A regression of annual pay (in thousands of dollars) on years of experience, a department indicator and a Female indicator gives a Female coefficient of $-3.0$ with standard error $1.0$. The model omits education and performance ratings. A plaintiff's expert concludes that the employer's discrimination caused the gap. Write a paragraph for a supervising attorney that (a) states what the coefficient means, (b) computes its $t$ statistic and an approximate 95% interval, and (c) explains two reasons the causal conclusion is not established.
hint: Use $t$ = estimate / standard error and the interval estimate $\pm 1.96$ standard errors.
solution: (a) Holding experience and department fixed, women in the data earn on average \$3,000 less than men, according to the model. (b) $t = -3.0/1.0 = -3.0$; the approximate 95% interval is $-3.0 \pm 1.96$, or about $(-4.96, -1.04)$ thousand dollars, so zero is not plausible and the coefficient is statistically significant. (c) First, omitted variables such as education and performance ratings may be related to both sex and pay, so the estimate may be biased in either direction. Second, a control such as department may itself reflect the alleged discrimination (a tainted control) or reflect choices the employer did not influence, and the regression cannot tell which. In addition, the model is observational, so the coefficient shows an association within the model rather than proof that discrimination caused the gap; the legal question of intent or disparate impact requires further evidence.
rubric: Interprets the coefficient as an average difference holding the other variables fixed.
rubric: Computes $t = -3.0$ and an interval of about $-4.96$ to $-1.04$.
rubric: Gives two reasons the causal conclusion fails, such as omitted variables, tainted controls or observational design.
rubric: Avoids saying the regression proves or disproves discrimination.
