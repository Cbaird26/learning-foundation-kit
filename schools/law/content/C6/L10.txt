@article
A regression coefficient or a correlation tells us that two things move together. A lawyer needs more: did the defendant's conduct **cause** the harm or the disparity? Statistics can inform that question but never answers it alone.

### Why association is not causation

Three common explanations for an association between $X$ and $Y$:

- **$X$ causes $Y$** (what the claimant asserts).
- **Reverse causation:** $Y$ causes $X$. Firms under investigation may hire more compliance staff, so compliance staff and investigations are positively associated.
- **Confounding:** a third variable $Z$ influences both. A **confounder** is related to the supposed cause and to the outcome; ignoring it biases the estimate. Experience may confound a pay comparison between men and women if the groups differ in experience.

Chance and selection into the data can also create associations.

### Simpson's paradox

A trend in the aggregate can reverse within every subgroup when the groups have very different sizes. It is a concrete way a confounder (here, the department applied to) can mislead. When overall rates and subgroup rates tell opposite stories, you must decide which comparison answers the legal question, and the answer depends on whether the grouping variable is a legitimate factor or itself a product of the alleged discrimination.

### Experiments versus observational data

In a **randomized experiment** subjects are assigned to groups by chance, so on average the groups are alike in everything but the treatment, which supports a causal conclusion. In an **observational study** the groups form themselves, so confounders are a standing threat. Legal questions are mostly observational: we cannot randomly assign employers or defendants.

### What regression adjustment can do

Adding control variables in a regression compares like with like on those variables, which can reduce confounding by **measured** factors. It cannot remove confounding by unmeasured factors, and it can backfire:

- **Omitted variable bias:** leaving out a relevant, correlated variable biases the coefficient on the one you included.
- **Tainted (bad) controls:** controlling for a variable that is itself affected by the alleged discrimination (such as job title or starting salary) can hide the effect being tested. Whether a control is legitimate is a legal and factual question, not only a statistical one.

### Causation in law

Law has its own causation tests (but-for causation, proximate cause), and in toxic-tort and employment cases courts sometimes accept epidemiological or statistical evidence of **general** causation (can the exposure cause this harm?). Individual, **specific** causation also requires facts about the plaintiff. Statistical evidence supports a causal inference only when the study design and the control for confounders are credible, which a court assesses under FRE 702.

@example
title: Simpson's paradox in hiring
problem: Two departments hired as follows. Easy department: men $60$ of $80$, women $16$ of $20$. Hard department: men $4$ of $20$, women $20$ of $80$. Compare the hiring rates overall and within each department.
step: Easy department: men $60/80 = 75\%$; women $16/20 = 80\%$. Women are ahead.
step: Hard department: men $4/20 = 20\%$; women $20/80 = 25\%$. Women are ahead again.
step: Overall: men $(60+4)/(80+20) = 64/100 = 64\%$; women $(16+20)/(20+80) = 36/100 = 36\%$. Men appear far ahead.
step: The reversal occurs because most women applied to the hard department and most men to the easy one. Department is a confounder of the overall comparison.
step: The legal question is what drives the department choice. If applicants are steered by the employer, the overall gap may itself be evidence of discrimination; if they choose freely, the within-department comparison is more informative. Statistics cannot decide this alone.

@mistakes
- Claiming a regression proves causation because many control variables were included.
- Controlling for a variable that the alleged discrimination itself affects.
- Comparing only aggregate rates when the groups differ in the mix of subgroups.
- Treating an association shown in an observational study as if participants had been randomly assigned.

@practice num
prompt: A group's promotion rate is $80\%$ in Grade 1 and $20\%$ in Grade 2. To compare groups fairly, an analyst standardizes to a common mix in which Grade 1 and Grade 2 each carry weight $0.5$. What is the group's standardized promotion rate, as an exact fraction?
answer: 1/2
check: 0.5 * 0.8 + 0.5 * 0.2
hint: Weight each grade's rate by its weight and add.
hint: $0.5(0.8) + 0.5(0.2)$.
solution: $0.4 + 0.1 = 0.5$. Using the same weights for both groups is how a stratified comparison adjusts for differences in mix.

@practice ms
prompt: Select all designs or steps that support a causal claim that a training program reduced errors.
choice*: Randomly assigning employees to the program or to a control group and comparing error rates.
choice*: Comparing outcomes in an observational study after adjusting for measured differences, while acknowledging that unmeasured confounders may remain.
choice: Comparing volunteers who chose the program with employees who did not, and ignoring why they chose.

hint: Which approaches make the groups comparable, or at least state what remains uncontrolled?
solution: Random assignment balances confounders on average. Adjustment helps for measured variables if its limits are stated. Self-selected comparisons and simple before-and-after trends leave confounding unaddressed.

@practice self
prompt: A regression of annual salary (in thousands of dollars) on experience in years and an indicator for female reports the following. Experience: estimate $2.5$, standard error $0.4$. Female: estimate $-4.0$, standard error $1.6$. Intercept: $30.0$. In four or five sentences, (a) interpret the Female coefficient, (b) compute its $t$ statistic and say whether it is significant at the 5% level, and (c) name two confounders that might change the estimate.
hint: Remember that $t$ is the estimate divided by its standard error, and that a confounder must be related to both sex and pay.
solution: (a) Holding years of experience fixed, women in the data earn on average \$4,000 less than men, according to the model. (b) $t = -4.0/1.6 = -2.5$. Since $|t| > 1.96$, the coefficient is statistically significant at the 5% level (p about 0.01). (c) Possible confounders: job level or title (women may be in lower-paid roles, though if the employer assigned those roles, job level may be a tainted control) and hours worked or performance ratings. The regression shows an association within the model, not that sex caused the pay gap.
rubric: Interprets the coefficient as an average difference holding experience fixed, not a rule for every woman.
rubric: Computes $t = -2.5$ and concludes it is significant at 5%.
rubric: Names two plausible confounders (such as job level, hours, performance, education or tenure at the firm).
rubric: States that the table shows association and does not by itself prove the cause, or notes that a tainted control could hide discrimination.
