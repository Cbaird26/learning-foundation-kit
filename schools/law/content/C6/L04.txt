@article
A forensic examiner reports that a DNA profile from the scene matches the defendant, and that the profile would be found in only about one unrelated person in a million. The **random match probability** (RMP) is the probability that a randomly chosen unrelated person would match by coincidence: $P(\text{match}\mid\text{not the source})$.

### The prosecutor's fallacy

The **prosecutor's fallacy** (also called the transposed conditional) treats that figure as if it were the probability that the defendant is innocent: "the chance that someone else left this sample is one in a million." That swaps $P(\text{match}\mid\text{innocent})$ for $P(\text{innocent}\mid\text{match})$. As Week 3 showed, the second probability depends on the prior, here the number of people who could have been the source.

Suppose the profile's RMP is $10^{-6}$ and the pool of possible sources has $N$ people, exactly one of whom is the true source (who matches for certain). If the suspect was picked from the pool with no other evidence, the other $N-1$ people produce about $(N-1)\times 10^{-6}$ coincidental matches, and

$$P(\text{source}\mid\text{match}) = \frac{1}{1 + (N-1)\times \text{RMP}}.$$

With a pool of about a million and one people, you expect one coincidental match besides the true source, so the posterior is only $1/2$, not $0.999999$.

### The defense attorney's fallacy

The opposite error argues, "in a city of two million, two people match, so the match is worthless: there is only a one in two chance it is the defendant." This is wrong when other evidence already points to the defendant, because the prior is then far larger than $1/N$. The match still multiplies the odds by a huge likelihood ratio. Evidence must be combined, not discounted.

### Base rates and database searches

When a suspect is found by searching a database of $n$ profiles for a match, the chance that at least one innocent person matches is $1 - (1 - \text{RMP})^n$, which can be large. The meaning of a match differs between a suspect already identified by other evidence and one found only by trawling.

### The product rule and its limits

Multiplying the frequencies of independent features gives the RMP for the combination. In *People v. Collins* (1968) the California Supreme Court reversed a conviction where a mathematics instructor multiplied assumed frequencies of several traits. The court criticized the lack of foundation for the individual frequencies, the unjustified assumption that the traits were independent, and the use of the product as if it were the probability of guilt, ignoring that other couples might share the traits. Today's DNA statistics rest on population databases and tested independence assumptions, but the logic of the critique remains.

### Reporting

Better practice reports a likelihood ratio, or the RMP together with clear language that it is not the probability the defendant is the source, and lets the fact finder combine it with the other evidence.

@example
title: How much does a one-in-a-million match prove?
problem: A town has $1{,}000{,}001$ people. Exactly one left a biological sample at a scene, and its profile is shared by $1$ in $1{,}000{,}000$ unrelated people. A person is chosen from the town with no other evidence against them and matches. What is the probability that the person is the source?
step: Everyone other than the true source is a possible coincidental match. There are $1{,}000{,}000$ of them, each matching with probability $10^{-6}$.
step: Expected coincidental matches: $1{,}000{,}000 \times 10^{-6} = 1$. Together with the true source, about two people match.
step: Apply the formula: $P(\text{source}\mid\text{match}) = \frac{1}{1 + 1} = \frac12$.
step: The fallacious claim "one in a million chance of innocence" is off by a factor of half a million. The match is strong evidence, since it raised the odds a million-fold, but whether it proves guilt depends on the prior.

@mistakes
- Stating the random match probability as the probability that the defendant is innocent (the prosecutor's fallacy).
- Dismissing a match because many people in a large city would also match, ignoring other evidence (the defense attorney's fallacy).
- Multiplying feature frequencies without checking that the features are independent.
- Ignoring how the suspect was found: a database trawl produces more coincidental hits than a suspect identified first by other evidence.

answer: 1/3
check: 1 / (1 + 20000 * (1 / 10000))
hint: Count the people who could match by coincidence: everyone except the true source.
hint: Expected coincidental matches: $20{,}000 \times \frac{1}{10{,}000} = 2$.
solution: $P = \frac{1}{1+2} = \frac13$.

answer: 3/2
check: 3000000 / 2000000
hint: Multiply the population by the match probability.

solution: Expected matches $= 3{,}000{,}000/2{,}000{,}000 = 3/2 = 1.5$.

@practice num
prompt: A serial number is made of five independent features whose frequencies in the population are $1/10$, $1/5$, $1/20$, $1/4$ and $1/10$. Find the probability that a random item shows all five features, as an exact fraction.
answer: 1/40000
check: (1/10) * (1/5) * (1/20) * (1/4) * (1/10)
hint: Independence lets you multiply the five frequencies.
hint: The denominators multiply to $10 \times 5 \times 20 \times 4 \times 10$.
solution: $\frac{1}{10\cdot 5\cdot 20\cdot 4\cdot 10} = \frac{1}{40{,}000}$. The answer is only as good as the independence assumption, as in *People v. Collins* (1968).

@practice num

Suppose the true source is not in the database and the profiles are unrelated and independent. What is the probability that at least one database profile matches by coincidence? Round to four decimal places.
answer: 0.3935
tol: 0.0005
check: 1 - (1 - 1e-6) ** 500000
hint: Use the complement: find the probability that none of the profiles match.
hint: Each profile fails to match with probability $1 - 10^{-6}$; the profiles are independent.
solution: $1 - (1-10^{-6})^{500000} \approx 1 - e^{-0.5} \approx 0.3935$. A coincidental hit is far from rare when a large database is searched.

@quiz num
prompt: Other evidence makes the odds that a suspect is the source $1$ to $3$ (prior odds $1/3$). A profile found at the scene matches the suspect and has a random match probability of $1$ in $1000$, so the likelihood ratio is $1000$. What is the posterior probability that the suspect is the source? Give an exact fraction.
answer: 1000/1003
check: ((1/3) * 1000) / (1 + (1/3) * 1000)
hint: Multiply the prior odds by the likelihood ratio.
hint: Posterior odds are $1000/3$. Convert to a probability.
solution: Posterior odds $= \frac{1}{3}(1000) = \frac{1000}{3}$, so $P = \frac{1000/3}{1 + 1000/3} = \frac{1000}{1003} \approx 0.997$.
