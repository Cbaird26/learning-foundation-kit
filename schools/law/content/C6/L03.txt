@article
Evidence rarely proves a hypothesis; it shifts how likely the hypothesis is. **Bayes' theorem** is the rule for that shift. Let $H$ be a hypothesis (the sample is the defendant's; the file is fraudulent) and $E$ the evidence (a match; a software flag). Then

$$P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H)\,P(H)}{P(E)}.$$

The denominator comes from the **law of total probability**, which splits the evidence into the case where $H$ is true and the case where it is false:

$$P(E) = P(E\mid H)P(H) + P(E\mid H^c)P(H^c).$$

### The three ingredients

- The **prior** $P(H)$: how likely $H$ was before the evidence. For a test it is the **prevalence** or **base rate**.
- The **likelihoods** $P(E\mid H)$ and $P(E\mid H^c)$. For a diagnostic or forensic test, $P(E\mid H)$ is the **sensitivity** (true-positive rate) and $P(E\mid H^c)$ is the **false-positive rate**.
- The **posterior** $P(H\mid E)$: how likely $H$ is after the evidence.

The central lesson: a test's accuracy figures are $P(E\mid H)$ and $P(E\mid H^c)$. What a court or juror wants is $P(H\mid E)$. They are not the same, and the gap depends on the prior.

### Natural frequencies

Bayes problems are easier with counts. Imagine a concrete group of, say, $10{,}000$ cases, split by the prior, then by the likelihoods, and ask: among the cases showing the evidence, what fraction have $H$ true? This is just Bayes' theorem done with whole numbers.

### The odds form

Write **odds** as $P(H)/P(H^c)$. Then

$$\text{posterior odds} = \text{prior odds} \times \text{likelihood ratio},\qquad \text{LR} = \frac{P(E\mid H)}{P(E\mid H^c)}.$$

Convert back with $P = \text{odds}/(1+\text{odds})$. The odds form separates how strong the evidence is (the LR) from what you believed beforehand (the prior odds), a distinction that matters when experts report likelihood ratios and the fact finder supplies the prior.

@example
title: A drug screen with a rare condition
problem: In a workforce, $2\%$ of employees use a banned drug. A screen flags $95\%$ of users (sensitivity) and falsely flags $4\%$ of non-users. An employee tests positive. What is the probability the employee is a user?
step: Take $10{,}000$ employees. Users: $2\% \times 10{,}000 = 200$. Non-users: $9{,}800$.
step: True positives: $95\%$ of $200 = 190$. False positives: $4\%$ of $9{,}800 = 392$.
step: Everyone who tests positive: $190 + 392 = 582$. The probability of being a user, given a positive, is $190/582 = 95/291 \approx 0.326$.
step: Check with the formula: $\frac{0.95(0.02)}{0.95(0.02) + 0.04(0.98)} = \frac{0.019}{0.0582} = 0.326$. A positive result from a "95% accurate" screen leaves about a one-in-three chance of use, because non-users vastly outnumber users.

@mistakes
- Reporting the sensitivity $P(E\mid H)$ as if it were $P(H\mid E)$.
- Ignoring the prior (base rate) and treating a rare condition as if it were a coin flip.
- Computing the false positives from the whole population instead of from the non-users only.
- Forgetting to include both branches in the denominator.

@practice num
prompt: A condition has prevalence $1\%$. A test has sensitivity $90\%$ and false-positive rate $10\%$. Using $1000$ people, find the probability that a person who tests positive has the condition. Give an exact fraction.
answer: 1/12
check: (0.9 * 10) / (0.9 * 10 + 0.1 * 990)
hint: Of $1000$ people, $10$ have the condition and $990$ do not.
hint: True positives: $9$. False positives: $0.10 \times 990 = 99$.
solution: Positives $= 9 + 99 = 108$, of which $9$ are true: $9/108 = 1/12$.

@practice num
prompt: A hypothesis has $P(H) = 0.3$. The evidence has $P(E\mid H) = 0.8$ and $P(E\mid H^c) = 0.1$. Find $P(E)$ as an exact fraction.
answer: 31/100
check: 0.8 * 0.3 + 0.1 * 0.7
hint: Use the law of total probability.
hint: $P(H^c) = 0.7$. Add $0.8(0.3)$ and $0.1(0.7)$.
solution: $P(E) = 0.24 + 0.07 = 0.31 = 31/100$.

prompt: A hypothesis has $P(H) = 0.3$. The evidence has $P(E\mid H) = 0.8$ and $P(E\mid H^c) = 0.1$. Find $P(H\mid E)$ as an exact fraction.

@practice num
prompt: Before a forensic report, the odds that a suspect is the source of a sample are $1$ to $4$ (prior odds $1/4$). The report's likelihood ratio is $6$. What is the posterior probability that the suspect is the source? Give an exact fraction.
answer: 3/5
check: ((1/4) * 6) / (1 + (1/4) * 6)
hint: Multiply the prior odds by the likelihood ratio.
hint: Posterior odds are $6/4 = 3/2$. Convert odds to probability with $\text{odds}/(1+\text{odds})$.
solution: Posterior odds $= \frac14 \times 6 = \frac32$, so $P = \frac{3/2}{1 + 3/2} = \frac{3}{5}$.

prompt: A lab states that its test "detects 99% of true cases." A lawyer says this means a positive result is 99% likely to be a true case. What is wrong with the statement?
choice*: The 99% is the probability of a positive given a true case, not the probability of a true case given a positive; the latter depends on the base rate and false-positive rate.
choice: Nothing; sensitivity and the probability of a true case given a positive are always equal.
choice: The 99% is the false-positive rate, which is unrelated to true cases.
choice: Bayes' theorem cannot be applied to laboratory tests.
hint: Write the sensitivity as a conditional probability and compare its order with the one the lawyer wants.
solution: Sensitivity is $P(\text{positive}\mid\text{case})$. The lawyer needs $P(\text{case}\mid\text{positive})$, which also requires the prior and the false-positive rate.

@quiz num
prompt: An audit of loan files finds that $5\%$ contain fraud. Software flags $80\%$ of fraudulent files and $10\%$ of clean files. A file is flagged. What is the probability it is fraudulent? Give an exact fraction.
answer: 8/27
check: (0.8 * 0.05) / (0.8 * 0.05 + 0.1 * 0.95)
hint: Compute the fraction of all files that are flagged and fraudulent, and the fraction that are flagged and clean.
hint: $0.04$ flagged fraudulent and $0.095$ flagged clean.
solution: $P = 0.04/(0.04 + 0.095) = 0.04/0.135 = 8/27 \approx 0.296$.

@quiz ms
prompt: Select all statements that are correct about Bayes' theorem.
choice*: The posterior probability depends on the prior probability as well as on the test's accuracy rates.
choice*: With the same sensitivity and false-positive rate, a lower prior gives a lower posterior.
choice: A positive result from a test with sensitivity above 95% guarantees the posterior exceeds 95%.
choice: The posterior probability always equals the sensitivity.
choice: The prior has no effect when the evidence is strong.
hint: Try the same test on a rare condition and on a common one.
solution: The posterior combines prior and likelihoods, so a lower prior gives a lower posterior. High sensitivity alone guarantees nothing, since false positives from a large non-case group can dominate.
