@article
A **probability** is a number from $0$ to $1$ that measures how likely an event is. Lawyers use probabilities whenever they ask how likely a test result, a witness error or a pattern in the data is by chance. Four rules do most of the work.

### Complement and addition

The **complement** of event $A$, written $A^c$, is "A does not happen":

$$P(A^c) = 1 - P(A).$$

This is the key shortcut for "at least one" questions: $P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - P(\text{none})$.

For "A or B", add and subtract the overlap so it is not counted twice:

$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B).$$

If $A$ and $B$ are **mutually exclusive** (they cannot both occur), $P(A\cap B)=0$ and the subtraction vanishes.

### Conditional probability

The probability of $A$ **given** that $B$ occurred restricts attention to the cases where $B$ happened:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \qquad P(B) > 0.$$

Rearranging gives the **multiplication rule**: $P(A\cap B) = P(B)\,P(A\mid B)$. Note that $P(A\mid B)$ and $P(B\mid A)$ are different quantities. Confusing them is the root of the prosecutor's fallacy in Week 4.

### Independence

Events are **independent** if knowing one occurred does not change the probability of the other: $P(A\mid B) = P(A)$, equivalently $P(A\cap B) = P(A)P(B)$. Independence is an assumption about the world that must be justified, not a default. Mutually exclusive events with positive probabilities are never independent, because if one happens the other cannot.

### Working from a table

Counts in a two-way table convert directly to probabilities. The marginal probability is a row or column total over the grand total; a conditional probability divides by the total of the row or column you are conditioning on. Always ask, "out of which group?" That denominator is the whole difference between $P(\text{denied}\mid\text{home})$ and $P(\text{home}\mid\text{denied})$.

@example
title: Insurance claims and conditional probability
problem: An insurer's file of $500$ claims has $200$ auto claims, of which $30$ were denied, and $300$ homeowner claims, of which $90$ were denied. Find $P(\text{denied})$, $P(\text{denied}\mid\text{homeowner})$ and $P(\text{homeowner}\mid\text{denied})$.
step: Total denied claims: $30 + 90 = 120$. So $P(\text{denied}) = 120/500 = 0.24$.
step: Condition on homeowner: restrict to the $300$ homeowner claims, of which $90$ were denied. $P(\text{denied}\mid\text{homeowner}) = 90/300 = 0.30$.
step: Condition on denied: restrict to the $120$ denied claims, of which $90$ were homeowner. $P(\text{homeowner}\mid\text{denied}) = 90/120 = 0.75$.
step: The two conditionals differ (0.30 versus 0.75). Three quarters of denials are homeowner claims largely because homeowner claims are 60% of the file, not because 75% of homeowner claims are denied.

@mistakes
- Treating $P(A\mid B)$ and $P(B\mid A)$ as the same number.
- Adding $P(A)$ and $P(B)$ for "A or B" without subtracting the overlap.
- Multiplying probabilities for "both" without checking that the events are independent.
- Using the grand total as the denominator when the question has a condition.

answer: 7/10
check: 0.5 + 0.3 - 0.1
hint: Use the addition rule, subtracting the overlap.
hint: $0.5 + 0.3 - 0.1$.
solution: $P(A\cup B) = 0.5 + 0.3 - 0.1 = 0.7 = 7/10$.

check: 0.9 * 0.8

@practice num
prompt: A lab runs three independent tests, each with probability $0.1$ of producing a false positive on a clean sample. What is the probability that at least one of the three gives a false positive? Give an exact fraction.
answer: 271/1000
check: 1 - 0.9 ** 3
hint: Use the complement: "at least one" is "not none".
hint: The probability that all three are correct negatives is $0.9^3 = 0.729$.
solution: $1 - 0.9^3 = 1 - 0.729 = 0.271 = 271/1000$.

@practice num
prompt: Of $1000$ traffic stops, $200$ involved a vehicle search and the other $800$ did not. Contraband was found in $50$ of the searches. What is the probability that contraband was found, given that the vehicle was searched? Give an exact fraction.
answer: 1/4
check: 50 / 200
hint: Condition on "searched": restrict to those 200 stops.
hint: Divide the number with contraband among searches by the number of searches.
solution: $P(\text{contraband}\mid\text{searched}) = 50/200 = 1/4$. The $800$ unsearched stops play no role, and dividing by $1000$ would answer a different question.

@practice mc
prompt: For events $A$ and $B$, $P(A) = 0.4$, $P(B) = 0.5$ and $P(A \cap B) = 0.2$. Which statement is correct?
choice*: A and B are independent, because $P(A)P(B) = 0.2 = P(A \cap B)$.
choice: A and B are mutually exclusive, because $P(A \cap B)$ is small.
choice: A and B are not independent, because $P(A) + P(B) = 0.9$.
choice: A and B are mutually exclusive, because $P(A) \neq P(B)$.
hint: Compare $P(A\cap B)$ with the product $P(A)P(B)$.
solution: $0.4 \times 0.5 = 0.2 = P(A\cap B)$, so the events are independent. They are not mutually exclusive, since $P(A\cap B) = 0.2 \neq 0$.

@quiz num
prompt: In a docket study, $P(A) = 0.6$ and $P(A \cap B) = 0.15$, where $A$ is "plaintiff represented by counsel" and $B$ is "case dismissed". Find $P(B \mid A)$ as an exact fraction.
answer: 1/4
check: 0.15 / 0.6
hint: Divide the joint probability by the probability of the condition.
hint: The condition is $A$.
solution: $P(B\mid A) = 0.15/0.6 = 0.25 = 1/4$.

@quiz mc
prompt: Two events each have positive probability and cannot both occur. Which statement is correct?
choice*: They are not independent, because learning that one occurred tells you the other did not.
choice: They are independent, because they never happen together.
choice: They are independent only if their probabilities add to 1.
choice: They are not independent, because $P(A \cup B)$ must exceed $1$.
hint: Compute $P(A \mid B)$ for mutually exclusive events.
solution: If $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive, $P(A\mid B) = 0$, which differs from $P(A) > 0$. So they are dependent.
