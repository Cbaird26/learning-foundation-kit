@article
A damages award attempts to place the plaintiff where they would have been without the wrong. When the loss lies in the future or is uncertain, two quantitative tools turn it into a single sum: **expected value** for uncertainty and **present value** for timing.

### Expected value

For outcomes with values $x_i$ and probabilities $p_i$ that sum to $1$,

$$E[X] = \sum p_i x_i.$$

Lawyers use it to value claims and settlements: a case with a $60\%$ chance of winning a judgment of \$250,000 has expected judgment value $0.6 \times 250{,}000 = 150{,}000$ dollars before costs, and net expected value after subtracting costs that are borne whatever the result. Expected value is an average over many hypothetical repetitions, so it is a useful benchmark even though the single case ends in one outcome. Risk tolerance, fee arrangements and the timing of payment affect whether a particular client should accept an offer equal to the expected value.

### Present value

Money received later is worth less than money received now, because money in hand can earn interest. If the **discount rate** is $r$ per year, a payment $F$ received in $t$ years has present value

$$PV = \frac{F}{(1+r)^t}.$$

For a level stream of $L$ at the end of each of $n$ years (an **annuity**),

$$PV = L\cdot\frac{1 - (1+r)^{-n}}{r}.$$

For a stream that grows, discount each year's amount separately and add. A higher discount rate gives a lower present value; a longer wait gives a lower present value.

### In damages

Lost earnings are usually computed as: the earnings the plaintiff would have received (often with expected raises), minus any earnings still available (mitigation), summed over the work-life period, then reduced to present value. Courts and states differ on the method, the proper discount rate, whether inflation is handled by raising the earnings or lowering the discount rate, and whether a jury is told to use a particular method. Economists commonly testify, and their assumptions (the growth rate, the discount rate, the length of work life) can be challenged. Prejudgment interest compensates for delay in the *past*, while discounting reflects early receipt of *future* loss.

Contract damages follow the same logic. **Expectation damages** aim to give the injured party the benefit of the bargain, for example the contract price difference plus incidental costs, and a lost-profits claim requires reasonable certainty about the loss, not a probability-weighted guess for a loss that is entirely speculative. Probability weighting is more common in valuing a litigation risk than in proving a lost-profits claim.

### Sensitivity

Because small changes in the discount rate move the result, a good report shows the damages at several rates or growth assumptions. Present-value arithmetic is simple, so the real disputes are over the inputs.

@example
title: Present value of lost earnings
problem: A plaintiff can no longer earn \$60,000 per year for the next $5$ years (received at the end of each year). Using a discount rate of $4\%$ per year, find the present value. If liability is established with probability $0.7$, find the expected present value of the claim.
step: Annuity factor: $\frac{1 - (1.04)^{-5}}{0.04}$. Since $1.04^5 \approx 1.21665$, $1.04^{-5} \approx 0.82193$, and the factor is $\frac{0.17807}{0.04} \approx 4.4518$.
step: Present value: $60{,}000 \times 4.4518 \approx 267{,}109$ dollars.
step: Check by discounting year by year: $\frac{60{,}000}{1.04} + \frac{60{,}000}{1.04^2} + \cdots + \frac{60{,}000}{1.04^5} \approx 57{,}692 + 55{,}473 + 53{,}339 + 51{,}288 + 49{,}316 \approx 267{,}109$.
step: Expected present value of the claim: $0.7 \times 267{,}109 \approx 186{,}977$ dollars.
step: The total of undiscounted payments is \$300,000. The difference of about \$33,000 is the cost of receiving the money early, which is why a lump sum for future losses is smaller than the sum of the payments.

@mistakes
- Adding up future payments without discounting.
- Applying the discount to a payment for the wrong number of years (a payment at the end of year $3$ is discounted by $(1+r)^3$).
- Multiplying a probability by the damages and treating the result as what a court will award; the expected value is a benchmark for settlement, not a finding of damages.
- Using a rate as a whole number ($4$ instead of $0.04$) in the formula.

@practice num
prompt: A plaintiff's lost earnings are \$80,000 at the end of year $1$, growing by $3\%$ per year, for $3$ years in all (so year $2$ is \$80,000 times $1.03$ and year $3$ is \$80,000 times $1.03^2$). Using a discount rate of $5\%$ per year, find the present value, rounded to the nearest dollar.
answer: 224245
tol: 0.5
unit: dollars
check: sum(80000 * 1.03 ** (t - 1) / 1.05 ** t for t in (1, 2, 3))
hint: Compute each year's earnings, then discount year $t$ by $1.05^t$.
hint: The earnings are \$80,000, \$82,400 and \$84,872.
solution: $\frac{80{,}000}{1.05} + \frac{82{,}400}{1.05^2} + \frac{84{,}872}{1.05^3} \approx 76{,}190.48 + 74{,}739.00 + 73{,}315.85 \approx 224{,}245$.

@quiz num
prompt: A plaintiff loses \$30,000 at the end of each of the next $4$ years. Using a discount rate of $6\%$ per year, find the present value of the loss, rounded to the nearest dollar.
answer: 103953
tol: 0.5
unit: dollars
check: 30000 * (1 - 1.06 ** -4) / 0.06
hint: Use the annuity formula $L\,[1-(1+r)^{-n}]/r$.
hint: The annuity factor is about $3.4651$.
solution: $30{,}000 \times 3.465106 \approx 103{,}953$.

choice*: A dollar received earlier can be invested to grow, so a lump sum paid today to cover future losses can be smaller than the sum of the future payments.
choice: The expert is penalizing the plaintiff for delay in bringing the suit.
