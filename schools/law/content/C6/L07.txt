@article
A **hypothesis test** asks whether data are consistent with a "nothing unusual" claim. The **null hypothesis** $H_0$ states that claim (a hiring process is neutral; a jury pool matches the community); the **alternative** $H_a$ states what the data would support instead. We assume $H_0$ and ask how surprising the data would then be.

### The test statistic and the p-value

For a proportion, with null value $p_0$, sample proportion $\hat{p}$ and sample size $n$:

$$z = \frac{\hat{p} - p_0}{\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}}.$$

The **p-value** is the probability, **assuming $H_0$ is true**, of getting a result at least as extreme as the one observed. For a two-sided test it is $2P(Z \ge |z|)$. Small p-values mean the data would be rare under $H_0$.

A common convention rejects $H_0$ when $p < \alpha$, usually $\alpha = 0.05$. The p-value is **not** the probability that $H_0$ is true, and it is not the probability that the result happened "by chance" in the everyday sense. It is $P(\text{data this extreme}\mid H_0)$, a transposed conditional like the one in Week 4 if misread.

### Two proportions: a hiring disparity

To compare selection rates $\hat{p}_1 = x_1/n_1$ and $\hat{p}_2 = x_2/n_2$, use the pooled proportion $\hat{p} = (x_1 + x_2)/(n_1 + n_2)$ and

$$z = \frac{\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2}{\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})\left(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}\right)}}.$$

The null hypothesis is that both groups share the same underlying selection probability.

### Errors

A **Type I error** rejects a true $H_0$; its probability is $\alpha$. A **Type II error** fails to reject a false $H_0$; its probability $\beta$ depends on the true effect and the sample size, and **power** is $1-\beta$. A small sample can fail to detect a real disparity, and "no significant difference" is then not proof of fairness.

### Standard deviations in discrimination cases

In *Castaneda v. Partida* (1977), a challenge to the selection of grand jurors, the Supreme Court observed that where the gap between the expected and observed numbers of a group exceeds two or three standard deviations, the hypothesis of random selection would be suspect to social scientists. *Hazelwood School District v. United States* (1977) applied the same idea to hiring, and stressed that the comparison must use the relevant pool, here the qualified teacher labor market rather than the student population. The statistic is the same $z$: the observed count minus its expectation, divided by the standard deviation. Under Title VII, such statistics support a prima facie case of a pattern or practice of discrimination, which the employer can rebut by attacking the data or the comparison.

### Using p-values responsibly

Report the effect (the size of the gap), a confidence interval, and the test. A test result depends on the question asked, the data collected and the model; Week 8 shows why "significant" does not mean "important."

@example
title: Testing a hiring disparity
problem: An employer hired $60$ of $200$ male applicants ($30\%$) and $27$ of $150$ female applicants ($18\%$). Test $H_0$: the hiring probabilities are equal, against a two-sided alternative at $\alpha = 0.05$.
step: Pooled proportion: $\hat{p} = (60 + 27)/(200 + 150) = 87/350 \approx 0.2486$.
step: Standard error: $\sqrt{0.2486(0.7514)(1/200 + 1/150)} = \sqrt{0.18679 \times 0.011667} \approx 0.04668$.
step: Test statistic: $z = (0.30 - 0.18)/0.04668 \approx 2.57$.
step: Two-sided p-value: $2P(Z \ge 2.57) \approx 0.010$, which is below $0.05$. We reject $H_0$.
step: Conclusion: a gap this large would occur in only about 1% of samples if the hiring probabilities were equal. That is evidence of a disparity, not proof of intent or of the cause; the next weeks ask about size and confounders.

@mistakes
- Reading the p-value as the probability that the null hypothesis is true.
- Saying "we accept the null" when $p$ is large; the data merely fail to reject it, especially with small samples.
- Using the wrong comparison pool, as in *Hazelwood*, so that the expected counts are meaningless.
- Computing the two-proportion standard error with unpooled proportions under the null hypothesis, or forgetting to add both $1/n$ terms.

@practice num
prompt: Test $H_0$: $p = 0.5$ using a random sample of $n = 100$ with $\hat{p} = 0.6$. Find the $z$ statistic.
answer: 2
check: (0.6 - 0.5) / math.sqrt(0.5 * 0.5 / 100)
hint: The standard error under $H_0$ is $\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}$.
hint: $SE = \sqrt{0.25/100} = 0.05$.
solution: $z = (0.6 - 0.5)/0.05 = 2$.

@practice num
prompt: A county's eligible population is $60\%$ in Group X. A grand jury of $400$ is drawn, and only $200$ of its members are in Group X. If selection were random, how many standard deviations below the expected count is the observed count? Use $\sigma = \sqrt{np(1-p)}$ and round to two decimal places, giving the positive number of standard deviations.
answer: 4.08
tol: 0.01
check: (400 * 0.6 - 200) / math.sqrt(400 * 0.6 * 0.4)
hint: The expected count is $np$; the standard deviation is $\sqrt{np(1-p)}$.
hint: Expected $240$, so the gap is $40$; $\sigma = \sqrt{96} \approx 9.80$.
solution: Expected $= 240$; $\sigma = \sqrt{400(0.6)(0.4)} = \sqrt{96} \approx 9.80$; the gap of $40$ is $40/9.80 \approx 4.08$ standard deviations, far beyond the "two or three" benchmark in *Castaneda* (1977).

@practice num
prompt: An employer hired $40$ of $100$ applicants from Group A and $25$ of $100$ applicants from Group B. Use the pooled two-proportion $z$ statistic to test equality of the hiring rates. Round $z$ to two decimal places.
answer: 2.26
tol: 0.01
check: (40 / 100 - 25 / 100) / math.sqrt((65 / 200) * (1 - 65 / 200) * (1 / 100 + 1 / 100))
hint: The pooled proportion is $(40 + 25)/200$.
hint: $\hat{p} = 0.325$, $SE = \sqrt{0.325(0.675)(0.02)} \approx 0.0662$.
solution: $z = 0.15/0.0662 \approx 2.26$.

@quiz num
prompt: Test $H_0$: $p = 0.30$ against a two-sided alternative. A random sample of $n = 400$ files has $100$ with a particular defect. Find the $z$ statistic, rounded to two decimal places.
answer: -2.18
tol: 0.01
check: (100 / 400 - 0.30) / math.sqrt(0.30 * 0.70 / 400)
hint: $\hat{p} = 100/400$. Use $p_0 = 0.30$ in the standard error.
hint: $SE = \sqrt{0.21/400} \approx 0.0229$.
solution: $\hat{p} = 0.25$; $z = (0.25 - 0.30)/0.0229 \approx -2.18$.

@quiz mc
prompt: A plaintiff's expert tests a hiring disparity and fails to reject the null hypothesis at $\alpha = 0.05$ with only $12$ applicants in the comparison group. Which conclusion is best?
choice*: The test may simply lack power; a failure to reject does not show that the hiring process is neutral.
choice: The result proves the process is neutral.
choice: The result is a Type I error.
choice: The null hypothesis has been shown to have a 95% probability of being true.
hint: Think about what a small sample does to the ability to detect a real difference.
