@article
Article I, section 8, clause 3 gives Congress power "to regulate Commerce with foreign Nations, and among the several States, and with the Indian Tribes." The commerce power is the source of much federal regulation, from civil rights statutes to criminal laws. Learn the doctrine through its history.

### History in one paragraph

*Gibbons v. Ogden* (1824) read "commerce" broadly to include navigation and commercial intercourse. Early twentieth-century cases limited Congress's power by distinguishing production from commerce, but after *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.* (1937) and *United States v. Darby* (1941), the Court upheld regulation of activities that affect interstate commerce. *Wickard v. Filburn* (1942) upheld penalties on a farmer who grew wheat above his quota for his own use, because the **aggregate** effect of many such farmers on the national wheat market was substantial. *Heart of Atlanta Motel v. United States* (1964) and *Katzenbach v. McClung* (1964) upheld the Civil Rights Act of 1964 as applied to a motel serving interstate travelers and to a restaurant serving food that had moved in commerce.

### The three categories

*United States v. Lopez* (1995) states that Congress may regulate:

1. the **channels** of interstate commerce (roads, waterways, air routes);
2. the **instrumentalities** of interstate commerce, and persons or things in interstate commerce, even if the threat comes only from intrastate activity;
3. **activities that substantially affect** interstate commerce.

Under the third category, Congress may regulate intrastate economic activity if, in the aggregate, it has a substantial effect on interstate commerce, and courts ask only whether Congress had a **rational basis** to conclude so.

### The Lopez and Morrison limits

*Lopez* struck down the Gun-Free School Zones Act, which criminalized possessing a gun near a school. The statute regulated neither commerce nor any economic enterprise, had no jurisdictional element limiting it to firearms that moved in commerce, and had no findings; the link between guns near schools and commerce was too attenuated. *United States v. Morrison* (2000) struck down the civil remedy in the Violence Against Women Act: gender-motivated violence is not economic activity, the statute lacked a jurisdictional element, and the link to commerce depended on a chain of attenuated effects. Courts consider (a) whether the regulated activity is economic, (b) whether there is a jurisdictional element, (c) congressional findings and (d) how attenuated the link is. A statute that is part of a broader scheme regulating an economic market may reach local activity: *Gonzales v. Raich* (2005) upheld the federal ban on home-grown marijuana for personal medical use as part of the comprehensive regulation of a national drug market.

### Limit on compelling commerce

*NFIB v. Sebelius* (2012): the commerce power regulates existing activity; it does not let Congress compel individuals to buy a product. The individual mandate failed under the Commerce Clause (though it was upheld as a tax).

### Policy

A broad commerce power permits national solutions to national markets, while the *Lopez* limits preserve a federalism boundary so that Congress cannot reach all human activity by pointing to a distant economic effect.

### Applying the rules

Ask in order: (1) Does the statute regulate a channel? (2) An instrumentality or a thing or person in commerce? (3) Does it regulate economic activity that, in the aggregate, substantially affects commerce, and does Congress have a rational basis for thinking so? (4) If the activity is noneconomic, is there a jurisdictional element or part of an essential economic scheme? (5) Does the law compel a person to enter commerce?

@example
title: Aggregation
problem: Congress sets a quota on wheat production to stabilize the national wheat market. Farmer grows wheat above his quota, all for use on his own farm. Congress argues that if many farmers did this, the market would be affected. May Congress regulate Farmer's wheat?
step: Category: this is intrastate activity, so it falls in the third category (substantial effects).
step: Economic activity: growing wheat is economic, and home-grown wheat competes with wheat in the market because farmers who use their own wheat do not buy it.
step: Aggregation: courts consider the combined effect of all farmers in Farmer's position, not just his own small amount.
step: Conclusion: Congress had a rational basis, so the regulation is valid (*Wickard*).

@example
title: A noneconomic statute without a jurisdictional element
problem: Congress makes it a federal crime to possess a handgun within 1,000 feet of any school, with no jurisdictional element and no findings. Defendant, charged, challenges the statute. Is it within the commerce power?
step: Category: possession near a school is not a channel or instrumentality, and the statute does not regulate things in commerce, so only the third category is available.
step: Economic activity: possessing a gun near a school is not itself economic.
step: Jurisdictional element and findings: none, so the statute does not limit itself to guns that moved in commerce.
step: Conclusion: the link to commerce is too attenuated, as in *Lopez*; the statute exceeds the commerce power.

@mistakes
- Applying Wickard's aggregation to any noneconomic activity; *Lopez* and *Morrison* require economic activity or a comprehensive economic scheme.
- Forgetting the second category, which covers persons and things in interstate commerce even when the threat is local, such as stolen goods.
- Saying the commerce power lets Congress compel people to buy a product; *NFIB* rejects that.
- Assuming congressional findings alone are enough.
