@article
The Tenth Amendment reserves to the states or the people the powers not delegated to the United States. The Court has treated it less as a source of freestanding limits and more as confirming that the Constitution created a system of **dual sovereignty**. The most important structural rule that follows is the **anti-commandeering doctrine**: Congress may not issue direct orders to state governments.

### The cases

- ***New York v. United States* (1992).** Federal law required states either to regulate low-level radioactive waste according to Congress's instructions or to take title to the waste. The Court held that Congress may not compel state legislatures to enact or administer a federal regulatory program. The "take title" option was unconstitutional commandeering. Congress may encourage states through conditions on spending and by offering a choice to regulate or have federal law preempt.
- ***Printz v. United States* (1997).** A federal gun law required local chief law enforcement officers to conduct background checks on handgun purchasers during an interim period. The Court held that Congress may not command state executive officers to administer a federal program, extending *New York* beyond legislatures.
- ***Murphy v. NCAA* (2018).** A federal statute barred states from authorizing sports gambling. The Court held that the doctrine applies to prohibitions as well as affirmative commands: Congress may not tell a state legislature what laws it may or may not enact. Preemption works only when the federal law regulates private actors, so a provision that merely dictates what states may do cannot be preemptive.

The reasons given are that commandeering blurs political accountability (voters blame the state for a federal policy), allows Congress to shift costs to the states, and threatens the balance between sovereigns.

### What Congress may still do

1. **Regulate private parties directly** under an enumerated power, with preemption of conflicting state law (Lesson 9).
2. **Attach conditions to federal money** under the Dole test (Lesson 5), so long as they are not coercive. Federal funding conditions are the usual alternative to commandeering.
3. **Apply generally applicable laws to states** as employers or market participants. *Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority* (1985) overruled *National League of Cities* and held that Congress may apply the Fair Labor Standards Act to a state transit authority, trusting the political process to protect states. *Reno v. Condon* (2000) upheld a law regulating states' sale of driver's-license information because it regulated the state as a participant in the market for personal information and applied generally, without requiring the states to enact a regulatory scheme.
4. **Direct state courts.** The Supremacy Clause binds state judges, so states may be required to hear federal claims on the same terms as state claims (*Testa v. Katt* (1947)). A state court may not discriminate against federal rights.
5. **Request voluntary cooperation.** States may choose to assist federal programs, and Congress may fund them.

### The clear-statement rule

Under *Gregory v. Ashcroft* (1991), Congress must make its intent plain before a federal statute is read to upset the usual balance of state and federal power over core state functions.

### Policy

Anti-commandeering preserves state political accountability and sovereignty; critics say it limits Congress's ability to use state officials who are already positioned to act. Conditions on spending remain the main tool.

### Applying the rules

Ask: (1) Does the federal law regulate private parties or states? (2) If states, is it generally applicable and aimed at the state as an actor like private parties, or does it order the state to legislate, enforce or administer? (3) If the latter, it is commandeering, unless it is a funding condition within *Dole* or directed to state courts.

@example
title: An order to state officers
problem: Congress passes a law requiring each state's chief law enforcement officer to run background checks on all prospective gun buyers and report the results to the federal government. A state sheriff challenges. Is the law valid?
step: The law directs state executive officers, so the question is commandeering.
step: *Printz* held that Congress may not conscript state officers to administer a federal regulatory program.
step: The law is not a funding condition and does not regulate private actors directly; it imposes a duty on officers.
step: Conclusion: the law is unconstitutional commandeering, as in *Printz*.

@example
title: A law of general applicability
problem: Congress applies the federal minimum-wage law to employees of state-run bus systems, as it does to private bus companies. State argues the Tenth Amendment forbids this. Is the law valid?
step: The law regulates state agencies, but as employers like private ones.
step: *Garcia* held that such generally applicable laws are valid under the commerce power, and states are protected mainly through the political process.
step: The law does not order the state to legislate or enforce.
step: Conclusion: valid.

@mistakes
- Saying Congress can never regulate states; it can, through generally applicable laws and funding conditions.
- Limiting anti-commandeering to affirmative orders; *Murphy* applies it to prohibitions on state legislation.
- Confusing preemption with commandeering: preemption requires federal law regulating private actors.
- Treating state courts like state executives; the Supremacy Clause binds state judges.
