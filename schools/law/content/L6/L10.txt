@article
Article II vests "the executive Power" in the President, who must "take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed" and is Commander in Chief. The framework for evaluating executive action is Justice Jackson's concurrence in *Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer* (1952), which the Court has treated as the governing analysis.

### The case

During the Korean War, the President ordered the seizure of the steel mills to avert a strike that he said would harm production. No statute authorized it, and Congress had considered and rejected authorizing seizures when it passed the Taft-Hartley Act. The Court held the order unconstitutional: the President's role as Commander in Chief does not include the power to seize private property in a labor dispute, and "the President's power to see that the laws are faithfully executed refutes the idea that he is to be a lawmaker."

### Jackson's three categories

1. **Authorized by Congress** (express or implied). The President's authority is at its maximum, because it includes all he has in his own right plus all Congress can delegate. Such action is supported by the strongest presumption of validity.
2. **Congress silent.** The President relies on his own independent powers, but there is a "zone of twilight" in which he and Congress may have concurrent authority or its distribution is uncertain. The result turns on "the imperatives of events and contemporary imponderables" rather than abstract theories. Congressional acquiescence in long practice matters.
3. **Contrary to the expressed or implied will of Congress.** The President's power is at its "lowest ebb": he may rely only on his own constitutional powers minus Congress's, and the action is valid only if the subject is exclusively within his power and beyond Congress's control.

### Cases applying the framework

- *Dames & Moore v. Regan* (1981): the President's suspension of claims against a foreign state, to carry out an executive agreement, was upheld; statutes gave partial authorization, and Congress had long acquiesced in claims settlements (category 2 leaning toward 1).
- *Medellín v. Texas* (2008): the President could not enforce an international court judgment against the states by memorandum, where the treaty was not self-executing and Congress had not acted.
- *Zivotofsky v. Kerry* (2015): the President has the exclusive power to recognize foreign sovereigns, so a statute requiring the State Department to list "Israel" as the birthplace on passports was invalid (category 3 with an exclusive power).
- *Hamdi v. Rumsfeld* (2004) and *Boumediene v. Bush* (2008): even in wartime the executive's detention decisions are subject to due process and habeas review.
- *Trump v. Hawaii* (2018): a broad statute (8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)) authorized the President to suspend entry of noncitizens, so the order was upheld deferentially.
- *NLRB v. Noel Canning* (2014): the Recess Appointments Clause presumptively covers only recesses of ten days or more; "pro forma" sessions are sessions.

### Statutory authority and major questions

When the President claims statutory authority, the first step is whether the statute authorizes the action. Under the **major questions doctrine** (*West Virginia v. EPA* (2022); *Biden v. Nebraska* (2023)), courts expect clear congressional authorization for executive action of vast economic and political significance. In *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* (February 2026, 6–3), the Court held that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorize tariffs. The majority relied on the statutory text; a plurality also applied the major questions doctrine. Because the power to impose taxes and tariffs belongs to Congress, the President needed clear statutory authority.

### Immunity and privilege

*United States v. Nixon* (1974) recognized executive privilege but held that a generalized claim must yield to a specific need for evidence in a criminal trial. *Clinton v. Jones* (1997) held that the President has no temporary immunity from civil suit over unofficial acts. *Trump v. United States* (2024) held that a former President has absolute immunity for acts within his core constitutional powers, presumptive immunity for official acts, and no immunity for unofficial acts.

### Lawmaking

*Clinton v. City of New York* (1998) struck down the Line Item Veto Act: the President may not cancel parts of enacted statutes, because Article I's bicameralism and presentment requirements (Article I, section 7) allow only approval or veto of a whole bill. A veto may be overridden by two thirds of each house.

### Policy

The framework distributes burdens: the President can act quickly when Congress agrees, but unilateral action against Congress's will faces strict review because accountability and the lawmaking process lie with Congress.

### Applying the framework

(1) Identify the source of authority claimed. (2) Check whether a statute authorizes the action; if so, category 1. (3) If Congress is silent, ask whether the President has independent power and any pattern of acquiescence (category 2). (4) If Congress has forbidden it or declined to authorize it, category 3, which requires an exclusive presidential power.

@example
title: A category three action
problem: Congress considers and rejects a bill authorizing the President to seize private factories during a labor dispute, and enacts other procedures instead. The President nonetheless orders the seizure of a major factory, citing his executive power and role as Commander in Chief. Is the order valid?
step: Source: no statute authorizes the seizure; Congress rejected it, so the action runs against Congress's will (category 3).
step: The President's power is at its lowest ebb; he may rely only on constitutional powers that Congress cannot limit.
step: Commander in Chief and the Take Care Clause do not give a power to seize private property in a labor dispute; seizure is a lawmaking function.
step: Conclusion: the order is invalid under *Youngstown*.

@example
title: Congressional acquiescence
problem: The President suspends pending claims in U.S. courts against a foreign government as part of an executive agreement that releases hostages. No statute directly authorizes this, but Congress has long accepted presidential settlement of claims. Is the action valid?
step: Source: no express authorization, but related statutes and a history of acquiescence; category 2 leaning toward category 1.
step: The practice shows Congress has accepted claims settlement as an aspect of foreign relations.
step: Conclusion: valid, as in *Dames & Moore*.

@mistakes
- Treating the President's role as Commander in Chief or the Take Care Clause as a general lawmaking power.
- Skipping the first step: whether the statute actually authorizes the action.
- Placing every silent-Congress case in category 1; silence is category 2.
- Forgetting that category 3 can still be valid if the power is exclusively the President's (Zivotofsky).
