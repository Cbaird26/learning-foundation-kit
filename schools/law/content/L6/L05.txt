@article
Article I, section 8, clause 1 gives Congress power "to lay and collect Taxes, Duties, Imposts and Excises, to pay the Debts and provide for the common Defence and general Welfare of the United States." Two distinct powers live here: the **taxing power**, and the **spending power**. Each lets Congress shape conduct that it could not regulate directly.

### The taxing power

Constraints are textual: duties, imposts and excises must be uniform throughout the United States; "direct" taxes must be apportioned among the states by population (Article I, sections 2 and 9), and the Sixteenth Amendment removed apportionment for taxes on incomes. Beyond that, a tax does not become invalid because it also regulates. *Sonzinsky v. United States* (1937) upheld a modest license tax on firearms dealers even though it discouraged the trade, since it raised revenue and was not obviously a penalty.

The limit is that Congress may not call a **penalty** a tax. *Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co.* (1922) struck down a tax on child-labor employers that functioned as a penalty for violating a regulation. In *NFIB v. Sebelius* (2012), the controlling opinion upheld the individual-mandate's "shared responsibility payment" as a tax because it functioned as one: it was a modest amount (less than the cost of insurance, so not prohibitory), had no scienter requirement, was paid to the Treasury with the tax return and collected by the IRS, and produced revenue. Labels are not controlling.

### Tariffs and tax power

Tariffs are taxes, and the taxing power belongs to Congress. In *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* (2026), the Court held 6–3 that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorize tariffs. The majority relied on the statutory text; a plurality also applied the major questions doctrine (Lesson 10).

### The spending power

In *United States v. Butler* (1936), the Court accepted that Congress's power to spend for the general welfare is not limited to the other enumerated powers, and courts defer greatly to Congress's judgment of what serves the general welfare. Congress may attach **conditions** to federal money that it could not impose directly. *South Dakota v. Dole* (1987) upheld a statute withholding five percent of a state's federal highway funds unless the state raised its drinking age to twenty-one. Under the **Dole test**, conditions on federal funds must:

1. serve the **general welfare**;
2. be **unambiguous**, so that states can knowingly decide whether to accept (*Pennhurst State School v. Halderman* (1981));
3. be **related** to the federal interest in particular national projects or programs;
4. not be barred by **other constitutional provisions** (Congress may not use spending to induce unconstitutional conduct); and
5. not be so **coercive** that "pressure turns into compulsion."

*NFIB* found a violation of the fifth limit: Congress threatened states with the loss of all of their existing Medicaid funding if they declined to adopt a major expansion. The funds at stake were a large part of state budgets (more than ten percent of a typical state's overall budget), so the expansion was effectively a command. The remedy was narrow: Congress may not withhold existing Medicaid funds, but new funds may be conditioned.

### Contract analogy and enforcement

Spending-clause statutes operate like contracts, which is why the clear-notice rule exists. In *Medina v. Planned Parenthood South Atlantic* (2025), the Court held 6–3 that the Medicaid "any qualified provider" provision does not clearly and unambiguously create individual rights enforceable by patients under 42 U.S.C. § 1983. Private plaintiffs can use § 1983 for a spending-clause statute only if it unambiguously confers individual rights, and such statutes are usually enforced by the funding agency withholding funds.

### Policy

The spending power gives Congress flexibility and states a choice, but it can pressure states without commandeering them (Lesson 6); the coercion limit marks where encouragement ends.

### Applying the test

For a grant condition, work through the five *Dole* factors in order. Condition ambiguity and relatedness are common exam traps. For coercion, compare the money at stake with the state's budget and whether the funds already exist.

@example
title: A highway-funds condition
problem: Congress withholds five percent of a state's federal highway funds unless the state adopts a mandatory helmet law for motorcyclists. State challenges the condition. Is it valid?
step: General welfare: highway safety serves the general welfare, and courts defer.
step: Clarity: the statute states the condition and the penalty, so the state can choose knowingly.
step: Relatedness: helmet laws relate to the safety of highway travel, a federal interest in the highway program. No independent bar exists, because helmet laws are not themselves unconstitutional.
step: Coercion: losing five percent is a mild encouragement, as in *Dole*. Conclusion: the condition is valid.

@example
title: A coercive condition
problem: Congress offers states new funds for a health program, and says a state that does not adopt the new program loses all existing funding for the existing program, which makes up more than a tenth of the state's budget. Is the condition valid?
step: The condition is clear and relates to the program, but the question is coercion.
step: *NFIB* held that threatening the loss of existing funds that represent a large part of the state's budget is a gun to the head, not an offer.
step: Conclusion: the condition is coercive and invalid as to existing funds; Congress may still condition the new funds.

@mistakes
- Treating Congress's spending power as limited to subjects it could regulate directly; *Butler* and *Dole* reject that.
- Forgetting that labels do not control: a payment collected by the IRS that functions as a tax can be a tax.
- Applying coercion to any sizeable condition; it requires pressure that is effectively compulsion, such as loss of existing funds that amount to a large share of the state's budget.
- Assuming individuals may sue to enforce any federal funding statute under § 1983.
