@review
## Week-by-week review

1. **Judicial review.** *Marbury* (1803): a statute conflicting with the Constitution is void; Congress cannot enlarge Article III's original jurisdiction. Limits: case or controversy, adequate and independent state grounds, and the Exceptions Clause (*McCardle*; *Klein*).
2. **Justiciability.** Standing (injury in fact, causation, redressability; *Lujan*, *TransUnion*, *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*), taxpayer and third-party rules, ripeness (*Abbott Labs*), mootness (voluntary cessation, capable of repetition), political questions (*Baker*, *Rucho*), and relief limited by *Trump v. CASA* (2025).
3. **McCulloch.** Enumerated powers, implied means under the Necessary and Proper Clause (*Comstock*), no freestanding power (*NFIB*), and intergovernmental immunity.
4. **Commerce.** Channels, instrumentalities, and substantial effects; aggregation (*Wickard*, *Raich*); limits (*Lopez*, *Morrison*); no power to compel commerce (*NFIB*).
5. **Taxing and spending.** Tax versus penalty; the five *Dole* conditions; coercion (*NFIB*); *Medina* (2025) on private enforcement; *Learning Resources* (2026) on tariffs.
6. **Tenth Amendment.** *New York*, *Printz* and *Murphy* bar commandeering; generally applicable laws (*Garcia*), funding conditions and state courts (*Testa*) are allowed.
7. **Section 5 and the Civil War amendments.** *Boerne*'s congruence and proportionality; *Kimel*, *Garrett*, *Hibbs*, *Lane*; the Thirteenth Amendment reaches private conduct; *Shelby County*, *Callais* (2026).
8. **Dormant commerce clause.** Discrimination is virtually per se invalid; neutral laws face *Pike* balancing; market participant, congressional authorization, *Tennessee Wine*, *Wayfair*, *National Pork Producers*.
9. **Preemption.** Express, field, conflict; presumption against preemption; *Arizona*, *Wyeth*, *Mensing*, *Riegel*; agencies get *Skidmore* weight after *Loper Bright* (2024).
10. **Presidential power.** *Youngstown* categories; *Dames & Moore*, *Zivotofsky*; major questions; *Learning Resources* (2026); *Trump v. United States* (2024).
11. **Appointments, removal, nondelegation.** *Lucia*, *Arthrex*; *Humphrey's Executor* overruled by *Trump v. Slaughter* (2026); *Chadha*; intelligible principle (*FCC v. Consumers' Research*).
12. **State action.** Public function (*Marsh*, *Halleck*), entanglement and joint action (*Lugar*, *Brentwood*), *Lindke* (2024).

## Common errors

- Answering the merits before justiciability.
- Treating the Necessary and Proper Clause or the Tenth Amendment as a source of power.
- Using *Pike* balancing on a facially discriminatory law.
- Treating *Humphrey's Executor* or *Chevron* as current law.
- Treating regulation or funding as enough for state action.

@exam mc
prompt: Congress, relying on its power over interstate commerce, enacts a statute with two provisions: (1) every business that ships chemical fertilizer across state lines must register with a federal agency, and (2) each state's chief environmental officer must inspect fertilizer warehouses at state expense and report to the agency. A state officer and a shipping company challenge the statute. What is the best analysis?
choice: Both provisions are valid, because the Supremacy Clause makes federal law binding on state officers.
choice: Both provisions are invalid, because the Commerce Clause does not reach fertilizer.
choice: Only the registration provision is invalid, because registration burdens private businesses.
choice*: The registration provision is likely valid as regulation of persons and things in interstate commerce, and the inspection provision is likely invalid as commandeering state executive officers.
hint: Treat each provision separately: who does each one regulate?
hint: Recall Lopez for the first and Printz for the second.
solution: Registration of businesses shipping goods across state lines regulates activity in interstate commerce, which the commerce power covers. A command to state officers to administer a federal program is commandeering (Printz; New York). The Supremacy Clause does not authorize commands to state officers, and nothing in the statute's facts takes fertilizer outside the commerce power.

@exam self
prompt: A federal law bans possession of a certain pesticide by anyone, as part of a comprehensive scheme regulating the national pesticide market, and has a clause preempting any state labeling requirement "different from or in addition to" the federal label. Farmer, who mixes the pesticide at home for use on her own crops and sells none, is charged. State separately requires a warning on the label that the federal label does not include, and taxes out-of-state pesticide sellers at double the rate for in-state sellers. Farmer and an out-of-state seller sue. Write an organized analysis of (a) the federal ban as applied to Farmer, (b) preemption of the state warning, and (c) the state tax.
hint: Take each question separately and use IRAC for each.
solution: (a) Federal ban: the commerce power covers intrastate economic activity that in the aggregate substantially affects interstate commerce, with a rational basis (Wickard), including local activity that is part of a comprehensive economic regulatory scheme (Raich); the ban is part of a scheme regulating a national market, so it is likely valid as applied even though Farmer sells nothing (contrast Lopez and Morrison). (b) Preemption: the clause expressly preempts any state labeling requirement different from or in addition to the federal one; a state warning that the federal label does not require is such a requirement, and the presumption against preemption cannot override the clear text; the state warning is likely preempted (cf. Riegel). (c) The tax: charging out-of-state sellers double the rate discriminates against interstate commerce in effect and on its face, which is virtually per se invalid unless it advances a legitimate local purpose that cannot be served by nondiscriminatory alternatives (Philadelphia v. New Jersey); protecting local sellers is not such a purpose, and no exception (market participant or congressional authorization) is described, so the tax is likely invalid. Standing: Farmer is charged and the seller pays the tax, so each has an injury, causation and redressability for the claim that concerns them.
rubric: Applies Wickard and Raich to the federal ban and distinguishes Lopez and Morrison.
rubric: Identifies the express preemption clause and concludes that the extra warning is preempted.
rubric: Applies the discrimination track of the dormant commerce clause to the tax and checks the exceptions.
rubric: Notes that each plaintiff has standing for the claim that affects it and states clear conclusions.
