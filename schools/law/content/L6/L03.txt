@article
The federal government is one of **enumerated powers**: it may act only if the Constitution grants authority. States hold the residual **police power** over health, safety and welfare. This is why every federal statute in an exam question needs a source of power, and why *McCulloch v. Maryland* (1819) is the starting point.

### The case

Congress chartered the Second Bank of the United States. Maryland imposed a tax on banks not chartered by the state, aimed at the Bank's branch. The Bank's cashier refused to pay. The Court decided two questions.

**1. Could Congress charter a bank?** Yes. The Constitution does not list a bank, but the Necessary and Proper Clause (Article I, section 8, clause 18) lets Congress make laws "necessary and proper" for carrying into execution its enumerated powers (taxing, borrowing, regulating commerce, raising armies). "Necessary" does not mean absolutely indispensable; it means convenient, useful or conducive. The Court stated the test: "Let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the constitution, and all means which are appropriate, which are plainly adapted to that end, which are not prohibited, but consist with the letter and spirit of the constitution, are constitutional." The Court also noted that the Tenth Amendment, unlike the Articles of Confederation, does not say "expressly delegated."

**2. Could Maryland tax the Bank?** No. The Constitution and laws made under it are supreme (Article VI), and "the power to tax involves the power to destroy." A state may not tax or otherwise burden a federal instrumentality in a way that would let it control or destroy a federal operation, because the people of one state cannot tax the people of all.

### Modern rules

- **Rationality review of means.** *United States v. Comstock* (2010) upheld federal civil commitment of sexually dangerous federal prisoners past their release dates. The Court asked whether the statute constitutes a means rationally related to the implementation of a constitutionally enumerated power, considering the clause's breadth, the long history of federal involvement, the interest in protecting the public, and the statute's accommodation of state interests.
- **The clause is not a freestanding power.** *NFIB v. Sebelius* (2012) held that Congress could not rely on the Commerce Clause or the Necessary and Proper Clause to require individuals to buy health insurance, because the clause supports laws that are "necessary and proper" to an enumerated power, not an independent power to create one, and a mandate to enter commerce is not a "proper" exercise of the commerce power. (The same decision upheld the mandate's penalty under the taxing power, the subject of Lesson 5.)
- **No general police power.** Congress may not enact a law just because it addresses a local problem; it needs an enumerated power to anchor it.
- **Intergovernmental immunity.** States may not directly tax the federal government or discriminate against it or those who deal with it. A nondiscriminatory, generally applicable tax, such as a state income tax on the salaries of federal employees, is allowed, and Congress may consent to or waive immunity.

### Constitutional amendment

Article V is the structural counterpart: an amendment may be proposed by two thirds of both houses (or a convention called by two thirds of the states) and ratified by three fourths of the states. Federalism appears in the states' control over ratification.

### Policy

Enumeration protects state autonomy and diffuses power; a broad Necessary and Proper Clause gives the national government room to adapt. *McCulloch* chose flexibility for means while keeping the list of ends limited.

### Applying the rules

For any federal law: (1) identify the enumerated power; (2) if the law is not itself a regulation under that power, ask whether it is a rational, appropriate means to carry out the power (*Comstock*); (3) check that the law does not itself create a new power or is "improper" (*NFIB*). For any state burden on the federal government: ask whether it taxes the United States directly, discriminates, or is a generally applicable neutral measure.

@example
title: Necessary and proper means
problem: Congress, exercising its power to spend for the general welfare, makes it a federal crime to bribe an official of an organization that receives substantial federal funds. Defendant, charged under the statute, argues Congress has no power to punish local bribery. Is the statute valid?
step: Identify the power: the Spending Clause lets Congress provide federal funds, an enumerated power.
step: Ask whether the criminal statute is a means to carry out that power. Protecting federal funds from corruption in recipient organizations is plainly adapted to the spending power.
step: Under McCulloch and Comstock, the means need only be rationally related to an enumerated power and not prohibited.
step: Conclusion: the statute is likely a valid use of the Necessary and Proper Clause.

@example
title: A discriminatory state tax
problem: State imposes a tax on the notes issued by a federally chartered bank and exempts notes issued by state-chartered banks. Is the tax valid?
step: Identify the actor burdened: a federally chartered bank, a federal instrumentality.
step: McCulloch bars a state from taxing a federal instrumentality in a way that burdens it and discriminates against it.
step: The tax singles out the federal bank by exempting state banks, so it is discriminatory as well as a direct tax.
step: Conclusion: the tax is invalid under the Supremacy Clause.

@mistakes
- Reading "necessary" to mean absolutely indispensable; *McCulloch* says convenient and useful.
- Treating the Necessary and Proper Clause as an independent source of power; it needs an enumerated power to support (*NFIB*).
- Saying states can never tax anything connected to the federal government; nondiscriminatory taxes are generally permitted.
- Believing Congress has a general police power over local matters.
