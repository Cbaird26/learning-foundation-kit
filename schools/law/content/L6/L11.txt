@article
The Constitution divides power among the branches and gives each tools to protect itself. This lesson covers three structural questions: who may appoint federal officers, who may remove them, and how much lawmaking power Congress may hand to agencies.

### Appointments

Under Article II, section 2, clause 2, the President nominates, and with the Senate's advice and consent appoints, ambassadors, judges and other **principal officers**. Congress may vest the appointment of **inferior officers** in the President alone, the courts of law or the heads of departments. *Buckley v. Valeo* (1976) holds that an "Officer of the United States" is anyone exercising significant authority under federal law pursuant to a continuing position; mere employees are not officers. *Lucia v. SEC* (2018) held that SEC administrative law judges were "Officers of the United States" (the government treated them as inferior officers) who must be appointed by the Commission, not staff, because they hold a continuing position and exercise significant discretion (taking testimony, ruling on evidence and issuing decisions). *Edmond v. United States* (1997): an inferior officer is one whose work is directed and supervised by a principal officer. *United States v. Arthrex, Inc.* (2021) held that patent judges who could issue final decisions unreviewable by any principal officer were exercising power incompatible with inferior-officer status, and cured the problem by making their decisions reviewable by the agency head. A remedy for an Appointments Clause violation is typically a new hearing before a properly appointed officer.

### Removal

*Myers v. United States* (1926) held that the President has the unrestricted power to remove executive officers who assist him. *Humphrey's Executor v. United States* (1935) permitted removal limits for members of a multimember, expert, bipartisan commission. The Court narrowed it in *Free Enterprise Fund v. PCAOB* (2010) (two layers of for-cause protection are invalid), *Seila Law LLC v. CFPB* (2020) (a single director removable only for cause is invalid) and *Collins v. Yellen* (2021) (the same for the FHFA director, with a remedy only on showing that the removal limit caused harm).

**Trump v. Slaughter** (June 2026, 6–3) overruled *Humphrey's Executor*. The Court held that the FTC Act's for-cause removal protection is unconstitutional, so Federal Trade Commissioners are removable at will, because the Constitution vests the executive power in the President. The Court left open the status of the Federal Reserve, and in *Trump v. Cook* (2026) it refused to allow the removal of a Federal Reserve governor to take effect while that case continues. It also left open offices outside the traditional executive branch. Justice Sotomayor, joined by Justices Kagan and Jackson, dissented and would have kept *Humphrey's Executor*. *Morrison v. Olson* (1988) upheld for-cause protection of an independent counsel with limited duties; *Slaughter* did not decide whether *Morrison* remains good law.

### Congress's limits on itself

- **Legislative veto.** *INS v. Chadha* (1983) held that a one-house veto of an executive decision is unconstitutional because it is lawmaking without bicameralism and presentment.
- **Congressional control of officers.** *Bowsher v. Synar* (1986): Congress may not give a legislative officer, removable by Congress, executive power.
- **Article III.** *SEC v. Jarkesy* (2024) held that the Seventh Amendment requires a jury when the SEC seeks civil penalties for securities fraud, so the agency cannot adjudicate such claims in-house under the public-rights doctrine.

### Nondelegation

Article I vests "all legislative Powers" in Congress. Congress may delegate if it lays down an **intelligible principle** (*J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States* (1928)). The Court has invalidated statutes on this ground only twice, in 1935 (*Panama Refining*; *Schechter Poultry*), and has upheld broad standards such as "requisite to protect the public health" (*Whitman v. American Trucking Ass'ns* (2001)) and the Sentencing Commission's guidelines authority in *Mistretta v. United States* (1989). *Gundy v. United States* (2019) upheld a delegation in a plurality opinion; several Justices have signaled interest in a stricter rule. In *FCC v. Consumers' Research* (2025), the Court upheld the Universal Service Fund's funding mechanism against a nondelegation challenge under the intelligible-principle test.

The **major questions doctrine** operates as a rule of statutory interpretation: courts require clear congressional authorization for agency action of vast economic and political significance (*West Virginia v. EPA* (2022); *Biden v. Nebraska* (2023); *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* (2026)). After *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo* (2024), which overruled *Chevron*, courts decide the best reading of a statute themselves and enforce the limits of any statutory delegation, giving agency views *Skidmore* respect.

### Policy

Appointments and removal rules ensure accountability to the President and, through him, the electorate; independent agencies are defended as insulating expert decisions from politics. Nondelegation protects representative lawmaking, but practicality has led courts to defer.

### Applying the rules

Appointments: (1) Is the person an officer (significant authority, continuing position)? (2) Principal or inferior? (3) Was the appointment made by the right actor? Removal: is the officer one the President must be able to remove at will? Delegation: is there an intelligible principle, and does the agency's action rest on clear statutory authority?

@example
title: Appointing an administrative law judge
problem: An agency's staff hires an administrative law judge who conducts hearings, takes testimony, rules on evidence and issues initial decisions that become final unless the agency head reviews them. Respondent, ordered to pay a penalty, argues the judge was not properly appointed. Is the argument sound?
step: Officer status: a continuing position with significant authority (taking testimony, ruling on evidence, issuing decisions) makes the judge an officer under *Buckley* and *Lucia*.
step: Inferior officer: the agency head can review the decisions, so the judge is an inferior officer.
step: Appointer: inferior officers must be appointed by the President, a court of law or a department head, not by staff.
step: Conclusion: the appointment violates the Appointments Clause; the usual remedy is a new hearing before a properly appointed officer.

@example
title: Removal of a commissioner
problem: A statute lets the President remove members of a five-member trade commission only for inefficiency, neglect of duty or malfeasance. The President removes a commissioner over a policy disagreement. Is the removal valid?
step: Under the earlier rule, *Humphrey's Executor* allowed such protection for multimember expert commissions.
step: *Trump v. Slaughter* (2026) overruled *Humphrey's Executor* and held the FTC Act's for-cause protection unconstitutional.
step: The same reasoning applies to a trade commission exercising executive power.
step: Conclusion: the removal is valid; the open questions concern the Federal Reserve and offices outside the traditional executive branch.

@mistakes
- Teaching *Humphrey's Executor* as current law; it was overruled in *Trump v. Slaughter* (2026).
- Saying *Slaughter* decided the Federal Reserve question; it left it open.
- Treating every agency adjudicator as an employee; those with significant authority and a continuing position are officers.
- Claiming the nondelegation doctrine is dead; courts still require an intelligible principle and clear authorization for major questions.
