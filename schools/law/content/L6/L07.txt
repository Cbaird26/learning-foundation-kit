@article
Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment gives Congress "power to enforce, by appropriate legislation, the provisions of this article." It is a source of federal power that reaches state governments directly. The central question is how far Congress may go beyond the courts' own reading of the Amendment.

### The rule

*Katzenbach v. Morgan* (1966) suggested that Congress might have power to interpret the Amendment's guarantees. *City of Boerne v. Flores* (1997) rejected that reading. Congress may **enforce** the Amendment, which includes remedial and **prophylactic** legislation that prohibits some conduct the Amendment itself does not forbid, but it may not change the Amendment's **substantive meaning**. Otherwise the Constitution would be alterable by ordinary statute. The test: there must be **congruence and proportionality** between the injury to be prevented or remedied (a pattern of unconstitutional state conduct) and the means adopted. Congress should identify a history of constitutional violations, and the remedy should not be out of proportion to the problem.

*Boerne* itself invalidated the Religious Freedom Restoration Act as applied to the states. After *Employment Division v. Smith* (1990) held that neutral, generally applicable laws need not satisfy strict scrutiny under the Free Exercise Clause, Congress required strict scrutiny of all substantial burdens on religion. The Court found no pattern of religious bigotry in state laws and a sweeping remedy; the statute redefined the right and was not congruent and proportional.

### Applications

| Case | Statute | Result | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Kimel v. Florida Board of Regents* (2000) | ADEA, damages against states | Invalid | Age gets only rational-basis review; little record of irrational age discrimination by states |
| *Board of Trustees v. Garrett* (2001) | ADA Title I, employment | Invalid as to damages against states | Disability gets rational-basis review; thin record of unconstitutional state conduct |
| *Nevada Dept. of Human Resources v. Hibbs* (2003) | FMLA family-care leave | Upheld | Sex discrimination gets heightened scrutiny; documented history of sex-stereotyped leave |
| *Tennessee v. Lane* (2004) | ADA Title II, access to courts | Upheld as applied | Implicates fundamental rights such as access to courts, with a record of exclusion |

The less demanding the judicial standard for the right at stake, the harder it is to justify broad prophylaxis.

### Why it matters: sovereign immunity

*Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Florida* (1996) held that Congress cannot abrogate state sovereign immunity under Article I powers such as the commerce power. Section 5 is a main route, since the Fourteenth Amendment postdates the Eleventh and limits state power (*Fitzpatrick v. Bitzer* (1976)). This is why *Kimel*, *Garrett*, *Hibbs* and *Lane* turn on Section 5 validity.

### Private conduct

Section 5 reaches state action. *The Civil Rights Cases* (1883) held that Congress cannot use it to prohibit purely private discrimination, and *United States v. Morrison* (2000) reaffirmed that rule.

### The other Civil War amendments

- **Thirteenth Amendment, Section 2.** Congress may rationally determine the "badges and incidents of slavery" and may reach private conduct (*Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co.* (1968), upholding a federal ban on private racial discrimination in property sales).
- **Fifteenth Amendment, Section 2.** *South Carolina v. Katzenbach* (1966) upheld the Voting Rights Act of 1965 under a rationality standard in light of the history of voting discrimination. *Shelby County v. Holder* (2013) held that the Act's coverage formula, based on decades-old data, could no longer justify the preclearance requirement. In *Louisiana v. Callais* (2026), the Court held a congressional map to be an unconstitutional racial gerrymander because the Voting Rights Act did not require the second majority-minority district; because the statute did not require that district, race-based districting could not be justified as compliance with it. The holding concerns the map under equal protection and the statute's reach, not Congress's power under the Fifteenth Amendment.

The Fourteenth Amendment's Citizenship Clause is a related substantive guarantee: *Trump v. Barbara* (2026) held that children born in the United States to parents who are unlawfully or temporarily present are citizens at birth. Congress's enforcement power is measured against the meaning the Court gives the text.

### Policy

*Boerne* protects the Court's role in saying what the Constitution means and protects federalism, while still giving Congress room to address documented discrimination. Critics say it hampers Congress's access to the fact-finding that courts lack.

### Applying the test

(1) Identify the constitutional right and the level of scrutiny courts apply. (2) Identify the pattern of unconstitutional state conduct in the record. (3) Compare the statute's remedy with that harm. (4) If the statute abrogates immunity, state that Section 5 is required.

@example
title: Age discrimination and states
problem: Congress authorizes damages suits against state employers for age discrimination, relying on Section 5. Employee sues State. State asserts sovereign immunity and challenges the abrogation. Is the abrogation valid?
step: Identify the right: equal protection, but age classifications receive only rational-basis review.
step: Record: there is little evidence of unconstitutional, irrational age discrimination by states.
step: Compare: the statute prohibits much conduct that would be constitutional under rational-basis review, so it is not congruent or proportional (*Kimel*).
step: Conclusion: the abrogation is invalid, so State is immune from damages (Employee may have other remedies, such as injunctive suits against officers).

@example
title: A statute that changes the meaning of a right
problem: Congress requires all states to justify any law that substantially burdens religion with strict scrutiny, citing Section 5. A state challenges it. Is the statute valid as applied to the states?
step: Free Exercise doctrine after *Smith* applies no heightened scrutiny to neutral laws of general applicability.
step: The statute imposes a different substantive standard rather than remedying a pattern of violations.
step: Conclusion: under *Boerne*, it lacks congruence and proportionality and is invalid as applied to states.

@mistakes
- Saying Congress can define the content of Fourteenth Amendment rights; *Boerne* holds it may only enforce them.
- Forgetting that the level of scrutiny courts give the right sets how much prophylaxis is justified (*Kimel* versus *Hibbs*).
- Applying Section 5 to private discrimination; the Thirteenth Amendment may reach private conduct, but Section 5 may not.
- Ignoring the sovereign-immunity setting that makes Section 5 matter.
