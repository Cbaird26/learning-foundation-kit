@article
The Commerce Clause gives Congress power over interstate commerce. The Court has long read it to carry a negative implication, the **dormant commerce clause**: states may not unduly burden or discriminate against interstate commerce, even when Congress has not acted. The rationale is to prevent economic protectionism and preserve a national market.

### The two-track test

**Track 1: discrimination.** A state law that discriminates against interstate commerce, in text, purpose or effect (differentially treating in-state and out-of-state economic interests so as to benefit the former), is **virtually per se invalid**. It survives only if it serves a legitimate local purpose that cannot be served by reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives. *Philadelphia v. New Jersey* (1978) struck down a ban on importing out-of-state solid waste. *Hughes v. Oklahoma* (1979) invalidated a bar on exporting minnows. *Dean Milk Co. v. Madison* (1951) struck a rule that effectively kept out milk processed far from the city. *Granholm v. Heald* (2005) held that allowing in-state wineries but not out-of-state wineries to ship directly to consumers violates the clause, and the Twenty-first Amendment does not save it. *Tennessee Wine and Spirits Retailers Association v. Thomas* (2019) struck a requirement that an applicant for a retail liquor license be a state resident for two years, holding that the Twenty-first Amendment did not shield protectionism.

**Track 2: undue burden (Pike balancing).** A nondiscriminatory law that regulates evenhandedly and has only incidental effects on interstate commerce is valid unless the burden is **clearly excessive in relation to the putative local benefits** (*Pike v. Bruce Church, Inc.* (1970)). This track rarely succeeds, though trucking and railroad regulations have fallen when they imposed heavy burdens for small safety benefits (*Kassel v. Consolidated Freightways* (1981)).

*National Pork Producers Council v. Ross* (2023) upheld California's Proposition 12 against a challenge that it had impermissible extraterritorial effects. The Court rejected a per se rule against state laws with out-of-state effects, since the clause targets discrimination, and, without a single majority rationale, rejected the *Pike* balancing claim: some justices found that no substantial burden on interstate commerce was plausibly alleged, and others doubted that courts can weigh such burdens against a state's moral and health interests.

### State taxes

Under *Complete Auto Transit v. Brady* (1977), a state tax on interstate commerce is valid if it applies to an activity with a **substantial nexus** to the state, is **fairly apportioned**, does **not discriminate**, and is **fairly related** to the services the state provides. *South Dakota v. Wayfair, Inc.* (2018) overruled the physical-presence rule of *Quill* and held that economic and virtual contacts, such as sales volume and number of transactions, can supply a substantial nexus.

### Exceptions

- **Market participant.** A state acting as a buyer or seller, not a regulator, may prefer its own citizens (*Hughes v. Alexandria Scrap* (1976); *Reeves, Inc. v. Stake* (1980)). The exception does not allow a state to regulate downstream markets.
- **Congressional authorization.** Congress may authorize what would otherwise be discriminatory, if it makes its intent unmistakably clear.
- **Public facilities.** Laws favoring a government-owned facility that treat in-state and out-of-state private businesses alike are valid (*United Haulers Association v. Oneida-Herkimer Authority* (2007)), unlike laws favoring private in-state businesses (*C & A Carbone v. Clarkstown* (1994)).

### Privileges and Immunities Clause (Article IV, section 2)

States may not discriminate against nonresidents with respect to fundamental privileges such as the right to pursue a livelihood, unless there is a substantial reason and a close relation between the discrimination and the state's goal (*United Building & Construction Trades Council v. Mayor of Camden* (1984)). Corporations are not protected "citizens." The clause protects individuals; the dormant commerce clause protects economic actors including corporations.

### Policy

The doctrine guards national economic union and protects out-of-state interests that cannot vote in the state. Critics say it lacks textual support, and some Justices would confine it to discrimination.

### Applying the test

(1) Does the law discriminate (facially, in purpose, or in effect)? If yes, ask for a legitimate local purpose that cannot be served by nondiscriminatory alternatives. (2) If neutral, apply *Pike*. (3) Check exceptions: market participant, congressional authorization, public-facility rule. (4) For individuals, consider the Privileges and Immunities Clause.

@example
title: A facially discriminatory law
problem: State bans the sale of fish caught outside the state, to protect local fishers. Dealer, who sells fish caught in another state, challenges the law. Is it valid?
step: The law treats in-state and out-of-state fish differently on its face, so it discriminates.
step: Discriminatory laws are virtually per se invalid unless they serve a legitimate local purpose that cannot be served by nondiscriminatory alternatives.
step: Protecting local fishers from competition is economic protectionism, which is not a legitimate local purpose.
step: Conclusion: the law is invalid under the dormant commerce clause.

@example
title: Market participant
problem: State runs its own cement plant and, during a shortage, sells cement only to in-state buyers. An out-of-state buyer sues under the dormant commerce clause. Is the preference valid?
step: The state is acting as a seller in a market it participates in, not regulating private sellers.
step: Under *Reeves*, a state may prefer its own citizens when acting as a market participant.
step: Conclusion: the preference is valid (the buyer could raise other claims).

@mistakes
- Applying *Pike* balancing to a facially discriminatory law; discrimination triggers a stricter test.
- Treating any burden on interstate commerce as invalid; evenhanded laws survive unless clearly excessive.
- Forgetting the exceptions, especially the market participant and congressional authorization.
- Assuming the Twenty-first Amendment lets states discriminate in alcohol markets (*Granholm*; *Tennessee Wine*).
