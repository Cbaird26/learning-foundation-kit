@article
**Justiciability** is the set of doctrines that decide whether a federal court may hear a dispute at all. They rest on Article III's limit to "cases" and "controversies" and on prudential concerns. Courts address them before the merits.

### Standing

A plaintiff must show three things (*Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife* (1992)):

1. **Injury in fact**: an invasion of a legally protected interest that is concrete and particularized, and actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.
2. **Causation**: the injury is fairly traceable to the defendant's challenged conduct, not to an independent third party's choices.
3. **Redressability**: a favorable decision is likely to remedy the injury.

The plaintiff bears the burden, and standing is assessed claim by claim and for each form of relief. *Spokeo, Inc. v. Robins* (2016) and *TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez* (2021) hold that a statutory violation alone does not create standing; the harm must be concrete (physical, monetary, or with a close relationship to a harm traditionally recognized, such as reputational harm). Under *TransUnion*, a risk of future harm does not support damages unless the harm materializes, though it can support injunctive relief when imminent. *Clapper v. Amnesty International USA* (2013) requires a threatened injury that is "certainly impending" (or a substantial risk).

*Lujan* itself held that "someday" intentions to visit a place, with no concrete plans, are not actual or imminent injury.

### Special situations

- **Generalized grievances.** A citizen's interest in seeing the law followed is not enough. Taxpayer standing is generally unavailable (*Frothingham*); the narrow exception in *Flast v. Cohen* (1968) covers challenges to congressional spending under the Establishment Clause, and *Hein v. Freedom From Religion Foundation* (2007) refused to extend it to executive spending from general appropriations.
- **Organizations.** An association may sue for its members if members would have standing, the interests are germane, and individual participation is unnecessary (*Hunt v. Washington State Apple Advertising Commission* (1977)). In *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* (2024), the Court held that doctors and medical groups lacked standing to challenge an FDA drug approval, and that an organization's diverted resources to oppose a policy are not a cognizable injury.
- **Third parties.** Ordinarily a plaintiff may assert only her own rights. Exceptions require a close relationship with the right-holder and a hindrance to the right-holder's own suit.
- **Non-prosecution.** A private person has no judicially cognizable interest in the prosecution or non-prosecution of another (*Linda R.S. v. Richard D.* (1973)); *United States v. Texas* (2023) held that states lacked standing to challenge federal arrest and prosecution priorities.
- **States.** States may receive "special solicitude" for certain injuries (*Massachusetts v. EPA* (2007)), but must still meet the three elements.

### Remedies and CASA

In *Trump v. CASA, Inc.* (2025), the Court held that universal injunctions likely exceed the equitable authority Congress gave federal courts. Relief should be no broader than necessary to give the plaintiffs complete relief. The Court left class actions and other routes open.

### Ripeness and mootness

**Ripeness** asks whether a dispute is premature. Under *Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner* (1967), courts weigh the fitness of the issues for decision and the hardship of withholding review. **Mootness** asks whether the live controversy has ended. Exceptions: **voluntary cessation** (the defendant's stopping the conduct moots the case only if it is absolutely clear the conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur, *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw* (2000); *FBI v. Fikre* (2024)); **capable of repetition yet evading review** (the duration is too short to litigate and there is a reasonable expectation the same party faces it again); and class actions. *Corner Post, Inc. v. Board of Governors* (2024) adds a timing point: an APA claim accrues when the plaintiff is injured.

### Political questions

A claim is nonjusticiable if it presents a political question. *Baker v. Carr* (1962) lists factors, including a textually demonstrable commitment to a coordinate branch, a lack of judicially discoverable and manageable standards, and the impossibility of deciding without an initial policy determination. *Nixon v. United States* (1993) held the Senate's impeachment trial procedures nonjusticiable; *Rucho v. Common Cause* (2019) held partisan gerrymandering claims nonjusticiable. Not every foreign-affairs case is political: *Zivotofsky v. Clinton* (2012) allowed review of a passport statute.

### Applying the rules

For each plaintiff and each remedy: injury, cause, redress. Then ask whether a special rule applies, and whether timing or a political question bars review.

@example
title: Standing for a plaintiff challenging a regulation
problem: A federal agency bans a pesticide that Farmer uses on her own crops, and she can no longer buy it. She sues the agency, arguing the ban is unauthorized, and asks the court to enjoin it. Does she have standing?
step: Injury in fact: she lost a product she uses and must pay more for alternatives, a concrete, particularized and actual economic injury.
step: Causation: the ban directly causes her loss; no independent third party stands in between.
step: Redressability: an injunction against enforcement would likely let her buy the pesticide again.
step: Conclusion: she has standing; her merits claim is separate. Any injunction should be limited to what gives her complete relief.

@example
title: Voluntary cessation
problem: Town repeals a sign ordinance one week after Merchant sues, but the council says it may bring it back. Is the suit moot?
step: The repeal ended the challenged conduct, so Town will argue mootness.
step: Under *Laidlaw*, a defendant claiming mootness by voluntary cessation must show it is absolutely clear the conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur.
step: The council's statement shows it could re-enact the ordinance.
step: Conclusion: the case is not moot and may proceed.

@mistakes
- Treating a statutory violation as automatically giving standing, ignoring the concreteness requirement of *Spokeo* and *TransUnion*.
- Assuming an organization has standing because it spent money opposing a policy; *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* rejects diversion of resources alone.
- Confusing ripeness (too early) with mootness (too late), and forgetting that voluntary cessation requires an absolutely clear showing.
- Calling any case touching foreign affairs a political question.

@quiz mc
prompt: Voters in State challenge the congressional district map as an extreme partisan gerrymander in violation of the federal Constitution. They have standing as voters in the affected districts. Should the federal court decide the claim on the merits?
choice: Yes, because a plaintiff with standing is always entitled to a decision on the merits.
choice: Yes, because Baker v. Carr established that all districting claims are justiciable.
choice*: No, because Rucho v. Common Cause held that federal courts cannot decide partisan gerrymandering claims; they are political questions.
choice: No, because voters lack standing to challenge district maps.
hint: Standing and the political question doctrine are separate hurdles.
solution: Rucho held that partisan gerrymandering claims present political questions beyond the reach of the federal courts, even though plaintiffs may have standing. Racial gerrymandering and one-person-one-vote claims remain justiciable.
