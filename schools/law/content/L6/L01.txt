@article
Constitutional Law I is about **structure**: who may exercise which government power, and which courts may say so. Every later lesson assumes the first one: federal courts can hold that an act of Congress, the President or a state violates the Constitution. That power is **judicial review**, and its canonical source is *Marbury v. Madison* (1803).

### The case

Marbury had been appointed a justice of the peace in the last hours of one administration, but the commission was never delivered. He asked the Supreme Court directly for a writ of mandamus compelling the new Secretary of State to deliver it, relying on section 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789, which seemed to authorize the Court to issue such writs in its original jurisdiction.

Chief Justice Marshall asked three questions in order. Did Marbury have a right to the commission? Yes: the appointment was complete when the President signed it and it was sealed. Did the law give him a remedy? Yes: where an official has a legal duty to a particular person, the law supplies a remedy, and this duty was ministerial, not political discretion. Could the Supreme Court issue the writ? No. Article III, section 2 gives the Court **original jurisdiction** only in cases affecting ambassadors, other public ministers and consuls, and cases in which a state is a party; in all other cases its jurisdiction is **appellate**. Section 13 purported to add to the original jurisdiction. Congress cannot enlarge the Court's original jurisdiction beyond what Article III lists, so that part of section 13 was void.

### The rule

The rule is the reasoning behind the Court's answer to the third question. The Constitution is written, fundamental and superior to ordinary legislation; an act repugnant to it is not law. Courts must decide cases according to law, so when a statute and the Constitution conflict, a court must apply the Constitution. In the Court's famous line, it is "emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is." Supporting textual points include the judicial power extending to cases "arising under this Constitution," the Supremacy Clause (Article VI) and the judicial oath.

### Extensions

- *Martin v. Hunter's Lessee* (1816) and *Cohens v. Virginia* (1821): the Supreme Court may review state-court decisions on federal law, which keeps federal law uniform.
- *Cooper v. Aaron* (1958): the Court described *Marbury* as establishing that the federal judiciary is supreme in expounding the Constitution, and held that state officials are bound by its decisions. Whether the other branches must treat the Court's readings as binding on them beyond the parties is debated; this course treats the holdings as the law of the cases.

### Limits on review

1. **Case or controversy.** Article III limits federal courts to cases and controversies, so they cannot issue advisory opinions (the next lesson develops justiciability).
2. **Adequate and independent state ground.** The Supreme Court will not review a state-court judgment that rests on a state-law ground that is independent of federal law and adequate to support the judgment, because the federal ruling could not change the result. Under *Michigan v. Long* (1983), the Court presumes jurisdiction unless the state court clearly says its decision rests on independent state grounds.
3. **Congress and appellate jurisdiction.** The Exceptions Clause lets Congress make "exceptions" to the Court's appellate jurisdiction. *Ex parte McCardle* (1869) accepted a repeal of a category of appellate jurisdiction, but *United States v. Klein* (1872) bars Congress from using jurisdictional limits to dictate the result of a pending case. Whether Congress could strip all review of constitutional claims is unresolved.

### Policy

Judicial review is defended as protecting a written constitution and individual rights from majorities, and criticized as counter-majoritarian because unelected judges override elected officials. Courts reduce that tension through justiciability doctrines and narrow rulings.

### Applying the rule

Ask: (1) Does the statute conflict with a provision of the Constitution? (2) Is there a case or controversy before a court with jurisdiction? (3) Does a state ground block review? A statute that adds to the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction is the *Marbury* pattern: the conclusion follows directly from Article III's list.

@example
title: A statute adds to original jurisdiction
problem: Congress enacts a statute that lets any person sue a federal agency for a declaratory judgment directly in the Supreme Court, as a case within the Court's original jurisdiction. Plaintiff files such a suit. May the Court hear it as an original action?
step: Issue: can Congress add to the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction beyond the categories in Article III, section 2?
step: Rule: original jurisdiction is limited to cases affecting ambassadors, other public ministers and consuls, and cases in which a state is a party. *Marbury* held that Congress cannot enlarge it.
step: Application: a suit by a private person against a federal agency fits none of those categories, so the statute purports to enlarge original jurisdiction.
step: Conclusion: the statute is unconstitutional as applied, and the Court should dismiss for lack of original jurisdiction. The plaintiff may sue in a district court and seek appellate review.

@example
title: A state court rests on state law
problem: A state supreme court holds that a police search violated both the Fourth Amendment and the state constitution, and states that it relies independently on the state constitution. The state petitions for certiorari. Will the Supreme Court decide the Fourth Amendment question?
step: Issue: does the Court have jurisdiction to review a state judgment resting on two grounds?
step: Rule: no review where the judgment rests on an adequate and independent state ground; *Michigan v. Long* requires a plain statement of that reliance.
step: Application: the state court clearly said the state constitution supports the result alone, so a federal ruling could not change the judgment.
step: Conclusion: the Court should decline review.

@mistakes
- Saying *Marbury* held that Congress cannot give the Supreme Court any jurisdiction; it held that Congress cannot enlarge the original jurisdiction Article III lists.
- Forgetting that Marbury had a right and a remedy; he lost only because the Court lacked jurisdiction to grant the writ.
- Assuming the Supreme Court can review every state-court ruling that mentions federal law, ignoring adequate and independent state grounds.
