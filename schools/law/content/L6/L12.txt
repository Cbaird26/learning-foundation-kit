@article
Most individual-rights guarantees (equal protection, due process, the First Amendment) restrict only government. The **state action doctrine** asks whether the challenged conduct is attributable to the government. The rule comes from *The Civil Rights Cases* (1883): the Fourteenth Amendment prohibits action by states, not private discrimination. The doctrine protects private liberty and preserves federalism by keeping most private wrongs under ordinary law. Exceptions: the Thirteenth Amendment reaches private conduct, and Congress may regulate private actors under the commerce power and other powers (Lessons 4 and 7).

### Exceptions: when private conduct is state action

1. **Public function.** A private entity performing a function **traditionally and exclusively** performed by government is a state actor. *Marsh v. Alabama* (1946) held that a company town with the characteristics of a municipality could not ban the distribution of religious literature. *Jackson v. Metropolitan Edison Co.* (1974) found no state action in a private utility's service termination, and *Flagg Brothers, Inc. v. Brooks* (1978) held that a private warehouseman's sale of goods under a state statute was not an exclusive public function. *Manhattan Community Access Corp. v. Halleck* (2019) held that a private nonprofit running public-access cable channels was not a state actor, because operating a private forum for speech is not a traditional, exclusive public function. Few functions qualify (running elections is one).
2. **Entanglement, symbiosis and compulsion.** The state is responsible if it has coerced or significantly encouraged the private decision, or become a joint participant. *Burton v. Wilmington Parking Authority* (1961) found state action in a restaurant leasing space in a publicly owned garage. By contrast, *Blum v. Yaretsky* (1982) held that state regulation and funding of nursing homes did not make their private decisions state action, and *Moose Lodge No. 107 v. Irvis* (1972) held that a state liquor license did not make a private club's discrimination state action.
3. **Joint action and state-created authority.** *Lugar v. Edmondson Oil Co.* (1982) states the two-part test for conduct that allegedly causes a deprivation of a federal right: (a) the deprivation must be caused by the exercise of a right or privilege created by the state or by a rule of conduct imposed by the state, and (b) the party charged must be a person who may fairly be said to be a state actor (e.g., because he is a state official, acted together with state officials or obtained significant aid from them). *West v. Atkins* (1988) held that a physician under contract to treat prison inmates acts under color of state law. *Edmonson v. Leesville Concrete Co.* (1991) held that private litigants using peremptory challenges in civil trials are state actors.
4. **Entwinement.** *Brentwood Academy v. Tennessee Secondary School Athletic Association* (2001) held that a nominally private athletic association was a state actor because of the pervasive entwinement of public schools and officials in its structure. By contrast, *NCAA v. Tarkanian* (1988) found no state action in a private association's discipline imposed through a university's decision, and *Rendell-Baker v. Kohn* (1982) found a private school's personnel decisions were not state action despite public funding.
5. **Government entities by statute.** *Lebron v. National Railroad Passenger Corp.* (1995) held that Amtrak is a government entity for First Amendment purposes because the government created it and controls its board.

### Judicial enforcement of private agreements

*Shelley v. Kraemer* (1948) held that a state court's enforcement of racially restrictive covenants was state action. The case is limited in practice; courts have not treated all judicial enforcement of private choices as state action.

### Officials and coercion

*Lindke v. Freed* (2024): a public official's social media activity is state action only if (1) the official had actual authority to speak on the state's behalf on the subject and (2) purported to exercise that authority in the posts. A personal page does not become official because the official posts about his job. *National Rifle Ass'n v. Vullo* (2024) is related: government officials may not coerce private parties to punish or suppress disfavored speech, and the Court looks at the conduct as a whole. *Moody v. NetChoice, LLC* (2024) confirms that a platform's curation of others' content is protected expressive activity, which is consistent with treating a platform's moderation decisions as its own choices, not government action (the case did not turn on state action).

### Policy

State action protects private autonomy and keeps courts out of private life. Critics say it lets government avoid constitutional duties by privatization, which is why entwinement and public function tests exist.

### Applying the rules

Ask: (1) Is the actor a government or a private entity? (2) Does it perform a traditional, exclusive public function? (3) Did the government coerce or significantly encourage the decision, or act jointly with the actor? (4) Is the entity so entwined with government that it is fairly seen as the state? (5) Use *Lugar* when a statutory or civil-rights claim is asserted.

@example
title: A company town
problem: A private company owns an entire town with homes, streets, stores and a sidewalk that serve as the business district, open to the public. It bans a resident from handing out leaflets on the sidewalk. Is the ban state action?
step: Candidate test: public function. The company performs all the functions of a municipality.
step: *Marsh v. Alabama* held such a town cannot be run in a manner that ignores First Amendment rights, because the property is open to the public and substitutes for a town.
step: Conclusion: state action; the ban violates the First Amendment.

@example
title: A regulated utility
problem: A heavily regulated private electric utility holds a state-granted monopoly and terminates a customer's service without a hearing. The customer sues, alleging due process violation. Is the termination state action?
step: Public function: supplying electricity is not a function traditionally and exclusively performed by the state.
step: Entanglement: extensive regulation and a monopoly do not by themselves make the utility's private decision state action; the state did not compel the specific termination.
step: Conclusion: not state action (*Jackson v. Metropolitan Edison*).

@mistakes
- Treating government regulation, funding or licensing as enough for state action; there must be a close nexus to the challenged act (*Blum*; *Moose Lodge*).
- Applying the public function test to any function that governments sometimes perform; it requires traditional and exclusive.
- Forgetting that the Thirteenth Amendment and federal statutes can reach private conduct without state action.
- Assuming a public official's social media page is always state action; apply the *Lindke* test.

@practice self
prompt: State contracts with Medic Co., a private company, to provide medical care to prison inmates. A Medic Co. physician refuses an inmate necessary treatment, and the inmate sues the physician for a constitutional violation. Decide whether the physician's conduct is state action, using the Lugar test.
solution: Issue: whether the physician acted under color of state law. Rule: under Lugar, (1) the deprivation must be caused by the exercise of a state-created right or a rule of conduct imposed by the state, and (2) the defendant must be fairly seen as a state actor, for example because he acts together with state officials or performs a government function; West v. Atkins held that a physician under contract to provide medical care to inmates is a state actor. Application: the state has a constitutional duty to provide medical care to inmates, whom it has deprived of the ability to care for themselves; it delegated that duty by contract; the physician exercises power possessed by virtue of state law; the treatment decision is attributed to the state. Conclusion: the conduct is state action, and the inmate may assert constitutional claims (the merits are separate).
rubric: States both parts of the Lugar test.
rubric: Cites West v. Atkins or reasons from the state's delegated duty.
rubric: Applies the rule to the contract and the inmate's dependence on the state.
rubric: Separates state action from the merits and concludes.
hint: Use IRAC: state the issue, the governing rule, apply it fact by fact, then conclude.
