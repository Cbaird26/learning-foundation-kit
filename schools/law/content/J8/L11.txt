### Causes

Historians agree that **slavery** was the central cause of the Civil War. By 1860 about four million people were enslaved in the South, and the economy of the cotton South depended on their forced labor. Northern states had abolished slavery, and conflict grew over whether it would spread into new territories. The **Compromise of 1850**, the **Kansas-Nebraska Act** (1854), which let settlers in those territories decide on slavery, and the Supreme Court's *Dred Scott* decision (1857), which said Black people could not be citizens, deepened the division. People also argued about **states' rights**; historians note that, in the secession debates, the right most often at stake was the right to hold enslaved people.

After Abraham Lincoln's election in 1860, which many Southern leaders saw as a threat to slavery, seven Southern states seceded and formed the **Confederate States of America**; four more joined after fighting began at **Fort Sumter** in April 1861. The states that stayed loyal were the **Union**.

### The war

In 1863 the **Emancipation Proclamation** declared enslaved people in areas in rebellion to be free, and it allowed Black men to serve in the Union army and navy. About 180,000 Black soldiers served. The Union victory at **Gettysburg** in July 1863 and the capture of **Vicksburg** gave the Union control of the Mississippi River. In the **Gettysburg Address**, Lincoln said the nation was fighting so that "government of the people, by the people, for the people, shall not perish from the earth." Lee surrendered at **Appomattox** in April 1865. Traditionally about 620,000 soldiers are counted as dead, and some recent estimates are as high as about 750,000 (estimates vary); either way it was the deadliest of any American war, and enslaved people freed themselves in large numbers by escaping to Union lines.

### Reconstruction

**Reconstruction** (1865 to 1877) was the effort to rebuild the South and define the rights of the formerly enslaved. Three amendments were added: the **13th** (1865), which says "Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude, except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted, shall exist within the United States, or any place subject to their jurisdiction"; the **14th** (1868), which defined citizenship and promised equal protection of the laws; and the **15th** (1870), which barred denying the vote because of race. Black men voted and held office, and schools and churches were built, with help from the **Freedmen's Bureau**. At the same time, white Southern governments passed **Black Codes** that limited Black freedom, and groups such as the Ku Klux Klan used violence. When federal troops left in 1877, Reconstruction ended, and Southern states soon created **Jim Crow** laws that enforced segregation and kept many Black citizens from voting.

### Analyzing a primary source

A **primary source** was created at the time of the event by someone who took part or witnessed it, such as a letter, speech or photograph. A **secondary source**, such as your textbook, is written later about the past. When you analyze a primary source ask: **Who** made it? **When** and **why**? **Who** was the audience? What does it say, and what point of view might shape it? Do other sources agree?

@example
title: Analyzing a quotation
problem: Use the 13th Amendment's text, "Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude, except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted, shall exist within the United States, or any place subject to their jurisdiction," to make one claim about 1865.
step: Identify the source: it is a primary source, a law adopted in 1865 and ratified by the states. It was written for the whole nation.
step: Read closely: it bans slavery and involuntary servitude throughout the United States.
step: Notice the exception: it allows involuntary servitude "as a punishment for crime."
step: Write a claim with evidence: "The 13th Amendment ended legal slavery across the United States, though its exception for punishment of crime left a legal opening that later affected how some Southern states treated Black citizens."

@mistakes
- Saying the war was only about "states' rights" without asking which rights, since the secession documents focused on slavery.
- Thinking the Emancipation Proclamation freed every enslaved person immediately; it applied to areas in rebellion, and the 13th Amendment ended slavery everywhere.
- Treating Reconstruction as a complete success or a complete failure; it had real gains and was cut short.
- Quoting a source without saying who wrote it and when.
