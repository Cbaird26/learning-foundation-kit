### The Industrial Revolution in the United States

After the Civil War the United States industrialized rapidly. New inventions such as the telephone (1876) and the practical electric light (1879) changed daily life. Improved methods of making steel allowed taller buildings, stronger rails and machines. The **transcontinental railroad**, completed in 1869 in Utah, linked the East and West coasts. Thousands of Chinese and Irish workers built it, often in dangerous conditions. Railroads moved goods and people and helped create national markets. They also took Native lands in the West, and settlement led to conflict with Plains nations.

Large companies grew powerful. Some, called **trusts**, controlled whole industries. Supporters said large firms made goods cheaper; critics said they crushed competition. Congress passed the **Sherman Antitrust Act** (1890) to limit such monopolies. This period is called the **Gilded Age**, a name suggesting a shiny surface covering serious problems such as poverty and corruption.

### Cities and workers

Factory work drew people to cities. Many families lived in crowded **tenements**. Factory jobs often meant long hours, low pay and dangerous machines, and children worked in many industries. Workers formed **labor unions** to demand better pay, shorter hours and safer conditions. The **American Federation of Labor** (1886) organized skilled workers. Strikes such as the **Pullman Strike** (1894) sometimes ended with federal troops being sent in. Business owners often said unions interfered with their right to run their companies, while workers argued that they needed to act together for fair treatment.

### Immigration

Between about 1880 and 1920, millions of immigrants came to the United States, many from Italy, Eastern Europe, Ireland, Germany and Russia, as well as from China, Japan and Mexico. They came for jobs, land, safety from violence and persecution, and religious freedom. Europeans were often processed at **Ellis Island** in New York Harbor (opened in 1892), and Asian immigrants at **Angel Island** in San Francisco Bay (opened in 1910), where they often faced longer and harsher questioning. Some Americans welcomed immigrants as workers and neighbors; others, called **nativists**, wanted to limit immigration. The **Chinese Exclusion Act** (1882) was the first major U.S. law to bar a group of immigrants by nationality. A poem by Emma Lazarus, written in 1883 and later placed on the Statue of Liberty, speaks of "Your huddled masses yearning to breathe free."

### Reading a source from this era

When you read a primary source from the industrial era, notice **who** is speaking (a worker, an owner, a reformer, an immigrant), **why**, and **for whom**. Two sources about the same strike may disagree because their authors had different interests. Use quotations as evidence for a claim, and explain how the quotation supports it.

@example
title: Evidence from a quotation
problem: Use the phrase "Your huddled masses yearning to breathe free" (a poem written in 1883) as evidence for a claim about how some Americans viewed immigrants.
step: Identify the source: a poem from 1883, written to raise money for a statue base; the writer was a public figure with an opinion, so it shows one point of view.
step: Read closely: the phrase describes immigrants as people "yearning to breathe free," that is, seeking freedom.
step: Make a claim: some Americans in the 1880s saw immigrants as people seeking freedom and welcomed them.
step: Add a limit: "But the Chinese Exclusion Act, passed in 1882, shows that other Americans supported laws to keep certain immigrants out, so the poem does not represent everyone's view."

@mistakes
- Treating all Americans as welcoming or all as hostile to immigrants; opinions were divided.
- Describing the Gilded Age as only a story of inventors and wealth, while leaving out workers' conditions.
- Using a quotation with no explanation of how it supports the claim.
- Saying all unions and employers were enemies; each had interests and reasons, and the outcomes varied.

Read this invented 1890 statement by a mill worker: "We work twelve hours, six days a week, and one of our friends lost two fingers in a machine." In 4 to 6 sentences explain why the two statements differ and what a historian should do before judging them.
solution: The statements differ because the speakers have different interests and experiences. The owner wants to protect his control of the mill and presents wages as fair, while the worker describes long hours and an injury to argue for better conditions. Each statement is a primary source that shows one point of view, so neither is complete on its own. A historian should check other evidence, such as payroll records, factory inspection reports and newspapers, before deciding how fair the wages and conditions were. The disagreement itself is also evidence of the conflict between owners and workers in the industrial era.
