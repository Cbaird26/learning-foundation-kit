@article
### Before and after 1492

Long before Europeans arrived, many Native nations lived across North America, such as the Powhatan in what is now Virginia, the Wampanoag in New England, and the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois Confederacy) in the Northeast. They had their own governments, farms, trade networks, and languages.

### The Columbian Exchange

The **Columbian Exchange** is the movement of plants, animals, people and diseases between the Eastern and Western Hemispheres after 1492.

| From the Americas to the rest of the world | From the Old World to the Americas |
|---|---|
| maize (corn), potatoes, tomatoes, beans, cacao, tobacco | horses, cattle, pigs, sheep, wheat, sugarcane, coffee |

Potatoes and maize eventually helped feed growing populations in Europe, Asia and Africa. Horses changed life for many Plains nations. But the most devastating effect was **disease**. Smallpox, measles and influenza had long existed in Europe, Asia and Africa, and people there had built up some immunity. Native peoples of the Americas had never been exposed, so these diseases spread rapidly. In many regions a large share of the population died; estimates vary widely, but many historians conclude that the majority died in some regions within a century. Disease, together with warfare, forced labor and the loss of land, reshaped Native societies.

### The English colonies

The English founded **Jamestown** in Virginia in 1607, the first lasting English settlement. Tobacco became its main cash crop. In 1619 Virginia's **House of Burgesses** met, an early elected assembly, and in the same year the first recorded Africans arrived in the colony. In 1620 the Pilgrims founded **Plymouth**, and in 1630 the Puritans founded the **Massachusetts Bay Colony**. Eventually there were **thirteen colonies** in three regions:

- **New England**: small farms, fishing, shipbuilding, towns with town meetings.
- **Middle colonies**: fertile land, wheat farming, many religions and nationalities.
- **Southern colonies**: warm climate, large plantations growing tobacco, rice and indigo.

### Slavery

Historians estimate that about 12.5 million Africans were forced onto ships in the transatlantic slave trade, and about 10.7 million survived the crossing; many others died on the **Middle Passage**. Enslaved people worked on Southern plantations and in the Caribbean, and they kept their languages, music, foods and religious traditions alive under brutal conditions. Colonial laws made slavery hereditary and based it on race.

### Perspectives

Colonists often came seeking land, profit or religious freedom for their own groups. Native peoples sometimes traded and made alliances with colonists and sometimes fought to defend their lands. Enslaved Africans did not choose to come at all. Historians try to include all these voices.

@example
title: A cause-and-effect chain for disease
problem: Explain why smallpox spread so quickly among Native peoples of the Americas after 1492.
step: Cause: Smallpox had long circulated in Europe, Asia and Africa, but it had not existed in the Americas before contact.
step: Because people in the Americas had no earlier exposure, they had little or no immunity.
step: The disease spread along trade routes and through settlements, often faster than Europeans traveled.
step: Effect: Many communities lost large numbers of people, which weakened them economically and politically and made it harder to resist conquest and loss of land.

@mistakes
- Saying disease happened because Native peoples were "weaker"; it was lack of previous exposure.
- Teaching the Columbian Exchange as if goods only went from Europe to the Americas.
- Treating "colonists" as one group; Puritans, Quakers, Virginia planters and enslaved Africans had very different experiences.
- Treating Native nations as passive; many shaped colonial events through trade, diplomacy and war.
