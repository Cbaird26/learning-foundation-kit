@article
An **ordinary differential equation** (ODE) relates an unknown function of one variable to its own derivatives. Its **order** is the highest derivative that appears. This week we study first order equations, written $y' = f(t,y)$ with $y' = dy/dt$.

A **solution** is a function $y(t)$, defined on an interval, that satisfies the equation at every point of that interval. For example $y' = 3y$ is solved by $y = Ce^{3t}$ for every constant $C$: a whole family. An **initial value problem** (IVP) adds a condition $y(t_0) = y_0$ that picks out one member of the family. For $y' = 3y$ with $y(0) = 5$ we get $C = 5$.

### Checking a solution

Substitute. For $y = 5e^{3t}$ we get $y' = 15e^{3t} = 3y$, so the equation holds, and $y(0) = 5$, so the initial condition holds. You need both checks.

### Slope fields

The equation $y' = f(t,y)$ tells you the slope of the solution through every point $(t,y)$ before you solve anything. Draw a short segment of slope $f(t,y)$ at each point of a grid. A solution curve is a curve that is tangent to the segments everywhere it passes.

For $y' = t - y$ the slope is $0$ on the line $y = t$, positive below it and negative above it. As $t$ increases, solution curves that start anywhere are bent toward the line $y = t - 1$, which is itself a solution.

### Autonomous equations and the phase line

If $t$ does not appear, $y' = f(y)$ is **autonomous**: the slope depends only on the height. An **equilibrium** is a constant solution $y = y^*$ with $f(y^*) = 0$. Between equilibria $f$ has one sign, so $y$ increases where $f > 0$ and decreases where $f < 0$.

An equilibrium is **stable** if nearby solutions move toward it and **unstable** if they move away. For $y' = y(1-y)$ the equilibria are $0$ and $1$; $f > 0$ on $(0,1)$ and $f < 0$ for $y > 1$ and for $y < 0$, so $1$ is stable and $0$ is unstable. Solutions never cross an equilibrium.

### Existence and uniqueness

> **Theorem.** If $f$ and $\partial f/\partial y$ are continuous on a rectangle around $(t_0, y_0)$, then the IVP $y' = f(t,y)$, $y(t_0) = y_0$ has exactly one solution on some interval containing $t_0$.

Two consequences: different solution curves of the same equation never touch, and "some interval" can be small. The equation $y' = y^2$ has a perfectly smooth right side, yet the solution with $y(0) = 1$ is
$$y = \frac{1}{1 - t},$$
which blows up as $t \to 1^-$. The **interval of validity** is the largest open interval containing $t_0$ on which the solution exists: here $(-\infty, 1)$.

If the hypotheses fail, uniqueness can fail too. The equation $y' = 3y^{2/3}$ with $y(0) = 0$ is solved both by $y \equiv 0$ and by $y = t^3$; here $\partial f/\partial y = 2y^{-1/3}$ is not continuous at $y = 0$.

Next week you will learn methods that produce formulas like $1/(1-t)$; this week the goal is to understand what a solution is and where it lives.

@example
title: A solution and its interval of validity
problem: Show that $y = \dfrac{1}{1-t}$ solves $y' = y^2$ with $y(0) = 1$, and state its interval of validity.
step: Differentiate: $y = (1-t)^{-1}$, so $y' = (1-t)^{-2}$.
step: Square the function: $y^2 = (1-t)^{-2}$. The two sides agree wherever the formula is defined.
step: Initial condition: $y(0) = \frac{1}{1-0} = 1$, as required.
step: The formula is undefined at $t = 1$, and $y \to \infty$ as $t \to 1^-$. The interval must contain $t_0 = 0$ and cannot cross $t = 1$, so it is $(-\infty, 1)$.

@example
title: Phase line for an autonomous equation
problem: Find the equilibria of $y' = (y-1)(y-3)$, decide their stability, and describe the solution with $y(0) = 2$.
step: Equilibria are the zeros of $f(y) = (y-1)(y-3)$: $y = 1$ and $y = 3$.
step: Test the sign of $f$ in each region. At $y = 0$: $(-1)(-3) = 3 > 0$. At $y = 2$: $(1)(-1) = -1 < 0$. At $y = 4$: $(3)(1) = 3 > 0$.
step: So $y$ rises for $y < 1$ and falls on $(1,3)$; both flows point toward $y = 1$, which is **stable**. Above $3$ the flow goes up and on $(1,3)$ it goes down, away from $3$, so $y = 3$ is **unstable**.
step: The solution with $y(0) = 2$ lies in $(1,3)$, where $f < 0$, so it decreases and approaches $y = 1$ without crossing it.

@mistakes
- Verifying the differential equation but not the initial condition (or the reverse).
- Calling an equilibrium stable just because $f$ is zero there; stability depends on the direction of the flow on both sides.
- Giving an interval of validity that does not contain $t_0$, or assuming a solution exists for all $t$ because $f$ is smooth.

@practice num
prompt: Solve $y' = 6t^2$ with $y(0) = 2$, and give $y(2)$.
answer: 18
check: 2 + quad(lambda s: 6*s**2, 0, 2)
hint: Integrate the right side with respect to $t$ and keep the constant of integration.
hint: An antiderivative of $6t^2$ is $2t^3$, so $y = 2t^3 + C$. Use $y(0) = 2$ to find $C$.
solution: $y = 2t^3 + C$ and $y(0) = C = 2$, so $y = 2t^3 + 2$ and $y(2) = 16 + 2 = 18$.

hint: $f > 0$ for $y < 4$ except at $y = 0$, and $f < 0$ for $y > 4$. Now follow the arrows near each equilibrium.
solution: $f > 0$ on $(-\infty, 0)$ and $(0, 4)$, and $f < 0$ on $(4, \infty)$. Near $y = 4$ the flow points toward it from both sides, so it is stable. Near $y = 0$ the flow points up on both sides: from below, solutions approach $0$; from above, they leave it.

@practice num
prompt: The solution of $y' = y^2$ with $y(0) = \tfrac12$ blows up at a finite time $T$. Find $T$.
answer: 2
check: quad(lambda y: 1/y**2, 0.5, inf)
hint: Look for a formula of the form $y = \dfrac{1}{c - t}$ and fit the initial condition.
hint: $y(0) = \frac12$ means $\frac{1}{c} = \frac12$. Blow-up happens where the denominator vanishes.
solution: Try $y = 1/(c - t)$. Then $y' = 1/(c-t)^2 = y^2$. The condition $y(0) = 1/c = \frac12$ gives $c = 2$, so $y = 1/(2 - t)$, which blows up at $T = 2$.

answer: 3
