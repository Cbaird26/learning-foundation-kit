@article
Nonlinear systems rarely have solution formulas. We learn about them by locating the equilibria and studying the motion nearby, where the system looks linear.

### Equilibria and the Jacobian

For the autonomous system
$$x' = f(x,y), \qquad y' = g(x,y),$$
an **equilibrium** $(x^*, y^*)$ satisfies $f = g = 0$ there. Write $u = x - x^*$ and $v = y - y^*$ and expand $f$ and $g$ to first order. Then $(u, v)' \approx J\,(u, v)$ with the **Jacobian**
$$J = \begin{pmatrix} f_x & f_y \\ g_x & g_y \end{pmatrix}\Bigg|_{(x^*, y^*)}.$$
This is the **linearization**. Remember to evaluate $J$ at each equilibrium separately: it changes from point to point.

### When linearization decides

Call the equilibrium **hyperbolic** if no eigenvalue of $J$ has real part zero. Then:

- both real parts negative: the equilibrium is asymptotically stable (a sink);
- some real part positive: the equilibrium is unstable (a saddle or a source);
- saddles, sinks and sources look locally like their linear versions.

If an eigenvalue has real part zero, for instance a linear center with eigenvalues $\pm i\beta$, the linearization is **inconclusive**: the neglected nonlinear terms decide whether orbits spiral in, spiral out or close up.

A second caution: a sink attracts only *nearby* solutions. Its basin of attraction may be small, and linearization says nothing about global behavior.

### Example: the damped pendulum

With $x = \theta$ and $y = \theta'$, the equation $\theta'' + b\theta' + \sin\theta = 0$ becomes $x' = y$, $y' = -\sin x - by$. The equilibria are $(k\pi, 0)$, and
$$J = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\ -\cos x & -b\end{pmatrix}.$$
At the hanging position $(0,0)$: $\tau = -b$, $\Delta = 1$, a stable spiral for $b < 2$. At the upright position $(\pi, 0)$: $\Delta = -1 < 0$, a saddle. So the upright pendulum is unstable and the hanging one is stable, as physical intuition says.

### Energy-type functions

A function $V(x,y)$ with $V > 0$ away from the equilibrium and $V = 0$ there is a **Lyapunov function** if $V' = V_x f + V_y g \le 0$ along solutions. Then solutions cannot climb away from the equilibrium, which is stable; if $V' < 0$ strictly (away from the equilibrium), it is asymptotically stable. This works even where linearization is inconclusive.

@example
title: Competing species
problem: Find and classify the equilibria of $x' = x(3 - x - 2y)$, $y' = y(2 - y - x)$ for $x, y \ge 0$.
step: Equilibria: $x = 0$ or $3 - x - 2y = 0$, and $y = 0$ or $2 - y - x = 0$. This gives $(0,0)$, $(3,0)$, $(0,2)$, and from $3 - x - 2y = 0 = 2 - x - y$ the point $(1,1)$.
step: The Jacobian is $J = \begin{pmatrix}3 - 2x - 2y & -2x\\ -y & 2 - 2y - x\end{pmatrix}$.
step: At $(0,0)$: $J = \operatorname{diag}(3, 2)$, a node source. At $(3,0)$: $J = \begin{pmatrix}-3 & -6\\ 0 & -1\end{pmatrix}$, eigenvalues $-3, -1$: a stable node. At $(0,2)$: $J = \begin{pmatrix}-1 & 0\\ -2 & -2\end{pmatrix}$, eigenvalues $-1, -2$: a stable node.
step: At $(1,1)$: $J = \begin{pmatrix}-1 & -2\\ -1 & -1\end{pmatrix}$, so $\tau = -2$ and $\Delta = 1 - 2 = -1 < 0$: a saddle.
step: Interpretation: coexistence at $(1,1)$ is unstable. Generically one species wins: the other dies out, and the outcome depends on the starting populations.

@example
title: When linearization fails
problem: For $x' = -y - x(x^2+y^2)$, $y' = x - y(x^2+y^2)$, decide the stability of the origin.
step: The cubic terms vanish at first order, so $J = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -1\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$ with eigenvalues $\pm i$: a linear center. This is inconclusive.
step: Try $V = x^2 + y^2 = r^2$. Along solutions $V' = 2xx' + 2yy' = 2x\left(-y - xr^2\right) + 2y\left(x - yr^2\right) = -2(x^2 + y^2)\,r^2 = -2r^4$.
step: $V' < 0$ away from the origin, so the origin is asymptotically stable. Since $V = r^2$ gives $2rr' = -2r^4$, we get $r' = -r^3$, so $r(t) = r_0/\sqrt{1 + 2r_0^2t} \to 0$.
step: The nonlinear terms turned the linear center into a spiral sink. With the opposite sign they would give a spiral source.

@mistakes
- Linearizing at the wrong point: the Jacobian must be evaluated at each equilibrium, not left as a matrix of functions or evaluated at the origin by habit.
- Concluding "center" for the nonlinear system from a linear center; with zero real part, the linearization decides nothing.
- Slips in partial derivatives, such as dropping a product-rule term: for $f = x(3 - x - 2y)$, $f_x = 3 - 2x - 2y$.
- Treating local stability as global; a stable equilibrium attracts only solutions that start close enough.

answer: 1

check: math.hypot(*np.ravel(ivp(lambda t, x: [-x[1] + x[0]*(1 - x[0]**2 - x[1]**2), x[0] + x[1]*(1 - x[0]**2 - x[1]**2)], [0.2, 0], 0, 40)))
hint: Let $r^2 = x^2 + y^2$. Compute $rr' = xx' + yy'$.

@quiz num
prompt: For the pendulum with damping $x' = y$, $y' = -\sin x - 0.4y$, find the real part of the eigenvalues of the Jacobian at the hanging equilibrium $(0, 0)$.
answer: -0.2
check: np.linalg.eigvals(np.array([[0, 1], [nderiv(lambda x: -sin(x), 0), -0.4]])).real[0]
hint: The Jacobian is $\begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\ -\cos x & -0.4\end{pmatrix}$. Evaluate it at $x = 0$.
hint: The characteristic equation is $\lambda^2 + 0.4\lambda + 1 = 0$.
solution: $J = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\ -1 & -0.4\end{pmatrix}$, so $\lambda^2 + 0.4\lambda + 1 = 0$ and $\lambda = -0.2 \pm i\sqrt{0.96}$. The real part is $-0.2$, so the hanging pendulum is a spiral sink.
