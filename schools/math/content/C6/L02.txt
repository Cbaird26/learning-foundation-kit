Last week you learned what a solution looks like. This week you produce formulas, for the two most useful kinds of first order equation.

### Separable equations

If $y' = g(t)\,h(y)$, move all the $y$'s to one side and all the $t$'s to the other, then integrate:
$$\int \frac{dy}{h(y)} = \int g(t)\,dt.$$
For $y' = ty$: $\int dy/y = \int t\,dt$ gives $\ln|y| = t^2/2 + c$, so $y = Ce^{t^2/2}$. Dividing by $h(y)$ is only legal where $h(y) \neq 0$. Constant solutions with $h(y^*) = 0$ must be checked separately; here $y \equiv 0$ is the case $C = 0$.

Always use the initial condition to fix the constant, and then ask where the result is defined.

### Linear equations

A first order equation is **linear** if it can be written in standard form
$$y' + p(t)\,y = q(t).$$
The trick: multiply by $\mu(t) = e^{P(t)}$, where $P' = p$. Then $\mu' = p\mu$ and the left side becomes an exact derivative:
$$(\mu y)' = \mu y' + p\mu y = \mu q \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y = \frac{1}{\mu}\left(\int \mu q\,dt + C\right).$$
For $y' + 2y = 6$: $\mu = e^{2t}$, $(e^{2t}y)' = 6e^{2t}$, so $y = 3 + Ce^{-2t}$. The solution is a constant **steady state** plus a **transient** that dies out.

The equation must be in standard form first: divide by the coefficient of $y'$ before reading off $p$.

### Interval of validity

For a linear equation the interval comes from the coefficients, not from the answer. If $p$ and $q$ are continuous on an open interval $I$ containing $t_0$, the IVP has a unique solution on all of $I$. Break points are where $p$ or $q$ fail to be continuous. For nonlinear separable equations, the solution may blow up inside such an interval, as $y' = y^2$ did last week, so you read the interval from the formula you find.

### Models

- **Newton cooling.** An object at temperature $T$ in surroundings at $T_e$: $T' = -k(T - T_e)$ with $k > 0$. The solution is $T = T_e + (T_0 - T_e)e^{-kt}$.
- **Mixing.** If $S$ is the amount of salt in a tank of constant volume $V$, then $S' = (\text{rate in}) - (\text{rate out})$, where "rate out" is $\dfrac{r_{\text{out}}}{V}\,S$.
- **Logistic growth.** $P' = rP(1 - P/K)$ is separable, with equilibria $0$ and $K$.

Each model is an equation you can set up in words ("rate of change equals what flows in minus what flows out") before you touch any calculus.

@example
title: A separable equation and where it lives
problem: Solve $y' = 1 + y^2$ with $y(0) = 1$ and give the interval of validity.
step: Separate variables, which is allowed because $1 + y^2 > 0$: $\dfrac{dy}{1 + y^2} = dt$.
step: Integrate both sides: $\arctan y = t + C$.
step: Use $y(0) = 1$: $\arctan 1 = \frac{\pi}{4}$, so $C = \frac{\pi}{4}$.
step: Solve for $y$: $y = \tan\!\left(t + \frac{\pi}{4}\right)$. This needs $t + \frac{\pi}{4}$ in $\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$, the branch containing $t = 0$.
step: The interval of validity is $-\frac{3\pi}{4} < t < \frac{\pi}{4}$. Check: $y' = \sec^2(t + \frac{\pi}{4}) = 1 + y^2$.

@example
title: A linear equation with a singular coefficient
problem: Solve $t\,y' + 2y = 4t$ with $y(1) = 3$ and give the interval of validity.
step: Standard form: divide by $t$, giving $y' + \dfrac{2}{t}\,y = 4$. Here $p = 2/t$ is continuous except at $t = 0$.
step: Integrating factor: $\mu = e^{\int 2/t\,dt} = e^{2\ln t} = t^2$ for $t > 0$.
step: Multiply through: $(t^2 y)' = 4t^2$, so $t^2 y = \frac43 t^3 + C$ and $y = \frac43 t + \dfrac{C}{t^2}$.
step: Initial condition: $y(1) = \frac43 + C = 3$, so $C = \frac53$ and $y = \frac43 t + \dfrac{5}{3t^2}$.
step: Since $t_0 = 1$ lies to the right of the break point $t = 0$, the interval of validity is $(0, \infty)$.

@mistakes
- Dropping the constant of integration, or inserting it after the initial condition has been applied.
- Dividing by $h(y)$ and forgetting the constant solutions where $h(y) = 0$.
- Using the integrating factor before the equation is in standard form, so that $p$ is read off wrongly, or multiplying only the left side by $\mu$.
- Reporting the interval where the final formula happens to be defined instead of the interval around $t_0$ that avoids the break points.

prompt: Solve $y' = ty$ with $y(0) = 1$ and give $y(2)$ as an exact value.
answer: e^2
check: np.ravel(ivp(lambda t, y: t*y, 1, 0, 2))[0]
hint: Separate: $\dfrac{dy}{y} = t\,dt$.
hint: Integrating gives $\ln|y| = t^2/2 + c$. Use $y(0) = 1$ to find the constant.
solution: $y = Ce^{t^2/2}$ and $y(0) = C = 1$. Then $y(2) = e^{4/2} = e^2$.

@practice num
prompt: A cup of coffee at $90\,^\circ\text{C}$ sits in a room at $20\,^\circ\text{C}$ and obeys $T' = -0.1\,(T - 20)$, with $t$ in minutes. How many minutes until it reaches $50\,^\circ\text{C}$? Round to $0.01$.
answer: 8.47
tol: 0.01
unit: min
check: root(lambda t: np.ravel(ivp(lambda s, T: -0.1*(T - 20), 90, 0, t))[0] - 50, 0.1, 100)
hint: Write $u = T - 20$. Then $u' = -0.1u$, which has the solution $u = u_0 e^{-0.1t}$.
hint: Here $u_0 = 70$, and you want $u = 30$. Take logarithms.
solution: $T = 20 + 70e^{-0.1t}$. Setting $T = 50$: $e^{-0.1t} = \frac{3}{7}$, so $t = 10\ln\frac73 \approx 8.47$ minutes.

choice*: $(-\infty, 2)$
choice: $(2, \infty)$
choice: $(0, 2)$
choice: $(-\infty, \infty)$
hint: Divide by $t - 2$ to put the equation in standard form. Where is $p(t)$ discontinuous?
hint: The break point is $t = 2$. Which side of it contains $t_0 = 0$?
solution: In standard form $y' + \dfrac{1}{t-2}\,y = 0$, so $p$ is continuous except at $t = 2$. Separating, $y = \dfrac{C}{t-2}$, and $y(0) = 1$ gives $C = -2$, so $y = \dfrac{2}{2-t}$. The interval containing $t_0 = 0$ that avoids $t = 2$ is $(-\infty, 2)$.

@practice num
prompt: A well-stirred tank holds $100$ L of pure water. Brine with $0.5$ kg of salt per liter enters at $2$ L/min, and the mixture leaves at $2$ L/min. How many kilograms of salt are in the tank after $50$ minutes? Round to $0.01$.
answer: 31.61
tol: 0.01
unit: kg
check: np.ravel(ivp(lambda t, S: 2*0.5 - 2*S/100, 0, 0, 50))[0]
hint: Let $S(t)$ be the salt in kg. Salt in per minute is $2 \times 0.5$; salt out per minute is $2 \times S/100$.
hint: $S' = 1 - S/50$ with $S(0) = 0$. Treat it as linear or as separable.
solution: $S' = 1 - S/50$, so $S - 50$ decays: $S = 50\left(1 - e^{-t/50}\right)$. At $t = 50$: $S = 50(1 - e^{-1}) \approx 31.61$ kg.

@quiz num
prompt: The solution of $y' = ty^2$ with $y(0) = 1$ becomes infinite at a positive time $T$. Find $T$ exactly.
answer: sqrt(2)
check: root(lambda T: quad(lambda s: s, 0, T) - quad(lambda y: 1/y**2, 1, inf), 0.1, 5)
hint: Separate variables: $\dfrac{dy}{y^2} = t\,dt$.
hint: $-\dfrac1y = \dfrac{t^2}{2} + C$. Use $y(0) = 1$, then find where $y$ becomes infinite.
solution: $-1/y = t^2/2 - 1$, so $y = \dfrac{2}{2 - t^2}$. This blows up when $t^2 = 2$, i.e. $T = \sqrt2$.

@quiz expr
prompt: Solve $y' + 3y = e^{-3t}$ with $y(0) = 2$.
vars: t
lead: $y(t) =$
answer: (t + 2)*e^(-3*t)
check: lambda t: np.ravel(ivp(lambda s, y: exp(-3*s) - 3*y, 2, 0, t))[0]
hint: The integrating factor is $e^{3t}$.
hint: $(e^{3t}y)' = 1$. Integrate and apply the initial condition.
solution: $(e^{3t}y)' = e^{3t}e^{-3t} = 1$, so $e^{3t}y = t + C$. Then $y(0) = C = 2$, and $y = (t+2)e^{-3t}$.
