@article
Undetermined coefficients works only for forcing such as polynomials, exponentials, sines and cosines. For $\tan t$, $e^t/t$ or $\sec t$ there is no family to guess. **Variation of parameters** works for any continuous forcing, provided you can integrate.

### The idea

The homogeneous solution is $C_1y_1 + C_2y_2$ with constants $C_1, C_2$. Let the "constants" vary: look for
$$y_p = u_1(t)\,y_1(t) + u_2(t)\,y_2(t).$$
This has two unknown functions but only one equation to satisfy, so we are free to add a condition. Impose $u_1'y_1 + u_2'y_2 = 0$. Then $y_p' = u_1y_1' + u_2y_2'$, and substituting into $y'' + py' + qy = f$ leaves $u_1'y_1' + u_2'y_2' = f$. (The terms with $u_1$ and $u_2$ themselves vanish because $y_1, y_2$ solve the homogeneous equation.) So
$$\begin{pmatrix} y_1 & y_2 \\ y_1' & y_2' \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} u_1' \\ u_2' \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ f \end{pmatrix}.$$

### The formula

The determinant of the matrix is the **Wronskian** $W = y_1y_2' - y_2y_1'$. Solving by Cramer's rule,
$$u_1' = -\frac{y_2\,f}{W}, \qquad u_2' = \frac{y_1\,f}{W}, \qquad y_p = -y_1\!\int\!\frac{y_2f}{W}\,dt + y_2\!\int\!\frac{y_1f}{W}\,dt.$$
Two requirements: the equation must be in standard form (coefficient of $y''$ equal to $1$), so that $f$ is the right side after dividing; and $W \neq 0$, which holds exactly when $y_1$ and $y_2$ are independent. For $\cos t$ and $\sin t$, $W = \cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1$.

### Particular solutions are not unique

Each antiderivative has an arbitrary constant, and that constant adds a multiple of $y_1$ or $y_2$ to $y_p$. This is no surprise: if $y_p$ and $\tilde y_p$ both solve the forced equation, their difference solves the homogeneous one. So *any* particular solution works, and the full set of solutions is $y_p + C_1y_1 + C_2y_2$. Different methods can produce answers that look different but differ by a homogeneous part.

### Resonance, revisited

For $y'' + y = \cos t$ (forcing frequency equals natural frequency) variation of parameters gives
$$y_p = -\cos t\int\sin t\cos t\,dt + \sin t\int\cos^2 t\,dt = \frac t2\sin t,$$
the same growing response that the modified trial $t(a\cos t + b\sin t)$ gives. The factor $t$ appears automatically, because the integral of $\cos^2 t$ contains the non-oscillating term $t/2$. That non-oscillating piece is what resonance looks like inside the formula.

@example
title: Forcing with sec t
problem: Find a particular solution of $y'' + y = \sec t$ on $-\pi/2 < t < \pi/2$.
step: Take $y_1 = \cos t$ and $y_2 = \sin t$, with $W = 1$. The equation is already in standard form with $f = \sec t$.
step: Compute $u_1' = -y_2f/W = -\sin t\sec t = -\tan t$, and $u_2' = y_1f/W = \cos t\sec t = 1$.
step: Integrate: $u_1 = \ln(\cos t)$ (positive $\cos t$ on this interval) and $u_2 = t$.
step: Assemble $y_p = u_1y_1 + u_2y_2 = \cos t\,\ln(\cos t) + t\sin t$.
step: Check by substitution if you wish: $y_p'' + y_p$ simplifies to $\sec t$. The interval is where $\sec t$ is continuous and contains $t = 0$.

@example
title: Two particular solutions of the same equation
problem: Find a particular solution of $y'' - 2y' + y = \dfrac{e^t}{t}$ for $t > 0$, and compare it with $te^t\ln t$.
step: The characteristic equation $(r-1)^2 = 0$ gives $y_1 = e^t$ and $y_2 = te^t$. Then $W = e^t(e^t + te^t) - te^t\cdot e^t = e^{2t}$.
step: With $f = e^t/t$: $u_1' = -\dfrac{te^t\cdot e^t/t}{e^{2t}} = -1$ and $u_2' = \dfrac{e^t\cdot e^t/t}{e^{2t}} = \dfrac1t$.
step: Integrate: $u_1 = -t$ and $u_2 = \ln t$ (for $t > 0$). Then $y_p = -te^t + te^t\ln t = te^t(\ln t - 1)$.
step: The function $te^t\ln t$ is also a particular solution. The difference between the two is $-te^t$, which is $-y_2$, a homogeneous solution, so both are correct.

@mistakes
- Applying the formula when the equation is not in standard form; if the equation is $2y'' + \dots = g$, use $f = g/2$.
- Getting the signs wrong: the minus sign belongs to $u_1'$, with $y_2$ in the numerator.
- Treating $W$ as $y_1y_2 - y_1'y_2'$; the Wronskian is $y_1y_2' - y_2y_1'$.
- Thinking a particular solution must match a textbook answer exactly; any particular solution is acceptable, and answers differ by homogeneous solutions.

@practice self
prompt: Consider $y'' + y = \cos t$. (a) Find a particular solution by undetermined coefficients. (b) Find one by variation of parameters. (c) Explain how the two answers are related and what they say about resonance.
hint: For (a), $\cos t$ solves $y'' + y = 0$, so the plain trial fails; multiply it by $t$.
hint: For (b), use $W = 1$, $u_1' = -\sin t\cos t$ and $u_2' = \cos^2 t$, and write $\cos^2 t = \frac12(1 + \cos 2t)$ to integrate.
solution: (a) $\cos t$ is a homogeneous solution, so try $y_p = t(a\cos t + b\sin t)$. Substituting gives $y_p'' + y_p = -2a\sin t + 2b\cos t$, so $a = 0$, $b = \frac12$ and $y_p = \frac t2\sin t$. (b) With $y_1 = \cos t$, $y_2 = \sin t$, $W = 1$ and $f = \cos t$: $y_p = -\cos t\int\sin t\cos t\,dt + \sin t\int\cos^2t\,dt = -\cos t\cdot\frac{\sin^2t}{2} + \sin t\left(\frac t2 + \frac{\sin 2t}{4}\right) = \frac t2\sin t$, since the other terms cancel. (c) Any two particular solutions differ by a homogeneous solution $C_1\cos t + C_2\sin t$; here the two answers agree exactly (other choices of integration constants would add such a term). Both show an oscillation whose amplitude $t/2$ grows without bound: the forcing frequency $1$ equals the natural frequency $1$, which is resonance.
rubric: Part (a) uses the modified trial $t(a\cos t + b\sin t)$ and finds $y_p = \frac t2\sin t$.
rubric: Part (b) sets up $u_1' = -\sin t\cos t$ and $u_2' = \cos^2 t$ with $W = 1$, and integrates correctly.
rubric: Part (c) states that two particular solutions differ by a homogeneous solution.
rubric: Part (c) identifies the growing envelope $t/2$ as resonance because the forcing frequency equals the natural frequency.
