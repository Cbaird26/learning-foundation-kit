@article
**Optimization** asks for the largest or smallest value of some quantity: the biggest area you can fence, the cheapest container, the fastest route. Calculus finds these by locating where a graph levels out.

### Where extremes can occur

A function has an **absolute maximum** on an interval at $c$ if $f(c) \ge f(x)$ for every $x$ in the interval; reverse the inequality for an **absolute minimum**. Two facts organize everything.

**Extreme Value Theorem.** If $f$ is continuous on a closed interval $[a, b]$, then $f$ attains both an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum on $[a, b]$.

**Fermat's theorem.** If $f$ has a local extremum at an interior point $c$ and $f'(c)$ exists, then $f'(c) = 0$.

A **critical number** of $f$ is a number $c$ in the domain where $f'(c) = 0$ or $f'(c)$ does not exist. So extremes occur only at critical numbers or at endpoints. The converse is false: $f(x) = x^3$ has $f'(0) = 0$ but no extremum at $0$.

### The closed interval method

To find the absolute extremes of a continuous $f$ on $[a, b]$:

1. Find all critical numbers in $(a, b)$.
2. Evaluate $f$ at each critical number and at $a$ and $b$.
3. The largest value is the absolute maximum and the smallest is the absolute minimum.

### Modeling word problems

Most of the difficulty is translating the story into a function of one variable.

1. Draw a picture and name the variables, with units.
2. Write the quantity to optimize (area, cost, volume, time).
3. Use the constraint to eliminate all but one variable.
4. State the **domain** of that variable; it often decides whether endpoints matter.
5. Find critical numbers and compare values, or study the sign of $f'$.
6. Answer the question that was asked, with units, and say why it is a maximum or a minimum.

### Justifying a maximum or minimum

If $f'$ changes from positive to negative at $c$, then $f$ rises and then falls, so $c$ is a local maximum; a change from negative to positive gives a local minimum. This is the **first derivative test**. If $c$ is the only critical number on an interval and $f$ has a local maximum there, it is the absolute maximum on that interval, because the graph has nowhere else to turn. Always write down such a justification. A derivative that equals zero is not by itself an answer.

@example
title: The closed interval method
problem: Find the absolute maximum and minimum values of $f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 - 9x + 5$ on $[-2, 4]$.
step: Differentiate and factor: $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 6x - 9 = 3(x - 3)(x + 1)$. The derivative exists everywhere.
step: Critical numbers: $f'(x) = 0$ at $x = -1$ and $x = 3$. Both lie inside $(-2, 4)$.
step: Evaluate $f$ at the endpoints and critical numbers: $f(-2) = 3$, $f(-1) = 10$, $f(3) = -22$, $f(4) = -15$.
step: Compare the four values. The absolute maximum is $10$, at $x = -1$, and the absolute minimum is $-22$, at $x = 3$.

@example
title: Fencing a pen beside a river
problem: A farmer has $200$ m of fencing and wants a rectangular pen along a straight river. No fence is needed along the river. Which dimensions give the largest area?
step: Let $x$ be the length in meters of each of the two sides perpendicular to the river and $y$ the length of the side parallel to it. Fence is used on three sides, so $2x + y = 200$ and $y = 200 - 2x$.
step: The quantity to maximize is $A = xy = x(200 - 2x) = 200x - 2x^2$.
step: Lengths cannot be negative, so the domain is $0 \le x \le 100$.
step: Differentiate: $A'(x) = 200 - 4x$. It is zero only at $x = 50$.
step: Compare values: $A(0) = 0$, $A(50) = 50 \cdot 100 = 5000$ and $A(100) = 0$. The maximum is at $x = 50$.
step: Answer: the two perpendicular sides are $50$ m, the side along the river is $100$ m, and the area is $5000\ \text{m}^2$.

@mistakes
- Treating every point where $f'(c) = 0$ as a maximum or minimum. Check the sign change of $f'$ or compare function values.
- Forgetting the endpoints in the closed interval method.
- Leaving two variables in the function. Use the constraint to eliminate one before differentiating.
- Giving the optimal value of the variable when the question asks for the maximum value (or the other way round).

@practice num
prompt: A ball is thrown upward from the ground and its height after $t$ seconds is $h(t) = 20t - 5t^2$ meters, until it lands at $t = 4$. Find its maximum height.
answer: 20
unit: m
check: np.max(20*np.linspace(0, 4, 400001) - 5*np.linspace(0, 4, 400001)**2)
hint: The maximum of a smooth function occurs where its derivative is zero (or at an endpoint).
hint: Solve $h'(t) = 20 - 10t = 0$ for $t$, then evaluate $h$ at that time.
solution: $h'(t) = 20 - 10t = 0$ at $t = 2$. Then $h(2) = 40 - 20 = 20$ m. The endpoints give $h(0) = h(4) = 0$, so the maximum height is $20$ m.

@practice num
prompt: Find the absolute maximum value of $f(x) = x^3 - 12x$ on the interval $[-3, 3]$.
answer: 16
check: np.max(np.linspace(-3, 3, 600001)**3 - 12*np.linspace(-3, 3, 600001))
hint: Find the critical numbers in $(-3, 3)$, then evaluate $f$ at them and at both endpoints.
hint: $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 12 = 0$ gives $x = \pm 2$. Compute $f(-3)$, $f(-2)$, $f(2)$ and $f(3)$.
solution: The critical numbers are $x = \pm 2$. The values are $f(-3) = 9$, $f(-2) = 16$, $f(2) = -16$ and $f(3) = -9$. The absolute maximum value is $16$, at $x = -2$.

@practice num
prompt: Two nonnegative numbers $x$ and $y$ satisfy $x + y = 30$. Find the largest possible value of the product $x y^2$.
answer: 4000
check: np.max((30 - np.linspace(0, 30, 300001))*np.linspace(0, 30, 300001)**2)
hint: Use the constraint to write $x = 30 - y$, so the quantity to maximize is a function of $y$ alone on the domain $0 \le y \le 30$.
hint: Maximize $P(y) = (30 - y)y^2 = 30y^2 - y^3$. Set $P'(y) = 0$ and compare with the endpoint values.
solution: $P(y) = 30y^2 - y^3$ and $P'(y) = 60y - 3y^2 = 3y(20 - y)$, so $y = 0$ or $y = 20$. Then $P(0) = 0$, $P(20) = 10 \cdot 400 = 4000$ and $P(30) = 0$. The largest product is $4000$, at $x = 10$ and $y = 20$.

@practice num
prompt: A square sheet of cardboard $12$ cm on a side has a small square of side $x$ cut from each corner. The four flaps are folded up to make an open-topped box. Find the largest possible volume of the box.
answer: 128
unit: cm^3
check: np.max(np.linspace(0, 6, 600001)*(12 - 2*np.linspace(0, 6, 600001))**2)
hint: The base of the box is a square of side $12 - 2x$ and the height is $x$. What is the domain of $x$?
hint: $V(x) = x(12 - 2x)^2$ on $0 \le x \le 6$. Differentiate with the product and chain rules, factor, and find the critical numbers.
solution: $V'(x) = (12 - 2x)^2 - 4x(12 - 2x) = (12 - 2x)(12 - 6x)$, which is zero at $x = 2$ and $x = 6$. Since $V(0) = V(6) = 0$ and $V(2) = 2 \cdot 8^2 = 128$, the maximum volume is $128\ \text{cm}^3$.

@practice self
prompt: An open-topped box with a square base of side $x$ meters and height $h$ meters must have volume $32\ \text{m}^3$. Find the dimensions that minimize the total surface area of the base and four sides, and justify that your answer is a minimum.
hint: Write the surface area as $S = x^2 + 4xh$, then use $x^2 h = 32$ to eliminate $h$.
hint: Minimize $S(x) = x^2 + \frac{128}{x}$ for $x > 0$. After finding the critical number, check the sign of $S'$ on both sides of it.
solution: From $x^2 h = 32$, $h = \frac{32}{x^2}$. Then $S(x) = x^2 + 4x\cdot\frac{32}{x^2} = x^2 + \frac{128}{x}$ for $x > 0$. $S'(x) = 2x - \frac{128}{x^2} = \frac{2x^3 - 128}{x^2}$, which is zero when $x^3 = 64$, that is $x = 4$. For $0 < x < 4$ we have $S' < 0$ and for $x > 4$ we have $S' > 0$, so $S$ decreases and then increases: $x = 4$ is the absolute minimum on $(0, \infty)$. Then $h = 32/16 = 2$. The box is $4\ \text{m} \times 4\ \text{m} \times 2\ \text{m}$ with surface area $48\ \text{m}^2$.
rubric: Writes the surface area in terms of $x$ and $h$ and uses the volume constraint to eliminate $h$.
rubric: States the domain ($x > 0$) and finds the critical number $x = 4$ from $S'(x) = 0$.
rubric: Justifies the minimum, for example by the sign change of $S'$ from negative to positive.
rubric: Gives both dimensions ($x = 4$ m, $h = 2$ m) with units.

@quiz num
prompt: Find the absolute minimum value of $f(x) = x + 2\cos x$ on the interval $[0, 2\pi]$. Give an exact value.
answer: 5pi/6 - sqrt(3)
check: np.min(np.linspace(0, 2*pi, 2000001) + 2*np.cos(np.linspace(0, 2*pi, 2000001)))
tol: 0.0001
hint: Set $f'(x) = 0$ and find the solutions in $(0, 2\pi)$. There are two.
hint: $f'(x) = 1 - 2\sin x = 0$ gives $\sin x = \frac{1}{2}$. Evaluate $f$ at both solutions and at $0$ and $2\pi$, then compare.
solution: $f'(x) = 1 - 2\sin x = 0$ at $x = \frac{\pi}{6}$ and $x = \frac{5\pi}{6}$. The values are $f(0) = 2$, $f\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right) = \frac{\pi}{6} + \sqrt{3} \approx 2.26$, $f\left(\frac{5\pi}{6}\right) = \frac{5\pi}{6} - \sqrt{3} \approx 0.89$ and $f(2\pi) = 2\pi + 2 \approx 8.28$. The absolute minimum is $\frac{5\pi}{6} - \sqrt{3}$.

@quiz mc
prompt: Consider $f(x) = x^3$ on the interval $[-1, 1]$. Which statement is correct?
choice: $f$ has a local minimum at $x = 0$, because $f'(0) = 0$.
choice: $f$ has a local maximum at $x = 0$, because $f'(0) = 0$.
choice*: $f'(0) = 0$, but $f$ has no local extremum at $0$; its absolute extremes on $[-1, 1]$ occur at the endpoints.
choice: $f$ has no absolute maximum on $[-1, 1]$, because $f$ is always increasing.
hint: Does $f'$ change sign at $x = 0$? Compute $f'(x) = 3x^2$ for small positive and small negative $x$.
hint: A continuous function on a closed interval always has an absolute maximum and minimum.
solution: $f'(x) = 3x^2 \ge 0$ never changes sign, so $f$ is increasing through $x = 0$ and $0$ is not an extremum even though $f'(0) = 0$. By the Extreme Value Theorem, $f$ attains its absolute minimum $-1$ at $x = -1$ and its absolute maximum $1$ at $x = 1$, both endpoints.
