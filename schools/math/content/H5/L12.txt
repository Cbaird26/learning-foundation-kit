@article
The chain rule differentiates a composition. **Substitution** runs it backwards. When an integrand looks like "a function of an inside function, times the derivative of that inside function", a change of variable turns it into a basic integral you already know.

### The idea

The chain rule says $\frac{d}{dx}F\big(g(x)\big) = F'\big(g(x)\big)\,g'(x)$. Read it backwards: if $F' = f$, then

$$\int f\big(g(x)\big)\,g'(x)\,dx = F\big(g(x)\big) + C.$$

Writing $u = g(x)$ and $du = g'(x)\,dx$, the left side becomes $\int f(u)\,du$, which equals $F(u) + C$.

### The method for indefinite integrals

1. Choose $u$. Look for the inside of a composition, or an expression whose derivative also appears in the integrand.
2. Compute $du = u'(x)\,dx$, adjusting for constant factors.
3. Rewrite the **whole** integral in terms of $u$. No $x$ may remain, including in $dx$.
4. Integrate with respect to $u$.
5. Replace $u$ by its expression in $x$.
6. Check by differentiating your answer.

For $\int 2x\cos(x^2)\,dx$, let $u = x^2$. Then $du = 2x\,dx$, and the integral becomes $\int \cos u\,du = \sin u + C = \sin(x^2) + C$.

If the derivative you need is off by a constant factor, compensate for it. For $\int x e^{x^2}\,dx$ with $u = x^2$ we have $du = 2x\,dx$, so $x\,dx = \frac{1}{2}\,du$, and the answer is $\frac{1}{2}e^{x^2} + C$.

A useful special case: when the numerator is the derivative of the denominator,

$$\int \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)}\,dx = \ln\lvert g(x)\rvert + C.$$

### Definite integrals

There are two safe ways. Either find the antiderivative in $x$ and evaluate at the original limits, or **change the limits** along with the variable. If $u = g(x)$, then $x = a$ becomes $u = g(a)$ and $x = b$ becomes $u = g(b)$:

$$\int_a^b f\big(g(x)\big)\,g'(x)\,dx = \int_{g(a)}^{g(b)} f(u)\,du.$$

Once the limits are changed you stay in $u$ and never go back to $x$. The new lower limit may be larger than the upper limit; that is fine.

### The big picture

Differentiation and integration undo each other. The Fundamental Theorem says $\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f\,dt = f(x)$ and $\int_a^b F'\,dx = F(b) - F(a)$. Every integration technique is a differentiation rule read backwards: substitution reverses the chain rule, and later you will meet integration by parts, which reverses the product rule.

@example
title: An indefinite integral with a constant factor
problem: Find $\displaystyle\int x^2 (x^3 + 5)^4\,dx$.
step: The inside function is $x^3 + 5$, and its derivative $3x^2$ is, up to a constant, the factor $x^2$ in front. Choose $u = x^3 + 5$.
step: Then $du = 3x^2\,dx$, so $x^2\,dx = \dfrac{1}{3}\,du$.
step: Rewrite entirely in $u$: $\displaystyle\int u^4 \cdot \frac{1}{3}\,du = \frac{1}{3}\int u^4\,du = \frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{u^5}{5} + C = \frac{u^5}{15} + C$.
step: Substitute back: $\dfrac{(x^3 + 5)^5}{15} + C$.
step: Check: $\dfrac{d}{dx}\dfrac{(x^3 + 5)^5}{15} = \dfrac{5(x^3 + 5)^4 \cdot 3x^2}{15} = x^2 (x^3 + 5)^4$.

@example
title: A definite integral with changed limits
problem: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2} \sin x\,\cos^2 x\,dx$.
step: The derivative of $\cos x$ is $-\sin x$, which appears in the integrand. Choose $u = \cos x$, so $du = -\sin x\,dx$ and $\sin x\,dx = -du$.
step: Change the limits: $x = 0$ gives $u = \cos 0 = 1$, and $x = \frac{\pi}{2}$ gives $u = \cos\frac{\pi}{2} = 0$.
step: Rewrite: $\displaystyle\int_{u=1}^{u=0} u^2\,(-du) = -\int_1^0 u^2\,du$.
step: Swapping the limits flips the sign: $-\int_1^0 u^2\,du = \int_0^1 u^2\,du = \left[\dfrac{u^3}{3}\right]_0^1 = \dfrac{1}{3}$.
step: Sanity check: the integrand is nonnegative on $[0, \frac{\pi}{2}]$, so a positive answer is what we expect.

@mistakes
- Leaving $x$ behind after substituting. The whole integrand, including $dx$, must be rewritten in terms of $u$.
- Forgetting the constant factor coming from $du$. With $u = x^2$ we get $x\,dx = \frac{1}{2}\,du$, so $\int x e^{x^2}\,dx = \frac{1}{2}e^{x^2} + C$, not $e^{x^2} + C$.
- In a definite integral, switching to $u$ but keeping the old $x$-limits, or changing the limits and then substituting back to $x$ as well.
- Dropping a minus sign: with $u = \cos x$ we have $du = -\sin x\,dx$.

@practice self
prompt: Explain, in your own words, why the substitution rule is the chain rule run backwards. Then use the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus to explain why differentiation and integration are inverse processes. Include a short example of your own for each explanation.
hint: Start from the chain rule formula for $\frac{d}{dx}F(g(x))$ and integrate both sides. What does integrating a derivative give back?
hint: For the Fundamental Theorem, ask what happens if you differentiate after integrating, and what happens if you integrate after differentiating.
solution: The chain rule gives $\frac{d}{dx}F\big(g(x)\big) = F'\big(g(x)\big)\,g'(x)$. Since integrating a derivative recovers the function (up to a constant), $\int F'\big(g(x)\big)\,g'(x)\,dx = F\big(g(x)\big) + C$. This is the substitution rule with $f = F'$ and $u = g(x)$. For example, $\frac{d}{dx}\sin(x^2) = 2x\cos(x^2)$, so $\int 2x\cos(x^2)\,dx = \sin(x^2) + C$. The Fundamental Theorem has two parts. Differentiating after integrating returns the original function: $\frac{d}{dx}\int_0^x \sin(t^2)\,dt = \sin(x^2)$, because the rate at which accumulated area grows is the height of the graph. Integrating after differentiating returns the net change: $\int_a^b F'(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a)$, for example $\int_0^2 3x^2\,dx = 2^3 - 0^3 = 8$. Each process undoes the other, apart from the constant that the limits fix.

rubric: Gives a correct example of a substitution and notes it can be checked by differentiating.
rubric: States both parts of the Fundamental Theorem: the derivative of an accumulation function, and the integral of a derivative as net change.
rubric: Gives a correct example for the Fundamental Theorem and mentions the role of the constant or the limits.
