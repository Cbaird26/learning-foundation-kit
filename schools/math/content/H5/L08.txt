@article
The first derivative tells you where a graph climbs or falls. The second derivative tells you how it bends. Together they let you sketch a curve and find its key features without plotting dozens of points.

### Increasing and decreasing

If $f'(x) > 0$ on an interval, $f$ is **increasing** there; if $f'(x) < 0$, $f$ is **decreasing**. To use this, find the critical numbers, mark them on a number line and test the sign of $f'$ in each piece. If $f'$ changes from $+$ to $-$ at a critical number $c$, there is a local maximum at $c$; from $-$ to $+$, a local minimum; with no change, no extremum. This is the **first derivative test**.

### Concavity

A graph is **concave up** where it bends like a cup (its slope is increasing) and **concave down** where it bends like a cap (its slope is decreasing).

- $f''(x) > 0$ on an interval means concave up.
- $f''(x) < 0$ on an interval means concave down.

An **inflection point** is a point of the graph where the concavity changes, so $f''$ changes sign there. Having $f''(c) = 0$ is not enough by itself: $f(x) = x^4$ has $f''(0) = 0$ but is concave up on both sides of $0$.

### The second derivative test

Suppose $f'(c) = 0$ and $f''$ is continuous near $c$.

- If $f''(c) > 0$, there is a local **minimum** at $c$ (a cup).
- If $f''(c) < 0$, there is a local **maximum** at $c$ (a cap).
- If $f''(c) = 0$, the test says nothing. Use the first derivative test instead.

For optimization, this gives a fast justification: if the only critical number has $f'' > 0$ everywhere on the domain, the graph is a cup and the critical point is the absolute minimum.

### A curve-sketching checklist

1. Domain, intercepts and symmetry.
2. Asymptotes: vertical lines where the function blows up, and horizontal lines from $\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} f(x)$.
3. Sign chart of $f'$: where $f$ increases or decreases, and the local extrema.
4. Sign chart of $f''$: concavity and inflection points.
5. Plot the key points and join them smoothly, following the signs.

A function can be increasing and concave down at the same time, as $\sqrt{x}$ is: the slope is positive but shrinking. Keep the two signs separate in your head.

@example
title: A full analysis of a quartic
problem: Analyze $f(x) = x^4 - 4x^3$: find where it increases and decreases, its local extrema, its concavity and its inflection points.
step: Differentiate: $f'(x) = 4x^3 - 12x^2 = 4x^2(x - 3)$. The critical numbers are $x = 0$ and $x = 3$.
step: Sign of $f'$: because $4x^2 \ge 0$, the sign is the sign of $x - 3$. So $f' < 0$ on $(-\infty, 0)$ and on $(0, 3)$, and $f' > 0$ on $(3, \infty)$. The function decreases until $x = 3$ and then increases: there is a local (and absolute) minimum $f(3) = -27$. At $x = 0$ the sign does not change, so there is no extremum there.
step: Second derivative: $f''(x) = 12x^2 - 24x = 12x(x - 2)$, which is zero at $x = 0$ and $x = 2$. Its sign is positive on $(-\infty, 0)$, negative on $(0, 2)$, positive on $(2, \infty)$.
step: Concavity: the graph is concave up on $(-\infty, 0)$ and $(2, \infty)$, and concave down on $(0, 2)$. The concavity changes at both $x = 0$ and $x = 2$, so the inflection points are $(0, 0)$ and $(2, -16)$.
step: Cross-check: $f''(3) = 36 > 0$ agrees that $x = 3$ is a minimum. At $x = 0$ we have $f''(0) = 0$, so the second derivative test is silent, and it was the first derivative test that settled it.

@example
title: Asymptote and extrema of a rational function
problem: Find the horizontal asymptote and the local extrema of $f(x) = \dfrac{x}{x^2 + 1}$.
step: Horizontal asymptote: divide top and bottom by $x^2$ to get $f(x) = \dfrac{1/x}{1 + 1/x^2}$, which tends to $0$ as $x \to \pm\infty$. So $y = 0$ is a horizontal asymptote.
step: Differentiate with the quotient rule: $f'(x) = \dfrac{(x^2 + 1) - x(2x)}{(x^2 + 1)^2} = \dfrac{1 - x^2}{(x^2 + 1)^2}$.
step: The denominator is always positive, so the sign of $f'$ is the sign of $1 - x^2$: negative for $x < -1$, positive on $(-1, 1)$, negative for $x > 1$.
step: So $f$ has a local minimum at $x = -1$, with $f(-1) = -\frac{1}{2}$, and a local maximum at $x = 1$, with $f(1) = \frac{1}{2}$.

@mistakes
- Declaring an extremum wherever $f'(c) = 0$. Without a sign change of $f'$, as at $x = 0$ for $x^4 - 4x^3$, there is none.
- Mixing up the two derivatives: the sign of $f'$ gives increasing or decreasing, the sign of $f''$ gives concavity.
- Calling every zero of $f''$ an inflection point. The concavity must actually change.
- Concluding "no extremum" when the second derivative test gives $f''(c) = 0$. The test is simply silent.

answer: 5
check: (lambda x: np.max(x**3 - 6*x**2 + 9*x + 1))(np.linspace(-1, 2, 300001))

solution: $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 12x + 9 = 3(x - 1)(x - 3)$, so $x = 1$ or $x = 3$. Since $f''(x) = 6x - 12$, we have $f''(1) = -6 < 0$ (local maximum) and $f''(3) = 6 > 0$ (local minimum). The local maximum value is $f(1) = 1 - 6 + 9 + 1 = 5$.

@practice tuple
prompt: Find the coordinates $(x, y)$ of the inflection point of $g(x) = 2x^3 - 9x^2 + 12x$.
answer: (3/2, 9/2)
check: (lambda g: (lambda c: (c, g(c)))(root(lambda x: nderiv(g, x, 2), 0, 5)))(lambda x: 2*x**3 - 9*x**2 + 12*x)
hint: The inflection point is where $g''$ changes sign. Compute $g''(x)$ and solve $g''(x) = 0$.
hint: $g''(x) = 12x - 18$. Once you have $x$, substitute it into $g$ to get the $y$-coordinate.
solution: $g''(x) = 12x - 18 = 0$ gives $x = \frac{3}{2}$, and $g''$ changes from negative to positive there. Then $g\left(\frac{3}{2}\right) = 2\cdot\frac{27}{8} - 9\cdot\frac{9}{4} + 18 = \frac{27}{4} - \frac{81}{4} + \frac{72}{4} = \frac{18}{4} = \frac{9}{2}$. The inflection point is $\left(\frac{3}{2}, \frac{9}{2}\right)$.

@practice self
prompt: A closed cylindrical can must hold $54\pi\ \text{cm}^3$. Find the radius $r$ and height $h$ that minimize its total surface area $S = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h$, and use the second derivative to justify that your answer is a minimum.
hint: The volume constraint is $\pi r^2 h = 54\pi$, so $h = \frac{54}{r^2}$. Substitute to get $S$ as a function of $r$ alone.
hint: $S(r) = 2\pi r^2 + \frac{108\pi}{r}$ for $r > 0$. Set $S'(r) = 0$ and then compute $S''(r)$.
solution: From $\pi r^2 h = 54\pi$ we get $h = \frac{54}{r^2}$, so $S(r) = 2\pi r^2 + \frac{108\pi}{r}$ for $r > 0$. Then $S'(r) = 4\pi r - \frac{108\pi}{r^2} = \frac{4\pi(r^3 - 27)}{r^2}$, which is zero at $r = 3$. Also $S''(r) = 4\pi + \frac{216\pi}{r^3}$, which is positive for every $r > 0$, so the graph of $S$ is concave up and the critical point is the absolute minimum. Then $h = \frac{54}{9} = 6$. The best can has $r = 3$ cm and $h = 6$ cm, and its surface area is $S(3) = 54\pi\ \text{cm}^2$.
rubric: Uses the volume constraint to write $S$ as a function of $r$ alone, with the domain $r > 0$.
rubric: Differentiates correctly and solves $S'(r) = 0$ to get $r = 3$.
rubric: Justifies the minimum with $S''(3) > 0$, and notes $S'' > 0$ on the whole domain so it is absolute.
rubric: States both dimensions ($r = 3$ cm, $h = 6$ cm) with units.

@quiz num
prompt: Find the $x$-coordinate of the inflection point of $f(x) = x e^{-x}$.
answer: 2
check: root(lambda x: nderiv(lambda t: t*exp(-t), x, 2), 0, 5)
hint: Compute $f'$ with the product rule, then differentiate again to get $f''$.
hint: $f'(x) = (1 - x)e^{-x}$. Differentiate once more, factor out $e^{-x}$, and find where the remaining factor is zero.
solution: $f'(x) = (1 - x)e^{-x}$ and $f''(x) = -e^{-x} - (1 - x)e^{-x} = (x - 2)e^{-x}$. Since $e^{-x} > 0$, $f''$ changes from negative to positive at $x = 2$, so the inflection point is at $x = 2$.
