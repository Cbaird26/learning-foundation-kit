@article
Draw a triangle on a sheet of paper: its angles add up to $180^\circ$. Draw one on a ball using great circles: the angles add up to more. On a saddle they add up to less. The excess is the total Gaussian curvature inside, and the Gauss–Bonnet theorem makes this precise and goes further: the total curvature of a closed surface does not depend on its shape at all, only on how many holes it has.

### Geodesic curvature and the local theorem

Let $S$ be an oriented surface with unit normal $N$ and let $\alpha$ be a unit-speed curve in $S$. Its acceleration splits as $\alpha'' = k_g\,(N\times\alpha') + \kappa_n N$, because $\alpha''\perp\alpha'$ and $\{\alpha', N\times\alpha', N\}$ is an orthonormal basis. The **geodesic curvature** is $k_g = \langle\alpha'', N\times\alpha'\rangle$ and $\kappa_n = \langle\alpha'', N\rangle$ is the normal curvature. A curve is a geodesic if and only if $k_g \equiv 0$. The vector $N\times\alpha'$ points to the left of $\alpha'$ as seen with $N$ pointing toward you.

**Theorem (local Gauss–Bonnet; stated without proof, with an outline).** Let $x\colon U \to S$ be an orthogonal chart ($F = 0$) of an oriented surface with $U$ homeomorphic to a disc, and let $R \subseteq x(U)$ be a simple region (homeomorphic to a closed disc) whose boundary is a simple closed piecewise regular curve, parametrized by arc length with $R$ on the left, with smooth arcs $\alpha_i$ and exterior angles $\theta_i \in (-\pi, \pi)$ at the vertices. Then
$$\sum_i\int_{\alpha_i}k_g\,ds + \iint_R K\,dA + \sum_i\theta_i = 2\pi.$$
*Outline.* Let $\varphi$ be the angle from $x_u$ to $\alpha'$ in the chart. Liouville's formula expresses $k_g = \frac{d\varphi}{ds} + \frac{G_uv' - E_vu'}{2\sqrt{EG}}$. The Umlaufsatz (Week 4) in the chart gives $\sum\int\varphi'\,ds + \sum\theta_i = 2\pi$. Green's theorem turns $\oint\frac{G_uv' - E_vu'}{2\sqrt{EG}}\,ds$ into an area integral, and the Gauss formula $K = -\frac{1}{2\sqrt{EG}}\left[\left(\frac{E_v}{\sqrt{EG}}\right)_v + \left(\frac{G_u}{\sqrt{EG}}\right)_u\right]$ shows it equals $-\iint_RK\,dA$.

**Corollary (geodesic triangles).** If $R$ is a triangle with geodesic sides and interior angles $\beta_1, \beta_2, \beta_3$, then $\beta_1 + \beta_2 + \beta_3 = \pi + \iint_RK\,dA$.

*Proof.* Here $k_g = 0$ on the sides and $\theta_i = \pi - \beta_i$. The local theorem gives $0 + \iint_RK\,dA + 3\pi - \sum\beta_i = 2\pi$. $\blacksquare$

On a sphere of radius $r$ with $K = 1/r^2$, the area of a geodesic triangle is $r^2(\beta_1 + \beta_2 + \beta_3 - \pi)$ (Girard's formula).

### The global theorem

The **Euler characteristic** of a triangulated compact surface with $V$ vertices, $E$ edges and $F$ triangles is $\chi = V - E + F$; it equals $2 - 2g$ for an orientable surface of genus $g$ (sphere $2$, torus $0$).

**Theorem (Gauss–Bonnet).** For a compact oriented regular surface $S \subset \mathbb{R}^3$, $\iint_SK\,dA = 2\pi\chi(S)$.

*Proof from the local theorem (assuming the standard fact that $S$ has a triangulation by small triangles with piecewise smooth sides, each inside an orthogonal chart).* Apply the local theorem to each of the $F$ triangles, with all boundaries oriented consistently. Each edge is traversed once in each direction by its two neighboring triangles, so the geodesic curvature integrals cancel in the sum, since $k_g$ changes sign when the direction reverses with $N$ fixed. The exterior angles are $\theta = \pi - \beta$ with $\beta$ the interior angle, so $\sum\theta = 3\pi F - \sum\beta$, and the interior angles around each vertex add to $2\pi$, giving $\sum\beta = 2\pi V$. The sum of the local theorems is $\iint_SK\,dA + 3\pi F - 2\pi V = 2\pi F$, so $\iint_SK\,dA = 2\pi V - \pi F$. Each triangle has three edges and each edge belongs to two triangles, so $3F = 2E$, hence $2\pi V - \pi F = 2\pi V - 3\pi F + 2\pi F = 2\pi(V - E + F)$. $\blacksquare$

### Checking the theorem by computation

**A spherical cap.** On a sphere of radius $r$ take the cap $0 \le \varphi \le \varphi_0$, bounded by the parallel $\varphi = \varphi_0$ of radius $r\sin\varphi_0$. With the outward normal and the boundary run counterclockwise (cap on the left), $k_g = \cot\varphi_0/r$, so $\oint k_g\,ds = \frac{\cot\varphi_0}{r}\cdot 2\pi r\sin\varphi_0 = 2\pi\cos\varphi_0$. The cap has no vertices, area $2\pi r^2(1 - \cos\varphi_0)$ and $K = 1/r^2$, so $\iint K\,dA = 2\pi(1 - \cos\varphi_0)$. The sum is $2\pi$, as the local theorem says. The program below computes $k_g$ from its definition by finite differences.

```python
import math


def cross(a, b):
    return (a[1] * b[2] - a[2] * b[1], a[2] * b[0] - a[0] * b[2],
            a[0] * b[1] - a[1] * b[0])


def dot(a, b):
    return sum(x * y for x, y in zip(a, b))


r, phi0 = 2.0, 1.0
rho = r * math.sin(phi0)
alpha = lambda s: (rho * math.cos(s / rho), rho * math.sin(s / rho), r * math.cos(phi0))


def geodesic_curvature(s, h=1e-4):
    p, c, m = alpha(s + h), alpha(s), alpha(s - h)
    T = [(x - y) / (2 * h) for x, y in zip(p, m)]
    A = [(x - 2 * z + y) / h**2 for x, y, z in zip(p, m, c)]
    return dot(A, cross([x / r for x in c], T))


n, length = 100, 2 * math.pi * rho
boundary = sum(geodesic_curvature((i + 0.5) * length / n) for i in range(n)) * length / n
area_term = (1 / r**2) * 2 * math.pi * r**2 * (1 - math.cos(phi0))
print(round(boundary, 4), round(area_term, 4), round(boundary + area_term, 4))
```

The output is `3.3948 2.8884 6.2832`, and $6.2832 \approx 2\pi$. This checks one cap numerically; the theorem is what covers all regions.

**The torus.** For $x(u, v) = ((R + r\cos u)\cos v, (R + r\cos u)\sin v, r\sin u)$, $R > r > 0$, Week 8 gives $K = \cos u/(r(R + r\cos u))$ and Week 5 gives $|x_u\times x_v| = r(R + r\cos u)$. So $K\,dA = \cos u\,du\,dv$ and
$$\iint_{T^2}K\,dA = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{2\pi}\cos u\,du\,dv = 0 = 2\pi\chi(T^2).$$
The two circles $u = 0$ and $v = 0$ missing from the chart have area $0$. The outer half $|u| < \pi/2$ (where $K > 0$) contributes $2\pi\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\cos u\,du = 4\pi$, the same as a sphere, and the inner half contributes $-4\pi$.

@example
title: A cap larger than a hemisphere
problem: On the unit sphere, take the cap $0 \le \varphi \le \varphi_0$ with $\varphi_0 = \pi/3$. Verify the local Gauss–Bonnet theorem.
step: The boundary is the parallel $\varphi = \pi/3$, counterclockwise as seen from the north pole, with the cap on its left.
step: Its geodesic curvature is $k_g = \cot(\pi/3) = 1/\sqrt3$ and its length is $2\pi\sin(\pi/3) = \pi\sqrt3$, so $\oint k_g\,ds = \pi$.
step: Since $K = 1$ and the area of the cap is $2\pi(1 - \cos(\pi/3)) = \pi$, we get $\iint K\,dA = \pi$.
step: There are no vertices, so the total is $\pi + \pi = 2\pi$, as required.
answer: $\oint k_g\,ds = \pi$ and $\iint K\,dA = \pi$, summing to $2\pi$.

@example
title: Area of a spherical triangle from its angles
problem: A geodesic triangle on a sphere of radius $2$ has interior angles $80^\circ$, $70^\circ$ and $60^\circ$. Find its area.
step: The angle sum is $210^\circ = 7\pi/6$ radians, so the excess over $\pi$ is $\pi/6$.
step: By the corollary, $\iint K\,dA = \pi/6$.
step: On this sphere $K = 1/4$ is constant, so the area is $\iint K\,dA/K = 4\cdot\pi/6$.
step: Evaluate: $2\pi/3 \approx 2.094$.
answer: The area is $2\pi/3 \approx 2.094$.

@mistakes
- Forgetting the exterior angles: for a curvilinear polygon the vertex terms $\sum\theta_i$ are part of the formula, with $\theta_i = \pi - \beta_i$ for interior angle $\beta_i$.
- Using a clockwise boundary: the theorem needs the region on the left, otherwise the signs of $k_g$ and the angles change.
- Applying the global formula to a surface with boundary or to a noncompact surface: $\iint K\,dA = 2\pi\chi$ needs $S$ compact without boundary.
- Believing $\iint_SK\,dA$ depends on the shape: any smooth closed surface of genus $g$ has total curvature $2\pi(2 - 2g)$, whether it is a round torus or a lumpy one.

model:
```python
def total_curvature(N, u0, u1, v0, v1, n, h):
    """Midpoint-rule integral of <N, N_u x N_v> (signed area of Gauss map)."""
    du = (u1 - u0) / n
    dv = (v1 - v0) / n
    total = 0.0
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(n):
            u = u0 + (i + 0.5) * du
            v = v0 + (j + 0.5) * dv
            nu = [(a - b) / (2 * h) for a, b in zip(N(u + h, v), N(u - h, v))]
            nv = [(a - b) / (2 * h) for a, b in zip(N(u, v + h), N(u, v - h))]
            c = (nu[1] * nv[2] - nu[2] * nv[1],
                 nu[2] * nv[0] - nu[0] * nv[2],
                 nu[0] * nv[1] - nu[1] * nv[0])
            total += sum(a * b for a, b in zip(N(u, v), c))
    return total * du * dv
```
hint: At each cell midpoint compute $N_u$ and $N_v$ by central differences, take their cross product, and dot it with $N$ at the midpoint.
hint: Multiply the sum by the cell area $\Delta u\,\Delta v$; do not take absolute values, since the sign records the orientation.
solution: Because $dN$ has determinant $K$, $N_u\times N_v = K\,x_u\times x_v$ and $\langle N_u\times N_v, N\rangle = K|x_u\times x_v|$ when $N$ is the normal of the chart. The program is a midpoint rule on that integrand. For the ellipsoid it gives about $4\pi$, which is $2\pi\chi(S^2)$ whatever the semi-axes; for the torus it gives $0$ over the whole surface because the positive and negative parts cancel. Taking absolute values would give the total absolute curvature $8\pi$, and reversing the normal flips the sign. The numbers are evidence of Gauss–Bonnet for these surfaces, not a proof.
