@article
Gaussian curvature is defined through the Gauss map, which looks at how the normal vector tilts in the space around the surface. Yet an ant confined to the surface, who can only measure lengths and angles, can still compute it. That is the content of Gauss's Theorema Egregium (1827), and it is the reason a flat sheet of paper cannot be wrapped around a sphere without stretching.

### Christoffel symbols

Let $x\colon U \to S$ be a regular chart with $x$ smooth. At each point $x_u, x_v, N$ is a basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$, so the second derivatives expand as
$$x_{uu} = \Gamma^1_{11}x_u + \Gamma^2_{11}x_v + eN,\quad x_{uv} = \Gamma^1_{12}x_u + \Gamma^2_{12}x_v + fN,\quad x_{vv} = \Gamma^1_{22}x_u + \Gamma^2_{22}x_v + gN.$$
The coefficients $\Gamma^k_{ij}$ are the **Christoffel symbols**; the $N$-components are $e, f, g$ because $\langle N, x_{ij}\rangle$ are those coefficients (Week 7).

**Proposition 1.** The Christoffel symbols are determined by $E, F, G$ and their first partial derivatives:
$$\begin{pmatrix}\Gamma^1_{ij}\\\Gamma^2_{ij}\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}E & F\\F & G\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\langle x_{ij}, x_u\rangle\\\langle x_{ij}, x_v\rangle\end{pmatrix},$$
where $\langle x_{uu}, x_u\rangle = \tfrac12E_u$, $\langle x_{uu}, x_v\rangle = F_u - \tfrac12E_v$, $\langle x_{uv}, x_u\rangle = \tfrac12E_v$, $\langle x_{uv}, x_v\rangle = \tfrac12G_u$, $\langle x_{vv}, x_u\rangle = F_v - \tfrac12G_u$ and $\langle x_{vv}, x_v\rangle = \tfrac12G_v$.

*Proof.* Take the dot product of the expansion of $x_{ij}$ with $x_u$ and with $x_v$; since $N \perp x_u, x_v$, this gives $\Gamma^1_{ij}E + \Gamma^2_{ij}F = \langle x_{ij}, x_u\rangle$ and $\Gamma^1_{ij}F + \Gamma^2_{ij}G = \langle x_{ij}, x_v\rangle$, a linear system with the invertible matrix of the first fundamental form. The right sides come from differentiating $E = \langle x_u, x_u\rangle$, $F = \langle x_u, x_v\rangle$, $G = \langle x_v, x_v\rangle$: for example $E_u = 2\langle x_{uu}, x_u\rangle$, $E_v = 2\langle x_{uv}, x_u\rangle$, $F_u = \langle x_{uu}, x_v\rangle + \langle x_u, x_{uv}\rangle$, so $\langle x_{uu}, x_v\rangle = F_u - \tfrac12E_v$; the others are the same. $\blacksquare$

### The Gauss formula

**Theorem (Theorema Egregium, Gauss 1827).** Let $x$ be a smooth regular chart. Then
$$EK = (\Gamma^2_{11})_v - (\Gamma^2_{12})_u + \Gamma^1_{11}\Gamma^2_{12} + \Gamma^2_{11}\Gamma^2_{22} - \Gamma^1_{12}\Gamma^2_{11} - (\Gamma^2_{12})^2 .$$
In particular $K$ depends only on $E, F, G$ and their derivatives up to order two.

*Proof.* Since $x$ is smooth, $(x_{uu})_v = (x_{uv})_u$. Expand both sides in the basis $x_u, x_v, N$ using the expansions above, together with $N_u = a_{11}x_u + a_{21}x_v$ and $N_v = a_{12}x_u + a_{22}x_v$ (Proposition 1 of Week 7 shows $N_u, N_v$ are tangent). The $x_v$-component of $(x_{uu})_v = (\Gamma^1_{11})_vx_u + \Gamma^1_{11}x_{uv} + (\Gamma^2_{11})_vx_v + \Gamma^2_{11}x_{vv} + e_vN + eN_v$ is $\Gamma^1_{11}\Gamma^2_{12} + (\Gamma^2_{11})_v + \Gamma^2_{11}\Gamma^2_{22} + e\,a_{22}$. The $x_v$-component of $(x_{uv})_u = (\Gamma^1_{12})_ux_u + \Gamma^1_{12}x_{uu} + (\Gamma^2_{12})_ux_v + \Gamma^2_{12}x_{uv} + f_uN + fN_u$ is $\Gamma^1_{12}\Gamma^2_{11} + (\Gamma^2_{12})_u + (\Gamma^2_{12})^2 + f\,a_{21}$. Equating them,
$$(\Gamma^2_{11})_v - (\Gamma^2_{12})_u + \Gamma^1_{11}\Gamma^2_{12} + \Gamma^2_{11}\Gamma^2_{22} - \Gamma^1_{12}\Gamma^2_{11} - (\Gamma^2_{12})^2 = f\,a_{21} - e\,a_{22}.$$
By Week 8 the matrix $A = (a_{kj}) = -\mathcal{I}^{-1}\mathcal{II}$, which gives $a_{21} = (Fe - Ef)/D$ and $a_{22} = (Ff - Eg)/D$ with $D = EG - F^2$. Then $f a_{21} - e a_{22} = \dfrac{Fef - Ef^2 - eFf + Eeg}{D} = \dfrac{E(eg - f^2)}{D} = EK$. $\blacksquare$

The left side involves only Christoffel symbols, which by Proposition 1 are built from $E, F, G$. The quantities $e, f, g$ and $N$ have disappeared. Quantities computable from $E, F, G$ are called **intrinsic**. Lengths, angles, areas and $K$ are intrinsic; $H$ and the principal curvatures separately are not (a cylinder and a plane have the same $E, F, G$ but different $H$).

**Orthogonal charts with $E = 1$.** If $E = 1$ and $F = 0$, then $\Gamma^1_{11} = \Gamma^2_{11} = \Gamma^1_{12} = 0$ and $\Gamma^2_{12} = G_u/(2G) = (\sqrt G)_u/\sqrt G$. The formula reduces to $K = -(\Gamma^2_{12})_u - (\Gamma^2_{12})^2$, and since $(\Gamma^2_{12})_u = (\sqrt G)_{uu}/\sqrt G - ((\sqrt G)_u/\sqrt G)^2$,
$$K = -\frac{(\sqrt G)_{uu}}{\sqrt G}.$$
This is the quickest way to compute curvature from a metric $du^2 + G\,dv^2$.

@example
title: Curvature of a metric from the intrinsic formula
problem: A surface has chart with $E = 1$, $F = 0$, $G = 4\sin^2(u/2)$. Find $K$.
step: Here $\sqrt G = 2\sin(u/2)$ for $0 < u < 2\pi$.
step: Differentiate: $(\sqrt G)_u = \cos(u/2)$ and $(\sqrt G)_{uu} = -\tfrac12\sin(u/2)$.
step: Apply $K = -(\sqrt G)_{uu}/\sqrt G = \tfrac12\sin(u/2)/(2\sin(u/2))$.
step: Simplify: $K = \tfrac14$. This is the sphere of radius $2$ in geodesic polar coordinates, whose curvature $1/r^2$ is $\tfrac14$.
answer: $K = 1/4$.

@example
title: A flat metric
problem: Find $K$ for the metric with $E = 1$, $F = 0$, $G = u^2$ on $u > 0$.
step: $\sqrt G = u$, so $(\sqrt G)_{uu} = 0$.
step: Hence $K = -0/u = 0$.
step: These are polar coordinates $(u, v)$ on the Euclidean plane: $x = u\cos v$, $y = u\sin v$ has $E = 1$, $F = 0$, $G = u^2$. The flat plane has $K = 0$, as it should.
answer: $K = 0$.

@mistakes
- Believing $K$ depends on how the surface sits in space: it is determined by $E, F, G$, so any bending without stretching preserves it (a cylinder is flat like the plane).
- Thinking all curvature quantities are intrinsic: the principal curvatures and $H$ change when a plane is rolled into a cylinder, while $K$ does not.
- Using $K = -(\sqrt G)_{uu}/\sqrt G$ when $E \ne 1$ or $F \ne 0$: it requires the chart $du^2 + G\,dv^2$.
- Omitting a hypothesis in the proof: the Gauss formula needs $(x_{uu})_v = (x_{uv})_u$, which holds for smooth charts.

@practice num
prompt: A surface has a chart with $E = 1$, $F = 0$ and $G = (1 + u^2)^2$. Find the Gaussian curvature at $u = 1$.
answer: -1
check: -(2.0 / (1 + 1**2))
hint: $\sqrt G = 1 + u^2$ for all $u$.
hint: Use $K = -(\sqrt G)_{uu}/\sqrt G$.
solution: $(\sqrt G)_{uu} = 2$, so $K = -2/(1 + u^2)$. At $u = 1$, $K = -2/2 = -1$.

@practice mc
prompt: Which statement is the Theorema Egregium?
choice*: The Gaussian curvature of a regular surface can be expressed using only the coefficients $E, F, G$ of the first fundamental form and their partial derivatives.
choice: The Gaussian curvature is the determinant of the second fundamental form.
choice: The mean curvature of a surface is determined by its first fundamental form.
choice: Every surface with constant Gaussian curvature is a sphere.
hint: The theorem's name refers to a remarkable fact about an extrinsic-looking quantity.
solution: $K$ is defined through the Gauss map, but the theorem says it is determined by the metric. The determinant of $\mathcal{II}$ alone is $eg - f^2$, which differs from $K$ unless $EG - F^2 = 1$. The mean curvature is not intrinsic, and the plane, the cylinder and the cone all have constant $K = 0$ without being spheres.

@practice self
prompt: A surface has a chart with $E = 1$, $F = 0$, $G = e^{2u}$. Compute the six Christoffel symbols and use the Gauss formula to prove that $K = -1$.
hint: With $E = 1$ and $F = 0$, only $\Gamma^2_{12}$, $\Gamma^1_{22}$ and $\Gamma^2_{22}$ can be nonzero. Use Proposition 1.
solution: With $E = 1$, $F = 0$, $G = e^{2u}$: $E_u = E_v = F_u = F_v = 0$, $G_u = 2e^{2u}$, $G_v = 0$. Using Proposition 1, the right sides give $\Gamma^1_{11} = \Gamma^2_{11} = 0$ (all of $\langle x_{uu}, x_u\rangle$, $\langle x_{uu}, x_v\rangle$ vanish), $\Gamma^1_{12} = \tfrac12E_v/E = 0$, $\Gamma^2_{12} = \tfrac12G_u/G = 1$, $\Gamma^1_{22} = (F_v - \tfrac12G_u)/E = -e^{2u}$ and $\Gamma^2_{22} = \tfrac12G_v/G = 0$. In the Gauss formula, $(\Gamma^2_{11})_v = 0$, $(\Gamma^2_{12})_u = 0$ and $\Gamma^1_{11}\Gamma^2_{12} = \Gamma^2_{11}\Gamma^2_{22} = \Gamma^1_{12}\Gamma^2_{11} = 0$, leaving $EK = -(\Gamma^2_{12})^2 = -1$. Since $E = 1$, $K = -1$.
rubric: Computes all six Christoffel symbols from Proposition 1 (nonzero ones: $\Gamma^2_{12} = 1$ and $\Gamma^1_{22} = -e^{2u}$).
rubric: Substitutes into the Gauss formula and identifies the surviving term.
rubric: Concludes $K = -1$ using $E = 1$.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which statements follow from the Theorema Egregium?
choice*: Two charts with the same functions $E, F, G$ on the same parameter domain give surfaces with the same Gaussian curvature at corresponding points.
choice*: The cylinder $x = (\cos u, \sin u, v)$ has $K = 0$ at every point, the same value as the plane.
choice: The principal curvatures of a surface are determined by $E, F, G$.
choice: The mean curvature $H$ is the same for isometric surfaces.
hint: Use the fact that the cylinder has $E = G = 1$, $F = 0$.
solution: The first statement is the theorem. For the cylinder $E = G = 1$ and $F = 0$, so $K = -(\sqrt G)_{uu}/\sqrt G = 0$. The principal curvatures and $H$ are not intrinsic: the cylinder has $\kappa_1 = 1$, $\kappa_2 = 0$ while the plane with the same $E, F, G$ has both zero.
