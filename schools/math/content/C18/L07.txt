@article
Stand on the top of a hill and on the saddle of a mountain pass. On the hill every path you take curves downward away from your tangent plane; at the pass some paths curve down and others up. The second fundamental form records exactly this: how fast the surface bends away from its tangent plane in each direction.

### The Gauss map

Let $x\colon U \to S$ be a regular chart with unit normal $N = x_u \times x_v/|x_u \times x_v|$ (an **orientation** of the chart; the opposite normal is $-N$). The **Gauss map** is $N\colon x(U) \to S^2$, sending $p$ to the unit normal at $p$. Its differential at $p$ is the linear map $dN_p\colon T_pS \to \mathbb{R}^3$ with $dN_p(\alpha'(0)) = (N\circ\alpha)'(0)$; in the chart, $dN_p(x_u) = N_u$ and $dN_p(x_v) = N_v$.

**Proposition 1.** $dN_p$ maps $T_pS$ into $T_pS$.

*Proof.* Differentiating $\langle N, N\rangle = 1$ along any curve in $S$ gives $2\langle dN_p(w), N\rangle = 0$, so $dN_p(w)$ is perpendicular to $N(p)$. But $T_pS = N(p)^\perp$. $\blacksquare$

So $dN_p$ is a linear operator on the plane $T_pS$ (the shape operator, up to a sign convention).

**Proposition 2.** $dN_p$ is self-adjoint: $\langle dN_p(w_1), w_2\rangle = \langle w_1, dN_p(w_2)\rangle$ for all $w_1, w_2 \in T_pS$.

*Proof.* By bilinearity it suffices to check the basis $x_u, x_v$, that is, $\langle N_u, x_v\rangle = \langle x_u, N_v\rangle$. Differentiate $\langle N, x_u\rangle = 0$ with respect to $v$ and $\langle N, x_v\rangle = 0$ with respect to $u$:
$$\langle N_v, x_u\rangle + \langle N, x_{uv}\rangle = 0,\qquad \langle N_u, x_v\rangle + \langle N, x_{vu}\rangle = 0.$$
Since $x_{uv} = x_{vu}$ for a smooth map, $\langle N_v, x_u\rangle = \langle N_u, x_v\rangle = -\langle N, x_{uv}\rangle$. $\blacksquare$

### The second fundamental form

**Definition.** The **second fundamental form** of $S$ at $p$ is the quadratic form $\mathrm{II}_p(w) = -\langle dN_p(w), w\rangle$ on $T_pS$. Its coefficients in a chart are
$$e = -\langle N_u, x_u\rangle = \langle N, x_{uu}\rangle,\quad f = -\langle N_u, x_v\rangle = \langle N, x_{uv}\rangle,\quad g = -\langle N_v, x_v\rangle = \langle N, x_{vv}\rangle,$$
and $\mathrm{II}(ax_u + bx_v) = ea^2 + 2fab + gb^2$. The two expressions for each coefficient agree by differentiating $\langle N, x_u\rangle = 0$ and $\langle N, x_v\rangle = 0$, as in the proof above (using Proposition 2 for $f$). Replacing $N$ by $-N$ changes the sign of $\mathrm{II}$ and leaves $\mathrm{I}$ alone.

**Proposition 3 (normal curvature).** Let $\alpha$ be a unit-speed curve in $S$ with $\alpha(0) = p$ and $\alpha'(0) = w$. Then $\langle\alpha''(0), N(p)\rangle = \mathrm{II}_p(w)$.

*Proof.* $\langle\alpha'(t), N(\alpha(t))\rangle = 0$ because $\alpha' \in T_{\alpha(t)}S$. Differentiate: $\langle\alpha'', N\rangle + \langle\alpha', dN(\alpha')\rangle = 0$. At $t = 0$ this reads $\langle\alpha'', N\rangle = -\langle dN_p(w), w\rangle$. $\blacksquare$

The number $\kappa_n(w) = \mathrm{II}_p(w)$ for a unit vector $w$ is the **normal curvature** in direction $w$: it is the component of the acceleration of any unit-speed curve with velocity $w$ along the normal, so all curves tangent to $w$ share it. For a curve with $\kappa > 0$ and angle $\theta$ between $N_{\text{curve}}$ and $N$, $\kappa_n = \kappa\cos\theta$.

### Graphs

For $x(u, v) = (u, v, h(u, v))$ let $W = \sqrt{1 + h_u^2 + h_v^2}$. Then $N = (-h_u, -h_v, 1)/W$ and $x_{uu} = (0, 0, h_{uu})$, $x_{uv} = (0, 0, h_{uv})$, $x_{vv} = (0, 0, h_{vv})$, so
$$e = \frac{h_{uu}}{W},\qquad f = \frac{h_{uv}}{W},\qquad g = \frac{h_{vv}}{W}.$$
At a point where $\nabla h = 0$ the tangent plane is horizontal and $\mathrm{II}$ is just the Hessian of $h$.

**The sphere.** For radius $r$ with outward unit normal $N(p) = p/r$ we have $dN_p = \tfrac1r\operatorname{Id}$, hence $\mathrm{II}_p(w) = -|w|^2/r$: every unit direction has normal curvature $-1/r$. With the inward normal the sign is $+1/r$. Only the magnitude $1/r$ is orientation-free.

@example
title: Second fundamental form of a graph at a point
problem: For $z = h(u, v) = u^2v$ find $e$, $f$, $g$ at $(u, v) = (1, 1)$ for the upward normal.
step: Compute the derivatives: $h_u = 2uv = 2$, $h_v = u^2 = 1$, $h_{uu} = 2v = 2$, $h_{uv} = 2u = 2$, $h_{vv} = 0$.
step: Then $W = \sqrt{1 + 4 + 1} = \sqrt6$.
step: Apply the graph formulas: $e = 2/\sqrt6$, $f = 2/\sqrt6$, $g = 0$.
step: So $\mathrm{II}(a, b) = \frac{2}{\sqrt6}(a^2 + 2ab)$ in the coordinate basis $x_u, x_v$; for example $\mathrm{II}(x_v) = 0$, so the coordinate curve $u = 1$ has zero normal acceleration at this point.
answer: $e = f = 2/\sqrt6 \approx 0.8165$ and $g = 0$.

@example
title: The circular cylinder
problem: For the cylinder $x(u, v) = (2\cos u,\ 2\sin u,\ v)$ with $N = (\cos u, \sin u, 0)$, find $\mathrm{II}$ and the normal curvature of a unit vector making angle $\psi$ with the vertical rulings.
step: Differentiate: $x_u = (-2\sin u, 2\cos u, 0)$, $x_v = (0, 0, 1)$ and $N_u = (-\sin u, \cos u, 0) = x_u/2$, $N_v = 0$.
step: Coefficients: $e = -\langle N_u, x_u\rangle = -\langle x_u/2, x_u\rangle = -2$, $f = -\langle N_u, x_v\rangle = 0$, $g = -\langle N_v, x_v\rangle = 0$.
step: A unit vector $w = ax_u + bx_v$ has $4a^2 + b^2 = 1$; the angle $\psi$ with $x_v$ gives $b = \cos\psi$ and $2a = \sin\psi$ up to sign.
step: Then $\mathrm{II}(w) = -2a^2 = -\tfrac12\sin^2\psi$.
answer: $\mathrm{II}(a, b) = -2a^2$; the normal curvature is $-\tfrac12\sin^2\psi$, equal to $0$ along the rulings and $-\tfrac12$ around the circle.

@mistakes
- Forgetting that $\mathrm{II}$ depends on the choice of $N$: reversing the orientation flips the sign of $e, f, g$ and of every normal curvature.
- Dividing by $W$ incorrectly in the graph formulas: the coefficient is $h_{uu}/W$ with $W = \sqrt{1 + h_u^2 + h_v^2}$ (not $W^2$), and the formulas hold for the upward normal.
- Reading $dN_p$ as a map into $\mathbb{R}^3$ without noticing it lands in $T_pS$: that is why it can be treated as a $2\times2$ matrix.
- Taking $\mathrm{II}$ to be positive definite: it is for the sphere with the inward normal, but not for a saddle.

answer: -1/8
check: -0.25 * math.cos(math.pi / 4) ** 2
hint: Here $N_u = x_u/4$ and $N_v = 0$, so $e = -\langle x_u/4, x_u\rangle = -4$ and $f = g = 0$.

@practice self
prompt: Let $x\colon U \to S$ be a regular chart on a connected open set $U$ such that $\mathrm{II} \equiv 0$ on $x(U)$. Prove that $x(U)$ lies in a plane.
hint: Show $N_u = N_v = 0$ by testing against the basis $x_u, x_v, N$ of $\mathbb{R}^3$. Then differentiate $\langle x, N\rangle$.
solution: $\mathrm{II} \equiv 0$ means $e = f = g = 0$. Then $\langle N_u, x_u\rangle = -e = 0$, $\langle N_u, x_v\rangle = -f = 0$, and $\langle N_u, N\rangle = 0$ from $\langle N, N\rangle = 1$. Since $x_u, x_v, N$ is a basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$, $N_u = 0$. In the same way $\langle N_v, x_u\rangle = -f = 0$, $\langle N_v, x_v\rangle = -g = 0$ and $\langle N_v, N\rangle = 0$ give $N_v = 0$. As $U$ is connected and both partial derivatives of $N$ vanish, $N$ is a constant vector $N_0$. Then $\partial_u\langle x, N_0\rangle = \langle x_u, N_0\rangle = 0$ and likewise for $v$, so $\langle x, N_0\rangle$ is constant, $= c$, and $x(U)$ lies in the plane $\langle y, N_0\rangle = c$.
rubric: Derives $e = f = g = 0$ implies $N_u = N_v = 0$ by orthogonality to the basis $x_u, x_v, N$.
rubric: Uses connectedness of $U$ to conclude $N$ is constant.
rubric: Differentiates $\langle x, N_0\rangle$ to show it is constant and identifies the plane.

solution: Differentiating $\langle N, x_u\rangle = 0$ in $v$ gives $\langle N_v, x_u\rangle = -\langle N, x_{uv}\rangle$, and $f = -\langle N_v, x_u\rangle = \langle N, x_{uv}\rangle$.
