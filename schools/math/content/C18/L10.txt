@article
You can roll a sheet of paper into a cylinder or a cone without tearing or stretching it, but you cannot flatten an orange peel onto a table without splitting it. Mathematically: some surfaces are *locally isometric* to the plane and the sphere is not. The Theorema Egregium turns this everyday fact into a proof.

### Isometries

**Definition.** Let $S, \bar S$ be regular surfaces. A diffeomorphism $\varphi\colon S \to \bar S$ is an **isometry** if $\langle d\varphi_p(w_1), d\varphi_p(w_2)\rangle = \langle w_1, w_2\rangle$ for all $p \in S$ and $w_1, w_2 \in T_pS$. A **local isometry at $p$** is an isometry between neighborhoods of $p$ and $\varphi(p)$. Isometries preserve the lengths of curves, because $|(\varphi\circ\alpha)'| = |d\varphi(\alpha')| = |\alpha'|$.

**Proposition 1.** Let $x\colon U \to S$ and $\bar x\colon U \to \bar S$ be regular charts on the same parameter domain $U$ with equal first fundamental forms: $E = \bar E$, $F = \bar F$, $G = \bar G$ as functions on $U$. Then $\varphi = \bar x\circ x^{-1}\colon x(U) \to \bar x(U)$ is an isometry. Conversely, if $\varphi$ is an isometry, then $\bar x = \varphi\circ x$ is a chart with the same $E, F, G$ as $x$.

*Proof.* $\varphi$ is a diffeomorphism because both charts are. We have $\varphi\circ x = \bar x$, so $d\varphi(x_u) = \bar x_u$ and $d\varphi(x_v) = \bar x_v$. Then $\langle d\varphi(x_u), d\varphi(x_u)\rangle = \bar E = E = \langle x_u, x_u\rangle$, and similarly for $F$ and $G$. The two bilinear forms $\langle d\varphi(\cdot), d\varphi(\cdot)\rangle$ and $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ agree on the basis $x_u, x_v$, hence on all of $T_pS$ by bilinearity. For the converse, $d\varphi$ preserves inner products, so $\bar E = \langle d\varphi(x_u), d\varphi(x_u)\rangle = E$, and the same for $F$ and $G$. $\blacksquare$

### Isometries preserve curvature

**Theorem 1.** If $\varphi\colon S \to \bar S$ is an isometry, then $\bar K(\varphi(p)) = K(p)$ for every $p \in S$.

*Proof.* Take a chart $x$ around $p$. By Proposition 1, $\bar x = \varphi\circ x$ is a chart with the same $E, F, G$ as functions of $(u, v)$. By the Theorema Egregium, $K$ is computed from $E, F, G$ and their derivatives by the same formula for both charts, so $K(x(u, v)) = \bar K(\bar x(u, v))$. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary (no flat map of the sphere).** Let $r > 0$. No nonempty open subset of the sphere of radius $r$ is isometric to an open subset of the plane.

*Proof.* The sphere has the chart $x(u, v) = (r\sin(u/r)\cos v,\ r\sin(u/r)\sin v,\ r\cos(u/r))$ for $0 < u < \pi r$. Then $x_u = (\cos(u/r)\cos v, \cos(u/r)\sin v, -\sin(u/r))$, so $E = 1$; $x_v = (-r\sin(u/r)\sin v,\ r\sin(u/r)\cos v,\ 0)$, so $G = r^2\sin^2(u/r)$; and $F = 0$. By Week 9, $K = -(\sqrt G)_{uu}/\sqrt G$ with $\sqrt G = r\sin(u/r)$, so $K = \frac{1}{r}\sin(u/r)\big/ (r\sin(u/r)) = 1/r^2 > 0$. (Every point of the sphere lies in such a chart after a rotation.) The plane has the chart $y(u, v) = (u, v, 0)$ with $E = G = 1$, $F = 0$, so $\bar K = 0$. If $\varphi$ were an isometry from an open $V \subseteq$ sphere onto an open piece of the plane, Theorem 1 would give $1/r^2 = K(p) = \bar K(\varphi(p)) = 0$, a contradiction. $\blacksquare$

So every map of part of the Earth distorts lengths or angles or areas somewhere (a fact that can also be seen from the Gauss map picture, but not so easily).

### The converse fails: catenoid and helicoid

Equal curvature is not the same as isometry: the map $(x, y) \mapsto (2x, y)$ of the plane to itself preserves $K = 0$ but stretches lengths. Conversely, the **catenoid** $x(u, v) = (\cosh u\cos v,\ \cosh u\sin v,\ u)$ and the **helicoid** $\bar x(u, v) = (\sinh u\cos v,\ \sinh u\sin v,\ v)$ have $E = \bar E = \cosh^2 u$, $F = \bar F = 0$, $G = \bar G = \cosh^2 u$ (check: $x_u = (\sinh u\cos v, \sinh u\sin v, 1)$ has $|x_u|^2 = \sinh^2 u + 1$ and $x_v = (-\cosh u\sin v, \cosh u\cos v, 0)$ has $|x_v|^2 = \cosh^2 u$; for the helicoid $|\bar x_u|^2 = \cosh^2 u$ and $|\bar x_v|^2 = \sinh^2 u + 1$). By Proposition 1 each small piece of the catenoid (restrict $v$ to an interval of length less than $2\pi$) is isometric to a piece of the helicoid, though one is a surface of revolution and the other is a ruled surface made of lines: the catenoid can be bent into the helicoid without stretching.

### Developable surfaces

A surface locally isometric to the plane is called **developable** (it can be rolled out flat). A connected regular surface with $K \equiv 0$ is locally isometric to the plane; this follows from a classical theorem on surfaces of constant curvature that we state without proof. Cylinders and cones are the standard examples, and we prove them directly.

*Cylinder.* $x(s, t) = (a\cos(s/a), a\sin(s/a), t)$ has $E = 1$, $F = 0$, $G = 1$, the same as the plane chart $y(s, t) = (s, t, 0)$. By Proposition 1 the cylinder is locally isometric to the plane: it is the plane rolled up.

*Cone.* For a half-angle $\alpha \in (0, \pi/2)$, $x(r, \theta) = (r\sin\alpha\cos\theta,\ r\sin\alpha\sin\theta,\ r\cos\alpha)$, $r > 0$, has $E = 1$, $F = 0$, $G = r^2\sin^2\alpha$. The plane map $y(r, \theta) = (r\cos(\theta\sin\alpha),\ r\sin(\theta\sin\alpha))$ has $y_r = (\cos\psi, \sin\psi)$, $y_\theta = \sin\alpha\,(-r\sin\psi, r\cos\psi)$ with $\psi = \theta\sin\alpha$, so $E = 1$, $F = 0$, $G = r^2\sin^2\alpha$ again. On $0 < \theta < 2\pi$ the angle $\psi$ stays in $(0, 2\pi\sin\alpha) \subset (0, 2\pi)$, so $y$ is injective: it unrolls the cone onto a **circular sector of angle $2\pi\sin\alpha$**. Slits of the same sector in the plane are glued along the two bounding rays to rebuild the cone.

@example
title: Unrolling a cone
problem: A cone has half-angle $\alpha = 30^\circ$. Describe the sector it unrolls onto, and find the area of the part with $0 < r \le 6$.
step: The sector angle is $2\pi\sin\alpha = 2\pi\cdot\tfrac12 = \pi$, a half-disc.
step: The sector's radius is the slant distance $r$, so $0 < r \le 6$ unrolls to a half-disc of radius $6$.
step: By Proposition 1 the unrolling is an isometry, so areas agree: $\tfrac12\cdot\pi\cdot 6^2 = 18\pi$.
step: Check with the area formula: $\sqrt{EG - F^2} = r\sin\alpha = r/2$, and $\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^6 \tfrac r2\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi\cdot\tfrac{36}{4} = 18\pi$.
answer: A half-disc of radius $6$ with area $18\pi \approx 56.55$.

@example
title: Curvature of the helicoid from the catenoid
problem: The catenoid has $K = -1/\cosh^4 u$ (Week 8). What is the Gaussian curvature of the helicoid $\bar x(u, v) = (\sinh u\cos v, \sinh u\sin v, v)$ at $\bar x(u, v)$?
step: Both charts have $E = G = \cosh^2 u$ and $F = 0$ on the same parameter domain (see above).
step: By Proposition 1 the map $x(u, v) \mapsto \bar x(u, v)$ is a local isometry.
step: By Theorem 1 the Gaussian curvature at $\bar x(u, v)$ equals that at $x(u, v)$.
step: Hence $\bar K = -1/\cosh^4 u$.
answer: $\bar K = -1/\cosh^4 u$, computed without differentiating the helicoid's normal.

@mistakes
- Assuming equal Gaussian curvature makes a map an isometry: $(x, y) \mapsto (2x, y)$ preserves $K = 0$ but not lengths.
- Assuming isometries preserve $H$ or the principal curvatures: a plane rolled into a cylinder keeps $K = 0$ but gains a nonzero mean curvature.
- Concluding that a surface with $K \equiv 0$ is a plane: cylinders and cones have $K = 0$ and are not planes (they are only locally isometric to one).
- Forgetting the region: the cone is isometric to the plane only after cutting along a generator (and away from the apex), and the unrolled sector has angle $2\pi\sin\alpha$, not $2\pi$.

@practice self
prompt: Prove that no open region of a sphere is isometric to a region of the plane. Be explicit about the charts and the value of $K$ on each side.
hint: Compute $E$, $F$, $G$ for $x(u, v) = (r\sin(u/r)\cos v, r\sin(u/r)\sin v, r\cos(u/r))$, then apply the formula for $K$ when $E = 1$, $F = 0$, and Theorem 1.
solution: For the sphere of radius $r$ use the chart $x(u, v) = (r\sin(u/r)\cos v,\ r\sin(u/r)\sin v,\ r\cos(u/r))$, $0 < u < \pi r$. Then $x_u = (\cos(u/r)\cos v, \cos(u/r)\sin v, -\sin(u/r))$ and $x_v = (-r\sin(u/r)\sin v, r\sin(u/r)\cos v, 0)$, so $E = 1$, $F = 0$ and $G = r^2\sin^2(u/r)$. Since $E = 1$ and $F = 0$, the Theorema Egregium gives $K = -(\sqrt G)_{uu}/\sqrt G$; with $\sqrt G = r\sin(u/r)$ we get $(\sqrt G)_{uu} = -\frac1r\sin(u/r)$ and $K = 1/r^2$. For the plane, the chart $(u, v)\mapsto(u, v, 0)$ has $E = G = 1$, $F = 0$, so $K = 0$. Suppose an isometry $\varphi$ took an open set $V$ of the sphere onto an open piece of the plane. For $p \in V$, Theorem 1 gives $K(p) = \bar K(\varphi(p))$, so $1/r^2 = 0$, which is false. Hence no such isometry exists.
