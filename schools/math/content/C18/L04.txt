@article
Walk once around a pond and count how many full turns your heading makes: one. Walk a figure eight and your heading turns once one way and once the other: zero net. This turning count is an integer, and it is built from the curvature. For plane curves the curvature can carry a sign, which is what makes the count possible.

### Signed curvature and the tangent angle

Let $\beta\colon I \to \mathbb{R}^2$ be a unit-speed plane curve with $T = \beta'$. Let $J$ be rotation by $+90^\circ$, so $J(x, y) = (-y, x)$, and put $N = JT$. Since $T' \perp T$, $T' = \kappa_s N$ for a smooth function $\kappa_s$, the **signed curvature**; $|\kappa_s|$ is the curvature of Week 2, and $\kappa_s > 0$ means the curve turns left. Also $N' = -\kappa_s T$.

**Lemma.** Fix $s_0$ and $\theta_0$ with $T(s_0) = (\cos\theta_0, \sin\theta_0)$ and set $\theta(s) = \theta_0 + \int_{s_0}^s \kappa_s$. Then $T(s) = (\cos\theta(s), \sin\theta(s))$ for all $s \in I$.

*Proof.* Let $w = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$. Then $w' = \theta'(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta) = \kappa_s Jw$, and $T' = \kappa_s JT$. The pair $T, w$ solves the same linear system $u' = \kappa_s Ju$ with the same value at $s_0$, so $T \equiv w$ by uniqueness for linear ordinary differential equations. $\blacksquare$

Consequently a plane curve is determined, up to a rigid motion, by $\kappa_s(s)$: integrate to get $\theta$, then $\beta(s) = \beta(s_0) + \int_{s_0}^s(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\,du$. (Compare the fundamental theorem of Week 3: here no positivity is needed, because $N = JT$ exists even where the curve is straight.)

### The rotation index

A unit-speed curve $\beta\colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^2$ is **closed** with period $L$ if $\beta(s + L) = \beta(s)$ for all $s$ (so all derivatives are periodic too). Then $T(L) = T(0)$, so $\cos\theta(L) = \cos\theta(0)$ and $\sin\theta(L) = \sin\theta(0)$, which forces $\theta(L) - \theta(0) \in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}$. The **rotation index** is
$$\operatorname{ind}(\beta) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^L \kappa_s\,ds = \frac{\theta(L) - \theta(0)}{2\pi} \in \mathbb{Z}.$$
A counterclockwise circle has index $1$, a clockwise one $-1$, a circle traversed twice has index $2$, and the figure eight has index $0$.

**Theorem (Hopf's Umlaufsatz; stated without proof).** A simple closed plane curve (injective on $[0, L)$) has rotation index $+1$ or $-1$. The sign is $+1$ exactly when the curve runs counterclockwise.

### The four-vertex theorem

A **vertex** of a plane curve is a point where $\kappa_s' = 0$. Every closed curve has at least two vertices, at a maximum and a minimum of $\kappa_s$. An ellipse $(a\cos t, b\sin t)$ with $a > b > 0$ has curvature $\kappa = ab/(a^2\sin^2 t + b^2\cos^2 t)^{3/2}$ with the denominator $b^2 + (a^2 - b^2)\sin^2 t$, and $\kappa' = 0$ exactly where $\sin t\cos t = 0$: four vertices, the ends of the axes.

**Theorem.** Let $\beta$ be a smooth unit-speed, simple, closed plane curve bounding a convex region, with signed curvature $\kappa_s > 0$ everywhere (strictly convex, oriented counterclockwise). Then $\kappa_s$ has at least four vertices.

*Proof.* *Step 1: an integral identity.* Since $\kappa_s$ is periodic, $\int_0^L \kappa_s'\,ds = 0$. Also $\int_0^L \kappa_s'\,\beta\,ds = -\int_0^L \kappa_s\,T\,ds = \int_0^L N'\,ds = 0$, by integrating by parts (the boundary terms cancel by periodicity), then using $N' = -\kappa_s T$ and periodicity of $N$. So for every affine function $\lambda(x) = c + p \cdot x$ we have $\int_0^L \kappa_s'(s)\,\lambda(\beta(s))\,ds = 0$.

*Step 2.* If $\kappa_s$ is constant, every point is a vertex. Otherwise let $s_1$ and $s_2$ be a maximum and a minimum point of $\kappa_s$, so $\kappa_s'(s_1) = \kappa_s'(s_2) = 0$, and suppose for contradiction that there are fewer than four vertices. Between consecutive zeros $\kappa_s'$ has constant sign. A sign change of $\kappa_s'$ at a third zero would make that zero a third strict local extremum of $\kappa_s$; but when there are finitely many, the strict local extrema of a periodic function alternate between maxima and minima around the circle, so their number is even, which forces a fourth zero. Hence $\kappa_s'$ has constant sign on each open arc between $s_1$ and $s_2$: $\kappa_s' \le 0$ on one arc and $\kappa_s' \ge 0$ on the other.

*Step 3: the line through two vertices.* Let $\ell$ be the line through $\beta(s_1)$ and $\beta(s_2)$. By strict convexity, $\ell$ meets the curve only at these two points (three collinear boundary points of a convex region would force a segment, where $\kappa_s = 0$), and the two arcs lie in opposite open half-planes (otherwise $\ell$ would support the region and the chord would lie on the boundary). Choose the affine function $\lambda$ vanishing on $\ell$, with sign such that $\lambda \ge 0$ on the arc where $\kappa_s' \ge 0$ and $\lambda \le 0$ on the other. Then $\kappa_s'\lambda \ge 0$ everywhere, yet its integral is $0$ by Step 1, so $\kappa_s'\lambda \equiv 0$. As $\lambda \ne 0$ off two points, $\kappa_s' \equiv 0$, so $\kappa_s$ is constant, contradicting the choice of $s_1, s_2$. $\blacksquare$

The theorem is true for every smooth simple closed curve (a harder result, due to Mukhopadhyaya in 1909 for convex curves and to Kneser in 1912 in general, which we do not prove). Simplicity matters. A limaçon with an inner loop, $r = 1 + 2\cos\varphi$, is closed with rotation index $2$ and crosses itself at the origin. In a numerical check (an observation, not a proof) its curvature, sampled at many values of $\varphi$, had only two critical points, at $\varphi = 0$ and $\varphi = \pi$: two vertices.

@example
title: Vertices of an ellipse
problem: Find the curvature of the ellipse $\alpha(t) = (3\cos t,\ 2\sin t)$ at its four vertices.
step: Differentiate: $\alpha' = (-3\sin t, 2\cos t)$ and $\alpha'' = (-3\cos t, -2\sin t)$.
step: Then $x'y'' - y'x'' = 6\sin^2 t + 6\cos^2 t = 6$, and $(x'^2 + y'^2)^{3/2} = (9\sin^2 t + 4\cos^2 t)^{3/2}$, so $\kappa(t) = 6/(4 + 5\sin^2 t)^{3/2}$.
step: The denominator is smallest at $t = 0, \pi$ (where $\sin t = 0$), giving $\kappa = 6/4^{3/2} = 3/4$, the maximum.
step: It is largest at $t = \pi/2, 3\pi/2$ (where $\sin^2 t = 1$), giving $\kappa = 6/9^{3/2} = 2/9$, the minimum.
step: $\kappa' = 0$ only where $\sin t\cos t = 0$, so there are exactly these four vertices.
answer: $\kappa = 3/4$ at $(\pm 3, 0)$ and $\kappa = 2/9$ at $(0, \pm 2)$.

@example
title: Reconstructing a plane curve from its signed curvature
problem: Find the unit-speed plane curve with $\kappa_s(s) = 1/(1 + s^2)$, $\beta(0) = (0, 1)$ and $T(0) = (1, 0)$.
step: The tangent angle is $\theta(s) = \int_0^s \frac{du}{1 + u^2} = \arctan s$, with $\theta(0) = 0$.
step: Hence $T = (\cos\arctan s,\ \sin\arctan s) = \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+s^2}},\ \frac{s}{\sqrt{1+s^2}}\right)$.
step: Integrate the first component: $x(s) = \operatorname{arcsinh} s$, since $\frac{d}{ds}\operatorname{arcsinh} s = (1 + s^2)^{-1/2}$.
step: Integrate the second: $y(s) = \sqrt{1 + s^2} + c$, and $y(0) = 1$ gives $c = 0$.
answer: $\beta(s) = (\operatorname{arcsinh} s,\ \sqrt{1+s^2})$, which is the curve $y = \cosh x$.

@mistakes
- Using the unsigned curvature to get the rotation index: $\int |\kappa_s|\,ds$ counts total absolute turning and is positive for every closed curve, while the index uses the signed curvature and is $0$ for the figure eight.
- Believing $\int\kappa_s\,ds = 2\pi$ for every closed curve: it equals $2\pi\operatorname{ind}$, and the Umlaufsatz gives $\operatorname{ind} = \pm 1$ only for simple curves.
- Applying the four-vertex theorem to a curve with $\kappa_s$ of mixed sign or a non-simple curve and expecting four vertices from the proof above: Step 3 used strict convexity.
- Counting plateaus or inflections of $\kappa_s$ inconsistently: a vertex is any point with $\kappa_s' = 0$.

model:
```python
import math


def rotation_index(points):
    """Total turning of a closed polygon divided by 2*pi."""
    n = len(points)
    total = 0.0
    for i in range(n):
        a, b, c = points[i], points[(i + 1) % n], points[(i + 2) % n]
        e1 = (b[0] - a[0], b[1] - a[1])
        e2 = (c[0] - b[0], c[1] - b[1])
        cross = e1[0] * e2[1] - e1[1] * e2[0]
        dot = e1[0] * e2[0] + e1[1] * e2[1]
        total += math.atan2(cross, dot)
    return round(total / (2 * math.pi))
```
hint: For each vertex $b$ with previous vertex $a$ and next vertex $c$, the turning angle is the angle between $b - a$ and $c - b$.
hint: Use indices modulo $n$ so that the last two edges are included, and do not subtract headings: use the cross and dot products.
solution: At each vertex compute the turning angle between consecutive edges with `atan2(cross, dot)`, which always lies in $(-\pi, \pi]$; the sum of these exterior angles is a multiple of $2\pi$ for a closed polygon, which is the polygonal analogue of the rotation index. Subtracting absolute heading angles instead would break at the branch cut of `atan2`. A polygon is only a sampled stand-in for a smooth curve, so the code illustrates the index and does not prove the Umlaufsatz.
