@article
An ant walks on a surface. It cannot see the space around the surface; all it knows is how long its steps are and the angles between its paths. Everything such an ant can measure is encoded in one quadratic form on each tangent plane, the first fundamental form. This week we write it in coordinates and use it to measure.

### The first fundamental form

Let $x\colon U \to S$ be a regular chart. At $q \in U$ the tangent plane $T_pS$ ($p = x(q)$) has basis $x_u, x_v$. The **first fundamental form** is the restriction of the dot product of $\mathbb{R}^3$ to $T_pS$: $\mathrm{I}_p(w_1, w_2) = \langle w_1, w_2\rangle$ and $\mathrm{I}_p(w) = |w|^2$. Its coefficients in the chart are
$$E = \langle x_u, x_u\rangle,\qquad F = \langle x_u, x_v\rangle,\qquad G = \langle x_v, x_v\rangle,$$
so for $w = a x_u + b x_v$ we have $\mathrm{I}(w) = Ea^2 + 2Fab + Gb^2$.

**Proposition 1.** For a regular chart, $E > 0$ and $EG - F^2 > 0$ at every point; the form is positive definite.

*Proof.* $E = |x_u|^2 > 0$ since $x_u \ne 0$. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, $F^2 = \langle x_u, x_v\rangle^2 \le |x_u|^2|x_v|^2 = EG$, with equality only if $x_u, x_v$ are linearly dependent, which regularity excludes. Hence $EG - F^2 > 0$. $\blacksquare$

### Lengths, angles, areas

Let $\alpha(t) = x(u(t), v(t))$ be a curve in the chart. By the chain rule $\alpha' = u'x_u + v'x_v$, so
$$\text{length of }\alpha\text{ on }[t_0, t_1] = \int_{t_0}^{t_1}\sqrt{E u'^2 + 2F u'v' + G v'^2}\;dt,$$
with $E, F, G$ evaluated at $(u(t), v(t))$. If two curves cross at $p$ with coordinate velocities $(a_1, b_1)$ and $(a_2, b_2)$, the angle $\theta$ between them satisfies
$$\cos\theta = \frac{E a_1a_2 + F(a_1b_2 + a_2b_1) + G b_1b_2}{\sqrt{Ea_1^2 + 2Fa_1b_1 + Gb_1^2}\,\sqrt{Ea_2^2 + 2Fa_2b_2 + Gb_2^2}}.$$
For example, the coordinate curves meet at the angle with $\cos\theta = F/\sqrt{EG}$; they are orthogonal exactly when $F = 0$.

**Definition.** The **area** of a region $R = x(Q)$, with $Q \subseteq U$ a bounded region whose closure lies in $U$, is
$$A(R) = \iint_Q |x_u \times x_v|\,du\,dv.$$

**Lemma.** $|x_u \times x_v|^2 = EG - F^2$.

*Proof.* If $\theta$ is the angle between $x_u$ and $x_v$, then $|x_u \times x_v|^2 = |x_u|^2|x_v|^2\sin^2\theta$ and $F^2 = |x_u|^2|x_v|^2\cos^2\theta$. Subtract: $EG - F^2 = |x_u|^2|x_v|^2(1 - \cos^2\theta)$. $\blacksquare$

So $A(R) = \iint_Q\sqrt{EG - F^2}\,du\,dv$ depends only on the first fundamental form. It also does not depend on the chart.

**Proposition 2.** If $\tilde x = x \circ \varphi$, where $\varphi\colon \tilde Q \to Q$ is a diffeomorphism, then $\iint_{\tilde Q}|\tilde x_{\tilde u} \times \tilde x_{\tilde v}| = \iint_Q|x_u \times x_v|$.

*Proof.* Write $\varphi(\tilde u, \tilde v) = (u, v)$. By the chain rule $\tilde x_{\tilde u} = u_{\tilde u}x_u + v_{\tilde u}x_v$ and $\tilde x_{\tilde v} = u_{\tilde v}x_u + v_{\tilde v}x_v$, so their cross product is $(u_{\tilde u}v_{\tilde v} - u_{\tilde v}v_{\tilde u})\,x_u \times x_v = \det(d\varphi)\,x_u \times x_v$. Therefore the integrand is $|x_u \times x_v|\circ\varphi\cdot|\det d\varphi|$, and the change of variables theorem gives the equality. $\blacksquare$

**Example: the sphere.** For radius $r$, $x(\varphi, \theta) = (r\sin\varphi\cos\theta,\ r\sin\varphi\sin\theta,\ r\cos\varphi)$ has $E = r^2$, $F = 0$, $G = r^2\sin^2\varphi$. The area of the part with $0 < \varphi < \pi$, $0 < \theta < 2\pi$ (the sphere minus a half great circle, which has area zero) is $\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\pi r^2\sin\varphi\,d\varphi\,d\theta = 4\pi r^2$.

### Computing the coefficients

```python
import math


def first_form(xu, xv, u, v):
    """Return (E, F, G) at (u, v) from the partial-derivative functions."""
    a, b = xu(u, v), xv(u, v)
    dot = lambda p, q: sum(s * t for s, t in zip(p, q))
    return dot(a, a), dot(a, b), dot(b, b)


r = 2.0
xu = lambda p, t: (r * math.cos(p) * math.cos(t), r * math.cos(p) * math.sin(t), -r * math.sin(p))
xv = lambda p, t: (-r * math.sin(p) * math.sin(t), r * math.sin(p) * math.cos(t), 0.0)
print([round(c, 4) for c in first_form(xu, xv, 1.0, 0.3)])
```

The output is `[4.0, 0.0, 2.8323]`, matching $E = r^2$, $F = 0$, $G = r^2\sin^2 1$. One point is a check of the formulas, not a proof.

@example
title: Length of a curve on a helicoid
problem: On the helicoid $x(u, v) = (u\cos v,\ u\sin v,\ v)$, find the length of the curve $u = t$, $v = t$ for $0 \le t \le 1$.
step: Compute the coefficients: $x_u = (\cos v, \sin v, 0)$, $x_v = (-u\sin v, u\cos v, 1)$, so $E = 1$, $F = 0$ and $G = u^2 + 1$.
step: Along the curve, $u' = v' = 1$ and $u = t$, so $Eu'^2 + 2Fu'v' + Gv'^2 = 1 + (t^2 + 1) = t^2 + 2$.
step: The length is $\int_0^1\sqrt{t^2 + 2}\,dt$.
step: Use $\int\sqrt{t^2 + c}\,dt = \tfrac12\left(t\sqrt{t^2 + c} + c\ln(t + \sqrt{t^2 + c})\right)$ with $c = 2$ and evaluate: $\tfrac12\left(\sqrt3 + 2\ln\frac{1 + \sqrt3}{\sqrt2}\right) \approx 1.5245$.
answer: The length is $\tfrac12\left(\sqrt3 + 2\operatorname{arcsinh}(1/\sqrt2)\right) \approx 1.5245$.

@example
title: Area of a piece of a helicoid
problem: Find the area of $x(u, v) = (u\cos v,\ u\sin v,\ v)$ for $0 \le u \le 1$, $0 \le v \le 2\pi$.
step: From the previous coefficients, $EG - F^2 = u^2 + 1$.
step: The area is $\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^1\sqrt{1 + u^2}\,du\,dv = 2\pi\int_0^1\sqrt{1 + u^2}\,du$.
step: With $\int\sqrt{1 + u^2}\,du = \tfrac12\left(u\sqrt{1 + u^2} + \operatorname{arcsinh} u\right)$ we get $\int_0^1 = \tfrac12(\sqrt2 + \operatorname{arcsinh} 1)$.
step: Multiply: $A = \pi(\sqrt2 + \operatorname{arcsinh} 1) \approx 7.2118$.
answer: $A = \pi(\sqrt2 + \ln(1 + \sqrt2)) \approx 7.2118$.

@mistakes
- Using $\sqrt{EG}$ for the area element: that is correct only when $F = 0$; in general it is $\sqrt{EG - F^2}$.
- Forgetting the cross term: the speed on the surface is $\sqrt{Eu'^2 + 2Fu'v' + Gv'^2}$, not $\sqrt{Eu'^2 + Gv'^2}$.
- Treating $u, v$ as Euclidean coordinates: lengths and angles in the parameter plane differ from those on the surface unless $E = G$ and $F = 0$ (see the helicoid, where $G = 1 + u^2$).
- Believing $E, F, G$ are intrinsic numbers of the surface: they depend on the chart. The form $\mathrm{I}_p$ itself, and the lengths, angles and areas it gives, do not.

model:
```python
import math


def area(xu, xv, u0, u1, v0, v1, n):
    """Midpoint-rule area of a parametrized surface patch."""
    du = (u1 - u0) / n
    dv = (v1 - v0) / n
    total = 0.0
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(n):
            u = u0 + (i + 0.5) * du
            v = v0 + (j + 0.5) * dv
            a, b = xu(u, v), xv(u, v)
            c = (a[1] * b[2] - a[2] * b[1],
                 a[2] * b[0] - a[0] * b[2],
                 a[0] * b[1] - a[1] * b[0])
            total += math.sqrt(c[0] ** 2 + c[1] ** 2 + c[2] ** 2)
    return total * du * dv
```
hint: Loop over cell indices $i, j$ and evaluate at $u_0 + (i + \tfrac12)\Delta u$ and $v_0 + (j + \tfrac12)\Delta v$.
hint: At each midpoint take the length of the cross product of the two partial derivative vectors.
solution: The area is $\iint|x_u \times x_v|$, and the midpoint rule replaces the integrand on each cell by its value at the center. For the sphere with $n = 2$ the two midpoints in $\varphi$ are $\pi/4$ and $3\pi/4$, so the rule gives $\pi^2\sqrt2$, not $4\pi$; refining the grid brings the value to $4\pi$ with error of order $1/n^2$. Using $\sqrt{EG}$ instead of $|x_u \times x_v|$ would be wrong whenever $F \ne 0$, as for the last surface. The numerical value is evidence of the area, while the formula is what gives the exact answer.
