@lesson
title: Regular surfaces and tangent planes
summary: Decide whether a set in space is a regular surface, find its tangent plane and unit normal from a parametrization or from an equation, and explain why singular points such as a cone's vertex are excluded.
objective: State the definition of a regular surface and a regular parametrization.
objective: Prove that graphs are regular surfaces and that regular level sets are regular surfaces with tangent plane perpendicular to the gradient.

@article
A curve is locally a line that has been bent; a surface should be locally a plane that has been bent. The definition below captures this: near each of its points, a surface is the image of an open piece of the plane under a smooth, non-degenerate map. The sphere, the torus and the graph of a function qualify. A cone does not, at its vertex.

### Regular surfaces

**Definition.** A subset $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3$ is a **regular surface** if for every $p \in S$ there are an open set $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$, an open set $V \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3$ containing $p$, and a smooth map $x\colon U \to \mathbb{R}^3$ with $x(U) = V \cap S$ such that

1. $x$ is a homeomorphism from $U$ onto $V \cap S$ (continuous and bijective, with continuous inverse), and
2. for every $q \in U$ the differential $dx_q\colon \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ is injective, that is, $x_u(q) \times x_v(q) \ne 0$.

Such an $x$ is a **local parametrization** (or chart) at $p$; condition 2 says it is **regular**. Here $x_u, x_v$ are the partial derivatives with respect to the coordinates $(u, v)$ of $U$.

**Proposition 1 (graphs).** If $f\colon U \to \mathbb{R}$ is smooth on an open $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$, then $S = \{(u, v, f(u, v))\}$ is a regular surface with the single chart $x(u, v) = (u, v, f(u, v))$.

*Proof.* $x$ is smooth. It is a bijection onto $S$ with continuous inverse, the restriction of the projection $(x, y, z) \mapsto (x, y)$. Finally $x_u = (1, 0, f_u)$ and $x_v = (0, 1, f_v)$ give $x_u \times x_v = (-f_u, -f_v, 1) \ne 0$. $\blacksquare$

**Proposition 2 (level sets).** Let $W \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3$ be open, $f\colon W \to \mathbb{R}$ smooth, and suppose $a$ is a **regular value**: $\nabla f(p) \ne 0$ at every $p$ with $f(p) = a$. Then $S = f^{-1}(a)$ is a regular surface.

*Proof.* Let $p \in S$. Some partial derivative of $f$ at $p$ is nonzero, say $f_z(p) \ne 0$. The implicit function theorem (hypotheses: $f$ smooth near $p$, $f(p) = a$, $f_z(p) \ne 0$) gives a neighborhood $V$ of $p$ in which $S \cap V$ is the graph $z = g(x, y)$ of a smooth function on an open set. By Proposition 1 that graph has a regular chart. The cases $f_x(p) \ne 0$ or $f_y(p) \ne 0$ are the same with the roles of the coordinates exchanged. $\blacksquare$

For example the sphere $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = r^2$, $r > 0$, is the level set $f = r^2$ of $f = |x|^2$, and $\nabla f = 2x \ne 0$ on it.

### Tangent planes

The **tangent plane** $T_pS$ of a regular surface at $p$ is the set of velocity vectors $\alpha'(0)$ of smooth curves $\alpha\colon (-\varepsilon, \varepsilon) \to S$ with $\alpha(0) = p$. If $x$ is a chart with $x(q) = p$, then $T_pS = dx_q(\mathbb{R}^2) = \operatorname{span}\{x_u(q), x_v(q)\}$: every vector $a x_u + b x_v$ is the velocity at $t = 0$ of $t \mapsto x(q + t(a, b))$, and conversely every smooth curve in $S$ can be written $x(u(t), v(t))$ with $u, v$ smooth (a lemma that follows from the inverse function theorem; we state it without proof, and for graphs it is immediate with $u = \alpha_1$, $v = \alpha_2$). A regular parametrization therefore gives a **unit normal**
$$N = \frac{x_u \times x_v}{|x_u \times x_v|}.$$

**Proposition 3.** If $S = f^{-1}(a)$ with $a$ a regular value, then $T_pS = \nabla f(p)^\perp$, and $N = \pm\nabla f/|\nabla f|$.

*Proof.* Let $\alpha$ be a smooth curve in $S$ with $\alpha(0) = p$. Then $f(\alpha(t)) = a$ for all $t$, and the chain rule gives $\nabla f(p)\cdot\alpha'(0) = 0$. Thus $T_pS \subseteq \nabla f(p)^\perp$. Both are $2$-dimensional (the first by the chart, the second because $\nabla f(p) \ne 0$), so they are equal. $\blacksquare$

**The torus.** For $R > r > 0$, $x(u, v) = ((R + r\cos u)\cos v,\ (R + r\cos u)\sin v,\ r\sin u)$ gives
$$x_u \times x_v = -r(R + r\cos u)\,(\cos u\cos v,\ \cos u\sin v,\ \sin u),\qquad |x_u \times x_v| = r(R + r\cos u) > 0,$$
so the parametrization is regular, and $N = -(\cos u\cos v, \cos u\sin v, \sin u)$ points toward the core circle of radius $R$. Restricted to $(0, 2\pi)^2$ (and shifted copies), $x$ is a homeomorphism onto an open piece of the torus, so these maps are charts.

### Failures

A failure of a *parametrization* is not a failure of the *surface*. The map $x(u,v) = (u^3, v, 0)$ has $x_u \times x_v = (0, 0, 3u^2)$, which vanishes at $u = 0$, but its image, the plane $z = 0$, is a regular surface. A genuine failure is the double cone $z^2 = x^2 + y^2$ at the origin $0$. If it were a regular surface, some neighborhood $S \cap V$ of $0$ would be homeomorphic to a disc, so removing $0$ would leave it connected. But in any small ball the cone minus $0$ splits into an upper and a lower nappe, and a connected subset of the cone minus the vertex lies in one nappe, while every neighborhood of $0$ in the cone meets both. Hence the vertex is not a regular point; also, $0$ is a critical point of $x^2 + y^2 - z^2$, so Proposition 2 does not apply there.

@example
title: Tangent plane of an ellipsoid
problem: Find the tangent plane to $x^2 + 2y^2 + 3z^2 = 6$ at $p = (1, 1, 1)$.
step: Check that $p$ lies on the surface: $1 + 2 + 3 = 6$.
step: The surface is $f^{-1}(6)$ for $f = x^2 + 2y^2 + 3z^2$, and $\nabla f = (2x, 4y, 6z)$ vanishes only at the origin, which is not on the surface, so $6$ is a regular value.
step: At $p$, $\nabla f(p) = (2, 4, 6)$, so by Proposition 3 the tangent plane is $\{x : (2,4,6)\cdot(x - p) = 0\}$.
step: Expand: $2(x - 1) + 4(y - 1) + 6(z - 1) = 0$, that is, $x + 2y + 3z = 6$.
answer: The tangent plane is $x + 2y + 3z = 6$, with unit normal $\pm(1, 2, 3)/\sqrt{14}$.

@example
title: A chart that fails at the vertex of a paraboloid
problem: For $x(u, v) = (u\cos v,\ u\sin v,\ u^2)$ with $u \ge 0$, find where $x$ is regular, and decide whether the paraboloid $z = x^2 + y^2$ is regular at the origin.
step: Differentiate: $x_u = (\cos v, \sin v, 2u)$ and $x_v = (-u\sin v, u\cos v, 0)$.
step: Cross product: $x_u \times x_v = (-2u^2\cos v,\ -2u^2\sin v,\ u)$, of length $u\sqrt{1 + 4u^2}$.
step: This is nonzero exactly when $u > 0$, so the polar map is a regular parametrization of the paraboloid minus its vertex, and is not regular at $u = 0$.
step: But the paraboloid is the graph of $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2$, so by Proposition 1 it is a regular surface at every point, including the origin, with chart $(x, y) \mapsto (x, y, x^2 + y^2)$; the tangent plane there is $z = 0$.
answer: The polar map is regular for $u > 0$; the paraboloid is a regular surface at the origin because it is a graph.

@mistakes
- Concluding that the surface is singular at a point where one chosen parametrization has $x_u \times x_v = 0$: $(u^3, v, 0)$ parametrizes a plane. Look for another chart.
- Forgetting condition 1: a map with $x_u \times x_v \ne 0$ can wrap or cross itself, for example a regular map that covers a surface twice, so its image need not be a regular surface.
- Taking $T_pS$ to be a plane through the origin and also an affine plane through $p$ without saying which: the vector space $T_pS$ passes through the origin, while the affine tangent plane is $p + T_pS$.
- Applying Proposition 2 when $\nabla f$ vanishes somewhere on the level set: the cone $x^2 + y^2 = z^2$ is not a regular surface at the origin.

answer: 3
check: (6 * 1 + 3 * 2 + 2 * 3) / 6
hint: $\nabla f = (yz, xz, xy)$; evaluate it at $p$.
hint: The plane is $\nabla f(p) \cdot (x - p) = 0$; set $y = z = 0$.
solution: $\nabla f(p) = (6, 3, 2)$, so the plane is $6x + 3y + 2z = 6 + 6 + 6 = 18$. Putting $y = z = 0$ gives $x = 3$.

@practice expr

answer: 4*x + y - 4
vars: x, y
domain: x: -2..2
check: lambda x, y: 2 + 4 * (x - 1) + 1 * (y - 2)
hint: The tangent plane of a graph is $z = f(p) + f_x(p)(x - 1) + f_y(p)(y - 2)$.

@practice self
prompt: Prove that the helicoid $x(u, v) = (u\cos v,\ u\sin v,\ v)$, $(u, v) \in \mathbb{R}^2$, is a regular surface covered by this single chart.
hint: Compute $x_u \times x_v$, then find a continuous inverse using the third coordinate.
solution: The map is smooth. We have $x_u = (\cos v, \sin v, 0)$ and $x_v = (-u\sin v, u\cos v, 1)$, so $x_u \times x_v = (\sin v,\ -\cos v,\ u)$, which has squared length $1 + u^2 > 0$; condition 2 holds. For condition 1, if $x(u, v) = (a, b, c)$ then $v = c$ and $u = a\cos c + b\sin c$, since $a\cos c + b\sin c = u(\cos^2 v + \sin^2 v)$. So $x$ is injective, and its inverse $(a, b, c) \mapsto (a\cos c + b\sin c,\ c)$ is continuous on the image. Thus $x$ is a homeomorphism onto $S = x(\mathbb{R}^2)$, and $S$ is a regular surface.
rubric: Computes $x_u \times x_v$ correctly and shows it never vanishes.
rubric: Proves injectivity by recovering $v$ from the third coordinate and then $u$.
rubric: Notes that the inverse formula is continuous, so $x$ is a homeomorphism onto its image.

@quiz ms
prompt: Let $f(x, y, z) = x^2 + y^2 - z^2$. For which values $a$ does Proposition 2 (regular value) show that $f^{-1}(a)$ is a regular surface?
choice*: $a = 1$ (a hyperboloid of one sheet)
choice*: $a = -1$ (a hyperboloid of two sheets)
choice: $a = 0$ (a double cone)
choice: Only $a = 1$, because the other level sets are not connected
hint: Find the critical points of $f$ and see which level sets contain them.
solution: $\nabla f = (2x, 2y, -2z)$ vanishes only at the origin, which lies on the level set $a = 0$ and nowhere else. So every $a \ne 0$ is a regular value; the level set $a = -1$ has two components, but a regular surface need not be connected. For $a = 0$ the hypothesis fails at the vertex, and indeed the cone is not regular there.
