@article
At a point of a surface, bend your tangent plane in every direction: the normal curvature changes with the direction, from a largest value to a smallest. Remarkably the extreme directions are perpendicular, and the product and the average of the two extreme values are the two most important curvature numbers of a surface.

### Principal curvatures

At $p \in S$ the operator $dN_p$ on the plane $T_pS$ is self-adjoint (Week 7). By the spectral theorem (for a self-adjoint operator on a finite-dimensional real inner product space) there is an orthonormal basis $e_1, e_2$ of $T_pS$ with $dN_p(e_i) = -\kappa_i e_i$ and $\kappa_1 \ge \kappa_2$. The eigenvalues $\kappa_1, \kappa_2$ of $-dN_p$ are the **principal curvatures** and the eigenvectors are the **principal directions**.

**Theorem (Euler's formula).** For a unit vector $w = \cos\theta\,e_1 + \sin\theta\,e_2$ the normal curvature is $\mathrm{II}_p(w) = \kappa_1\cos^2\theta + \kappa_2\sin^2\theta$. In particular $\kappa_1$ and $\kappa_2$ are the maximum and minimum of $\mathrm{II}_p$ on unit vectors.

*Proof.* $\mathrm{II}_p(w) = -\langle dN_p(w), w\rangle = \langle\kappa_1\cos\theta\,e_1 + \kappa_2\sin\theta\,e_2,\ \cos\theta\,e_1 + \sin\theta\,e_2\rangle = \kappa_1\cos^2\theta + \kappa_2\sin^2\theta$, using orthonormality. Since $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$ this is a weighted average of $\kappa_1 \ge \kappa_2$, lying between them and equal to them at $\theta = 0, \pi/2$. $\blacksquare$

The **Gaussian curvature** is $K = \det dN_p = \kappa_1\kappa_2$ and the **mean curvature** is $H = \tfrac12(\kappa_1 + \kappa_2) = -\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}dN_p$. Replacing $N$ by $-N$ changes the sign of both $\kappa_i$, so $K$ is unchanged but $H$ changes sign. A point is **umbilic** if $\kappa_1 = \kappa_2$. If $H = 0$ then $\kappa_2 = -\kappa_1$ and $K = -\kappa_1^2 \le 0$.

### Formulas in a chart

**Theorem.** In a regular chart,
$$K = \frac{eg - f^2}{EG - F^2},\qquad H = \frac{Eg - 2Ff + Ge}{2(EG - F^2)}.$$

*Proof.* Write $dN(x_j) = \sum_k a_{kj}x_k$ and let $A = (a_{kj})$, $\mathcal{I} = \begin{pmatrix}E & F\\F & G\end{pmatrix}$, $\mathcal{II} = \begin{pmatrix}e & f\\f & g\end{pmatrix}$. Taking the dot product of $dN(x_j)$ with $x_i$ gives $\sum_k a_{kj}\mathcal{I}_{ki} = (\mathcal{I}A)_{ij}$, and also equals $-\mathcal{II}_{ij}$. Hence $A = -\mathcal{I}^{-1}\mathcal{II}$, so $-dN$ has matrix $\mathcal{I}^{-1}\mathcal{II}$ in the basis $x_u, x_v$. With $D = EG - F^2$ we have $\mathcal{I}^{-1} = \frac1D\begin{pmatrix}G & -F\\-F & E\end{pmatrix}$, and
$$\mathcal{I}^{-1}\mathcal{II} = \frac1D\begin{pmatrix}Ge - Ff & Gf - Fg\\-Fe + Ef & -Ff + Eg\end{pmatrix}.$$
The determinant of a product is the product of determinants, so $K = \det(-dN) = \det\mathcal{II}/\det\mathcal{I} = (eg - f^2)/D$. The trace is $(Ge - 2Ff + Eg)/D = \kappa_1 + \kappa_2 = 2H$. $\blacksquare$

### Surfaces of revolution

Let $x(u, v) = (\rho(u)\cos v,\ \rho(u)\sin v,\ z(u))$ with $\rho > 0$ and $(\rho', z') \ne (0, 0)$. Put $S_0 = \rho'^2 + z'^2$. Then $x_u = (\rho'\cos v, \rho'\sin v, z')$, $x_v = (-\rho\sin v, \rho\cos v, 0)$, so $E = S_0$, $F = 0$, $G = \rho^2$. The unit normal is $N = (-z'\cos v, -z'\sin v, \rho')/\sqrt{S_0}$ and the second derivatives give
$$e = \frac{\rho'z'' - \rho''z'}{\sqrt{S_0}},\qquad f = 0,\qquad g = \frac{\rho z'}{\sqrt{S_0}}.$$
Because $F = f = 0$, the matrix $\mathcal{I}^{-1}\mathcal{II}$ is diagonal, the coordinate directions are the principal directions, and
$$\kappa_{\text{meridian}} = \frac{\rho'z'' - \rho''z'}{S_0^{3/2}},\qquad \kappa_{\text{parallel}} = \frac{z'}{\rho\sqrt{S_0}},\qquad K = \frac{z'(\rho'z'' - \rho''z')}{\rho\,S_0^2}.$$
For a unit-speed profile ($S_0 = 1$) differentiating $\rho'^2 + z'^2 = 1$ gives $\rho'\rho'' + z'z'' = 0$, and then $z'(\rho'z'' - \rho''z') = -\rho''$, so $K = -\rho''/\rho$.

For a sphere of radius $r$, take the profile $\rho = r\sin(u/r)$, $z = r\cos(u/r)$, which has unit speed: $K = -\rho''/\rho = 1/r^2$.

@example
title: The catenoid is a minimal surface
problem: For the catenoid $x(u, v) = (\cosh u\cos v,\ \cosh u\sin v,\ u)$, find $\kappa_{\text{meridian}}$, $\kappa_{\text{parallel}}$, $K$ and $H$.
step: Here $\rho = \cosh u$ and $z = u$, so $\rho' = \sinh u$, $\rho'' = \cosh u$, $z' = 1$ and $z'' = 0$.
step: $S_0 = \sinh^2 u + 1 = \cosh^2 u$, so $\sqrt{S_0} = \cosh u$.
step: $\rho'z'' - \rho''z' = 0 - \cosh u = -\cosh u$, so $\kappa_{\text{meridian}} = -\cosh u/\cosh^3 u = -1/\cosh^2 u$.
step: $\kappa_{\text{parallel}} = z'/(\rho\sqrt{S_0}) = 1/(\cosh u\cdot\cosh u) = 1/\cosh^2 u$.
step: Therefore $K = \kappa_{\text{meridian}}\kappa_{\text{parallel}} = -1/\cosh^4 u$ and $H = \tfrac12(\kappa_{\text{meridian}} + \kappa_{\text{parallel}}) = 0$.
answer: $K = -1/\cosh^4 u < 0$ and $H = 0$; the principal curvatures are $\pm 1/\cosh^2 u$.

@example
title: Principal curvatures of a graph at a critical point
problem: For $z = h(u, v) = u^2 + 3v^2$ with upward normal, find the principal curvatures, $K$ and $H$ at the origin.
step: At the origin $h_u = h_v = 0$, so $E = G = 1$, $F = 0$ and $W = 1$.
step: $e = h_{uu} = 2$, $f = h_{uv} = 0$ and $g = h_{vv} = 6$.
step: Then $K = (eg - f^2)/(EG - F^2) = 12$ and $H = (Eg - 2Ff + Ge)/(2(EG - F^2)) = (6 + 2)/2 = 4$.
step: The principal curvatures solve $\kappa^2 - 2H\kappa + K = 0$, that is $\kappa^2 - 8\kappa + 12 = 0$, so $\kappa_1 = 6$ and $\kappa_2 = 2$ (the coordinate directions are principal because $f = 0$ and $E = G$).
answer: $\kappa_1 = 6$, $\kappa_2 = 2$, $K = 12$, $H = 4$.

@mistakes
- Using $eg - f^2$ without dividing by $EG - F^2$: the two agree only where $EG - F^2 = 1$ (or both vanish), as at the origin of a graph where $\mathrm{I}$ is the identity.
- Reading the sign of $H$ as intrinsic: it depends on the choice of $N$, while $K$ and the absolute values $|\kappa_i|$ do not.
- Assuming a minimal surface ($H = 0$) is flat: $H = 0$ gives $K = -\kappa_1^2 \le 0$, with $K = 0$ only where $\kappa_1 = 0$ (the catenoid has $K < 0$ everywhere).
- Using the unit-speed formula $K = -\rho''/\rho$ for a profile that is not parametrized by arc length.

@practice self
prompt: A surface of revolution has the unit-speed profile $(\rho(u), z(u))$, that is $\rho'^2 + z'^2 = 1$, and Gaussian curvature $K = z'(\rho'z'' - \rho''z')/\rho$. Prove that $K = -\rho''/\rho$, and use it to find $K$ for the profile $\rho(u) = e^{-u}$ for $u \ge 0$, $z' = \sqrt{1 - e^{-2u}}$ (the pseudosphere).
hint: Differentiate $\rho'^2 + z'^2 = 1$ to relate $\rho'\rho''$ and $z'z''$, then multiply out $z'(\rho'z'' - \rho''z')$.
solution: Differentiating $\rho'^2 + z'^2 = 1$ gives $\rho'\rho'' + z'z'' = 0$, so $z'z'' = -\rho'\rho''$. Then $z'(\rho'z'' - \rho''z') = \rho'(z'z'') - \rho''z'^2 = -\rho'^2\rho'' - \rho''z'^2 = -\rho''(\rho'^2 + z'^2) = -\rho''$. Therefore $K = -\rho''/\rho$. For $\rho = e^{-u}$, $\rho'' = e^{-u} = \rho$, so $K = -1$ at every point (the profile has unit speed because $\rho'^2 + z'^2 = e^{-2u} + 1 - e^{-2u} = 1$).
rubric: Differentiates the unit-speed condition to get $\rho'\rho'' + z'z'' = 0$.
rubric: Substitutes to obtain $z'(\rho'z'' - \rho''z') = -\rho''$.
rubric: Computes $\rho'' = \rho$ for $e^{-u}$ and concludes $K = -1$.
