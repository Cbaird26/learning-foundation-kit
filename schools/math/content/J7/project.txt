@project
title: Home weather station
overview: You will record temperature, cloud cover and rainfall for two weeks with simple homemade instruments, compare your records with the forecast, and explain the differences using what you learned about air masses, fronts, pressure and climate. A good project is organized, uses units, and draws conclusions that the data support.
goal: Collect accurate daily weather measurements for 14 days.
goal: Compare your measurements with the forecast and calculate how far off the forecast was.
goal: Explain the differences using air masses, fronts, pressure, the water cycle and the difference between weather and climate.
step: Choose a shaded, open spot outdoors for a thermometer, away from walls and direct sun. If you have no thermometer, use the daily observed highs and lows reported by a local weather service instead, and write that you used them.
step: Make a rain gauge from a straight-sided, flat-bottomed clear container. Mark a ruler or tape scale in millimeters on the outside. Put it in an open place, and empty it after each reading.
step: Each day at the same time, record the high and low temperature (°C), cloud cover (estimate in tenths of the sky: 0 clear to 10 overcast), rainfall since the last reading (mm), wind direction and any notes. Also write down the forecast high, forecast cloud cover and whether rain was forecast, from a trusted local forecast, the evening before.
step: At the end of two weeks, make a table and a graph of observed and forecast daily highs. Calculate the mean observed high, the total rainfall, and the mean absolute error (the average of the absolute differences between forecast and observed highs).
step: Pick the two days where the forecast was most wrong and the two where it was best. Use your notes to explain what the weather was doing (for example, a front arriving earlier than forecast, extra cloud keeping nights warm, or a high-pressure area).
step: Compare your two weeks with the typical climate for the same weeks of the year, if you can find it, and say whether your two weeks were typical and why two weeks cannot describe climate.
step: Write a one-page conclusion summarizing what you found, limits of your instruments, and how you would improve the project.
deliverable: A data table with 14 days of temperature, cloud cover, rainfall and forecast values, and a graph of forecast and observed highs.
deliverable: A one-page written analysis that includes the mean high, the total rainfall, the mean absolute error of the forecast and at least two explained forecast differences.
deliverable: A short paragraph on weather versus climate that uses your data.
rubric: Data are complete, with units, and recorded at a consistent time each day.
rubric: Calculations (mean, total, mean absolute error) are correct and shown.
rubric: Explanations of the differences use correct Earth science ideas (air masses, fronts, pressure, condensation).
rubric: The analysis separates weather from climate and does not claim more than the data show.
rubric: The limits of the instruments (placement, rounding, short time span) are discussed.
safety: Do this project with a grown-up's permission. Use only household materials. Do not look at the Sun, do not stand outdoors during lightning or severe weather, and place equipment where it is safe to reach.
tutor: Which weather features in my records would suggest a front passed over my house, and how can I tell what kind of front it was?
tutor: How should I decide whether the forecast errors in my data are big or small, and what would make them larger or smaller?
tutor: Why can't two weeks of data tell me anything about my area's climate?

@review
## Week-by-week review

1. **Earth's layers:** crust, mantle, liquid outer core and solid inner core; lithosphere and asthenosphere; P waves pass through liquids, S waves do not.
2. **Plate tectonics:** divergent, convergent and transform boundaries; evidence from fossils, matching rocks and seafloor spreading; 1 cm per year is 10 km per million years.
3. **Rocks and minerals:** minerals are natural, inorganic, solid and crystalline; Mohs hardness; density is mass divided by volume; igneous, sedimentary and metamorphic rocks.
4. **Weathering, erosion, deposition:** weathering breaks down, erosion moves, deposition drops; smaller pieces have more total surface area; the rock cycle has many paths.
5. **Water cycle and oceans:** evaporation, condensation, precipitation, runoff; 97% of water is salty; seawater is about 35 g of salt per kg; wind drives surface currents and density drives deep currents.
6. **Atmosphere and weather:** troposphere holds weather; 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen; low pressure brings clouds, high pressure clear skies; fronts lift warm air to make clouds and rain.
7. **Climate:** climate is a long-term average; latitude, altitude, water and mountains shape it; greenhouse gases absorb infrared radiation and warm Earth.
8. **Natural resources:** renewable and nonrenewable energy; energy in kWh is power times time divided by 1,000; compare weather data with a climate average.
9. **Natural hazards:** magnitude steps of 10 in amplitude and about 32 in energy; S-P time gives distance; drop, cover and hold on; make a plan.
10. **Earth, Moon, Sun:** tilt causes seasons; phases come from viewing the lit half; solar eclipses at new moon, lunar eclipses at full moon.
11. **Solar system:** terrestrial, gas and ice giants; $T^2 = a^3$; 1 AU takes light 8.3 minutes.
12. **Stars and galaxies:** fusion, life cycles, the H-R diagram, light-years, galaxies.

## Most common errors

- Mixing up depth with radius, or centimeters with kilometers.
- Thinking metamorphic rock must melt, or that weathering and erosion are the same thing.
- Calling clouds water vapor, or forgetting that salt stays behind when seawater evaporates.
- Using one cold day as evidence about climate.
- Blaming distance from the Sun for seasons, or Earth's shadow for moon phases.
- Forgetting to cube the distance before taking the square root in Kepler's law.

@exam num
prompt: Over five days, a town's forecast highs were 22, 25, 19, 24 and 21 °C. The observed highs were 24, 23, 19, 27 and 20 °C. What is the mean absolute error of the forecast, in °C?
answer: 1.6
check: sum(abs(f - o) for f, o in zip([22, 25, 19, 24, 21], [24, 23, 19, 27, 20])) / 5
unit: °C
tol: 0.01
hint: Find the difference between forecast and observed for each day, ignoring the sign.
hint: The five absolute differences are 2, 2, 0, 3 and 1. Take their mean.
solution: Absolute errors: $|22-24| = 2$, $|25-23| = 2$, $|19-19| = 0$, $|24-27| = 3$, $|21-20| = 1$. Total $= 8$, and $\frac{8}{5} = 1.6$ °C.

prompt: A sample of ocean crust taken 1,500 km from a mid-ocean ridge on a plate that moved away from the ridge at a steady 2.5 cm per year. How old is the crust at that spot, in millions of years? (1 km = 100,000 cm.)
answer: 60
check: 1500 * 100000 / 2.5 / 1000000
unit: million years
tol: 1%
hint: Convert the distance to centimeters, then divide by the rate to get years.
hint: 2.5 cm per year is 25 km per million years.
solution: $2.5$ cm/yr $= 25$ km per million years, so the age is $\frac{1500}{25} = 60$ million years.

@exam self
prompt: A city is under a forecast for heavy rain as a cold front arrives after several hot, humid days. Explain (a) how the front causes the rain, (b) what the water cycle steps involved are, and (c) why one hot, humid week and one stormy day do not tell you the city's climate. Name at least two data you would collect to describe its climate.
hint: Answer in three parts, one for each of (a), (b) and (c).
hint: For (c), think about how long a record must be and which averages you would compute.
solution: (a) Dense cold air pushes under the warm, humid air and forces it upward. The rising air cools to its dew point, vapor condenses into cumulonimbus clouds, and the droplets fall as heavy showers along the front. (b) Evaporation from the ocean and land and transpiration from plants supplied the vapor, condensation formed the clouds, and precipitation returns water to the surface, where it runs off or infiltrates the ground. (c) Weather is what the atmosphere does in a short time; climate is the average over about 30 years or more. To describe climate, collect decades of daily temperature and rainfall data and compute monthly mean temperature and mean total rainfall.
rubric: Explains lifting of warm air by the denser cold air, cooling and condensation.
rubric: Names at least three water-cycle processes correctly.
rubric: Distinguishes short-term weather from a long-term average (about 30 years).
rubric: Names at least two kinds of data (for example temperature and rainfall) and says they should be averaged over many years.
