@article
Suppose someone claims there is a largest integer, call it $N$. But $N + 1$ is also an integer and $N + 1 > N$, so the claim collapses. That is the whole idea of **proof by contradiction**: to prove a statement $S$, assume $\neg S$ and show that this leads to something impossible. Correct reasoning never produces an impossibility from true statements, so the assumption $\neg S$ must have been false, and $S$ is true.

### The shape of the proof

**Proof.** Suppose, for contradiction, that $\neg S$. (Reason carefully.) This gives $R$ and also $\neg R$, a contradiction. Therefore $S$ is true. $\blacksquare$

The statement $R$ can be a known fact, the original assumption, or anything at all, as long as you also derive its negation. Always say clearly what contradicts what.

### Conditional statements

The negation of "if $P$, then $Q$" is "$P$ and $\neg Q$": the implication fails exactly when $P$ holds and $Q$ does not. So to prove $P \Rightarrow Q$ by contradiction, **assume both $P$ and $\neg Q$** and look for an impossibility. The contrapositive assumes only $\neg Q$ and aims at $\neg P$. Contradiction is more flexible, since any absurdity will do, but it gives a less focused target, so prefer a direct or contrapositive proof when one is short.

**Proposition.** If $r$ is rational and $x$ is irrational, then $r + x$ is irrational.

**Proof.** Suppose $r$ is rational, $x$ is irrational, and $r + x$ is rational. Write $r = \frac{a}{b}$ and $r + x = \frac{c}{d}$ with integers $a, b, c, d$ and $b, d \neq 0$. Then
$$x = (r + x) - r = \frac{c}{d} - \frac{a}{b} = \frac{bc - ad}{bd},$$
and $bd \neq 0$, so $x$ is a quotient of integers, that is, rational. This contradicts the assumption that $x$ is irrational. Therefore $r + x$ is irrational. $\blacksquare$

### When to reach for contradiction

Contradiction shines when the claim says something **does not exist** or **is not of a certain kind**: "irrational", "no largest", "no integer solution". The negation hands you a concrete object to compute with ("suppose $\sqrt{2} = a/b$").

A real number is **rational** if it equals $\frac{a}{b}$ for integers $a, b$ with $b \neq 0$. Any such fraction can be cancelled to **lowest terms**, meaning $a$ and $b$ have no common factor greater than $1$.

A computer search cannot replace the argument, but it can make a claim plausible:

```python
solutions = [(a, b) for b in range(1, 300) for a in range(1, 500) if a * a == 2 * b * b]
print(solutions)   # []
```

The empty list says only that nothing was found below the bounds. The first example below shows that nothing exists at all.

@example
title: The square root of 2 is irrational
problem: Prove that $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational. You may use the result from the lesson on the contrapositive: if $n^2$ is even, then $n$ is even.
step: Suppose, for contradiction, that $\sqrt{2}$ is rational. Then $\sqrt{2} = \frac{a}{b}$ for integers $a$ and $b \neq 0$, and we cancel common factors so that $a$ and $b$ are in lowest terms.
step: Square both sides: $2 = \frac{a^2}{b^2}$, so $a^2 = 2b^2$. Hence $a^2$ is even.
step: Since $a^2$ is even, $a$ is even by the quoted result. Write $a = 2c$ for an integer $c$.
step: Substitute: $4c^2 = 2b^2$, so $b^2 = 2c^2$. Hence $b^2$ is even, and so $b$ is even.
step: Now $a$ and $b$ are both even, so they share the factor $2$. That contradicts lowest terms. Therefore $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational. $\blacksquare$

@example
title: There are infinitely many primes
problem: Prove that there are infinitely many prime numbers. You may use that every integer greater than $1$ has at least one prime divisor.
step: Suppose, for contradiction, that there are only finitely many primes, and list them all: $p_1, p_2, \dots, p_k$.
step: Form $N = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k + 1$. Since $N > 1$, it has a prime divisor $q$.
step: The list contains every prime, so $q = p_i$ for some $i$. Hence $q$ divides the product $p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k$.
step: Then $q$ divides $N - p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k = 1$, because divisibility passes to differences. But a prime is at least $2$ and cannot divide $1$. This is a contradiction.
step: So the list of primes cannot be finite. $\blacksquare$

@mistakes
- Assuming the wrong negation. The negation of "$\sqrt{2}$ is irrational" is "$\sqrt{2}$ is rational", and the negation of "for all" is "there exists", not "for none".
- Reaching a strange-looking statement and calling it a contradiction without naming the two facts that cannot both hold.
- Forgetting to put the fraction in lowest terms first. "Both $a$ and $b$ are even" contradicts nothing unless lowest terms was assumed.
- Writing a proof by contradiction whose assumption is never used. That is a direct proof in disguise, and it is clearer without the detour.

@practice self
prompt: Prove by contradiction: if $r$ is a nonzero rational number and $x$ is irrational, then $rx$ is irrational.
solution: Suppose, for contradiction, that $r \neq 0$ is rational, $x$ is irrational, and $rx$ is rational. Write $r = \frac{a}{b}$ with integers $a \neq 0$ and $b \neq 0$ (here $a \neq 0$ because $r \neq 0$), and $rx = \frac{c}{d}$ with integers $c$ and $d \neq 0$. Then $x = \frac{rx}{r} = \frac{c/d}{a/b} = \frac{bc}{ad}$, and $ad \neq 0$. So $x$ is a quotient of integers with nonzero denominator, hence rational. This contradicts that $x$ is irrational. Therefore $rx$ is irrational. $\blacksquare$

@practice self
prompt: Prove that there are no integers $a$ and $b$ with $a^2 - 4b = 2$.
solution: Suppose, for contradiction, that integers $a$ and $b$ satisfy $a^2 - 4b = 2$. Then $a^2 = 4b + 2 = 2(2b + 1)$, so $a^2$ is even, and therefore $a$ is even: $a = 2c$ for some integer $c$. Substituting gives $4c^2 - 4b = 2$, so $2c^2 - 2b = 1$, that is, $1 = 2(c^2 - b)$. This says $1$ is even, but $1 = 2 \cdot 0 + 1$ is odd. This is a contradiction, so no such integers exist. $\blacksquare$
rubric: The proof begins by assuming that integers $a$ and $b$ with $a^2 - 4b = 2$ exist.
rubric: It shows $a^2$ is even, deduces that $a$ is even, and substitutes $a = 2c$ correctly.
rubric: It reaches $2(c^2 - b) = 1$ and names the contradiction: $1$ is odd, not even.
hint: Begin by assuming that such integers exist, then isolate $a^2$.
hint: Show $a^2$ is even, deduce something about $a$, and substitute back into the equation.

@quiz self
prompt: Prove by contradiction that $\sqrt{3}$ is irrational. You may use that for an integer $n$, if $3 \mid n^2$ then $3 \mid n$.
solution: Suppose, for contradiction, that $\sqrt{3} = \frac{a}{b}$ with integers $a$ and $b \neq 0$ in lowest terms. Then $a^2 = 3b^2$, so $3 \mid a^2$, and therefore $3 \mid a$. Write $a = 3c$. Then $9c^2 = 3b^2$, so $b^2 = 3c^2$, hence $3 \mid b^2$ and $3 \mid b$. Now $3$ divides both $a$ and $b$, which contradicts lowest terms. Therefore $\sqrt{3}$ is irrational. $\blacksquare$
