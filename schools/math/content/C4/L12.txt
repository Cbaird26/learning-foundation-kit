@article
A proof is a chain of statements, each justified by earlier ones, by definitions, or by known theorems. If one link is broken the chain proves nothing, even when the conclusion happens to be true. This lesson is about reading proofs the way a referee does.

### A method for reading a proof

1. **State the claim.** What is assumed, and what is to be shown?
2. **Read line by line.** For each line ask: which earlier line, definition or theorem justifies this?
3. **Mark the first unjustified step.** Everything after it is unreliable, even if it looks fine.
4. **Give a verdict.** If the step is false, find a counterexample. If it only lacks a reason, supply the reason.

Hunt for the **first** bad step, not the most absurd line. In an argument that ends "so $2 = 1$", the absurd line is the last one, and the culprit is much earlier.

### A field guide to common flaws

| Flaw | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Proof by example | "It works for $n = 1, 2, 3$, so it holds for all $n$." |
| Assuming the conclusion | The argument starts from the statement to be proved, or uses it in disguise. |
| Converse error | From "if $P$ then $Q$" and $Q$, concluding $P$. |
| Division by zero | Cancelling a factor that might be $0$. |
| Quantifier swap | From "for every $x$ there is a $y$" to "there is a $y$ that works for every $x$". |
| False lemma | Using a "fact" that is not a theorem. |
| Induction slip | A missing or wrong base case, or an inductive step that fails for small $k$. |

Each of these is a failure of justification, not of arithmetic. Fixing the step usually means adding a hypothesis, a case or a missing argument.

### Testing a suspicious step by computer

When a step says "this always holds", search for a counterexample before spending time on a proof. Suppose an argument uses "if $a \mid bc$, then $a \mid b$ or $a \mid c$":

```python
bad = [(a, b, c) for a in range(2, 20) for b in range(1, 20) for c in range(1, 20)
       if (b * c) % a == 0 and b % a != 0 and c % a != 0]
print(bad[0])   # (4, 2, 2)
```

The triple $(4, 2, 2)$ is a counterexample: $4 \mid 2 \cdot 2$ but $4 \nmid 2$. One counterexample ends the matter. (The statement is true when $a$ is prime, which is why it is so tempting.)

### Writing a critique

A good critique names the line, says why it does not follow, and then either gives a counterexample showing that the claim itself is false, or states the missing reason and repairs the proof. Be specific: "line 5 divides by $a - b$, which is $0$" is a critique; "this feels wrong" is not.

@example
title: A proof that 2 = 1
problem: Find the first unjustified step. (1) Let $a = b$, both nonzero. (2) Then $a^2 = ab$. (3) Subtract $b^2$: $a^2 - b^2 = ab - b^2$. (4) Factor: $(a + b)(a - b) = b(a - b)$. (5) Divide both sides by $a - b$: $a + b = b$. (6) Since $a = b$, this says $2b = b$. (7) Divide by $b$: $2 = 1$.
step: The conclusion $2 = 1$ is false, so some line must fail. Check the lines in order, not from the end.
step: Lines 2, 3 and 4 are valid. Multiplying $a = b$ by $a$ gives line 2, subtracting $b^2$ from both sides gives line 3, and both sides of line 4 are correct factorisations of the sides of line 3.
step: Line 5 divides by $a - b$. But line 1 says $a = b$, so $a - b = 0$, and division by zero is not allowed. Cancelling a factor is justified only when the factor is known to be nonzero. Line 5 is the first unjustified step.
step: Lines 6 and 7 follow from line 5, and dividing by $b$ in line 7 is legitimate because $b \neq 0$. They are not the error, but they inherit it. The repair is to remember that line 4 says $0 = 0$, which carries no information.

@example
title: All horses have the same colour?
problem: Find the first unjustified step in this "proof" that in any group of $n \ge 1$ horses all have the same colour. Base case: one horse has one colour. Inductive step: assume the claim for every group of $k$ horses and take horses $h_1, \dots, h_{k+1}$. The first $k$ horses share a colour, and the last $k$ horses $h_2, \dots, h_{k+1}$ share a colour. These two groups overlap, so all $k + 1$ horses have the same colour.
step: The claim is false in the real world, so some step fails. The base case is fine: a group with one horse has one colour.
step: An inductive step must work for every $k \ge 1$ from the base case on. Test the smallest value, $k = 1$. The horses are $h_1, h_2$, the "first $k$ horses" are just $\{h_1\}$, and the "last $k$ horses" are just $\{h_2\}$.
step: Do these two groups overlap? No, they share no horse. The sentence "these two groups overlap" is true for $k \ge 2$ (they share $h_2, \dots, h_k$) but false for $k = 1$.
step: So the first unjustified step is the overlap claim, in the case $k = 1$, which is exactly the case needed to get from one horse to two.
step: Moral: check that the inductive step is valid for every $k$ from the base case on, including the smallest. A valid step for $k \ge 2$ cannot be started from a base case at $n = 1$.

@mistakes
- Judging a proof by its conclusion. A true conclusion does not validate the argument, and a suspicious conclusion does not by itself locate the error.
- Pointing at the last absurd line (such as $2 = 1$) instead of the first line that does not follow from earlier ones.
- "Repairing" a flawed proof by quietly changing the claim to an easier one without saying so.
- Calling a step "obvious" when it needs a reason. Every step must follow from earlier lines, definitions or known theorems.

@practice mc
prompt: Claim: if $n^2$ is divisible by $3$, then $n$ is divisible by $3$. Argument by contrapositive: (1) The contrapositive is: if $n$ is divisible by $3$, then $n^2$ is divisible by $3$. (2) Suppose $n = 3k$ for an integer $k$. (3) Then $n^2 = 9k^2 = 3(3k^2)$, which is divisible by $3$. (4) Hence the claim is proved. Which line is the first that is not justified?
choice*: Line 1: that statement is the converse, not the contrapositive.
choice: Line 2: $n = 3k$ does not follow from divisibility by $3$.
choice: Line 3: $9k^2$ does not equal $3(3k^2)$.
choice: Line 4: a contrapositive argument can never prove a claim.
hint: Write the contrapositive of "if $P$ then $Q$" yourself, with $P$ = "$n^2$ is divisible by $3$" and $Q$ = "$n$ is divisible by $3$".
hint: The contrapositive negates both parts and swaps them. Compare with line 1.
solution: The contrapositive of "$P$ implies $Q$" is "not $Q$ implies not $P$": if $n$ is not divisible by $3$ then $n^2$ is not divisible by $3$. Line 1 states $Q \Rightarrow P$ instead, the converse. Lines 2 and 3 are correct algebra, but they prove the converse, which says nothing about the claim.

@practice self
prompt: A student claims that $n! > 2^n$ for every integer $n \ge 1$. Proof attempt by induction. Base case: for $n = 1$, we have $1! = 1$ and $2^1 = 2$, so the inequality holds. Inductive step: assume $k! > 2^k$ for some $k \ge 1$. Then $(k + 1)! = (k + 1) \cdot k! > (k + 1) \cdot 2^k \ge 2 \cdot 2^k = 2^{k+1}$. So by induction the claim holds for all $n \ge 1$. (a) Identify the first unjustified step. (b) State and prove a correct version of the claim.
solution: (a) The base case is wrong: $1! = 1$ and $2^1 = 2$, so $1 > 2$ is false (the claim also fails for $n = 2$, where $2 < 4$, and $n = 3$, where $6 < 8$). The inductive step is a valid implication, but a valid step without a true base case proves nothing. (b) Correct claim: $n! > 2^n$ for all integers $n \ge 4$. Base case: $4! = 24 > 16 = 2^4$. Inductive step: let $k \ge 4$ and assume $k! > 2^k$. Then $(k + 1)! = (k + 1) \cdot k! > (k + 1) \cdot 2^k \ge 2 \cdot 2^k = 2^{k+1}$, using $k + 1 \ge 2$. By induction, $n! > 2^n$ for all $n \ge 4$. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Part (a) identifies the base case as the error and shows that $1 > 2$ is false.
rubric: Part (b) states a corrected claim with a correct starting value (such as $n \ge 4$) and checks the base case honestly.
rubric: Part (b) gives a complete inductive step that uses the hypothesis and ends with a conclusion by induction.
hint: Check the base case arithmetic honestly. Is $1 > 2$ true?
hint: Compute $n!$ and $2^n$ for $n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5$ and find where the inequality starts to hold and stays true.

@practice self
prompt: A student claims: for integers $a$ and $b$, if $a^2 = b^2$ then $a = b$. Argument: (1) Suppose $a^2 = b^2$. (2) Then $a^2 - b^2 = 0$, so $(a - b)(a + b) = 0$. (3) Divide both sides by $a + b$ to get $a - b = 0$, so $a = b$. (a) Identify the first unjustified step. (b) Show the claim is false. (c) State and prove a correct version.
solution: (a) Lines 1 and 2 are valid. Line 3 divides by $a + b$, which might be $0$. That is the first unjustified step. (b) Take $a = 2$ and $b = -2$. Then $a^2 = 4 = b^2$, but $a \neq b$. (c) Correct claim: if $a^2 = b^2$, then $a = b$ or $a = -b$. Proof: from $(a - b)(a + b) = 0$, a product of two integers is zero only if one of the factors is zero, so $a - b = 0$ or $a + b = 0$, that is, $a = b$ or $a = -b$. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Part (a) names line 3 and explains that $a + b$ may be zero.
rubric: Part (b) gives an explicit counterexample and checks both $a^2 = b^2$ and $a \neq b$.
rubric: Part (c) states a correct conclusion ($a = b$ or $a = -b$) and proves it using the zero-product property.
hint: Which factor are we dividing by in line 3, and can we be sure it is nonzero?
hint: Try numbers with equal squares but different values, then see what the factorisation says about them.

@quiz num
prompt: The "lemma" "if $a \mid c$ and $b \mid c$, then $ab \mid c$" is false in general. Take $c = 12$. How many ordered pairs $(a, b)$ of positive divisors of $12$ are counterexamples, meaning $ab \nmid 12$?
answer: 18
check: sum(1 for a in range(1, 13) if 12 % a == 0 for b in range(1, 13) if 12 % b == 0 and 12 % (a * b) != 0)
hint: List the positive divisors of $12$. How many ordered pairs are there?
hint: Count the pairs whose product does divide $12$, and subtract from the total.
solution: The divisors are $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$, giving $36$ ordered pairs. The pairs with $ab \mid 12$ number $6 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 2 + 1 = 18$, for $a = 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$ respectively. So $36 - 18 = 18$ pairs are counterexamples, for example $(4, 4)$ and $(4, 6)$.

@quiz self
prompt: A student claims there is no largest even integer. Proof attempt: (1) Suppose, for contradiction, that there is a largest even integer $N$. (2) Consider $N + 1$. (3) Then $N + 1 > N$. (4) This contradicts that $N$ is the largest even integer. (5) So there is no largest even integer. (a) Identify the first unjustified step and explain why. (b) Repair the proof.
solution: (a) Lines 1 to 3 are fine, but line 4 is not justified. The number $N + 1$ is larger than $N$, but it is odd, so it is not an even integer and does not contradict the assumption that $N$ is the largest even integer. (b) Use $N + 2$ instead. If $N = 2k$, then $N + 2 = 2(k + 1)$ is even and $N + 2 > N$, which contradicts that $N$ is the largest even integer. Hence there is no largest even integer. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Part (a) names line 4 and explains that $N + 1$ is odd, so no contradiction has been reached.
rubric: Part (b) uses a number such as $N + 2$ and proves it is even by writing it as $2$ times an integer.
rubric: Part (b) states the contradiction with the maximality of $N$ and concludes.
hint: What exactly does "$N$ is the largest even integer" forbid? Which integers is $N$ being compared against?
hint: Is the number constructed in line 2 in the set that $N$ is supposed to be the largest member of?
