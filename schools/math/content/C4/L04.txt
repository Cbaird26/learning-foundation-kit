@article
Some statements resist a direct attack. Try to prove "if $n^2$ is even, then $n$ is even" directly: from $n^2 = 2k$ there is no obvious way to produce $n = 2m$. The way out is a different statement that says exactly the same thing.

### The contrapositive

The **contrapositive** of $P \Rightarrow Q$ is $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$: "if not $Q$, then not $P$". The two are **logically equivalent**: in every situation, one is true exactly when the other is. Only four truth assignments exist, so we can check all of them:

```python
from itertools import product

def implies(a, b):
    return (not a) or b

for P, Q in product([True, False], repeat=2):
    print(P, Q, implies(P, Q), implies(not Q, not P), implies(Q, P))
```

The third and fourth columns (original and contrapositive) agree on every row; the fifth (the converse) does not. Because the check is exhaustive, it really is a proof of the equivalence.

### Contrapositive, converse, inverse

| Statement | Form | Always the same truth value as $P \Rightarrow Q$? |
|---|---|---|
| Contrapositive | $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$ | yes |
| Converse | $Q \Rightarrow P$ | no |
| Inverse | $\neg P \Rightarrow \neg Q$ | no |

Take "if $6 \mid n$, then $3 \mid n$", which is true. Its converse "if $3 \mid n$, then $6 \mid n$" fails at $n = 3$, and so does its inverse "if $6 \nmid n$, then $3 \nmid n$". The contrapositive "if $3 \nmid n$, then $6 \nmid n$" is true, as it must be.

### The shape of the proof

**Proof.** We prove the contrapositive. Suppose $\neg Q$. (Unpack and compute.) Therefore $\neg P$. $\blacksquare$

Say "we prove the contrapositive" at the start so the reader knows that assuming $\neg Q$ is the plan and not an error. After that it is simply a direct proof of a new statement.

### Choosing a method

Use the contrapositive when $\neg Q$ gives you a better handle than $P$ does. Typical signs: the hypothesis is a negative ("not divisible", "$\neq$") or an awkward condition such as "$n^2$ is even", while the negated conclusion gives a clean equation ("$n$ is odd" means $n = 2k + 1$).

Negate with care. For integers, "not even" means odd. The statement "$a$ and $b$ are both odd" is negated by "$a$ is even **or** $b$ is even", not by "both are even".

@example
title: A statement about squares
problem: Prove that if $n^2$ is even, then $n$ is even.
step: The hypothesis "$n^2$ is even" is awkward to use, so prove the contrapositive: if $n$ is not even (that is, odd), then $n^2$ is not even (that is, odd).
step: Suppose $n$ is odd, so $n = 2k + 1$ for some integer $k$.
step: Square it: $n^2 = 4k^2 + 4k + 1 = 2(2k^2 + 2k) + 1$.
step: Since $2k^2 + 2k$ is an integer, $n^2$ is odd. The contrapositive is proved, so the original statement holds. $\blacksquare$

@example
title: Negating a conjunction
problem: Prove that if $ab$ is odd, then $a$ and $b$ are both odd.
step: The conclusion "$a$ and $b$ are both odd" is negated by "$a$ is even or $b$ is even". So the contrapositive is: if $a$ is even or $b$ is even, then $ab$ is even.
step: Suppose $a$ is even or $b$ is even. We treat the two cases separately; they may overlap, which is harmless.
step: Case $a$ even. Then $a = 2k$ for some integer $k$, so $ab = 2(kb)$.
step: Case $b$ even. Then $b = 2m$ for some integer $m$, so $ab = 2(am)$.
step: In both cases $ab$ is $2$ times an integer, so $ab$ is even. The contrapositive is proved, hence so is the original statement. $\blacksquare$

@mistakes
- Proving the converse $Q \Rightarrow P$ instead of the contrapositive $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$.
- Negating a conclusion carelessly: "not both odd" means at least one is even, and "not a multiple of 4" does not mean odd.
- Not announcing that you are proving the contrapositive, so the reader sees an assumption of the negated conclusion and thinks the proof is circular.
- Believing the inverse $\neg P \Rightarrow \neg Q$ is equivalent to the original statement.

@practice self
prompt: Let $f:\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$ be the function $f(n) = 3n + 1$. Prove by the contrapositive that if $f(n)$ is odd, then $n$ is even.
solution: We prove the contrapositive: if $n$ is odd, then $f(n)$ is even. Suppose $n = 2k + 1$ for some integer $k$. Then $f(n) = 3(2k + 1) + 1 = 6k + 4 = 2(3k + 2)$. Since $3k + 2$ is an integer, $f(n)$ is even, that is, not odd. The contrapositive is proved, so if $f(n)$ is odd then $n$ is even. $\blacksquare$

rubric: The proof concludes that $f(n)$ is even and links this back to the original statement.
hint: Negate both parts and swap them: what does "not odd" mean for $f(n)$, and "not even" for $n$?

@practice self
prompt: Let $n$ be an integer. Prove by the contrapositive that if $8 \nmid n^2 - 1$, then $n$ is even.
solution: We prove the contrapositive: if $n$ is odd, then $8 \mid n^2 - 1$. Suppose $n = 2k + 1$. Then $n^2 - 1 = 4k^2 + 4k = 4k(k + 1)$. The product $k(k + 1)$ of two consecutive integers is even, so $k(k + 1) = 2j$ for some integer $j$. Hence $n^2 - 1 = 4 \cdot 2j = 8j$, so $8 \mid n^2 - 1$. The contrapositive is proved, so if $8 \nmid n^2 - 1$ then $n$ is even. $\blacksquare$
rubric: The contrapositive is stated correctly: if $n$ is odd then $8 \mid n^2 - 1$.
rubric: With $n = 2k + 1$, the proof factors $n^2 - 1 = 4k(k + 1)$.
rubric: The proof shows or cites that $k(k + 1)$ is even (for example by cases) and obtains $n^2 - 1 = 8j$.
hint: Write the contrapositive, then substitute $n = 2k + 1$ into $n^2 - 1$ and factor.
hint: You should reach $4k(k + 1)$. What do you know about the product of two consecutive integers?

@quiz self
prompt: Let $m$ and $n$ be integers. Prove by the contrapositive that if $mn$ is even, then $m$ is even or $n$ is even.
solution: We prove the contrapositive: if $m$ and $n$ are both odd, then $mn$ is odd. Suppose $m = 2k + 1$ and $n = 2j + 1$ for integers $k$ and $j$. Then $mn = 4kj + 2k + 2j + 1 = 2(2kj + k + j) + 1$. Since $2kj + k + j$ is an integer, $mn$ is odd. The contrapositive is proved, so if $mn$ is even then $m$ is even or $n$ is even. $\blacksquare$
rubric: The negation of the conclusion is handled correctly: "not (m even or n even)" becomes "m and n are both odd".
rubric: The proof uses two different integers for $m$ and $n$ and expands the product correctly.
rubric: The proof ends with $mn = 2 \cdot (\text{integer}) + 1$ and links back to the original statement.
