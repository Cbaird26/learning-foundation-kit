@article
Many clinical questions count successes in a fixed number of independent tries: how many of $20$ patients respond to a drug, how many of $50$ donors are Rh-negative. The **binomial distribution** models these.

### Binomial setting

$X$ is **binomial**$(n, p)$ if the situation satisfies the four conditions (remember BINS):

1. **B**inary: each trial has two outcomes, called success and failure.
2. **I**ndependent: the trials do not influence each other.
3. **N**umber of trials $n$ is fixed in advance.
4. **S**ame probability of success $p$ on every trial.

Sampling without replacement from a population is treated as independent when the sample is less than $10\%$ of the population. The distribution of "number of trials until the first success" is not binomial, since $n$ is not fixed; counts of random arrivals per hour also do not fit.

### The formula

$$P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k}\,p^{k}\,(1 - p)^{n-k}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \dots, n,$$

where $\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}$ counts the ways to arrange $k$ successes among $n$ trials, and $p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$ is the probability of any one such arrangement.

**At most** and **at least** probabilities add the relevant terms. $P(X \le 1) = P(0) + P(1)$. For "at least one", use the complement:
$$P(X \ge 1) = 1 - P(X = 0) = 1 - (1 - p)^n.$$

### Mean and standard deviation

$$\mu = np, \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{np(1 - p)}.$$

If a drug causes nausea in $10\%$ of patients, you expect $np = 10$ cases among $100$ patients, with typical variation of $\sigma = \sqrt{100(0.1)(0.9)} = 3$.

### A clinical scenario

When you meet a binomial scenario, write down four things: what is a trial, what is a success, $n$ and $p$. Then translate the question into $X = k$, $X \le k$ or $X \ge k$ and decide whether to add terms or use a complement. A statement like "the drug is 90% effective" has $p = 0.9$ as long as every patient has the same chance; real patients may differ, which is a limitation of the model.

@example
title: Nausea as a side effect
problem: A drug causes nausea in $10\%$ of patients. Among $8$ patients, find the probability that exactly $2$ have nausea and the probability that at least one does.
step: $X \sim \text{binomial}(n = 8, p = 0.1)$. A trial is a patient, and a success is nausea.
step: $P(X = 2) = \binom{8}{2}(0.1)^2(0.9)^6 = 28(0.01)(0.531441) \approx 0.1488$.
step: $P(X \ge 1) = 1 - P(X = 0) = 1 - 0.9^8 = 1 - 0.4305 = 0.5695$.
step: More than half the time at least one of the $8$ patients experiences nausea, even though each patient has only a $10\%$ chance.

@example
title: Mean and spread of a count
problem: In a clinic, $30\%$ of patients are smokers. Among $60$ randomly chosen patients, what are the mean and standard deviation of the number of smokers?
step: $n = 60$ and $p = 0.3$.
step: $\mu = np = 18$ smokers.
step: $\sigma = \sqrt{60(0.3)(0.7)} = \sqrt{12.6} \approx 3.55$ smokers.

@mistakes
- Using the binomial model when trials are not independent (for example, a contagious disease in a household).
- Forgetting the $\binom{n}{k}$ factor and computing only $p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$.
- Computing "at least $2$" as $P(X = 2)$ instead of $1 - P(0) - P(1)$.
- Reporting $np$ as the exact number of successes rather than the long-run average.

prompt: A fair coin is tossed $5$ times. What is the probability of exactly $3$ heads? Give an exact fraction.
answer: 5/16
check: comb(5, 3) * 0.5**3 * 0.5**2
hint: This is binomial with $n = 5$ and $p = 1/2$.
hint: $\binom{5}{3} = 10$ and each arrangement has probability $(1/2)^5$.
solution: $\binom{5}{3}(1/2)^5 = 10/32 = 5/16$.

@practice num
prompt: Each of $10$ patients independently has probability $0.2$ of a complication. What is the probability that none of the $10$ has a complication? Round to four decimal places.
answer: 0.1074
check: 0.8**10
tol: 0.001
hint: Let a success be "complication" with $p = 0.2$ and find $P(X = 0)$.
hint: $P(X = 0) = \binom{10}{0}(0.2)^0(0.8)^{10}$.
solution: $P(X = 0) = 0.8^{10} \approx 0.1074$.

@practice num
prompt: A vaccine's side effect occurs in $10\%$ of recipients. Among $12$ recipients, what is the probability that at most $1$ has the side effect? Round to four decimal places.
answer: 0.659
check: comb(12, 0) * 0.9**12 + comb(12, 1) * 0.1 * 0.9**11
tol: 0.001
hint: "At most $1$" means $0$ or $1$ side effects: add two terms.
hint: $P(0) = 0.9^{12}$ and $P(1) = 12(0.1)(0.9)^{11}$.
solution: $P(0) = 0.9^{12} \approx 0.2824$ and $P(1) = 12(0.1)(0.9^{11}) \approx 0.3766$. The sum is about $0.659$.

@practice num
prompt: Among $100$ patients, each independently has a $30\%$ chance of a complication. Find the standard deviation of the number of complications, rounded to two decimal places.
answer: 4.58
check: sqrt(100 * 0.3 * 0.7)
tol: 0.01
hint: Use $\sigma = \sqrt{np(1-p)}$.
solution: $\sigma = \sqrt{100(0.3)(0.7)} = \sqrt{21} \approx 4.58$.

@quiz num
prompt: Each patient in a trial independently has probability $0.25$ of an adverse event. Among $6$ patients, what is the probability of at least $2$ adverse events? Round to three decimal places.
answer: 0.466
check: 1 - 0.75**6 - 6 * 0.25 * 0.75**5
tol: 0.002
hint: Use the complement: $1 - P(0) - P(1)$.
solution: $P(0) = 0.75^6 \approx 0.1780$ and $P(1) = 6(0.25)(0.75^5) \approx 0.3560$. So $P(X \ge 2) = 1 - 0.1780 - 0.3560 \approx 0.466$.

@quiz num
prompt: A medication fails to work for $4\%$ of patients. Among $250$ patients, how many failures do you expect on average?
answer: 10
check: 250 * 0.04
hint: The expected value of a binomial variable is $np$.
solution: $\mu = np = 250(0.04) = 10$ failures.
