@article
A **parameter** is a number describing a population (the true mean $\mu$ or proportion $p$); a **statistic** is a number computed from a sample ($\bar{x}$ or $\hat{p}$). Because different samples give different statistics, a statistic is itself a random variable. Its distribution over all possible samples of the same size is its **sampling distribution**. It is the bridge between a sample and a conclusion about the population.

### Bias and variability

A statistic is **unbiased** if the center of its sampling distribution equals the parameter. Both $\bar{x}$ (for $\mu$) and $\hat{p}$ (for $p$) are unbiased when samples are random. **Variability** is the spread of the sampling distribution; a larger sample reduces it. Bias and variability are separate: a bullseye picture shows low bias with high variability (darts centered but scattered), or high bias with low variability (tight cluster off center).

### The sampling distribution of a sample mean

For a random sample of size $n$ from a population with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$:

$$\mu_{\bar{x}} = \mu, \qquad \sigma_{\bar{x}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}.$$

The standard deviation of a statistic's sampling distribution is called its **standard error** (SE). Quadrupling the sample size halves the standard error. If the population is normal, $\bar{x}$ is exactly normal. Otherwise, the **central limit theorem (CLT)** says that for large $n$ (often $n \ge 30$, sooner for symmetric populations, later for strongly skewed ones) the sampling distribution of $\bar{x}$ is approximately normal.

### The sampling distribution of a sample proportion

For a random sample of size $n$ from a population with proportion $p$:

$$\mu_{\hat{p}} = p, \qquad \sigma_{\hat{p}} = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}.$$

The distribution of $\hat{p}$ is approximately normal when $np \ge 10$ and $n(1-p) \ge 10$ (a common rule; some texts use $5$). The standard-deviation formulas assume the sample is less than about $10\%$ of the population (so sampling without replacement behaves like independent draws).

### Using the model

To find a probability about $\bar{x}$ or $\hat{p}$, convert to a $z$-score using the standard error:
$$z = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \qquad\text{or}\qquad z = \frac{\hat{p} - p}{\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}}.$$
Use $\Phi$ from week 3. Note that averages are far less variable than individual values: it is common for a single patient to have a blood pressure above $135$ and extremely rare for the mean of $36$ patients to do so.

@example
title: Probability about a sample mean
problem: Systolic blood pressure in a population has $\mu = 120$ mm Hg and $\sigma = 15$ mm Hg. For a random sample of $n = 36$ adults, find the standard error and $P(\bar{x} > 124)$.
step: $\text{SE} = 15/\sqrt{36} = 15/6 = 2.5$ mm Hg.
step: With $n = 36$ the CLT gives an approximately normal $\bar{x}$. $z = (124 - 120)/2.5 = 1.6$.
step: $P(\bar{x} > 124) = 1 - \Phi(1.6) \approx 1 - 0.9452 = 0.0548$.

@example
title: Probability about a sample proportion
problem: In a large population, $30\%$ have a certain gene variant. In a random sample of $100$ people, find $P(\hat{p} > 0.40)$.
step: Check conditions: $np = 30 \ge 10$ and $n(1-p) = 70 \ge 10$, so the normal model applies.
step: $\text{SE} = \sqrt{0.3(0.7)/100} = \sqrt{0.0021} \approx 0.0458$.
step: $z = (0.40 - 0.30)/0.0458 \approx 2.18$, and $P(\hat{p} > 0.40) = 1 - \Phi(2.18) \approx 0.0146$.

@mistakes
- Using $\sigma$ instead of $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ when asking about a sample mean.
- Believing the CLT makes the *population* or a single sample's histogram normal; it concerns the distribution of the sample mean.
- Believing that the standard error of the mean falls in proportion to $n$ rather than to $\sqrt{n}$.
- Applying the normal model for $\hat{p}$ without checking $np$ and $n(1-p)$.

@practice num
prompt: A population has standard deviation $\sigma = 20$. What is the standard error of the sample mean for $n = 25$?
answer: 4
check: 20 / sqrt(25)
hint: Use $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$.
solution: $20/\sqrt{25} = 20/5 = 4$.

@practice num
prompt: A population proportion is $p = 0.5$. What is the standard error of $\hat{p}$ for a sample of $n = 400$?
answer: 0.025
check: sqrt(0.5 * 0.5 / 400)
tol: 0.0005
hint: Use $\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$.
solution: $\sqrt{0.25/400} = \sqrt{0.000625} = 0.025$.

@practice num
prompt: A lab value has population mean $100$ and standard deviation $12$. For a random sample of $36$ patients, find $P(\bar{x} < 97)$. Round to four decimal places.
answer: 0.0668
check: 0.5 * (1 + erf(((97 - 100) / (12 / sqrt(36))) / sqrt(2)))
tol: 0.001
hint: Compute the standard error first, $12/\sqrt{36}$.
hint: $z = (97 - 100)/2 = -1.5$.
solution: $\text{SE} = 2$, $z = -1.5$ and $\Phi(-1.5) \approx 0.0668$.

@practice num
prompt: With a sample of $n = 50$ the standard error of a mean is $3$. What sample size would give a standard error of $1.5$?
answer: 200
check: 50 * (3 / 1.5)**2
hint: The standard error is proportional to $1/\sqrt{n}$.
hint: Halving the standard error requires multiplying $n$ by $4$.
solution: $\text{SE} = \sigma/\sqrt{n}$, so cutting it in half requires $\sqrt{n}$ to double and $n$ to quadruple: $4(50) = 200$.

@practice mc
prompt: Which statement about the central limit theorem is correct?
choice*: For a large random sample, the sampling distribution of the sample mean is approximately normal even when the population is not.
choice: For a large sample, the data's own histogram becomes normal.
choice: It says that $\bar{x}$ always equals $\mu$ for large $n$.
choice: It applies only to populations that are already normal.
hint: The theorem is about the distribution of $\bar{x}$ over repeated samples, not about one sample's data.
solution: The CLT describes the shape of the sampling distribution of $\bar{x}$, which becomes approximately normal as $n$ grows, regardless of the population's shape. The sample's own histogram still looks like the population, and $\bar{x}$ varies from sample to sample.

@quiz num
prompt: In a large population, $20\%$ of patients carry a certain marker. For a random sample of $100$ patients, find $P(\hat{p} > 0.25)$ using the normal model. Round to four decimal places.
answer: 0.1056
check: 1 - 0.5 * (1 + erf(((0.25 - 0.20) / sqrt(0.2 * 0.8 / 100)) / sqrt(2)))
tol: 0.001
hint: The standard error is $\sqrt{0.2(0.8)/100} = 0.04$.
solution: $z = (0.25 - 0.20)/0.04 = 1.25$ and $1 - \Phi(1.25) \approx 1 - 0.8944 = 0.1056$.

@quiz mc
prompt: Which pair describes a sampling distribution of a statistic with low bias but high variability?
choice*: Its center is at the true parameter, but its values are widely scattered from sample to sample.
choice: Its values are tightly clustered, but around a number different from the parameter.
choice: Its values are tightly clustered around the true parameter.
choice: Its center is far from the parameter and the values are widely scattered.
hint: Bias concerns the center; variability concerns the spread.
solution: Low bias means the center of the sampling distribution equals the parameter, and high variability means the values are spread widely. Tightly clustered values around the wrong number are biased but have low variability.
