@review
**Weeks 1 and 2: rings and ideals.** A ring has addition (abelian group), associative multiplication with $1$, and distributivity. Units, zero divisors, integral domains, subrings (contain $1$). Ideals absorb multiplication; $R/I$ is a ring; the kernel of a homomorphism is an ideal; the first isomorphism theorem $R/\ker\varphi \cong \varphi(R)$. Ideals of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ correspond to divisors of $n$.

**Weeks 3 and 4: prime and maximal ideals, fractions, characteristic.** $R/P$ is a domain if and only if $P$ is prime; $R/M$ is a field if and only if $M$ is maximal (commutative $R$). The field of fractions of a domain; the characteristic of a domain is $0$ or prime; the Frobenius map $a \mapsto a^p$ is a ring homomorphism in characteristic $p$.

**Weeks 5 and 6: polynomials.** Division algorithm when the divisor's leading coefficient is a unit; factor theorem; at most $n$ roots over a domain. Irreducibility: degree $2$ or $3$ means no root; rational root theorem; Gauss's lemma; reduction modulo a prime for monic polynomials; Eisenstein (possibly after $x \mapsto x + 1$); $\Phi_p$ is irreducible.

**Weeks 7 and 8: factorization and extensions.** Euclidean $\Rightarrow$ PID $\Rightarrow$ UFD; counterexamples $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ and $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$. Minimal polynomial, $F(\alpha) \cong F[x]/(m)$, the tower law $[E:F] = [E:K][K:F]$.

**Weeks 9 and 10: finite fields and constructions.** Splitting fields exist and are unique. $|\mathbb{F}| = p^n$; one field of each prime-power order; $\mathbb{F}_{p^m} \subseteq \mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ if and only if $m \mid n$; $\mathbb{F}_q^\times$ is cyclic. Constructible numbers form a field with degree a power of $2$; consequences: no cube doubling, no trisection of $60^\circ$, no regular $7$-gon.

**Weeks 11 and 12: Galois theory.** $|\operatorname{Gal}(E/F)| \le [E:F]$, Artin's inequality, the fundamental theorem (subgroups $\leftrightarrow$ intermediate fields, $[E^H:F] = [G:H]$, normal $\leftrightarrow$ Galois). Solvable groups; Galois's criterion; $A_5$ is simple; $x^5 - 6x + 3$ has group $S_5$.

**Key formulas.** $[E^H:F] = |G|/|H|$; cubic discriminant $-4p^3 - 27q^2$ for $x^3 + px + q$; $(a + b)^p = a^p + b^p$ in characteristic $p$.

**Self-check.** Can you (1) find all ideals of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ and their quotients, (2) prove a quotient is a field exactly when the ideal is maximal, (3) decide irreducibility over $\mathbb{Q}$ with a proof, (4) compute the degree of $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha, \beta)$, (5) construct $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ and find a primitive element, (6) list the subgroup lattice and the fixed fields of a Galois group, (7) state and sketch the proof of Galois's criterion?

**Common errors.** Treating "no roots" as irreducible for degree $\ge 4$; using the root bound over rings with zero divisors; assuming the degree is multiplicative without a tower; calling every extension Galois; reversing the inclusions in the Galois correspondence; confusing a normal subgroup with a Galois subextension; reading a computed example as a proof.

@exam self
prompt: Let $E = \mathbb{Q}(i, \sqrt{3})$. Prove that $[E:\mathbb{Q}] = 4$, determine $\operatorname{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q})$, and list every field $K$ with $\mathbb{Q} \subseteq K \subseteq E$ together with the subgroup of the Galois group that fixes it.
hint: Show $\sqrt{3} \notin \mathbb{Q}(i)$ because $\mathbb{Q}(i)$ is not real, then use the tower law. $E$ is the splitting field of $(x^2 + 1)(x^2 - 3)$.
hint: An automorphism is determined by $i \mapsto \pm i$ and $\sqrt{3} \mapsto \pm\sqrt{3}$. Consider the element $i\sqrt{3}$.
solution: The polynomial $x^2 + 1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$, so $[\mathbb{Q}(i):\mathbb{Q}] = 2$. The element $\sqrt{3}$ is real, but every element of $\mathbb{Q}(i) \setminus \mathbb{Q}$ is non-real, and $\sqrt{3} \notin \mathbb{Q}$, so $\sqrt{3} \notin \mathbb{Q}(i)$. Hence $x^2 - 3$ has no root in $\mathbb{Q}(i)$ and is irreducible there, giving $[E:\mathbb{Q}(i)] = 2$ and $[E:\mathbb{Q}] = 4$ with basis $1, i, \sqrt{3}, i\sqrt{3}$. The field $E$ is the splitting field of the separable polynomial $(x^2 + 1)(x^2 - 3)$, so it is Galois with $|G| = 4$. Define $\sigma$ by $i \mapsto -i$, $\sqrt{3} \mapsto \sqrt{3}$ and $\tau$ by $i \mapsto i$, $\sqrt{3} \mapsto -\sqrt{3}$; these exist since $|G| = 4$ and an automorphism is determined by the images of $i$ and $\sqrt{3}$, which have four possibilities. Then $\sigma^2 = \tau^2 = 1$ and $\sigma\tau = \tau\sigma$, so $G = \{1, \sigma, \tau, \sigma\tau\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, with five subgroups. Fixed fields: the trivial subgroup fixes $E$; $\langle\sigma\rangle$ fixes $\sqrt{3}$, so its fixed field is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3})$; $\langle\tau\rangle$ fixes $i$, giving $\mathbb{Q}(i)$; $\langle\sigma\tau\rangle$ sends $i \mapsto -i$ and $\sqrt{3} \mapsto -\sqrt{3}$, so it fixes $i\sqrt{3} = \sqrt{-3}$ and its fixed field is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})$; $G$ fixes $\mathbb{Q}$. Each fixed field has degree $2 = [G:H]$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ for $|H| = 2$, as the fundamental theorem requires, and there are no other intermediate fields.

@exam num
prompt: How many monic irreducible polynomials of degree $2$ are there over $\mathbb{F}_7 = \mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z}$?
answer: 21
check: sum(1 for b in range(7) for c in range(7) if all((x * x + b * x + c) % 7 for x in range(7)))
hint: A monic quadratic over a field is irreducible if and only if it has no root. Count the reducible ones $(x - r)(x - s)$.
solution: There are $7^2 = 49$ monic quadratics $x^2 + bx + c$. A reducible one is $(x - r)(x - s)$ with $r, s \in \mathbb{F}_7$: $7$ with $r = s$ and $\binom{7}{2} = 21$ with $r \ne s$, so $28$ reducible ones. That leaves $49 - 28 = 21$ irreducible monic quadratics, each giving the field $\mathbb{F}_{49}$ as $\mathbb{F}_7[x]/(m)$.

choice*: $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$
choice: $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$
choice: the dihedral group of order $8$
choice: $S_4$
hint: The roots are the primitive 8th roots of unity, so the splitting field is $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_8)$ and its group is $(\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z})^\times$.
solution: The polynomial $x^4 + 1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ (Eisenstein at $2$ after $x \mapsto x + 1$) and its roots are $\zeta^1, \zeta^3, \zeta^5, \zeta^7$ for $\zeta = e^{2\pi i/8}$. The splitting field is $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)$ of degree $4$, so the group has order $4$, and it is isomorphic to $(\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z})^\times = \{1, 3, 5, 7\}$, in which every element squares to $1$. So the group is $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, which is also why $x^4 + 1$ is reducible modulo every prime.
