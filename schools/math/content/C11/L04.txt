@article
You built $\mathbb{Q}$ from $\mathbb{Z}$ by writing $a/b$ and declaring $a/b = c/d$ when $ad = bc$. Nothing in that recipe used the particular integers except two facts: you can multiply, and you can cancel a nonzero factor. So the same recipe should build a field out of *any* integral domain. This lesson carries it out, and then asks the opposite question: how does a ring remember how many times you must add $1$ to reach $0$?

### The field of fractions

Let $D$ be an integral domain. On the set of pairs $(a, b)$ with $a, b \in D$ and $b \ne 0$ define $(a, b) \sim (c, d)$ if $ad = bc$.

**Lemma.** $\sim$ is an equivalence relation.

*Proof.* Reflexivity and symmetry are clear. For transitivity suppose $ad = bc$ and $cf = de$, with $b, d, f \ne 0$. Then $adf = bcf = bde$, so $d(af - be) = 0$; since $d \ne 0$ and $D$ has no zero divisors, $af = be$. This is exactly where "domain" is used. $\blacksquare$

Write $a/b$ for the class of $(a, b)$ and let $\operatorname{Frac}(D)$ be the set of classes, with
$$\frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d} = \frac{ad + bc}{bd}, \qquad \frac{a}{b} \cdot \frac{c}{d} = \frac{ac}{bd}.$$
The denominators $bd$ are nonzero because $D$ is a domain. One checks that these operations do not depend on the chosen representatives and that the ring axioms hold. Then $\operatorname{Frac}(D)$ is a field: $0/1$ is zero, $1/1$ is one, and if $a/b \ne 0$ then $a \ne 0$ and $b/a$ is its inverse. The map $\iota\colon D \to \operatorname{Frac}(D)$, $\iota(a) = a/1$, is an injective ring homomorphism, since $a/1 = 0/1$ means $a \cdot 1 = 0$.

**Theorem (universal property).** If $\varphi\colon D \to K$ is an injective ring homomorphism into a field $K$, there is a unique ring homomorphism $\Phi\colon \operatorname{Frac}(D) \to K$ with $\Phi(a/1) = \varphi(a)$, namely $\Phi(a/b) = \varphi(a)\varphi(b)^{-1}$.

*Proof.* If $b \ne 0$ then $\varphi(b) \ne 0$ by injectivity, so the formula makes sense. It is well defined: $ad = bc$ gives $\varphi(a)\varphi(d) = \varphi(b)\varphi(c)$, so $\varphi(a)\varphi(b)^{-1} = \varphi(c)\varphi(d)^{-1}$. It preserves sums and products by the computation rules in $K$. Uniqueness: any such $\Phi$ satisfies $\Phi(a/b)\Phi(b/1) = \Phi(a/1)$, forcing $\Phi(a/b) = \varphi(a)\varphi(b)^{-1}$. $\blacksquare$

So $\operatorname{Frac}(\mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Q}$; $\operatorname{Frac}(\mathbb{Z}[i]) = \mathbb{Q}(i) = \{a + bi : a, b \in \mathbb{Q}\}$, where division uses $\frac{1}{c + di} = \frac{c - di}{c^2 + d^2}$; and $\operatorname{Frac}(F[x]) = F(x)$, the field of rational functions. The construction needs a domain: in $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ we get $(0, 1) \sim (0, 2)$ (since $0 \cdot 2 = 1 \cdot 0$) and $(0, 2) \sim (3, 1)$ (since $0 \cdot 1 = 2 \cdot 3 = 6 = 0$), but $(0, 1) \not\sim (3, 1)$ because $0 \cdot 1 \ne 1 \cdot 3$. So $\sim$ is not transitive.

### The characteristic

The **characteristic** $\operatorname{char} R$ of a ring is the least integer $n \ge 1$ with $n \cdot 1 = 1 + \dots + 1 = 0$, or $0$ if there is none. So $\operatorname{char}\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} = n$ and $\operatorname{char}\mathbb{Q} = 0$. The characteristic of $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ is $6$: $n \cdot (1, 1) = (n \bmod 2,\ n \bmod 3)$ vanishes exactly when $6 \mid n$.

**Theorem.** If $D$ is an integral domain, then $\operatorname{char} D$ is $0$ or a prime number.

*Proof.* Suppose $\operatorname{char} D = n = ab$ with $1 < a, b < n$. Then $0 = n \cdot 1 = (a \cdot 1)(b \cdot 1)$, so $a \cdot 1 = 0$ or $b \cdot 1 = 0$, contradicting minimality of $n$. Also $n \ne 1$ because $1 \ne 0$ in $D$. $\blacksquare$

In particular a field has characteristic $0$ (and contains a copy of $\mathbb{Q}$) or a prime $p$ (and contains a copy of $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$, its **prime subfield**). A finite field has prime characteristic, because the multiples $n \cdot 1$ cannot all be distinct.

### The Frobenius map

**Theorem.** In a commutative ring $R$ of prime characteristic $p$, $(a + b)^p = a^p + b^p$ for all $a, b \in R$. Hence $\operatorname{Frob}(a) = a^p$ is a ring homomorphism $R \to R$.

*Proof.* By the binomial theorem $(a + b)^p = \sum_{k=0}^{p} \binom{p}{k} a^k b^{p-k}$. For $0 < k < p$ the integer $\binom{p}{k} = \frac{p!}{k!(p-k)!}$ is divisible by $p$, since $p$ divides the numerator and does not divide $k!(p-k)!$ (all of its factors are less than $p$). So the middle terms are multiples of $p \cdot 1 = 0$. Multiplicativity and $1^p = 1$ are clear. $\blacksquare$

Primality of $p$ is essential. In $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$, $(1 + 1)^2 = 0$ but $1^2 + 1^2 = 2$. On a field of characteristic $p$, $\operatorname{Frob}$ is injective (a field homomorphism), and it is bijective when the field is finite.

@example
title: Division in the field of fractions of the Gaussian integers
problem: Write $\dfrac{1 + 2i}{3 - i} \in \operatorname{Frac}(\mathbb{Z}[i]) = \mathbb{Q}(i)$ in the form $a + bi$ with $a, b \in \mathbb{Q}$.
step: In a field of fractions, $\frac{1}{c + di} = \frac{c - di}{c^2 + d^2}$, because $(c + di)(c - di) = c^2 + d^2$ is a nonzero integer.
step: Multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate $3 + i$ of the denominator: $\dfrac{(1 + 2i)(3 + i)}{(3 - i)(3 + i)}$.
step: Expand: $(1 + 2i)(3 + i) = 3 + i + 6i + 2i^2 = 1 + 7i$ and $(3 - i)(3 + i) = 9 + 1 = 10$.
step: Divide: $\dfrac{1 + 7i}{10} = \dfrac{1}{10} + \dfrac{7}{10}i$.
answer: $\dfrac{1}{10} + \dfrac{7}{10}i$.

@example
title: Frobenius in characteristic 3
problem: Expand $(x + 2)^9$ in the polynomial ring $(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z})[x]$.
step: The ring $(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z})[x]$ is commutative of characteristic $3$, so $(a + b)^3 = a^3 + b^3$ for all $a, b$ in it.
step: Apply it twice: $(x + 2)^9 = \bigl((x + 2)^3\bigr)^3 = (x^3 + 2^3)^3 = (x^3 + 2)^3 = x^9 + 2^3$, using $2^3 = 8 = 2$ in $\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$.
step: Evaluate the constant: $2^3 \equiv 2$, so $(x + 2)^9 = x^9 + 2$. A direct binomial expansion has ten terms; the middle eight vanish modulo $3$.
answer: $x^9 + 2$.

@mistakes
- Forming fractions over a ring with zero divisors: $\operatorname{Frac}(\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z})$ does not exist, since $\sim$ is not an equivalence relation there. The domain hypothesis is needed.
- Canceling common factors incorrectly: $a/b = (ac)/(bc)$ needs $c \ne 0$, and $\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}$ means $ad = bc$, not $a = c$ and $b = d$.
- Using $(a + b)^n = a^n + b^n$ for $n$ that is not a power of the prime characteristic: in characteristic $2$, $(a + b)^3 = a^3 + a^2 b + a b^2 + b^3$, which is not $a^3 + b^3$ in general.
- Believing the characteristic of a product $R \times S$ is the product or the sum of the characteristics. It is the least common multiple when both are positive: $\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}) = 12$.

@practice num
prompt: What is the characteristic of the ring $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/10\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/15\mathbb{Z}$?
answer: 30
check: math.lcm(6, 10, 15)
hint: $n \cdot (1, 1, 1) = 0$ exactly when $n$ is a multiple of each modulus.
solution: The multiple $n \cdot (1, 1, 1) = (n \bmod 6,\ n \bmod 10,\ n \bmod 15)$ is zero exactly when $6$, $10$ and $15$ all divide $n$, that is when $\operatorname{lcm}(6, 10, 15) = 30$ divides $n$. So the characteristic is $30$.

@practice tuple
prompt: In the field $\mathbb{Q}(i) = \operatorname{Frac}(\mathbb{Z}[i])$, write $\dfrac{5 - i}{2 + 3i}$ as $a + bi$ with $a, b \in \mathbb{Q}$. Give the pair $(a, b)$ as exact fractions.
answer: (7/13, -17/13)

@practice mc
prompt: Which of these numbers can **not** be the characteristic of an integral domain?
choice*: $91 = 7 \cdot 13$
choice: $0$
choice: $2$
choice: $101$
hint: The characteristic of an integral domain is $0$ or a prime. Check which choice is neither.
solution: If $\operatorname{char} D = ab$ with $1 < a, b < ab$, then $(a \cdot 1)(b \cdot 1) = 0$ with neither factor zero, contradicting that $D$ is a domain. So composite numbers such as $91$ are excluded, while $0$ ($\mathbb{Z}$), $2$ ($\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$) and $101$ ($\mathbb{Z}/101\mathbb{Z}$) occur.

@practice ms
prompt: Which statements hold in every commutative ring $R$ of characteristic $3$?
choice*: $(a + b)^3 = a^3 + b^3$ for all $a, b \in R$.
choice*: $3a = 0$ for all $a \in R$.
choice: $a^3 = a$ for all $a \in R$.
choice: $(a + b)^2 = a^2 + b^2$ for all $a, b \in R$.
choice: $R$ is a field.
hint: Use $3 \cdot 1 = 0$ and the Frobenius map. Test the false-looking statements in $(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z})[x]$.
solution: Since $3 \cdot 1 = 0$, $3a = (3 \cdot 1)a = 0$. The Frobenius identity holds as $3$ is prime. In $(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z})[x]$ the element $x$ has $x^3 \ne x$, and $(1 + 1)^2 = 4 = 1 \ne 2 = 1^2 + 1^2$ modulo $3$, so the third and fourth fail. A ring of characteristic $3$ need not be a field, for example $\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$.

@practice self
prompt: Let $R$ be a commutative ring with $1$ and $I$ a proper ideal. Prove that $R/I$ is a field if and only if $I$ is maximal, by the following route. First show that a commutative ring $S$ with $1 \ne 0$ is a field if and only if its only ideals are $\{0\}$ and $S$. Then use the correspondence between ideals of $R/I$ and ideals of $R$ that contain $I$.
hint: For a field, a nonzero ideal contains a unit. For the converse, if $a \ne 0$ then $(a)$ is a nonzero ideal.
hint: If $\pi\colon R \to R/I$ is the quotient map and $\bar J$ is an ideal of $R/I$, then $\pi^{-1}(\bar J)$ is an ideal of $R$ containing $I$, and $\pi(\pi^{-1}(\bar J)) = \bar J$.
solution: Lemma. Let $S$ be commutative with $1 \ne 0$. If $S$ is a field and $J \ne 0$ is an ideal, pick $a \in J$ with $a \ne 0$; then $1 = a^{-1}a \in J$, so $J = S$. Conversely suppose the only ideals are $0$ and $S$, and let $a \ne 0$. The ideal $(a)$ is nonzero, hence equals $S$, so $1 = ra$ for some $r$, and $a$ is a unit; thus $S$ is a field. Correspondence. Let $\pi\colon R \to R/I$. If $J$ is an ideal of $R$ with $I \subseteq J$, then $\pi(J)$ is an ideal of $R/I$ (absorption passes through the surjection $\pi$), and if $\bar J$ is an ideal of $R/I$ then $\pi^{-1}(\bar J)$ is an ideal of $R$ containing $I$, with $\pi(\pi^{-1}(\bar J)) = \bar J$ and $\pi^{-1}(\pi(J)) = J + I = J$. These maps preserve inclusion. Hence $R/I$ has exactly two ideals, $0$ and itself, if and only if the only ideals of $R$ containing $I$ are $I$ (corresponding to $0$) and $R$ (corresponding to $R/I$), that is, if and only if $I$ is maximal. Since $R/I \ne 0$ because $I$ is proper, the lemma gives: $R/I$ is a field if and only if $I$ is maximal.
rubric: Proves both directions of the lemma, using units for one and the principal ideal $(a)$ for the other.
rubric: Describes the maps $J \mapsto \pi(J)$ and $\bar J \mapsto \pi^{-1}(\bar J)$ between ideals and checks they are mutually inverse on ideals containing $I$.
rubric: Combines the lemma and the correspondence to conclude, and notes that $R/I \ne 0$ because $I$ is proper.

solution: In $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ and in $(\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z})[x]$ the identity $1$ has additive order $6$. In $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ it has order $\operatorname{lcm}(2, 3) = 6$. The other two have characteristic $12$ and $2$.

@quiz num
prompt: In the polynomial ring $(\mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z})[x]$, the identity $(x + 3)^{49} = x^{49} + c$ holds for some constant $c \in \{0, 1, \dots, 6\}$. Find $c$.
answer: 3
check: pow(3, 49, 7)
hint: Apply the Frobenius identity twice: $49 = 7 \cdot 7$.
solution: $(x + 3)^{49} = \bigl((x + 3)^7\bigr)^7 = (x^7 + 3^7)^7 = x^{49} + 3^{49}$. Fermat's little theorem gives $3^7 \equiv 3$ modulo $7$, so $3^{49} = (3^7)^7 \equiv 3^7 \equiv 3$. Thus $c = 3$.
