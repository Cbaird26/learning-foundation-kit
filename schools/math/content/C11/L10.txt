@article
Greek geometers asked three questions: can one construct, with an unmarked straightedge and a compass alone, a cube of twice the volume of a given cube, an angle one third of a given angle, a square of the same area as a given circle? For two thousand years nobody succeeded. The answer, given in the nineteenth century, is no, and the proof is algebra: degrees of field extensions.

### Constructible numbers

Start with the points $(0, 0)$ and $(1, 0)$ in the plane. A point is **constructible** if it can be reached by finitely many steps of the following kind, where all points used are already constructible: draw the **line** through two points; draw the **circle** with a constructible center through a constructible point; add the **intersection points** of two distinct such lines or circles. A real number $a$ is **constructible** if $(a, 0)$ is.

**Theorem 1.** The constructible numbers form a subfield of $\mathbb{R}$, and if $a > 0$ is constructible then so is $\sqrt{a}$. (*Outline.*) Sums and differences come from laying off lengths with the compass. Products and quotients come from similar triangles: if segments of length $1$, $a$ and $b$ are marked on two rays from a point, the line through the endpoints of $1$ and $b$, and its parallel through the endpoint of $a$, cut off a segment of length $ab$ (and, with the roles exchanged, $a/b$). For $\sqrt{a}$, mark a diameter of length $1 + a$ and erect the perpendicular at the point dividing it into $1$ and $a$; the perpendicular chord's half-length is $\sqrt{a}$ because the altitude of a right triangle is the geometric mean of the two parts of the hypotenuse. In particular every rational number is constructible.

### The degree condition

**Theorem 2.** If $a$ is a constructible number, then $[\mathbb{Q}(a):\mathbb{Q}]$ is a power of $2$.

*Proof.* We show: if a point is constructible, there is a chain of fields $\mathbb{Q} = F_0 \subseteq F_1 \subseteq \dots \subseteq F_k \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ with $[F_i:F_{i-1}] \in \{1, 2\}$ containing both of its coordinates. Then $a \in F_k$ has $[F_k:\mathbb{Q}]$ a power of $2$, and $[\mathbb{Q}(a):\mathbb{Q}]$ divides it by the tower law. Induct on the number of construction steps. Let $F \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ be a field containing the coordinates of all points constructed so far (initially $F = \mathbb{Q}$).

A line through two points with coordinates in $F$ has an equation $ux + vy + w = 0$ with $u, v, w \in F$. A circle with center $(x_0, y_0)$ through $(x_1, y_1)$, all in $F$, has equation $x^2 + y^2 + dx + ey + f = 0$ with $d, e, f \in F$ (its radius squared is in $F$). New points are intersections:

- *Two lines.* Solving two linear equations with coefficients in $F$ gives coordinates in $F$.
- *A line and a circle.* Solve the line equation for one variable (say $y = -(ux + w)/v$) and substitute: a quadratic $Ax^2 + Bx + C = 0$ with $A, B, C \in F$. Its roots lie in $F$ or in $F(\sqrt{D})$ where $D = B^2 - 4AC \in F$, a field extension of degree $2$; the other coordinate is then a polynomial in $x$ over $F$.
- *Two circles.* Subtracting the two circle equations leaves a line equation with coefficients in $F$ (the radical axis), and the intersection points are those of this line with one circle: the previous case.

In each case both coordinates of the new point lie in $F$ or in a field $F(\sqrt{D})$ with $[F(\sqrt{D}):F] = 2$. $\blacksquare$

The converse is false: a number can have degree $4$ and still not be constructible. Theorem 2 is a test for impossibility only.

### The three classical problems

*Doubling the cube.* This requires the length $\sqrt[3]{2}$, of degree $3$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, not a power of $2$. *Trisecting an angle.* An angle $\theta$ is constructible exactly when $\cos\theta$ is. Since $\cos 3\theta = 4\cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta$, the number $y = 2\cos 20^\circ$ satisfies $y^3 - 3y - 1 = 0$ (put $\theta = 20^\circ$, $\cos 60^\circ = \frac12$). The cubic has no rational root ($f(1) = -3$, $f(-1) = 1$), so it is irreducible, $[\mathbb{Q}(y):\mathbb{Q}] = 3$, and $20^\circ$ is not constructible: the angle $60^\circ$ cannot be trisected, although some angles ($90^\circ$) can. *Squaring the circle.* This requires $\sqrt{\pi}$, and $\pi$ is transcendental (Lindemann, 1882), so $\sqrt{\pi}$ is not algebraic and hence not constructible. (The first two impossibilities were proved by Wantzel in 1837.)

### Regular polygons

The regular $n$-gon is constructible if and only if $n = 2^k p_1 \cdots p_r$ with distinct **Fermat primes** $p_i$ (primes of the form $2^{2^m} + 1$: the known ones are $3, 5, 17, 257, 65537$). This is the Gauss–Wantzel theorem; Gauss constructed the regular $17$-gon in 1796. We prove only a piece: the regular $7$-gon is not constructible. Equivalently, $\varphi(n)$ must be a power of $2$.

@example
title: Doubling the cube is impossible
problem: Show that a cube of volume $2$ cannot be obtained from a cube of volume $1$ by ruler and compass.
step: A cube of volume $1$ has edge $1$. A cube of volume $2$ has edge $\sqrt[3]{2}$, so we must construct the length $a = \sqrt[3]{2}$ from the unit length.
step: The polynomial $x^3 - 2$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ by Eisenstein at $p = 2$. It is the minimal polynomial of $a$, so $[\mathbb{Q}(a):\mathbb{Q}] = 3$.
step: By Theorem 2 a constructible number has degree a power of $2$, and $3$ is not a power of $2$. Hence $a$ is not constructible.
answer: The cube cannot be doubled with ruler and compass.

@example
title: The regular heptagon is not constructible
problem: Show that the regular $7$-gon, inscribed in the unit circle, cannot be constructed.
step: A vertex is $(\cos\frac{2\pi}{7}, \sin\frac{2\pi}{7})$. If it were constructible, so would be its $x$-coordinate and hence $c = 2\cos\frac{2\pi}{7} = \zeta + \zeta^{-1}$, where $\zeta = e^{2\pi i/7}$.
step: From $\zeta^6 + \zeta^5 + \dots + 1 = 0$, divide by $\zeta^3$: $(\zeta^3 + \zeta^{-3}) + (\zeta^2 + \zeta^{-2}) + (\zeta + \zeta^{-1}) + 1 = 0$. With $\zeta^2 + \zeta^{-2} = c^2 - 2$ and $\zeta^3 + \zeta^{-3} = c^3 - 3c$ this reads $c^3 + c^2 - 2c - 1 = 0$.
step: The cubic $x^3 + x^2 - 2x - 1$ has no rational root: the candidates are $\pm 1$, and the values are $-1$ and $1$. A cubic without a rational root is irreducible, so $[\mathbb{Q}(c):\mathbb{Q}] = 3$.
step: The degree $3$ is not a power of $2$, so $c$ is not constructible, and neither is the regular heptagon.
answer: The regular $7$-gon is not constructible; the same argument works for $9$ (using $\cos 40^\circ$ of degree $3$) and fails for $17$ (degree $8$).

@mistakes
- Reading Theorem 2 as an "if and only if". A number whose degree is a power of $2$ need not be constructible; only the contrapositive of Theorem 2 (degree not a power of $2$ implies not constructible) is proved here.
- Saying "angle trisection is impossible". It is impossible for some angles, such as $60^\circ$, and possible for others, such as $90^\circ$ (since $30^\circ$ is constructible).
- Forgetting that $[\mathbb{Q}(a):\mathbb{Q}]$ must be computed with the minimal polynomial. The number $\sqrt[3]{2}$ is a root of $x^6 - 4$, of degree $6$, which is not its minimal polynomial; the degree is $3$.
- Treating "constructible" as a statement about real numbers only: points are constructed from lines and circles; a real number is constructible when it is a coordinate (or length) of a constructible point.

@practice self
prompt: Prove that an angle of $60^\circ$ cannot be trisected with ruler and compass. You may use that a constructible angle $\theta$ has a constructible $\cos\theta$ and the degree condition: a constructible number has degree a power of $2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$.
hint: Trisecting $60^\circ$ means constructing $20^\circ$. Put $\theta = 20^\circ$ in $\cos 3\theta = 4\cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta$.
hint: Let $y = 2\cos 20^\circ$ and find a cubic for $y$; test for rational roots.
solution: Suppose $20^\circ$ were constructible. Then $\cos 20^\circ$ is a constructible number, and so is $y = 2\cos 20^\circ$. From $\cos 3\theta = 4\cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta$ with $\theta = 20^\circ$ we get $\frac12 = 4c^3 - 3c$ for $c = \cos 20^\circ$; multiplying by $2$ gives $1 = 8c^3 - 6c = y^3 - 3y$, so $y^3 - 3y - 1 = 0$. The rational root theorem leaves only $\pm 1$ as candidates, and $1 - 3 - 1 = -3$ and $-1 + 3 - 1 = 1$ are nonzero. A cubic with no rational root is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$, so it is the minimal polynomial of $y$ and $[\mathbb{Q}(y):\mathbb{Q}] = 3$. By the degree condition this must be a power of $2$, which $3$ is not. Contradiction: $20^\circ$ is not constructible, so $60^\circ$ cannot be trisected.
rubric: Derives the cubic $y^3 - 3y - 1 = 0$ from the triple-angle identity with the correct value $\cos 60^\circ = \frac12$.
rubric: Proves irreducibility via the rational root test, since a cubic has a factorization only with a linear factor.
rubric: Applies the degree condition and concludes that $3$ is not a power of $2$.
