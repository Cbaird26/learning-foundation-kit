@article
The polynomial $x^4 + 1$ has no root in $\mathbb{Q}$, and in fact no root in $\mathbb{R}$. Is it irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$? And $x^4 + 4$ has no rational root either, yet $x^4 + 4 = (x^2 + 2x + 2)(x^2 - 2x + 2)$. "No roots" is not the same as "irreducible"; this lesson gives tests that are.

### Irreducible polynomials

Let $F$ be a field. A polynomial $f \in F[x]$ of degree $\ge 1$ is **irreducible over $F$** if $f = gh$ with $g, h \in F[x]$ forces $\deg g = 0$ or $\deg h = 0$. By the factor theorem, a polynomial of degree $2$ or $3$ is irreducible if and only if it has no root in $F$ (a factorization of a cubic or quadratic has a linear factor). For degree $4$ and higher a polynomial can be reducible without a root, as $x^4 + 4$ shows.

**Rational root theorem.** Let $f = a_nx^n + \dots + a_0 \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ with $a_n, a_0 \ne 0$. If $r/s$, in lowest terms, is a root of $f$, then $r \mid a_0$ and $s \mid a_n$.

*Proof.* Multiply $f(r/s) = 0$ by $s^n$: $a_nr^n + a_{n-1}r^{n-1}s + \dots + a_0s^n = 0$. Every term except $a_nr^n$ is divisible by $s$, so $s \mid a_nr^n$; since $\gcd(r, s) = 1$, $s \mid a_n$. Every term except $a_0s^n$ is divisible by $r$, so $r \mid a_0$. $\blacksquare$

### Gauss's lemma

The **content** of $f \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ is the gcd of its coefficients; $f$ is **primitive** if its content is $1$.

**Gauss's lemma.** The product of two primitive polynomials in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is primitive. Consequently, a primitive $f \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ that factors in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ into polynomials of positive degree also factors in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ into polynomials of the same degrees.

*Proof.* If a prime $p$ divided every coefficient of $fg$, then $\bar f \bar g = 0$ in $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})[x]$, an integral domain, so $\bar f = 0$ or $\bar g = 0$, that is, $p$ divides the content of $f$ or of $g$: a contradiction. For the second claim let $f = gh$ in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$. Clear denominators and content: $g = \frac{a}{b}g_0$ and $h = \frac{c}{d}h_0$ with $g_0, h_0 \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ primitive and positive integers $a, b, c, d$ (with $\gcd(a,b) = \gcd(c,d) = 1$). Then $bdf = ac\,g_0h_0$, and $g_0h_0$ is primitive, so comparing contents, $bd = ac$. Hence $f = g_0h_0$ in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$. $\blacksquare$

### Reduction modulo a prime

**Theorem.** Let $f \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ be monic and let $p$ be a prime. If the reduction $\bar f \in (\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})[x]$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$, then $f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$.

*Proof.* A monic $f$ is primitive. If $f$ were reducible over $\mathbb{Q}$, Gauss's lemma gives $f = gh$ in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ with $\deg g, \deg h \ge 1$; the leading coefficients multiply to $1$, so both are $\pm 1$ and reduction mod $p$ keeps the degrees. Then $\bar f = \bar g\bar h$ is a nontrivial factorization, a contradiction. $\blacksquare$

The converse fails badly: $x^4 + 1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ (see below) but is reducible modulo *every* prime. For $p = 2$, $x^4 + 1 = (x + 1)^4$. For odd $p$, at least one of $2$, $-1$, $-2$ is a square modulo $p$ (the product of two non-squares is a square, and $2 \cdot (-1) = -2$). If $a^2 = 2$ then $x^4 + 1 = (x^2 + 1)^2 - 2x^2 = (x^2 - ax + 1)(x^2 + ax + 1)$; if $i^2 = -1$ then $x^4 + 1 = (x^2 - i)(x^2 + i)$; if $b^2 = -2$ then $x^4 + 1 = (x^2 - 1)^2 + 2x^2 = (x^2 - bx - 1)(x^2 + bx - 1)$.

### Eisenstein's criterion

**Theorem (Eisenstein).** Let $f = a_nx^n + \dots + a_0 \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ with $n \ge 1$ and let $p$ be a prime with $p \nmid a_n$, $p \mid a_i$ for $0 \le i < n$, and $p^2 \nmid a_0$. Then $f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$.

*Proof.* Dividing by the content (which $p$ does not divide, since $p \nmid a_n$) keeps the hypotheses, so assume $f$ primitive. Suppose $f = gh$ in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ with $\deg g, \deg h \ge 1$ (Gauss). Modulo $p$, $\bar g\bar h = \bar a_n x^n$. Let $x^i$ be the lowest power with nonzero coefficient in $\bar g$ and $x^j$ in $\bar h$. The coefficient of $x^{i+j}$ in $\bar g \bar h$ is the product of those two coefficients, nonzero, so $i + j = n$. But $i \le \deg \bar g \le \deg g$ and $j \le \deg h$ with $\deg g + \deg h = n$, so $i = \deg g \ge 1$ and $j = \deg h \ge 1$. Hence the constant terms of $\bar g$ and $\bar h$ are $0$: $p$ divides both, and $p^2 \mid a_0 = g(0)h(0)$, a contradiction. $\blacksquare$

**Cyclotomic polynomials.** For a prime $p$ let $\Phi_p = x^{p-1} + \dots + x + 1 = \frac{x^p - 1}{x - 1}$. Then $\Phi_p(x + 1) = \frac{(x + 1)^p - 1}{x} = \sum_{k=1}^{p}\binom{p}{k}x^{k-1}$ is monic, has all non-leading coefficients $\binom{p}{k}$ divisible by $p$ and constant term $p$, which $p^2$ does not divide. By Eisenstein it is irreducible; a factorization of $\Phi_p(x)$ would give one of $\Phi_p(x+1)$ by substituting $x \mapsto x + 1$, so $\Phi_p$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$.

### Trial division modulo $p$

Candidate rational roots can be listed by machine, which is a search, not a proof of irreducibility:

```python
from fractions import Fraction


def rational_root_candidates(a0, an):
    """All r/s with r | a0 and s | an, for nonzero integers a0, an."""
    divs = lambda m: [d for d in range(1, abs(m) + 1) if m % d == 0]
    return sorted({Fraction(sign * r, s)
                   for r in divs(a0) for s in divs(an) for sign in (1, -1)})


print(rational_root_candidates(6, 2))
```

It prints the twelve candidates $\pm 6, \pm 3, \pm 2, \pm 1, \pm 3/2, \pm 1/2$ as exact fractions, and substituting them into $f$ finishes the root test.

@example
title: Reduction modulo 2
problem: Show that $f = x^3 + 6x^2 + 5x + 7$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$.
step: $f$ is monic, so by the reduction theorem it suffices to find a prime $p$ with $\bar f$ irreducible over $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$. Try $p = 2$: $\bar f = x^3 + x + 1$, since $6 \equiv 0$, $5 \equiv 1$ and $7 \equiv 1$.
step: A cubic over a field is irreducible if and only if it has no root. Test $x = 0$: $\bar f(0) = 1$; test $x = 1$: $\bar f(1) = 1 + 1 + 1 = 1$ in $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. No roots.
step: So $\bar f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, and since $f$ is monic, $f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$. (As a check, the rational root theorem leaves $\pm 1, \pm 7$, and $f(1) = 19$, $f(-1) = 7$, $f(7) = 343 + 294 + 35 + 7 \ne 0$, $f(-7) = -343 + 294 - 35 + 7 \ne 0$.)
answer: $f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$.

@example
title: Eisenstein after a shift
problem: Show that $x^4 + 1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$.
step: Eisenstein does not apply to $x^4 + 1$ directly (every prime divides the coefficients $0, 0, 0$ but nothing divides the constant $1$). Substitute $x \mapsto x + 1$, which preserves irreducibility because it is an invertible change of variable in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$.
step: Expand: $(x + 1)^4 + 1 = x^4 + 4x^3 + 6x^2 + 4x + 2$.
step: With $p = 2$: the leading coefficient $1$ is odd, the coefficients $4, 6, 4, 2$ are all even, and the constant $2$ is not divisible by $4$. Eisenstein applies.
step: So $(x + 1)^4 + 1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$, hence so is $x^4 + 1$, even though $x^4 + 1$ is reducible modulo every prime.
answer: $x^4 + 1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$.

@mistakes
- Concluding irreducibility from "no rational roots" for degree $\ge 4$: $x^4 + 4 = (x^2 + 2x + 2)(x^2 - 2x + 2)$ has no rational root.
- Using the reduction test backwards: a polynomial irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ can be reducible modulo every prime ($x^4 + 1$), so a reducible $\bar f$ proves nothing.
- Applying the reduction test to a non-monic polynomial without checking that the leading coefficient survives modulo $p$: in $2x^2 + 1$, $p = 2$ gives the degree-$0$ reduction $1$.
- Checking Eisenstein's conditions on the wrong coefficients: $p$ must divide every coefficient except the leading one, and $p^2$ must not divide the constant term.

model:
```python
from itertools import product


def _trim(f, p):
    f = [c % p for c in f]
    while f and f[-1] == 0:
        f.pop()
    return f


def _remainder(f, g, p):
    """Remainder of f by the monic polynomial g over Z/pZ."""
    r = _trim(f, p)
    while len(r) >= len(g):
        c = r[-1]
        shift = len(r) - len(g)
        for i, gc in enumerate(g):
            r[i + shift] = (r[i + shift] - c * gc) % p
        r = _trim(r, p)
    return r


def is_irreducible_mod_p(f, p):
    """Is f irreducible over Z/pZ? (constant term first)"""
    f = _trim(f, p)
    n = len(f) - 1
    if n < 1:
        return False
    for d in range(1, n // 2 + 1):
        for low in product(range(p), repeat=d):
            g = list(low) + [1]
            if not _remainder(f, g, p):
                return False
    return True
```
hint: Divide `f` by every monic polynomial `g` of degree `d` for `d` from $1$ to `n // 2`; the list `list(low) + [1]` builds a monic `g` from its lower coefficients.
solution: Reduce the coefficients and trim trailing zeros; a polynomial of degree below $1$ is not irreducible. If $f = gh$ with both factors of positive degree, one factor has degree at most $n // 2$ and can be made monic by scaling (a field has inverses), so it suffices to try every monic divisor of degree $1, \dots, n // 2$. Division by a monic $g$ needs no inverse. If any remainder is zero, `f` is reducible. This brute-force test finds that $x^4 + 1$ is reducible modulo $7$ even though it is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$; checking small primes is evidence, and the proof that $x^4 + 1$ is reducible mod every prime is the argument in the article.

check: sum(1 for b in range(5) for c in range(5) if all((x * x + b * x + c) % 5 for x in range(5)))
