@article
Metalogic is logic about logic: we prove theorems about formal languages, proofs and models. Its raw material is **set theory**. A first-order model is a set with some relations on it, a formula is a finite string drawn from a set of symbols, and a theory is a set of sentences. So before we prove anything about logic, we need the basic vocabulary of sets and a feel for how proofs about sets go. This week is naive set theory, taught at the level needed for the rest of the course.

### Sets and extensionality

A **set** is determined by its members. This is the principle of **extensionality**: if $A$ and $B$ have exactly the same members, then $A = B$. So $\{2, 3\}$, $\{3, 2\}$ and $\{3, 2, 3\}$ are one set, and order and repetition do not matter. Write $x \in A$ for "$x$ is a member of $A$". The **empty set** $\varnothing$ has no members, and by extensionality there is only one of them.

$A$ is a **subset** of $B$, written $A \subseteq B$, when every member of $A$ is a member of $B$. It is a **proper subset**, $A \subsetneq B$, when also $A \neq B$. Two facts follow at once: $\varnothing \subseteq A$ for every $A$ (there is no member of $\varnothing$ that could fail to be in $A$), and $A = B$ if and only if $A \subseteq B$ and $B \subseteq A$. Do not confuse membership with inclusion: $\{1\} \in \{\{1\}, 2\}$, but $\{1\} \not\subseteq \{\{1\}, 2\}$, because $1$ is not a member of the second set.

### Operations

For sets $A, B$ inside a fixed **universe** $U$:

| Operation | Notation | Members |
|---|---|---|
| union | $A \cup B$ | $x$ with $x \in A$ or $x \in B$ |
| intersection | $A \cap B$ | $x$ with $x \in A$ and $x \in B$ |
| difference | $A \setminus B$ | $x$ with $x \in A$ and $x \notin B$ |
| complement | $\overline{A}$ | $U \setminus A$ |
| power set | $\mathcal{P}(A)$ | all subsets of $A$ |
| Cartesian product | $A \times B$ | all ordered pairs $(a, b)$ with $a \in A$, $b \in B$ |

If $A$ has $n$ members, then $\mathcal{P}(A)$ has $2^n$ members: each member of $A$ is independently in or out of a subset. If $\lvert A \rvert = m$ and $\lvert B \rvert = n$ then $\lvert A \times B \rvert = mn$. Ordered pairs satisfy $(a, b) = (c, d)$ if and only if $a = c$ and $b = d$.

### Proving identities: double inclusion

To prove $X = Y$, prove $X \subseteq Y$ and $Y \subseteq X$. Each half is a chain of "if and only if" or "so" steps about an arbitrary member $x$. Here is the template, applied to one of De Morgan's laws, $\overline{A \cup B} = \overline{A} \cap \overline{B}$.

Take any $x$. Then $x \in \overline{A \cup B}$ iff $x \notin A \cup B$ (definition of complement) iff it is not the case that ($x \in A$ or $x \in B$) (definition of union) iff $x \notin A$ and $x \notin B$ (De Morgan's law of propositional logic) iff $x \in \overline{A}$ and $x \in \overline{B}$ (definition of complement) iff $x \in \overline{A} \cap \overline{B}$ (definition of intersection). Since the two sets have the same members, they are equal by extensionality. When every step is a biconditional, the two inclusions are done at once; when a step goes only one way, split the proof in two.

Set identities are propositional logic in disguise: $\cup$ matches $\vee$, $\cap$ matches $\wedge$, complement matches $\neg$, and $\subseteq$ matches $\to$.

### Russell's paradox

Early set theory assumed **naive comprehension**: for every condition $\varphi(x)$ there is a set $\{x : \varphi(x)\}$ of exactly the things satisfying it. Russell (1901; he told Frege in 1902) took the condition "$x \notin x$":

| P1 | If naive comprehension holds, then there is a set $R$ such that $\forall x\,(x \in R \leftrightarrow x \notin x)$. |
| P2 | If $\forall x\,(x \in R \leftrightarrow x \notin x)$, then $R \in R \leftrightarrow R \notin R$. |
| P3 | It is not the case that $R \in R \leftrightarrow R \notin R$. |
| C | So naive comprehension does not hold. |

The argument is valid: P1 and P2 give "if naive comprehension holds then $R \in R \leftrightarrow R \notin R$", and P3 denies that consequent. P2 is universal instantiation, and P3 is a truth of propositional logic: no statement is equivalent to its own negation (if it were true it would be false, and conversely). So the only premise a defender of naive comprehension can reject is the inference in P1, which is just the principle itself. Standard set theory, ZFC, replaces comprehension by **separation**: given a set $A$, you may form $\{x \in A : \varphi(x)\}$, a subset of an existing set. Russell's argument then only shows that there is no set of all sets.

### Sets in Python

Python's `set` has `|` (union), `&` (intersection), `-` (difference), `^` (symmetric difference) and `<=` (subset). A set cannot contain a set, but it can contain a `frozenset`, so a power set is a set of frozensets. Because the experiments are finite, Python can **refute** an identity by finding a counterexample, and can confirm it on a small universe, though a successful check on a few sets is never a proof.

@example
title: Proving a distributive law
problem: Prove that $A \cap (B \cup C) = (A \cap B) \cup (A \cap C)$ for all sets $A, B, C$.
step: Fix an arbitrary $x$ and unfold the left side. $x \in A \cap (B \cup C)$ iff $x \in A$ and $x \in B \cup C$, by the definition of intersection.
step: By the definition of union, that holds iff $x \in A$ and ($x \in B$ or $x \in C$).
step: By the distributive law of propositional logic, $P \wedge (Q \vee R)$ is equivalent to $(P \wedge Q) \vee (P \wedge R)$. So the condition holds iff ($x \in A$ and $x \in B$) or ($x \in A$ and $x \in C$).
step: By the definitions of intersection and union again, that holds iff $x \in (A \cap B) \cup (A \cap C)$.
step: Every step was a biconditional about the arbitrary $x$, so the two sets have the same members, and they are equal by extensionality.
answer: The identity holds, proved by a chain of biconditionals and extensionality.

@example
title: Finding a counterexample in Python
problem: Is $(A \cup B) \setminus B = A$ an identity? Decide, and show how Python can find a counterexample.
step: Test with small sets: take $A = \{1\}$ and $B = \{1, 2\}$. Then $A \cup B = \{1, 2\}$ and $(A \cup B) \setminus B = \varnothing$, which is not $A$.
step: In Python, `({1} | {1, 2}) - {1, 2} == {1}` evaluates to `False`, because the left side is `set()`.
step: What goes wrong is the overlap: removing $B$ also removes the members of $A$ that lie in $B$. The correct identity is $(A \cup B) \setminus B = A \setminus B$.
answer: Not an identity; $A = \{1\}$, $B = \{1, 2\}$ is a counterexample. The correct version is $(A \cup B) \setminus B = A \setminus B$.

@mistakes
- Confusing $\in$ with $\subseteq$: $\{1\} \in \{\{1\}, 2\}$ is true, while $\{1\} \subseteq \{\{1\}, 2\}$ is false.
- Writing $\varnothing = \{\varnothing\}$. The empty set has no members; $\{\varnothing\}$ has exactly one, namely $\varnothing$.
- Treating a few examples as a proof. A Python check on small sets can refute an identity but cannot prove it for all sets.
- Proving only one inclusion and then asserting equality. Equality needs both $X \subseteq Y$ and $Y \subseteq X$.

model:
```python
from itertools import combinations


def powerset(s):
    """Return the set of all subsets of s, as frozensets."""
    items = list(s)
    return {frozenset(c)
            for k in range(len(items) + 1)
            for c in combinations(items, k)}
```
hint: A subset with $k$ members is a combination of $k$ items; `itertools.combinations(items, k)` lists them.

solution: Turn the set into a list, generate the combinations of every size from 0 up to the full size, and collect them as frozensets in a set. The empty set arises from the size-0 combination, so `powerset(set())` is `{frozenset()}`.

prompt: Prove that $A \setminus (B \cup C) = (A \setminus B) \cap (A \setminus C)$ for all sets $A$, $B$ and $C$, justifying each step.
hint: Fix an arbitrary $x$ and unfold both sides to statements about $x \in A$, $x \in B$ and $x \in C$.

solution: Let $x$ be arbitrary. $x \in A \setminus (B \cup C)$ iff $x \in A$ and $x \notin B \cup C$ (definition of difference) iff $x \in A$ and not ($x \in B$ or $x \in C$) (definition of union) iff $x \in A$ and $x \notin B$ and $x \notin C$ (De Morgan). On the other side, $x \in (A \setminus B) \cap (A \setminus C)$ iff ($x \in A$ and $x \notin B$) and ($x \in A$ and $x \notin C$) (definitions of intersection and difference) iff $x \in A$ and $x \notin B$ and $x \notin C$ (idempotence of "and"). The two conditions coincide, so the sets have the same members and are equal by extensionality.

model:
```python
from itertools import combinations


def is_identity(f, g, universe):
    """Test f(A, B) == g(A, B) for all subsets A, B of the universe."""
    items = list(universe)
    subsets = [frozenset(c)
               for k in range(len(items) + 1)
               for c in combinations(items, k)]
    return all(f(a, b) == g(a, b) for a in subsets for b in subsets)
```
hint: First list every subset of `universe` as a frozenset, as in `powerset`.
hint: Use `all(...)` over every ordered pair of subsets.
solution: Build the list of all subsets, then check the equation on every pair with `all`. The empty universe has one subset, the empty set, so one pair is checked. A `True` answer shows only that the identity holds on that universe; it is not a proof for all sets, but a `False` answer is a genuine refutation.
