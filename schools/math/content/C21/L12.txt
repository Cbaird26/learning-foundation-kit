@article
Hilbert hoped that mathematics could be put in a complete and consistent formal system, with consistency established by finitary means. In 1931 Gödel showed that for any theory containing enough arithmetic and having an effectively given set of axioms, completeness and the hoped-for proof of consistency are both impossible. The results are exact, and they are often misreported, so this lesson begins with the hypotheses.

### The theorems

Say a theory $T$ in the language of arithmetic is **effectively axiomatized** if its set of axioms is decidable, **consistent** if $T \nvdash \bot$, **$\omega$-consistent** if there is no formula $\varphi(x)$ such that $T \vdash \exists x\,\varphi(x)$ while $T \vdash \neg\varphi(\bar n)$ for every $n$, and **complete** if for every sentence $A$, $T \vdash A$ or $T \vdash \neg A$. Let $Q$ be Robinson arithmetic, and $\mathrm{Prov}_T(x)$ the provability formula of last week.

**First Incompleteness Theorem** (Gödel 1931). Let $T$ be a consistent, effectively axiomatized theory extending $Q$. Then $T$ is incomplete: there is an arithmetical sentence that $T$ neither proves nor refutes. More exactly, applying the diagonal lemma to $\neg\mathrm{Prov}_T(y)$ gives a sentence $G_T$ with $T \vdash G_T \leftrightarrow \neg\mathrm{Prov}_T(\overline{\ulcorner G_T\urcorner})$; if $T$ is consistent then $T \nvdash G_T$, and if $T$ is $\omega$-consistent then $T \nvdash \neg G_T$. **Rosser** (1936) used a different sentence to remove the extra assumption: consistency alone suffices for $T$ to prove neither $R_T$ nor $\neg R_T$.

*Proof of the unprovability of $G_T$.* Suppose $T \vdash G_T$, and let $n$ code a derivation of it. Then $\mathrm{Prf}_T(\bar n, \overline{\ulcorner G_T\urcorner})$ is representable and true, so $T$ proves it and hence proves $\mathrm{Prov}_T(\overline{\ulcorner G_T\urcorner})$. But $T \vdash G_T$ and $G_T$ is equivalent to $\neg\mathrm{Prov}_T(\overline{\ulcorner G_T\urcorner})$, so $T$ also proves the negation. $T$ is inconsistent, contrary to hypothesis. For $\neg G_T$: if $T \vdash \neg G_T$ then $T \vdash \mathrm{Prov}_T(\overline{\ulcorner G_T\urcorner})$, i.e. $T \vdash \exists y\,\mathrm{Prf}_T(y, \overline{\ulcorner G_T\urcorner})$. But, $T$ being consistent, no number codes a proof of $G_T$, so $T \vdash \neg\mathrm{Prf}_T(\bar n, \overline{\ulcorner G_T\urcorner})$ for every $n$: $T$ is $\omega$-inconsistent. $\square$

If $T$ is consistent, $G_T$ is **true** in the standard model: it says that no number codes a proof of $G_T$, and, since $T \nvdash G_T$, none does. But this truth is established from the assumption that $T$ is consistent, which $T$ itself cannot prove:

**Second Incompleteness Theorem.** Let $T$ be a consistent, effectively axiomatized theory extending PA (or any theory satisfying the Hilbert-Bernays-Löb derivability conditions). Then $T \nvdash \mathrm{Con}_T$, where $\mathrm{Con}_T := \neg\mathrm{Prov}_T(\overline{\ulcorner\bot\urcorner})$. *Idea:* the first half of the previous argument can be carried out inside $T$, which gives $T \vdash \mathrm{Con}_T \to G_T$. If $T \vdash \mathrm{Con}_T$, then $T \vdash G_T$, contradicting the first theorem.

### The hypotheses matter

| Theory | Consistent | Effectively axiomatized | Extends $Q$ | Complete |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| True arithmetic (all sentences true in $\mathbb{N}$) | yes | no | yes | yes |
| Peano arithmetic (PA) | yes, being true in $\mathbb{N}$ | yes | yes | no |
| Presburger arithmetic (no $\times$) | yes | yes | no | yes (and decidable) |
| $Q$ | yes | yes | yes | no |

True arithmetic is complete and consistent but cannot be effectively axiomatized: no algorithm lists its theorems. Presburger arithmetic escapes by lacking multiplication. Adding $G_{PA}$ (or $\mathrm{Con}_{PA}$) to PA gives a stronger theory that proves what PA could not, but the theorems apply to it again: it has its own unprovable sentence. Gentzen (1936) proved the consistency of PA, using induction up to the ordinal $\varepsilon_0$, a principle that PA does not have. Natural arithmetical statements independent of PA are also known, such as Goodstein's theorem, which Kirby and Paris (1982) showed PA cannot prove. The independence of the continuum hypothesis from ZFC is a different kind of result, though it also shows that ZFC is incomplete.

### What the theorems do not say

They do not say that there are truths that *no one* can ever know: $G_T$ is provable in a stronger theory, and we know it is true if we accept that $T$ is consistent. They do not say that mathematics is inconsistent or that "everything is relative". The second theorem does not say that PA's consistency is doubtful; it says that PA cannot prove it, while stronger arguments (Gentzen's) can. It does mean that Hilbert's program, in its original form of finitary consistency proofs for all of mathematics, cannot succeed if finitary reasoning is formalizable in the theory.

### Truth and proof

**Tarski's undefinability theorem** (1930s): there is no formula $\mathrm{Tr}(x)$ of the language of arithmetic such that $\mathbb{N} \vDash \mathrm{Tr}(\overline{\ulcorner A\urcorner}) \leftrightarrow A$ for every sentence $A$. The diagonal lemma applied to $\neg\mathrm{Tr}(y)$ yields a sentence equivalent to its own falsehood. So arithmetical truth is not arithmetically definable, while provability in an effective theory is. Together with incompleteness this shows that arithmetical truth outruns provability in any effective theory. Platonists take this to show that arithmetical truth is independent of any formal proof system; formalists and some constructivists answer that it shows only that "truth in $\mathbb{N}$" needs a justification beyond the formal systems, or that the notion is less determinate than assumed. The theorems alone do not decide between these views.

### Minds and machines

Lucas (1961) and later Penrose argued from the theorems that human minds are not machines:

| P1 | If the mind is a machine, then the arithmetical statements the mind can know to be true are exactly the theorems of some consistent, effectively axiomatized theory $F$ extending $Q$. |
| P2 | For every such $F$, the Gödel sentence $G_F$ is not a theorem of $F$. |
| P3 | For every such $F$, the mind can know that $G_F$ is true. |
| C | So the mind is not a machine. |

The argument is valid: if the mind is a machine, then by P1 and P3, $G_F$ is a theorem of $F$, contradicting P2. P2 is the first incompleteness theorem. The debate is about P1 and P3. For P3: one can see that $G_F$ is true only if one knows that $F$ is consistent, and by the second theorem $F$ cannot establish that itself; for a very complicated $F$, a candidate description of one's own mind, there may be no way to know it is consistent. Benacerraf (1967) argued that the most the theorems yield is a disjunction: either the mind is not a machine or its machine description cannot be known to be consistent. In his 1951 Gibbs lecture Gödel drew only a disjunction, though his wider views leaned against mechanism: either the human mind infinitely surpasses finite machines, or there are absolutely unsolvable Diophantine problems of a certain kind. For P1: human mathematicians may be inconsistent, and what they "can know" is not a clearly defined set. Most logicians and philosophers regard the Lucas-Penrose argument as unsuccessful, though discussion continues. The theorems do not settle whether minds are machines.

@example
title: Does the first theorem apply?
problem: For each theory decide whether the First Incompleteness Theorem applies, and what it then tells us: (a) Presburger arithmetic; (b) true arithmetic $\mathrm{Th}(\mathbb{N})$; (c) $PA + \mathrm{Con}_{PA}$.
step: The theorem needs three hypotheses: consistent, effectively axiomatized, extends $Q$.
step: (a) Presburger arithmetic has no multiplication, so it does not extend $Q$. The theorem does not apply, and indeed the theory is complete and decidable.
step: (b) $\mathrm{Th}(\mathbb{N})$ is consistent and extends $Q$, but its set of axioms (all true sentences) is not decidable, so it is not effectively axiomatized. The theorem does not apply, and the theory is complete.
step: (c) $PA + \mathrm{Con}_{PA}$ is true in $\mathbb{N}$, hence consistent, is effectively axiomatized (one axiom added to a decidable set), and extends $Q$. The theorem applies: it is incomplete, with its own Gödel sentence, and by the second theorem it does not prove $\mathrm{Con}_{PA + \mathrm{Con}_{PA}}$.
answer: Only (c) satisfies all hypotheses; (a) fails "extends $Q$" and (b) fails "effectively axiomatized". Patching PA with its consistency statement does not make it complete.

@example
title: Locating the weak premise
problem: A critic replies to the Lucas-Penrose argument: "To see that $G_F$ is true I must already know that $F$ is consistent, and I cannot know that for an arbitrary $F$." Which premise is attacked, and does the reply defeat the argument?
step: Set the argument out as P1 to P3 and C. The claim "the mind can know $G_F$ is true" is premise P3.
step: The reply says P3 holds only conditionally: the mind can know $G_F$ is true if it can know $F$ is consistent, and it cannot do this for every $F$, in particular not for an $F$ that exactly captures the mind itself.
step: If the reply is right, P3 as stated is unsupported, so the argument is not shown to be sound. It is still valid.
step: The defender can answer that a mathematician can in fact recognize the soundness of the systems she works in; the critic replies that this is not the same as recognizing it of a system claimed to be herself. The dispute turns on what self-knowledge of consistency is available. So the reply does not refute the conclusion; it removes the argument's proof of it.
answer: The reply attacks premise P3; it shows that the argument has an unsupported premise but does not prove that the mind is a machine.

@mistakes
- Stating the theorem without its hypotheses: it needs consistency, effective axiomatization and enough arithmetic, and complete consistent theories such as true arithmetic and Presburger arithmetic exist.
- Saying that Gödel proved the existence of truths that can never be known or proved by anyone; the Gödel sentence of $T$ is provable in stronger theories.
- Reading the second theorem as showing that PA is inconsistent or that its consistency is doubtful. It shows that PA cannot prove its own consistency.
- Saying that the incompleteness theorems prove that minds are not machines. They do not; at most they feed a contested argument with a disputed premise.

@practice mc
prompt: Which is the correct statement of Gödel's second incompleteness theorem?
choice*: If $T$ is a consistent, effectively axiomatized theory extending PA, then $T$ does not prove the arithmetical sentence expressing its own consistency.

A stronger theory can prove the consistency of a weaker one (Gentzen), so the last statement is false.

prompt: What does Rosser's improvement of Gödel's first theorem show?
choice*: Consistency alone, without $\omega$-consistency, suffices for there to be a sentence that the theory neither proves nor refutes.
choice: Every consistent theory is $\omega$-consistent.
choice: Every effectively axiomatized theory is complete.
choice: The Gödel sentence is provable if the theory is $\omega$-consistent.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain in about five sentences why the second incompleteness theorem is said to undermine Hilbert's program, and state one qualification to that claim.
hint: Recall what Hilbert wanted from a consistency proof and what the theorem says a strong theory cannot prove.
solution: Hilbert's program asked for a proof, by finitary and secure means, that formal mathematics is consistent. The second theorem says that a consistent, effectively axiomatized theory containing enough arithmetic cannot prove its own consistency. If finitary reasoning can be formalized in such a theory, then a finitary consistency proof for the theory would be a proof inside the theory, which the theorem forbids. So the original program cannot be carried out for strong theories. One qualification: the theorem does not rule out proofs of consistency by means that go beyond the theory, as Gentzen's proof of the consistency of PA using induction up to $\varepsilon_0$; and whether such principles are acceptable as "finitary" or secure is philosophically contested, as is whether all finitary reasoning is formalizable in PA.
