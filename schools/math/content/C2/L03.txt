@lesson

@article
In Calculus II a function had one slope at each point. A function of several variables has a different slope in every direction you can walk. The **gradient** packages all of them into one vector:

$$\nabla f=\langle f_x,\ f_y,\ f_z\rangle .$$

### Directional derivative

Start at a point $P$ and walk in the direction of a **unit** vector $\mathbf u$. The rate at which $f$ changes is

$$D_{\mathbf u}f(P)=\nabla f(P)\cdot\mathbf u .$$

This is the ordinary derivative of $f$ along the line $P+s\mathbf u$ at $s=0$. If the direction you are given is not a unit vector, divide it by its length first.

### What the gradient tells you

Write $\nabla f\cdot\mathbf u=|\nabla f|\cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between $\nabla f$ and $\mathbf u$. Then:

- $f$ increases fastest in the direction of $\nabla f$, at the rate $|\nabla f|$.
- $f$ decreases fastest in the direction of $-\nabla f$.
- $f$ does not change to first order in directions with $\theta=90^\circ$.

The last item says that $\nabla f$ is **perpendicular to the level curve** (or level surface) through the point. For a surface $F(x,y,z)=c$ this gives the tangent plane at $P_0=(x_0,y_0,z_0)$:

$$\nabla F(P_0)\cdot\langle x-x_0,\ y-y_0,\ z-z_0\rangle=0 .$$

### The chain rule

If a point moves along a curve $\mathbf r(t)$, then $f$ changes along the curve at the rate

$$\frac{d}{dt}f(\mathbf r(t))=\nabla f\cdot\mathbf r'(t)=f_x\,x'(t)+f_y\,y'(t)+f_z\,z'(t).$$

This is the directional derivative again, with velocity $\mathbf r'$ supplying the direction and the speed. When the variables depend on two parameters, $x=x(s,t)$ and $y=y(s,t)$, apply the rule to one parameter at a time:

$$\frac{\partial w}{\partial s}=\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial s}+\frac{\partial w}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial s}.$$

A good memory aid: draw a tree from $w$ to $x,y$ to $s,t$. For each **path** from $w$ down to $s$, multiply the derivatives along the path; then add the paths.

@example
title: Directional derivative and steepest ascent
problem: Let $f(x,y)=x^2+3xy$ and $P=(1,2)$. Find (a) the rate of change of $f$ at $P$ in the direction of $\langle 3,4\rangle$, and (b) the largest possible rate of change at $P$ and the direction in which it occurs.
step: The gradient is $\nabla f=\langle 2x+3y,\ 3x\rangle$, so $\nabla f(1,2)=\langle 8,\ 3\rangle$.
step: The vector $\langle 3,4\rangle$ has length $5$, so the unit vector is $\mathbf u=\langle 3/5,\ 4/5\rangle$.
step: Then $D_{\mathbf u}f(P)=\frac35\cdot 8+\frac45\cdot 3=\frac{36}{5}=7.2$.
step: The largest rate is $|\nabla f(P)|=\sqrt{64+9}=\sqrt{73}\approx 8.54$, in the direction $\langle 8,3\rangle/\sqrt{73}$. This is bigger than $7.2$, as it must be.

@example
title: The chain rule along a curve
problem: Let $w=x^2y$ with $x=2t$ and $y=t^3$. Find $dw/dt$ at $t=1$ with the chain rule, then verify by substituting first.
step: The partial derivatives are $w_x=2xy$ and $w_y=x^2$; the derivatives of the curve are $x'=2$ and $y'=3t^2$.
step: The chain rule gives $\dfrac{dw}{dt}=2xy\cdot 2+x^2\cdot 3t^2$.
step: At $t=1$ we have $x=2$ and $y=1$, so $\dfrac{dw}{dt}=8+12=20$.
step: Verify: substituting gives $w=(2t)^2t^3=4t^5$, and $\frac{d}{dt}(4t^5)=20t^4=20$ at $t=1$.

@mistakes
- Using a direction vector that is not a unit vector in $D_{\mathbf u}f=\nabla f\cdot\mathbf u$. Divide by its length first.
- Believing the gradient points along the level curve. It is perpendicular to it.
- Confusing the gradient, which is a vector, with the largest rate of change, which is its length $|\nabla f|$.
- Dropping a path in the chain rule: if $w$ depends on $s$ through both $x$ and $y$, there are two terms to add.

@practice tuple
prompt: Find the gradient of $f(x,y,z)=xy^2+z^3$ at the point $(1,2,1)$.
answer: (4, 4, 3)
check: (nderiv(lambda x: x*2**2 + 1**3, 1), nderiv(lambda y: 1*y**2 + 1**3, 2), nderiv(lambda z: 1*2**2 + z**3, 1))
hint: Find the three partial derivatives, holding the other variables constant.
hint: $\nabla f=\langle y^2,\ 2xy,\ 3z^2\rangle$. Substitute $(1,2,1)$.
solution: $\nabla f=\langle y^2,\,2xy,\,3z^2\rangle$, so $\nabla f(1,2,1)=\langle 4,\,4,\,3\rangle$.

@practice num
prompt: Let $f(x,y)=x^2y-y^3$. Find the directional derivative of $f$ at $(2,1)$ in the direction of the vector $\langle 3,-4\rangle$.
answer: 8/5
check: nderiv(lambda s: (2 + 0.6*s)**2*(1 - 0.8*s) - (1 - 0.8*s)**3, 0)
hint: Compute $\nabla f(2,1)$ first, then turn $\langle 3,-4\rangle$ into a unit vector.
hint: $\nabla f=\langle 2xy,\ x^2-3y^2\rangle=\langle 4,1\rangle$ and $\mathbf u=\langle 3/5,-4/5\rangle$.
solution: $D_{\mathbf u}f=\frac35\cdot 4+\left(-\frac45\right)\cdot 1=\frac{12-4}{5}=\frac85$.

@practice mc
prompt: At a point $P$ the gradient of $f$ is $\nabla f(P)=\langle 3,4\rangle$. Which statement is correct?
choice*: $f$ does not change to first order if you move from $P$ in the direction $\langle 4,-3\rangle$.
choice: $f$ increases fastest if you move from $P$ in the direction $\langle 4,-3\rangle$.
choice: The largest possible rate of change of $f$ at $P$ is $7$.
choice: The level curve of $f$ through $P$ is tangent to $\langle 3,4\rangle$.
hint: Compute the dot product of $\langle 4,-3\rangle$ with $\langle 3,4\rangle$.
hint: The largest rate is $|\nabla f|$, a length, not a sum of components.
solution: Since $\langle 4,-3\rangle\cdot\langle 3,4\rangle=0$, that direction is tangent to the level curve and the rate there is zero. The fastest increase is along $\langle 3,4\rangle$ at rate $\sqrt{9+16}=5$, not $7$. The gradient is perpendicular to the level curve, not tangent to it.

@practice num
prompt: Let $f(x,y)=x^2-xy$ and let $x=\cos t$, $y=\sin t$. Find $\dfrac{df}{dt}$ at $t=\pi/2$.
answer: 1
check: nderiv(lambda t: cos(t)**2 - cos(t)*sin(t), pi/2)
hint: Use $\frac{df}{dt}=f_x\,x'+f_y\,y'$.
hint: At $t=\pi/2$: $x=0$, $y=1$, $x'=-1$, $y'=0$. Also $f_x=2x-y$ and $f_y=-x$.
solution: $f_x=2x-y=-1$ and $f_y=-x=0$ at $(0,1)$. With $x'=-\sin t=-1$ and $y'=\cos t=0$, $\frac{df}{dt}=(-1)(-1)+0\cdot 0=1$.

@practice num
prompt: The plane tangent to the surface $x^2+2y^2+3z^2=6$ at the point $(1,1,1)$ crosses the $x$-axis at $x=$ ?
answer: 6
check: 1 + (nderiv(lambda y: 1 + 2*y**2 + 3, 1) + nderiv(lambda z: 1 + 2 + 3*z**2, 1))/nderiv(lambda x: x**2 + 2 + 3, 1)
hint: A normal vector to the tangent plane is the gradient of $F(x,y,z)=x^2+2y^2+3z^2$.
hint: $\nabla F(1,1,1)=\langle 2,4,6\rangle$. Write $2(x-1)+4(y-1)+6(z-1)=0$ and set $y=z=0$.
solution: The plane is $2(x-1)+4(y-1)+6(z-1)=0$, i.e. $x+2y+3z=6$. On the $x$-axis $y=z=0$, so $x=6$.

@quiz num
prompt: Let $f(x,y,z)=x^2y+yz^2$. What is the largest possible rate of change of $f$ at the point $(1,2,1)$?
answer: 6
check: norm([nderiv(lambda x: x**2*2 + 2*1**2, 1), nderiv(lambda y: 1*y + y*1, 2), nderiv(lambda z: 1*2 + 2*z**2, 1)])
hint: The largest rate of change at a point is the length of the gradient.
hint: $\nabla f=\langle 2xy,\ x^2+z^2,\ 2yz\rangle$.
solution: $\nabla f(1,2,1)=\langle 4,2,4\rangle$ and $|\nabla f|=\sqrt{16+4+16}=6$.

@quiz expr
prompt: Let $w=x^2y$ with $x=st$ and $y=s+t$. Find $\partial w/\partial s$ as a function of $s$ and $t$.
vars: s, t
lead: $\dfrac{\partial w}{\partial s} =$
answer: 3*s^2*t^2 + 2*s*t^3
check: lambda s, t: nderiv(lambda u: (u*t)**2*(u + t), s)
hint: There are two paths from $w$ to $s$: through $x$ and through $y$.
hint: $\frac{\partial w}{\partial s}=2xy\cdot t+x^2\cdot 1$. Then substitute $x=st$ and $y=s+t$.
solution: $\frac{\partial w}{\partial s}=2xy\,t+x^2=2st(s+t)t+s^2t^2=3s^2t^2+2st^3$. Check: $w=s^3t^2+s^2t^3$, and differentiating in $s$ gives the same result.
