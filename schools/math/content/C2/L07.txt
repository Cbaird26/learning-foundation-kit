@article
Polar coordinates made disks easy. Many other regions become easy after a different change of variables: an ellipse becomes a disk, a slanted parallelogram becomes a rectangle. The price is a correction factor, the **Jacobian**.

### The Jacobian

A change of variables is a map $(u,v)\mapsto(x,y)=(x(u,v),\,y(u,v))$. Its **Jacobian matrix** collects all four partial derivatives, and its determinant is the **Jacobian determinant**:

$$\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}=\begin{vmatrix}x_u&x_v\\ y_u&y_v\end{vmatrix}=x_uy_v-x_vy_u .$$

### What it means

Near a point the map looks like its linear approximation (Week 2). A tiny rectangle with sides $du$ and $dv$ is sent to a parallelogram spanned by $\langle x_u,y_u\rangle\,du$ and $\langle x_v,y_v\rangle\,dv$. The area of that parallelogram is a determinant:

$$dA=\left|\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right|du\,dv .$$

So the **size** of the Jacobian is the local factor by which area is stretched. Its **sign** records whether the map flips orientation, as a mirror does. A Jacobian of $-3$ means areas triple and orientation reverses.

### The change-of-variables formula

$$\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA=\iint_S f\big(x(u,v),y(u,v)\big)\left|\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right|du\,dv ,$$

where $S$ is the region in the $uv$-plane that the map sends onto $R$. The map must be one-to-one (apart from boundary points) with nonzero Jacobian. In polar coordinates the matrix is $\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-r\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&r\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$ and the determinant is $r$, which is where the extra $r$ comes from. For three variables the same idea uses a $3\times 3$ determinant; it equals $r$ for cylindrical coordinates and $\rho^2\sin\phi$ for spherical coordinates.

### The reciprocal rule

Boundaries are often given as $u(x,y)=\text{constant}$ and $v(x,y)=\text{constant}$, so it is easier to compute $\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}$. The two Jacobians are reciprocals:

$$\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}=\frac{1}{\;\dfrac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}\;}.$$

### Choosing new variables

Look at the boundary of the region. If it is made of curves $u=\text{const}$ and $v=\text{const}$, those $u$ and $v$ are your new coordinates, and $S$ becomes a rectangle. If that fails, look for a combination that appears in the integrand, such as $x+y$ or $xy$.

@example
title: Deriving and reading the polar Jacobian
problem: Compute $\partial(x,y)/\partial(r,\theta)$ for $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$, and say what it tells you about a small patch at radius $r$.
step: The partial derivatives are $x_r=\cos\theta$, $x_\theta=-r\sin\theta$, $y_r=\sin\theta$, $y_\theta=r\cos\theta$.
step: The determinant is $\cos\theta\cdot r\cos\theta-(-r\sin\theta)\sin\theta=r(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta)=r$.
step: A small rectangle $dr\times d\theta$ in the $r\theta$-plane has area $dr\,d\theta$, and its image has area $r\,dr\,d\theta$. The factor $r$ is positive, so orientation is preserved.
step: Reading it: the same $d\theta$ sweeps a longer arc at larger $r$, so patches far from the origin are stretched more. At $r=0$ the Jacobian is $0$ and the map collapses a whole segment to one point.

@example
title: An ellipse becomes a disk
problem: Evaluate $\iint_R x^2\,dA$, where $R$ is the ellipse $\dfrac{x^2}{4}+y^2\le 1$.
step: Let $x=2u$ and $y=v$. Then the ellipse becomes $u^2+v^2\le 1$, the unit disk $S$.
step: The Jacobian is $\begin{vmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{vmatrix}=2$, so $dA=2\,du\,dv$. The integrand is $x^2=4u^2$.
step: The integral is $\iint_S 4u^2\cdot 2\,du\,dv=8\iint_S u^2\,dA$.
step: In polar coordinates $\iint_S u^2\,dA=\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta\int_0^1 r^3\,dr=\pi\cdot\frac14$, so the result is $8\cdot\frac{\pi}{4}=2\pi$.

@mistakes
- Forgetting the absolute value of the Jacobian. Area is never negative; the sign only records orientation.
- Using $\partial(u,v)/\partial(x,y)$ where $\partial(x,y)/\partial(u,v)$ is needed. They are reciprocals, not equal.
- Rewriting the integrand and the region in $u,v$ but forgetting the Jacobian factor altogether.
- Substituting for $x$ and $y$ in the integrand but keeping the old limits for $x$ and $y$.

@practice num
prompt: Let $x=u^2-v^2$ and $y=2uv$. Find the Jacobian determinant $\dfrac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}$ at $(u,v)=(1,2)$.
answer: 20
check: det([[nderiv(lambda u: u**2 - 2**2, 1), nderiv(lambda v: 1**2 - v**2, 2)], [nderiv(lambda u: 2*u*2, 1), nderiv(lambda v: 2*1*v, 2)]])
hint: Form the matrix of partial derivatives with the rows for $x,y$ and the columns for $u,v$.
hint: $\begin{pmatrix}2u&-2v\\2v&2u\end{pmatrix}$ at $(1,2)$ is $\begin{pmatrix}2&-4\\4&2\end{pmatrix}$.
solution: $\det\begin{pmatrix}2&-4\\4&2\end{pmatrix}=2\cdot 2-(-4)(4)=20$. In general the determinant is $4(u^2+v^2)$.

@practice num
prompt: Let $u=x+2y$ and $v=3x-y$. Find $\dfrac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}$.
answer: -1/7
check: 1/det([[nderiv(lambda x: x + 2*1, 1), nderiv(lambda y: 1 + 2*y, 1)], [nderiv(lambda x: 3*x - 1, 1), nderiv(lambda y: 3*1 - y, 1)]])
hint: It is easier to compute $\partial(u,v)/\partial(x,y)$ first and then take the reciprocal.
hint: $\partial(u,v)/\partial(x,y)=\begin{vmatrix}1&2\\3&-1\end{vmatrix}$.
solution: $\dfrac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}=1\cdot(-1)-2\cdot 3=-7$. The Jacobians of a map and its inverse are reciprocals, so $\dfrac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}=-\dfrac17$.

@practice mc
prompt: A smooth map $(u,v)\mapsto(x,y)$ has $\dfrac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}=-3$ at a point. What does this say about a tiny region near that point?
choice*: Its area is multiplied by $3$ and its orientation is reversed.
choice: Its area is divided by $3$ and its orientation is reversed.
choice: Its area is multiplied by $3$ and its orientation is preserved.
choice: Its area becomes negative, which means the map is not smooth.
hint: The absolute value of the Jacobian is the area scale factor. What does the sign record?
solution: The size $|-3|=3$ is the local factor by which area is multiplied, and the negative sign means the map reverses orientation, like a reflection. Areas are never negative, and a negative Jacobian does not mean the map fails to be smooth.

@practice num
prompt: Let $R$ be the parallelogram bounded by $x+y=1$, $x+y=3$, $x-y=0$ and $x-y=2$. Use $u=x+y$ and $v=x-y$ to evaluate $\iint_R (x^2-y^2)\,dA$.
answer: 4
check: quad2(lambda x, y: x**2 - y**2, 0.5, 2.5, lambda x: max(x - 2, 1 - x), lambda x: min(x, 3 - x))
hint: Solve for $x=(u+v)/2$ and $y=(u-v)/2$. Then $x^2-y^2=uv$, and the region becomes a rectangle.
hint: $\partial(x,y)/\partial(u,v)=\begin{vmatrix}1/2&1/2\\1/2&-1/2\end{vmatrix}=-\tfrac12$, so $dA=\tfrac12\,du\,dv$ with $1\le u\le 3$ and $0\le v\le 2$.
solution: $\iint_R(x^2-y^2)\,dA=\int_1^3\int_0^2 uv\cdot\tfrac12\,dv\,du=\tfrac12\cdot\left(\int_1^3u\,du\right)\left(\int_0^2v\,dv\right)=\tfrac12\cdot 4\cdot 2=4$.

@practice num
prompt: Let $R$ be the region in the first quadrant bounded by $xy=1$, $xy=4$, $y=x$ and $y=2x$. Use $u=xy$ and $v=y/x$ to find its area. Give an exact answer.
answer: 3ln(2)/2
check: quad2(lambda x, y: 1, 1/sqrt(2), 2, lambda x: max(x, 1/x), lambda x: min(2*x, 4/x))
hint: In the $uv$-plane the region is the rectangle $1\le u\le 4$, $1\le v\le 2$. Compute $\partial(u,v)/\partial(x,y)$ and invert it.
hint: $\dfrac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}=\begin{vmatrix}y&x\\-y/x^2&1/x\end{vmatrix}=\dfrac{2y}{x}=2v$, so $dA=\dfrac{du\,dv}{2v}$.
solution: Area $=\int_1^4\int_1^2\dfrac{dv}{2v}\,du=3\cdot\dfrac{\ln 2}{2}=\dfrac{3\ln 2}{2}\approx 1.04$.

@quiz num
prompt: Let $x=u^2v$ and $y=u+v^2$. Find $\dfrac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}$ at $(u,v)=(1,2)$.
answer: 15
check: det([[nderiv(lambda u: u**2*2, 1), nderiv(lambda v: 1**2*v, 2)], [nderiv(lambda u: u + 2**2, 1), nderiv(lambda v: 1 + v**2, 2)]])
hint: Rows are $x$ and $y$, columns are $u$ and $v$.
hint: The matrix is $\begin{pmatrix}2uv&u^2\\1&2v\end{pmatrix}$.
solution: At $(1,2)$ the matrix is $\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\1&4\end{pmatrix}$, with determinant $16-1=15$.

@quiz num
prompt: Let $E$ be the ellipse $4x^2+9y^2\le 36$. Evaluate $\iint_E y^2\,dA$. Give an exact answer.
answer: 6pi
check: quad2(lambda x, y: y**2, -3, 3, lambda x: -sqrt((36 - 4*x**2)/9), lambda x: sqrt((36 - 4*x**2)/9))
hint: Let $x=3u$ and $y=2v$, so that $E$ becomes the unit disk $u^2+v^2\le 1$.
hint: The Jacobian is $6$ and $y^2=4v^2$. You need $\iint_{u^2+v^2\le 1}v^2\,dA=\pi/4$.
solution: $\iint_E y^2\,dA=\iint_{u^2+v^2\le 1}4v^2\cdot 6\,du\,dv=24\cdot\frac{\pi}{4}=6\pi$.
