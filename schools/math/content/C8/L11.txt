@article
So far the limits in this course were limits of numbers. Now we take limits of *functions*. A sequence $(f_n)$ of functions $f_n:D\to\mathbb R$ gives, for each fixed $x\in D$, an ordinary sequence of numbers $f_1(x),f_2(x),\dots$

### Pointwise convergence

$f_n\to f$ **pointwise** on $D$ if $f_n(x)\to f(x)$ for every $x\in D$. In quantifiers:
$$\forall x\in D\ \ \forall\varepsilon>0\ \ \exists N:\quad n\ge N\ \Longrightarrow\ |f_n(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon.$$
The integer $N$ may depend on $x$ as well as on $\varepsilon$. To find the limit function, fix $x$, treat it as a constant, and let $n\to\infty$.

For example, $\frac xn\to0$ for every real $x$, and $\left(1+\frac xn\right)^n\to e^x$ for every real $x$. The set matters: $x^n$ converges for $x$ in $(-1,1]$ and diverges for every other real $x$ (at $x=-1$ it alternates, and for $|x|>1$ it blows up).

### Three things that go wrong

We would like the limit to inherit the good properties of the $f_n$. Pointwise convergence guarantees almost none of them.

**1. Continuity is lost.** Let $f_n(x)=x^n$ on $[0,1]$. For $0\le x<1$ we get $x^n\to0$, and at $x=1$ we get $1^n=1$. So $f_n\to f$ pointwise, where $f=0$ on $[0,1)$ and $f(1)=1$. Every $f_n$ is continuous; $f$ is not.

**2. Integrals can jump.** For $n\ge2$ let $f_n$ be the triangular spike with $f_n(x)=n^2x$ on $[0,\frac1n]$, $f_n(x)=2n-n^2x$ on $[\frac1n,\frac2n]$, and $f_n(x)=0$ afterwards. Each spike has height $n$ and area $1$. For fixed $x>0$ we have $f_n(x)=0$ as soon as $\frac2n<x$, and $f_n(0)=0$ always. So $f_n\to0$ pointwise, and yet
$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^1f_n=1\ne0=\int_0^1\lim_{n\to\infty}f_n.$$

**3. Derivatives need not converge.** Let $f_n(x)=\frac{\sin nx}{n}$. Since $|f_n|\le\frac1n$, the limit is $0$, whose derivative is $0$. But $f_n'(x)=\cos nx$ equals $1$ at $x=0$ for every $n$, and at $x=\pi$ it equals $(-1)^n$, which does not converge.

### A diagnostic number

Given a candidate limit $f$, measure the largest gap
$$d_n=\sup_{x\in D}|f_n(x)-f(x)|.$$
For $x^n$ on $[0,1)$ we get $d_n=1$ for every $n$, and for the spikes $d_n=n$. These gaps do not shrink: some point $x_n$ always lags behind. When $d_n\to0$, the graph of $f_n$ lies in a thin tube around $f$ for large $n$, and this stronger convergence, called **uniform convergence**, repairs the first two problems. It is the subject of next week. The third example is subtler: there $d_n=\frac1n\to0$, yet derivatives still misbehave, because what must be controlled is $f_n'$ itself.

@example
title: A continuous limit that is still not uniform
problem: Find the pointwise limit of $f_n(x)=\dfrac{nx}{1+n^2x^2}$ on $[0,1]$, and show that the largest gap $d_n$ does not tend to $0$.
step: Fix $x=0$: $f_n(0)=0$ for every $n$, so the limit at $0$ is $0$.
step: Fix $x>0$: divide top and bottom by $n^2$ to get $f_n(x)=\dfrac{x/n}{1/n^2+x^2}$. As $n\to\infty$ this tends to $\dfrac{0}{x^2}=0$.
step: So $f_n\to f\equiv0$ pointwise on $[0,1]$. The limit is a perfectly nice continuous function.
step: Look at the point that moves with $n$: $x_n=\frac1n$. Then $f_n(x_n)=\dfrac{1}{1+1}=\dfrac12$ for every $n$.
step: Hence $d_n\ge\frac12$ for all $n$, and $d_n\not\to0$. Each graph has a hump of height $\frac12$ that slides toward $0$ without shrinking: pointwise convergence, but no tube around $f$.

@mistakes
- Letting $x$ vary with $n$ while computing the pointwise limit. Fix $x$ first, then let $n\to\infty$. (Moving points are for the diagnostic $d_n$.)
- Assuming that a pointwise limit of continuous functions is continuous.
- Assuming $\lim\int f_n=\int\lim f_n$ just because the limit function exists.

@practice num
prompt: Let $f_n(x)=nxe^{-nx^2}$ on $[0,1]$. The pointwise limit is $0$. Find $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^1f_n(x)\,dx$.
answer: 1/2
check: quad(lambda x: 60*x*exp(-60*x*x), 0, 1)
hint: Substitute $u=nx^2$, so that $du=2nx\,dx$.
solution: With $u=nx^2$, $\int_0^1nxe^{-nx^2}\,dx=\frac12\int_0^ne^{-u}\,du=\frac12\left(1-e^{-n}\right)\to\frac12$. The integral of the pointwise limit is $0$, so $\lim\int f_n\ne\int\lim f_n$.

@practice ms
prompt: Select all true statements.
choice*: A pointwise limit of continuous functions can be discontinuous.
choice: If each $f_n$ is continuous on $[0,1]$ and $f_n\to f$ pointwise, then $f$ is continuous.
choice*: It can happen that $f_n\to f$ pointwise on $[a,b]$ while $\int_a^bf_n\not\to\int_a^bf$.
choice: If $f_n\to f$ pointwise and every $f_n$ is differentiable, then $f_n'\to f'$ pointwise.
choice*: If a sequence of functions converges pointwise on $D$, the limit function is unique.
hint: Recall the examples $x^n$, the spikes, and $\frac{\sin nx}n$.
solution: $x^n$ on $[0,1]$ gives a discontinuous limit, and the spikes give $\int f_n=1\not\to0$. The statement that the pointwise limit of continuous functions is continuous and the statement that $f_n'\to f'$ are false for exactly these examples and for $\frac{\sin nx}n$. The limit is unique because, at each $x$, a numerical sequence has at most one limit.

@practice self
prompt: Let $f_n(x)=\dfrac{x^n}{1+x^n}$ for $x\ge0$. Find the pointwise limit function $f$ on $[0,\infty)$, and explain why $f$ is not continuous even though every $f_n$ is.
hint: Treat three cases separately: $0\le x<1$, $x=1$ and $x>1$.
hint: For $x>1$ rewrite $f_n(x)=\frac1{1+x^{-n}}$.
solution: If $0\le x<1$, then $x^n\to0$, so $f_n(x)\to\frac0{1+0}=0$. If $x=1$, then $f_n(1)=\frac12$ for every $n$. If $x>1$, then $x^{-n}\to0$ and $f_n(x)=\frac1{1+x^{-n}}\to1$. So $f(x)=0$ on $[0,1)$, $f(1)=\frac12$, and $f(x)=1$ on $(1,\infty)$. Each $f_n$ is continuous as a quotient of continuous functions with nonzero denominator. But $f$ jumps at $x=1$: the left-hand values are $0$ and the right-hand values are $1$, so $f$ is not continuous.

@quiz num
prompt: Let $f_n(x)=\dfrac{x}{1+nx}$ on $[0,1]$. It converges pointwise to $0$. Compute $d_{19}=\sup_{x\in[0,1]}|f_{19}(x)|$.
answer: 1/20
check: max(x/(1 + 19*x) for x in np.linspace(0, 1, 100001))
hint: Is $f_{19}$ increasing or decreasing in $x$? Compare $\frac{x}{1+19x}=\frac1{19+1/x}$ for different $x$.
solution: $f_{19}(x)=\frac{x}{1+19x}=\frac1{19+1/x}$ is increasing in $x$ on $(0,1]$, so the supremum is at $x=1$: $f_{19}(1)=\frac1{20}$.

@quiz mc
prompt: Which sequence satisfies $\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^1f_n\ne\int_0^1\lim_{n\to\infty}f_n$ (where the pointwise limit exists)?
choice: $f_n(x)=x^n$
choice: $f_n(x)=\dfrac xn$
choice*: $f_n(x)=n$ for $0<x<\frac1n$ and $f_n(x)=0$ otherwise
choice: $f_n(x)=\dfrac{\sin nx}n$
hint: For each choice compute $\int_0^1f_n$ and the integral of the pointwise limit.
solution: For $x^n$ the integral is $\frac1{n+1}\to0$ and the limit function is $0$ except at $x=1$, with integral $0$. For $\frac xn$ both sides are $0$. For $\frac{\sin nx}n$ the integral is $\frac{1-\cos n}{n^2}\to0$ and the limit is $0$. The rectangles have $\int_0^1f_n=n\cdot\frac1n=1$ for all $n$, but they tend to $0$ pointwise, so the limit of the integrals is $1$ while the integral of the limit is $0$.
