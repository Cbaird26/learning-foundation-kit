@article
A **series** $\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_k$ is an infinite sum made precise through its **partial sums** $s_n=a_1+a_2+\dots+a_n$. The series converges to $S$ when the sequence $(s_n)$ converges to $S$. Everything you proved about sequences now applies to $(s_n)$.

### Geometric series and the term test

For $|r|<1$ we have $\sum_{k=0}^\infty r^k=\frac1{1-r}$, because $s_n=\frac{1-r^{n+1}}{1-r}$. If $|r|\ge1$ the terms do not shrink and the series diverges.

**Term test.** If $\sum a_k$ converges, then $a_k\to0$. Indeed $a_n=s_n-s_{n-1}\to S-S=0$. The converse is false. The **harmonic series** $\sum\frac1k$ diverges even though $\frac1k\to0$: group the terms as $\frac12$, then $\frac13+\frac14\ge\frac12$, then $\frac15+\dots+\frac18\ge\frac12$, and so on, which gives $s_{2^m}\ge1+\frac m2$.

### Series with nonnegative terms

If every $a_k\ge0$ the partial sums increase, so by monotone convergence the series converges **if and only if its partial sums are bounded**. This is the engine behind three tools:

- **Comparison.** If $0\le a_k\le b_k$ and $\sum b_k$ converges, then $\sum a_k$ converges. Equivalently, if $\sum a_k$ diverges, so does $\sum b_k$.
- **$p$-series.** $\sum\frac1{k^p}$ converges exactly when $p>1$.
- **Ratio test.** If $\left|\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}\right|\to\rho$, the series converges absolutely when $\rho<1$ and diverges when $\rho>1$. When $\rho=1$ the test says nothing: $\sum\frac1k$ and $\sum\frac1{k^2}$ both give $\rho=1$, yet one diverges and the other converges.

### Absolute versus conditional convergence

A series is **absolutely convergent** if $\sum|a_k|$ converges. Absolute convergence implies convergence. The Cauchy criterion for series asks that blocks $|a_{m+1}+\dots+a_n|$ become small, and the triangle inequality bounds such a block by $|a_{m+1}|+\dots+|a_n|$, which is small when $\sum|a_k|$ converges.

A series that converges but does not converge absolutely is **conditionally convergent**. The standard source is the **alternating series test**: if $b_k\ge0$ decreases to $0$, then $\sum(-1)^{k+1}b_k$ converges, and stopping after $n$ terms makes an error of size at most $b_{n+1}$. So $1-\frac12+\frac13-\cdots$ converges, but only conditionally, since its absolute values form the harmonic series.

The distinction has teeth. Terms of an absolutely convergent series may be rearranged without changing the sum. Rearranging a conditionally convergent series can change the sum, or make it diverge.

@example
title: The ratio test in action
problem: Decide whether $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{k}{2^k}$ converges.
step: All terms are positive, so absolute convergence and convergence coincide. Write $a_k=\frac{k}{2^k}$ and form the ratio $\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}=\frac{(k+1)/2^{k+1}}{k/2^k}$.
step: Simplify: $\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}=\frac{k+1}{2^{k+1}}\cdot\frac{2^k}{k}=\frac{k+1}{2k}=\frac12\left(1+\frac1k\right)$.
step: As $k\to\infty$ the ratio tends to $\rho=\frac12$.
step: Since $\rho<1$, the ratio test says the series converges absolutely.
step: Sanity check: $s_{10}=\frac12+\frac12+\frac38+\dots+\frac{10}{1024}=1.98828\ldots$, close to the exact sum $2$.

@mistakes
- Concluding that a series converges because $a_k\to0$. The term test can only prove divergence.
- Reading $\rho=1$ in the ratio test as "diverges" or "converges". It tells you nothing, so switch to comparison or another test.
- Using comparison backwards: a series smaller than a divergent series can still converge, and a series larger than a convergent series can still diverge.

@practice self
prompt: Prove that the harmonic series $\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1k$ diverges.
hint: Look at the partial sums $s_{2^m}$ and group the terms in blocks that double in length.
hint: Show that each block $\frac1{2^{j-1}+1}+\dots+\frac1{2^j}$ is at least $\frac12$. Then use that a convergent sequence is bounded.
solution: For $m\ge1$, write $s_{2^m}=1+\frac12+\left(\frac13+\frac14\right)+\dots+\left(\frac1{2^{m-1}+1}+\dots+\frac1{2^m}\right)$. The $j$-th block has $2^{j-1}$ terms, each at least $\frac1{2^j}$, so each block is at least $\frac12$. Hence $s_{2^m}\ge1+\frac m2$, which is unbounded. A convergent sequence is bounded, so $(s_n)$ does not converge, and the series diverges.
rubric: Groups the terms into blocks of lengths $1,2,4,\dots$ (or an equivalent scheme).
rubric: Proves each block is at least $\frac12$ by bounding every term in the block below by its last term.
rubric: Obtains $s_{2^m}\ge1+\frac m2$ and concludes the partial sums are unbounded.
rubric: Connects unboundedness to divergence (convergent sequences are bounded).

@quiz num
prompt: Find $\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{3^k+2^k}{6^k}$. Give an exact value.
answer: 7/2
check: nsum(lambda k: (3**k + 2**k)/6**k, 0, 150)
hint: Split the fraction into two geometric series.
hint: $\frac{3^k}{6^k}=\left(\frac12\right)^k$, and $\frac{2^k}{6^k}$ is also a power of a single ratio.
solution: The series splits into $\sum\left(\frac12\right)^k+\sum\left(\frac13\right)^k$, both starting at $k=0$. Their sums are $\frac1{1-1/2}=2$ and $\frac1{1-1/3}=\frac32$. Total: $2+\frac32=\frac72$. Splitting is legal because both series converge.
