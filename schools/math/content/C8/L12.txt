@article
Last week pointwise convergence let us down: continuity, integrals and derivatives all failed to pass to the limit. A stronger notion repairs two of the three and tells us exactly what to demand for the third.

### Uniform convergence

$f_n\to f$ **uniformly** on $D$ if
$$\forall\varepsilon>0\ \ \exists N\ \ \forall n\ge N\ \ \forall x\in D:\quad |f_n(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon.$$
In pointwise convergence the quantifier $\forall x$ comes first, so $N$ may depend on $x$. Here a single $N$ serves every $x$ at once. Equivalently,
$$d_n=\sup_{x\in D}|f_n(x)-f(x)|\to0.$$
Geometrically, for large $n$ the whole graph of $f_n$ lies in an $\varepsilon$-tube around the graph of $f$. Uniform convergence implies pointwise convergence, but not conversely.

**Testing.** Find the pointwise limit $f$ first. Then compute $d_n$, usually by calculus, or exhibit points $x_n$ with $|f_n(x_n)-f(x_n)|\not\to0$ to show failure.

### Theorem 1: continuity passes to uniform limits

If each $f_n$ is continuous on $D$ and $f_n\to f$ uniformly, then $f$ is continuous.

*Proof.* Fix $c\in D$ and $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $n$ with $d_n<\frac\varepsilon3$, then $\delta>0$ such that $|x-c|<\delta$ implies $|f_n(x)-f_n(c)|<\frac\varepsilon3$. For such $x$,
$$|f(x)-f(c)|\le|f(x)-f_n(x)|+|f_n(x)-f_n(c)|+|f_n(c)-f(c)|<\varepsilon.\ \square$$
In particular, $x^n$ cannot converge uniformly on $[0,1]$, because its pointwise limit is discontinuous.

### Theorem 2: integrals pass to uniform limits

If each $f_n$ is Riemann integrable on $[a,b]$ and $f_n\to f$ uniformly, then $f$ is integrable and $\int_a^bf_n\to\int_a^bf$. The key estimate is
$$\left|\int_a^b(f_n-f)\right|\le(b-a)\,d_n.$$

### Theorem 3: derivatives need more

Suppose each $f_n$ is differentiable on $[a,b]$, $f_n(x_0)$ converges for some $x_0$, and the **derivatives** $f_n'$ converge uniformly to a function $g$. Then $f_n$ converges uniformly to a differentiable $f$, and $f'=g$.

Uniform convergence of $f_n$ alone is not enough: $f_n(x)=\frac{\sin nx}n\to0$ uniformly, yet $f_n'(0)=1$ for every $n$, while the limit $0$ has derivative $0$.

### The Weierstrass M-test

If $|g_k(x)|\le M_k$ for all $x\in D$ and $\sum M_k<\infty$, then $\sum g_k$ converges uniformly on $D$. For instance, $\sum\frac{\sin kx}{k^2}$ converges uniformly on $\mathbb R$ by comparison with $\sum\frac1{k^2}$, so its sum is continuous and can be integrated term by term.

### Summary

| Property of the limit | Pointwise convergence | Uniform convergence |
|---|---|---|
| $f$ is continuous | not guaranteed | guaranteed |
| $\int f_n\to\int f$ | not guaranteed | guaranteed |
| $f_n'\to f'$ | not guaranteed | needs $f_n'$ to converge uniformly |

@example
title: Uniform on a smaller set, not on the whole set
problem: Compare pointwise and uniform convergence of $f_n(x)=x^n$ on $[0,1)$ and on $[0,a]$ for a fixed $a<1$.
step: Pointwise: for each fixed $x\in[0,1)$, $x^n\to0$. So the pointwise limit on both sets is $f\equiv0$.
step: On $[0,1)$: $d_n=\sup_{0\le x<1}x^n=1$ for every $n$, because $x^n$ gets arbitrarily close to $1$ when $x$ is close to $1$. Since $d_n\not\to0$, the convergence is not uniform.
step: On $[0,a]$: $x^n$ is increasing in $x$, so $d_n=a^n$, which tends to $0$ because $0<a<1$. The convergence is uniform.
step: Explicit $N$: given $\varepsilon>0$, $a^n<\varepsilon$ as soon as $n>\frac{\ln\varepsilon}{\ln a}$, and this $N$ works for every $x\in[0,a]$ at once.
step: Moral: uniform convergence depends on the domain. Here it holds on every $[0,a]$ with $a<1$ but not on $[0,1)$.

@mistakes
- Concluding uniform convergence from pointwise convergence plus a continuous limit. The sliding hump $\frac{nx}{1+n^2x^2}$ has limit $0$ but $d_n=\frac12$.
- Computing $\sup|f_n-f|$ at a convenient fixed point. The supremum usually sits at a point $x_n$ that moves with $n$.
- Differentiating term by term because $f_n\to f$ uniformly. You need $f_n'$ to converge uniformly.

@practice mc
prompt: Which statement is equivalent to "$f_n\to f$ uniformly on $D$"?
choice*: $\sup_{x\in D}|f_n(x)-f(x)|\to0$ as $n\to\infty$.
choice: $f_n(x)\to f(x)$ for each $x\in D$.
choice: $f_n(x)\to f(x)$ for each $x\in D$, and $f$ is continuous.
choice: $\sup_{n}|f_n(x)-f(x)|\to0$ as $x$ varies over $D$.
hint: Uniform convergence means one $N$ that works for all $x$ at once.
solution: If $\sup_x|f_n(x)-f(x)|\to0$, then for any $\varepsilon$ there is a single $N$ with $|f_n(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N$ and all $x$. The two choices that only ask for $f_n(x)\to f(x)$ at each $x$ (one of them adding that $f$ is continuous) describe pointwise convergence (the sliding hump shows a continuous limit is not enough).

@practice num
prompt: On $[0,0.9]$ the functions $f_n(x)=x^n$ converge uniformly to $0$. Find the smallest $N$ such that $\sup_{x\in[0,0.9]}|f_n(x)|<0.001$ for all $n\ge N$.
answer: 66
check: min(n for n in range(1, 1000) if 0.9**n < 0.001)
hint: Since $x^n$ is increasing in $x$, the supremum is $0.9^n$. Solve $0.9^n<0.001$.
solution: $d_n=0.9^n$. We need $n>\frac{\ln0.001}{\ln0.9}\approx65.6$, so the smallest valid $N$ is $66$. Because $0.9^n$ decreases in $n$, every larger $n$ also works.

@practice ms
prompt: Select all true statements.
choice*: If $f_n\to f$ uniformly on $D$, then $f_n\to f$ pointwise on $D$.
choice: If $f_n\to f$ pointwise and $f_n$ and $f$ are all continuous on $[0,1]$, then the convergence is uniform.
choice*: If each $f_n$ is continuous on $D$ and $f_n\to f$ uniformly, then $f$ is continuous.
choice: If $f_n\to f$ uniformly and each $f_n$ is differentiable, then $f_n'\to f'$.
choice*: If $f_n\to f$ uniformly on $[a,b]$ and each $f_n$ is Riemann integrable, then $\int_a^bf_n\to\int_a^bf$.
hint: For each false statement look for a counterexample among $\frac{nx}{1+n^2x^2}$ and $\frac{\sin nx}n$.
solution: The statement that uniform convergence implies pointwise convergence follows from the definition, the statement about continuity of the limit is Theorem 1, and the statement about integrals is Theorem 2. The claim that pointwise convergence of continuous functions to a continuous limit is uniform fails for $\frac{nx}{1+n^2x^2}$ (all continuous, limit $0$, but $d_n=\frac12$). The claim that $f_n'\to f'$ fails for $\frac{\sin nx}n$.

@practice num
prompt: Each series $\sum_{k=0}^\infty\left(\frac x2\right)^k$ converges uniformly on $[0,1]$ by the M-test with $M_k=2^{-k}$, so it may be integrated term by term. Find $\displaystyle\int_0^1\sum_{k=0}^\infty\left(\frac x2\right)^kdx$. Give an exact value.
answer: 2*ln(2)
check: quad(lambda x: sum((x/2)**k for k in range(0, 100)), 0, 1)
hint: Integrate term by term, or sum the geometric series first and then integrate.
hint: The geometric sum is $\frac1{1-x/2}$. What is the integral of that from $0$ to $1$?
solution: The geometric series sums to $\frac1{1-x/2}=\frac2{2-x}$ for $x\in[0,1]$. Then $\int_0^1\frac2{2-x}\,dx=\bigl[-2\ln(2-x)\bigr]_0^1=-2\ln1+2\ln2=2\ln2$. Term by term: $\sum\frac1{2^k(k+1)}=2\ln2$ as well, which is legitimate because the convergence is uniform.

@practice self
prompt: Let $f_n(x)=\dfrac{x}{1+nx^2}$ on $\mathbb R$. (a) Find the pointwise limit $f$. (b) Compute $d_n=\sup_{x\in\mathbb R}|f_n(x)-f(x)|$ and decide whether the convergence is uniform. (c) Compare $f_n'(0)$ with $f'(0)$, and say what this shows about interchanging limits and derivatives.
hint: For (b) find where $|f_n(x)|$ is largest; compare with the place $x_n=\frac1{\sqrt n}$.
hint: For (c) compute $f_n'(x)=\frac{1-nx^2}{(1+nx^2)^2}$ and evaluate at $0$.
solution: (a) At $x=0$, $f_n(0)=0$. For $x\ne0$, $\frac{x}{1+nx^2}\to0$ as $n\to\infty$. So $f\equiv0$. (b) $|f_n(x)|=\frac{|x|}{1+nx^2}$ is largest at $|x|=\frac1{\sqrt n}$, where it equals $\frac{1/\sqrt n}{2}=\frac1{2\sqrt n}$. So $d_n=\frac1{2\sqrt n}\to0$, and the convergence is uniform on $\mathbb R$. (c) $f_n'(0)=1$ for every $n$, so $\lim f_n'(0)=1$, while $f'(0)=0$. Uniform convergence of $f_n$ did not give $\lim f_n'=(\lim f_n)'$; to interchange the limit and the derivative one needs the derivatives $f_n'$ to converge uniformly.
rubric: Part (a): finds the pointwise limit $f\equiv0$ by treating $x=0$ and $x\ne0$ (or by a single bound $|f_n(x)|\le\ldots$).
rubric: Part (b): locates the maximum of $|f_n|$ at $|x|=\frac1{\sqrt n}$, obtains $d_n=\frac1{2\sqrt n}$, and concludes uniform convergence since $d_n\to0$.
rubric: Part (c): computes $f_n'(0)=1$ and $f'(0)=0$ and states that they disagree.
rubric: Explicitly contrasts pointwise and uniform behavior: names which hypothesis (uniform convergence of $f_n'$) is missing for the derivative theorem.

@quiz num
prompt: Let $f_n(x)=nxe^{-nx}$ on $[0,\infty)$, which converges pointwise to $0$. Find $d_n=\sup_{x\ge0}|f_n(x)|$. (The value does not depend on $n$.)
answer: 1/e
check: max(5*x*np.exp(-5*x) for x in np.linspace(0, 10, 1000001))
hint: Differentiate $nxe^{-nx}$ and find the critical point.
hint: The maximum occurs at $x=\frac1n$.
solution: $f_n'(x)=ne^{-nx}(1-nx)=0$ at $x=\frac1n$, where $f_n$ changes from increasing to decreasing. So $d_n=f_n\left(\frac1n\right)=e^{-1}$ for every $n$. Since $d_n\not\to0$, the convergence is pointwise but not uniform on $[0,\infty)$.

@quiz mc
prompt: A student argues: "$f_n(x)=\frac{\sin(n^2x)}n\to0$ uniformly on $\mathbb R$, so $f_n'(x)\to0'=0$." What is wrong with this argument?
choice*: Uniform convergence of $f_n$ does not control $f_n'$. Here $f_n'(x)=n\cos(n^2x)$, which equals $n$ at $x=0$ for every $n$.
choice: The convergence of $f_n$ is not uniform.
choice: The function $0$ has no derivative.
choice: The derivative of $\frac{\sin(n^2x)}n$ is $\frac{\cos(n^2x)}n$.
hint: Compute $f_n'$ explicitly and test it at $x=0$.
solution: The functions do converge uniformly, since $d_n=\sup\frac{|\sin(n^2x)|}n=\frac1n\to0$. But $f_n'(x)=n\cos(n^2x)$ does not tend to $0$; at $x=0$ it equals $n$. The derivative theorem requires $f_n'$, not $f_n$, to converge uniformly.
