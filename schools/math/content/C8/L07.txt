@article
Continuity at a point lets the tolerance $\delta$ depend on where you stand. That is a weak requirement: a function can be continuous everywhere yet keep getting steeper, so that no single $\delta$ works everywhere. **Uniform continuity** asks for one $\delta$ that serves the whole domain.

### The definition

$f:D\to\mathbb R$ is **uniformly continuous** on $D$ if
$$\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists\delta>0\ \forall x,y\in D:\quad |x-y|<\delta\ \Longrightarrow\ |f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon.$$
Continuity at every point reads $\forall\varepsilon\ \forall y\ \exists\delta\ \forall x\dots$ The only change is where $\exists\delta$ sits. In uniform continuity it comes before the points, so $\delta$ cannot depend on location. Uniform continuity implies continuity; the converse is false in general.

### Disproving it with sequences

The negation says: there is an $\varepsilon_0>0$ such that for every $\delta>0$ some $x,y\in D$ satisfy $|x-y|<\delta$ and $|f(x)-f(y)|\ge\varepsilon_0$. In sequence form:

**$f$ is not uniformly continuous on $D$ if there are sequences $x_n,y_n\in D$ with $|x_n-y_n|\to0$ but $|f(x_n)-f(y_n)|\ge\varepsilon_0$ for all $n$.**

Standard examples: $x^2$ on $\mathbb R$ (it steepens as $|x|$ grows), $\frac1x$ on $(0,1)$ (it steepens near $0$), and $\sin\frac1x$ on $(0,1)$ (it oscillates ever faster near $0$, although it is bounded).

### Proving it

The simplest sufficient condition is the **Lipschitz** condition: $|f(x)-f(y)|\le L|x-y|$ for all $x,y\in D$. Then $\delta=\frac\varepsilon L$ works for every $\varepsilon$. For example, $x^2$ is Lipschitz on $[0,3]$ with $L=6$, because $|x^2-y^2|=|x-y|\,|x+y|\le6|x-y|$.

Lipschitz is not necessary. The function $\sqrt x$ on $[0,1]$ is uniformly continuous, but its slope near $0$ is unbounded.

### The big theorem

**Theorem.** If $K$ is compact and $f:K\to\mathbb R$ is continuous, then $f$ is uniformly continuous on $K$.

*Proof.* Suppose not. Then there are $\varepsilon_0>0$ and points $x_n,y_n\in K$ with $|x_n-y_n|<\frac1n$ and $|f(x_n)-f(y_n)|\ge\varepsilon_0$. By compactness a subsequence $x_{n_j}\to x\in K$. Then also $y_{n_j}\to x$, since $|y_{n_j}-x|\le|y_{n_j}-x_{n_j}|+|x_{n_j}-x|\to0$. Continuity at $x$ gives $f(x_{n_j})\to f(x)$ and $f(y_{n_j})\to f(x)$, so $|f(x_{n_j})-f(y_{n_j})|\to0$, contradicting $\ge\varepsilon_0$. $\square$

Where was compactness used? To keep the sequence from escaping: it traps $x_n$ near a point of the domain. On $(0,1)$ or $\mathbb R$ the sequences are free to escape, which is exactly what the counterexamples do. This theorem will later show that continuous functions are Riemann integrable.

@example
title: Continuous but not uniformly continuous
problem: Show that $f(x)=x^2$ is not uniformly continuous on $\mathbb R$, but is uniformly continuous on $[0,3]$.
step: Choose sequences that stay close but drift to where $f$ is steep: $x_n=n+\frac1n$ and $y_n=n$. Then $|x_n-y_n|=\frac1n\to0$.
step: Compute the gap in values: $f(x_n)-f(y_n)=\left(n+\frac1n\right)^2-n^2=2+\frac1{n^2}\ge2$ for all $n$.
step: Take $\varepsilon_0=2$. For any $\delta>0$ pick $n$ with $\frac1n<\delta$; then $|x_n-y_n|<\delta$ but $|f(x_n)-f(y_n)|\ge\varepsilon_0$. So $f$ is not uniformly continuous on $\mathbb R$.
step: On $[0,3]$: $|x^2-y^2|=|x-y|\,|x+y|\le6|x-y|$. Given $\varepsilon>0$ take $\delta=\frac\varepsilon6$.
step: The domain made the difference. $[0,3]$ is compact, so the theorem guarantees uniform continuity; $\mathbb R$ is not.

@mistakes
- Putting $\delta$ before $\varepsilon$, or letting $\delta$ depend on $x$ in a proof of uniform continuity. In the definition $\delta$ depends on $\varepsilon$ alone.
- Believing "continuous and bounded implies uniformly continuous". The function $\sin\frac1x$ on $(0,1)$ is both and is not uniformly continuous.
- Using the compact-domain theorem on a domain that is not compact, such as $\frac1x$ on $(0,1]$ or $x^2$ on $\mathbb R$.

@practice num
prompt: Find the smallest constant $L$ such that $|x^3-y^3|\le L\,|x-y|$ for all $x,y\in[0,2]$. (Hint: $x^3-y^3=(x-y)(x^2+xy+y^2)$.)
answer: 12
check: max(abs(nderiv(lambda t: t**3, x)) for x in np.linspace(0, 2, 2001))
hint: After factoring, $L$ must be at least the supremum of $x^2+xy+y^2$ over $x,y\in[0,2]$.
hint: The expression $x^2+xy+y^2$ increases when either variable increases. Where on $[0,2]^2$ is it largest?
solution: $|x^3-y^3|=|x-y|\,(x^2+xy+y^2)$ and $x^2+xy+y^2\le4+4+4=12$ on $[0,2]^2$, so $L=12$ works. It cannot be smaller: take $x=2$ and $y$ close to $2$, for which the ratio $x^2+xy+y^2$ is close to $12$.

At $c=0.01$ the same $\varepsilon$ forces $\delta=\frac1{10100}$, so $\delta$ keeps shrinking as $c\to0$: no single $\delta$ works on $(0,1)$.

@practice self
prompt: Prove that $f(x)=\sqrt x$ is uniformly continuous on $[0,\infty)$, even though its slope is unbounded near $0$.
hint: Show that $|\sqrt x-\sqrt y|\le\sqrt{|x-y|}$ for all $x,y\ge0$. Assume $x\ge y$ and compare squares.

By symmetry $|\sqrt x-\sqrt y|\le\sqrt{|x-y|}$ for all $x,y\ge0$. Given $\varepsilon>0$ let $\delta=\varepsilon^2$. If $|x-y|<\delta$ then $|\sqrt x-\sqrt y|\le\sqrt{|x-y|}<\sqrt\delta=\varepsilon$. The $\delta$ depends only on $\varepsilon$.

@quiz num
prompt: Let $f(x)=\sin\frac1x$ on $(0,1)$ and, for $n\in\mathbb N$, let $x_n=\dfrac1{2n\pi+\pi/2}$ and $y_n=\dfrac1{2n\pi-\pi/2}$. Both sequences tend to $0$ and $|x_n-y_n|\to0$. Find the common value of $|f(x_n)-f(y_n)|$ for every $n$.
answer: 2
check: abs(sin(1/(1/(6*pi + pi/2))) - sin(1/(1/(6*pi - pi/2))))
hint: Compute $\frac1{x_n}$ and $\frac1{y_n}$ first, then evaluate the sine.
solution: $f(x_n)=\sin\left(2n\pi+\frac\pi2\right)=1$ and $f(y_n)=\sin\left(2n\pi-\frac\pi2\right)=-1$. So $|f(x_n)-f(y_n)|=2$ for every $n$. Taking $\varepsilon_0=2$ shows $\sin\frac1x$ is not uniformly continuous on $(0,1)$.
