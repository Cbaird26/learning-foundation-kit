@article
A **sequence** is a function from $\mathbb N$ to $\mathbb R$, written $(a_n)$. It **converges** to $L$ when its terms eventually stay as close to $L$ as anyone demands.

### The definition

$a_n\to L$ means: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is an $N$ such that
$$n\ge N\ \Longrightarrow\ |a_n-L|<\varepsilon.$$
Think of a challenge game. The challenger picks $\varepsilon$ first. You answer with an $N$, which may depend on $\varepsilon$. Smaller $\varepsilon$ usually forces a larger $N$.

### How a proof is written

Do scratch work first: simplify $|a_n-L|$ and look for a simpler upper bound that you can make small. Then write the proof forwards: "Let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $N$ so that... Then for $n\ge N$..." The worked example below follows this pattern.

### Basic facts

- **Uniqueness.** A sequence has at most one limit.
- **Boundedness.** Every convergent sequence is bounded. The converse is false: $(-1)^n$ is bounded but does not converge.
- **Limit laws.** If $a_n\to a$ and $b_n\to b$, then $a_n+b_n\to a+b$ and $a_nb_n\to ab$, and $a_n/b_n\to a/b$ when $b\ne0$ (then $b_n\ne0$ for all large $n$).
- **Squeeze.** If $x_n\le a_n\le y_n$ and $x_n\to L$ and $y_n\to L$, then $a_n\to L$.

### Monotone convergence

If $(a_n)$ is increasing and bounded above, then $a_n\to\sup\{a_n\}$. Let $s$ be the supremum and let $\varepsilon>0$. Since $s-\varepsilon$ is not an upper bound, some $a_N>s-\varepsilon$, and monotonicity keeps every later term in $(s-\varepsilon,s]$. This is completeness at work, and it proves convergence without knowing the limit in advance.

Suppose a recursive sequence like $a_{n+1}=\frac12\left(a_n+\frac2{a_n}\right)$ is known to converge to some $L>0$. Letting $n\to\infty$ in the recursion gives $L=\frac12\left(L+\frac2L\right)$, so $L^2=2$. The limit-solving step is only legitimate after convergence is proved.

### Subsequences and Cauchy sequences

A **subsequence** keeps some terms in order: $a_{n_1},a_{n_2},\dots$ with $n_1<n_2<\cdots$.

**Bolzano–Weierstrass:** every bounded sequence of reals has a convergent subsequence.

A sequence is **Cauchy** if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $N$ with $m,n\ge N\Rightarrow|a_m-a_n|<\varepsilon$. In $\mathbb R$, a sequence is Cauchy exactly when it converges. This is another face of completeness, and it fails in $\mathbb Q$: decimal truncations of $\sqrt2$ are Cauchy in $\mathbb Q$ but have no limit there. Its practical advantage is that you can test it without knowing the limit.

Beware: it is not enough that $|a_{n+1}-a_n|\to0$. The sums $1+\frac12+\dots+\frac1n$ have this property, yet they are not Cauchy and do not converge.

@example
title: Convergence from the definition
problem: Prove from the definition that $\dfrac{3n+1}{n+2}\to3$.
step: Scratch work. Compute $\left|\frac{3n+1}{n+2}-3\right|=\left|\frac{3n+1-3n-6}{n+2}\right|=\frac5{n+2}$.
step: Simplify the bound. Since $n+2>n$, we have $\frac5{n+2}<\frac5n$, and $\frac5n$ is easy to make small.
step: Proof. Let $\varepsilon>0$. By the Archimedean property choose $N\in\mathbb N$ with $N>\frac5\varepsilon$.
step: For every $n\ge N$: $\left|\frac{3n+1}{n+2}-3\right|=\frac5{n+2}<\frac5n\le\frac5N<\varepsilon$.
step: Since $\varepsilon>0$ was arbitrary, the definition holds and $\frac{3n+1}{n+2}\to3$.

@mistakes
- Choosing $N$ before $\varepsilon$, as in "let $N=100$". The $N$ must be produced from the given $\varepsilon$.
- Computing a few terms and calling it a proof. Numbers suggest a limit; only the definition proves one.
- Believing $|a_{n+1}-a_n|\to0$ implies convergence. The harmonic sums are the standard counterexample.

@practice num
prompt: For $a_n=\dfrac{2n^2+1}{n^2+3}$ and limit $2$, find the smallest $N$ such that $|a_n-2|<0.01$ for all $n\ge N$.
answer: 23
check: min(n for n in range(1, 10**5) if abs((2*n**2 + 1)/(n**2 + 3) - 2) < 0.01)
hint: Compute $a_n-2$ over the common denominator $n^2+3$.
hint: You should find $|a_n-2|=\frac5{n^2+3}$. Solve $\frac5{n^2+3}<0.01$.
solution: $a_n-2=\frac{2n^2+1-2n^2-6}{n^2+3}=\frac{-5}{n^2+3}$. We need $n^2+3>500$, i.e. $n^2>497$. Since $22^2=484$ and $23^2=529$, the smallest $N$ is $23$. The terms $|a_n-2|$ decrease, so all later $n$ also work.

@practice mc
prompt: Which statement is the correct negation of "$a_n\to L$"?
choice*: There is an $\varepsilon>0$ such that for every $N$ there is some $n\ge N$ with $|a_n-L|\ge\varepsilon$.
choice: For every $\varepsilon>0$ there is an $N$ such that $|a_n-L|\ge\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N$.
choice: There is an $\varepsilon>0$ and an $N$ such that $|a_n-L|\ge\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N$.
choice: For every $\varepsilon>0$ and every $N$ there is $n\ge N$ with $|a_n-L|\ge\varepsilon$.
hint: Negating "for every, there exists, for all" turns it into "there exists, for every, there exists".
solution: Negate each quantifier in turn and negate the final inequality: $\exists\varepsilon\,\forall N\,\exists n\ge N$ with $|a_n-L|\ge\varepsilon$. The choice "there is an $\varepsilon>0$ and an $N$ such that $|a_n-L|\ge\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N$" is stronger: it says the terms are eventually always far from $L$, which is not needed to fail convergence (consider $(-1)^n$ and $L=1$).

prompt: Prove from the definition that $\dfrac{2n+3}{3n-1}\to\dfrac23$.
hint: Compute $\frac{2n+3}{3n-1}-\frac23$ over the common denominator $3(3n-1)$.
hint: The numerator simplifies to a constant. Then bound the denominator from below, for instance $3n-1\ge2n$ when $n\ge1$.
solution: Scratch work: $\frac{2n+3}{3n-1}-\frac23=\frac{3(2n+3)-2(3n-1)}{3(3n-1)}=\frac{11}{3(3n-1)}$. For $n\ge1$ we have $3n-1\ge2n$, so $\left|\frac{2n+3}{3n-1}-\frac23\right|\le\frac{11}{6n}$. Proof: let $\varepsilon>0$ and choose $N>\frac{11}{6\varepsilon}$ by the Archimedean property. For $n\ge N$, the difference is at most $\frac{11}{6n}\le\frac{11}{6N}<\varepsilon$. Hence the sequence converges to $\frac23$.

@quiz num
prompt: Let $a_1=1$ and $a_{n+1}=\frac12\left(a_n+\frac6{a_n}\right)$. The sequence converges to a positive limit. Find that limit exactly.
answer: sqrt(6)
check: (lambda g: g(g, 60, 1.0))(lambda g, k, a: a if k == 0 else g(g, k - 1, (a + 6/a)/2))
hint: Assume $a_n\to L>0$ and let $n\to\infty$ on both sides of the recursion.
hint: Solve $L=\frac12\left(L+\frac6L\right)$ for the positive root.
solution: Taking limits gives $L=\frac12\left(L+\frac6L\right)$, so $2L=L+\frac6L$, i.e. $L=\frac6L$ and $L^2=6$. The positive solution is $L=\sqrt6$.

@quiz mc
prompt: Which of these sequences is NOT a Cauchy sequence?
choice: $a_n=\dfrac{n}{n+1}$
choice*: $a_n=1+\dfrac12+\dfrac13+\cdots+\dfrac1n$
choice: $a_n=2^{-n}$
choice: $a_n=\dfrac{(-1)^n}{n}$
hint: Every one of these has $|a_{n+1}-a_n|\to0$. Compare $a_{2n}$ with $a_n$ instead.
solution: The other three converge ($\frac n{n+1}\to1$, while $2^{-n}$ and $\frac{(-1)^n}n$ tend to $0$), so they are Cauchy. For the harmonic sums, $a_{2n}-a_n=\frac1{n+1}+\cdots+\frac1{2n}\ge n\cdot\frac1{2n}=\frac12$ for every $n$, so no $N$ works for $\varepsilon=\frac12$.
