@article
A closed form is a candidate for a derivative; an exact form is a derivative. On $\mathbb{R}^n$ every closed form of positive degree is exact (the Poincaré lemma), but on the punctured plane the form $d\theta$ is closed and not exact. The failure measures the shape of the space: this is de Rham cohomology.

### Definition and first computations

Let $Z^k(M)=\ker(d\colon\Omega^k\to\Omega^{k+1})$ and $B^k(M)=d\Omega^{k-1}\subseteq Z^k$ (valid since $d^2=0$). The **de Rham cohomology** is $H^k(M)=Z^k/B^k$. A smooth map $F\colon M\to N$ induces $F^*\colon H^k(N)\to H^k(M)$, well defined because $F^*d=dF^*$, with $(G\circ F)^*=F^*G^*$. Since exact $0$-forms do not exist, $H^0(M)$ is the space of locally constant functions: $\mathbb{R}^{c}$ if $M$ has $c$ components.

**Theorem 1 (homotopy invariance; stated without proof).** If $F,G\colon M\to N$ are smoothly homotopic, then $F^*=G^*$ on $H^k(N)$. (The proof builds a homotopy operator $K$ with $G^*-F^*=dK+Kd$ by integrating along the homotopy.) Hence homotopy equivalent manifolds have isomorphic cohomology, and a contractible manifold has $H^k=0$ for $k>0$.

**Lemma 2 (Poincaré lemma for 1-forms).** If $U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$ is open and star-shaped about $0$, every closed 1-form on $U$ is exact.

*Proof.* Let $\omega=\sum\omega_i\,dx^i$ with $\partial_j\omega_i=\partial_i\omega_j$ and set $f(x)=\int_0^1\sum_i\omega_i(tx)\,x_i\,dt$. Differentiating under the integral and using closedness, $\partial_jf(x)=\int_0^1\big[\omega_j(tx)+\sum_it\,x_i\,\partial_j\omega_i(tx)\big]dt=\int_0^1\big[\omega_j(tx)+t\sum_ix_i\,\partial_i\omega_j(tx)\big]dt=\int_0^1\frac{d}{dt}\big(t\,\omega_j(tx)\big)dt=\omega_j(x)$. So $df=\omega$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 3.** $H^1(S^1)\cong\mathbb{R}$ via $[\omega]\mapsto\int_{S^1}\omega$ (counterclockwise orientation), and $H^k(S^1)=0$ for $k\ge2$.

*Proof.* Every 1-form on a $1$-manifold is closed, and $H^k=0$ for $k\ge2$ because there are no nonzero forms. The map $I[\omega]=\int_{S^1}\omega$ is well defined and linear: for $\omega=df$, $\int df=0$ by Stokes (no boundary). It is onto: $\int_{S^1}(x\,dy-y\,dx)=2\pi$. It is injective: suppose $\int_{S^1}\omega=0$. Let $p\colon\mathbb{R}\to S^1$, $p(t)=(\cos t,\sin t)$, so $p^*\omega=g(t)\,dt$ with $g$ smooth and $2\pi$-periodic and $\int_0^{2\pi}g\,dt=\int_{S^1}\omega=0$. Then $h(t)=\int_0^tg$ is $2\pi$-periodic ($h(t+2\pi)-h(t)=\int_t^{t+2\pi}g=\int_0^{2\pi}g=0$), so $h=f\circ p$ for a function $f$ on $S^1$, smooth because $p$ is a local diffeomorphism. Then $p^*df=dh=g\,dt=p^*\omega$, and since $p$ is a surjective local diffeomorphism, $df=\omega$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 4.** $H^0(\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{0\})=\mathbb{R}$, $H^1(\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{0\})\cong\mathbb{R}$, generated by $\dfrac{-y\,dx+x\,dy}{x^2+y^2}$, and $H^k=0$ for $k\ge2$.

*Proof.* Let $\iota\colon S^1\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{0\}$ and $r(x)=x/|x|$. Then $r\circ\iota=\mathrm{id}$, and $\iota\circ r\simeq\mathrm{id}$ by the smooth homotopy $H(x,s)=(1-s)x+s\,x/|x|$, which never vanishes because it is a positive multiple of $x$. By Theorem 1, $\iota^*$ and $r^*$ are inverse isomorphisms $H^k(\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{0\})\cong H^k(S^1)$. The generator is $r^*$ of the class with integral $2\pi$, and $\iota^*\frac{-y\,dx+x\,dy}{x^2+y^2}=x\,dy-y\,dx$ on $S^1$ has integral $2\pi$. Compare with Stokes's theorem (previous lesson), which already shows this form is not exact. $\blacksquare$

Also (stated without proof): $H^k(S^n)=\mathbb{R}$ for $k=0,n$ and $0$ otherwise ($n\ge1$), and $H^n(M)\cong\mathbb{R}$ for a compact connected oriented $n$-manifold, the isomorphism being integration.

### Riemannian metrics

A **Riemannian metric** on $M$ is a smooth choice of an inner product $g_p$ on each $T_pM$: in a chart $g=\sum g_{ij}\,dx^i\otimes dx^j$ with $(g_{ij})$ smooth, symmetric and positive definite.

**Theorem 5.** Every smooth manifold admits a Riemannian metric.

*Proof.* Cover $M$ by chart domains $U_\alpha$ with coordinates $x_\alpha$ and let $g_\alpha=\sum_i(dx_\alpha^i)^2$, a metric on $U_\alpha$. With a subordinate partition of unity put $g=\sum_\alpha\rho_\alpha g_\alpha$, locally finite, hence smooth. At each point $g_p(v,v)=\sum_\alpha\rho_\alpha(p)\,g_\alpha(v,v)\ge0$, with equality only if every term with $\rho_\alpha(p)>0$ vanishes, that is $v=0$ (at least one $\rho_\alpha(p)>0$). It is symmetric bilinear. $\blacksquare$

If $F\colon M\to(N,h)$ is an **immersion**, then $F^*h(v,w)=h(dF\,v,dF\,w)$ is a Riemannian metric on $M$ (positive definiteness is exactly the injectivity of $dF$); this is the **induced metric** on a submanifold. For a parametrization with Jacobian $J=DF$, in coordinates $G=J^\top J$ (the **first fundamental form**). The **length** of a curve is $\int|\gamma'|_g\,dt$, and on an oriented chart the **volume form** is $d\mathrm{vol}_g=\sqrt{\det(g_{ij})}\,dx^1\wedge\dots\wedge dx^n$ (positive, and independent of the oriented chart because $\det g_{ij}$ transforms by $(\det D\varphi)^2$).

For $F(\theta,\phi)=(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta)$ one finds $G=\mathrm{diag}(1,\sin^2\theta)$, $d\mathrm{vol}=\sin\theta\,d\theta\wedge d\phi$ and $\operatorname{area}(S^2)=4\pi$. Numerically, the area of the paraboloid $z=x^2+y^2$ over the unit disc (first example) can be checked by the midpoint rule:

```python
from math import pi, sqrt


def paraboloid_area(n):
    """Midpoint rule in r for the area of z = x^2 + y^2 over the unit disc."""
    h = 1.0 / n
    s = 0.0
    for k in range(n):
        r = (k + 0.5) * h
        s += sqrt(1 + 4 * r * r) * r * h
    return 2 * pi * s


print(abs(paraboloid_area(1000) - pi * (5 * sqrt(5) - 1) / 6) < 1e-5)
```

This prints `True`; a numerical agreement is not a proof of the formula, which comes from integrating $\sqrt{1+4r^2}\,r$ exactly.

title: Area of a paraboloid from the induced metric
problem: Compute the area of the graph $z=x^2+y^2$ over the unit disc $\{x^2+y^2\le1\}$ with the metric induced from $\mathbb{R}^3$.
step: Parametrize by $F(x,y)=(x,y,x^2+y^2)$, with Jacobian $J=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\\2x&2y\end{pmatrix}$.
step: First fundamental form: $G=J^\top J=\begin{pmatrix}1+4x^2&4xy\\4xy&1+4y^2\end{pmatrix}$, with $\det G=(1+4x^2)(1+4y^2)-16x^2y^2=1+4x^2+4y^2$.
step: Hence $d\mathrm{vol}=\sqrt{1+4x^2+4y^2}\,dx\wedge dy$ and, in polar coordinates, $\text{area}=\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^1\sqrt{1+4r^2}\,r\,dr\,d\theta$.
step: With $u=1+4r^2$, $\int_0^1\sqrt{1+4r^2}\,r\,dr=\frac18\cdot\frac23\big[u^{3/2}\big]_1^5=\frac{5\sqrt5-1}{12}$. So the area is $2\pi\cdot\frac{5\sqrt5-1}{12}=\frac{\pi(5\sqrt5-1)}{6}\approx5.330$.

@example
title: The induced metric and area of the torus
problem: For $F(u,v)=\big((R+r\cos u)\cos v,\ (R+r\cos u)\sin v,\ r\sin u\big)$ with $R>r>0$, compute the induced metric and the area of the torus.
step: The partial derivatives are $F_u=(-r\sin u\cos v,\ -r\sin u\sin v,\ r\cos u)$ and $F_v=(-(R+r\cos u)\sin v,\ (R+r\cos u)\cos v,\ 0)$.
step: $G_{uu}=r^2\sin^2u+r^2\cos^2u=r^2$, $G_{vv}=(R+r\cos u)^2$ and $G_{uv}=F_u\cdot F_v=r(R+r\cos u)\sin u(\sin v\cos v-\sin v\cos v)=0$. So $G=\mathrm{diag}\big(r^2,(R+r\cos u)^2\big)$.
step: Then $d\mathrm{vol}=r(R+r\cos u)\,du\wedge dv$, positive since $R>r$.
step: $\text{area}=\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{2\pi}r(R+r\cos u)\,du\,dv=2\pi\cdot r\cdot2\pi R=4\pi^2Rr$, since $\int_0^{2\pi}\cos u\,du=0$.
answer: $G=\mathrm{diag}(r^2,(R+r\cos u)^2)$ and the area is $4\pi^2Rr$.
@mistakes
- Believing that closed forms are exact on every manifold: the Poincaré lemma needs a star-shaped (or contractible) domain, and $d\theta$ on $S^1$ or $\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{0\}$ is closed and not exact.
- Computing $H^1(\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{0\})$ by the Poincaré lemma on the whole domain: the domain is not star-shaped, and a form such as $\frac{-y\,dx+x\,dy}{x^2+y^2}$ is exact on each simply connected piece but not globally.
- Using $JJ^\top$ instead of $J^\top J$ for the induced metric: $J^\top J$ is the $m\times m$ Gram matrix on the parameter space; $JJ^\top$ has the wrong size and rank.
- Forgetting the square root in the volume form $\sqrt{\det g}$ (the Jacobian of a change of variables enters $\det g$ squared).

model:
```python
from fractions import Fraction


def induced_metric(J):
    """Gram matrix J^T J of the columns of J, in exact arithmetic."""
    rows = len(J)
    cols = len(J[0])
    return [[sum(Fraction(J[k][i]) * Fraction(J[k][j]) for k in range(rows))
             for j in range(cols)] for i in range(cols)]
```
hint: Entry $(i,j)$ of $G$ is the dot product of column $i$ of $J$ with column $j$.
hint: The result has one row and one column per column of $J$, not per row.
solution: $G_{ij}=\sum_kJ_{ki}J_{kj}$, the inner product of the $i$th and $j$th partial-derivative vectors. Its size is the number of columns (the dimension of the parameter space), so the version $JJ^\top$ fails the shape tests (including the $2\times3$ example, where $J^\top J$ is a rank-$2$ matrix of size $3\times3$). Evaluating $G$ at one point is the pointwise computation; the area is obtained by integrating $\sqrt{\det G}$.
@practice self
prompt: Let $p\ne q$ be two points of $\mathbb{R}^2$ and $M=\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{p,q\}$. Let $\omega_p=\dfrac{-(y-p_2)\,dx+(x-p_1)\,dy}{(x-p_1)^2+(y-p_2)^2}$ and $\omega_q$ be defined similarly. Prove that $\omega_p$ and $\omega_q$ are closed on $M$ and that their classes in $H^1(M)$ are linearly independent, so $\dim H^1(M)\ge2$.
hint: Integrate over two small circles, one around $p$ and one around $q$, and use that $\omega_q$ is exact on a disc around $p$ not containing $q$.
solution: Closedness is a local computation, as for the form $d\theta$ (translate the origin to $p$): with $P=-Y/(X^2+Y^2)$, $Q=X/(X^2+Y^2)$ in the variables $X=x-p_1$, $Y=y-p_2$ one has $\partial_XQ=\partial_YP$. Let $\varepsilon<|p-q|/2$ and let $C_p$, $C_q$ be the counterclockwise circles of radius $\varepsilon$ about $p$ and $q$; both lie in $M$. On $C_p$: $\omega_p=dt$ in the angle parameter, so $\oint_{C_p}\omega_p=2\pi$. The form $\omega_q$ is closed on the open disc $D$ about $p$ of radius $|p-q|$, which does not contain $q$ and is star-shaped about $p$; by the Poincaré lemma for 1-forms (after translating $p$ to $0$) $\omega_q=df$ on $D$, so $\oint_{C_p}\omega_q=\oint_{C_p}df=0$. By symmetry $\oint_{C_q}\omega_q=2\pi$ and $\oint_{C_q}\omega_p=0$. Suppose $a\,\omega_p+b\,\omega_q=dh$ on $M$ for a smooth $h$. Then integrating over the closed curve $C_p$ gives $2\pi a=\oint_{C_p}dh=0$, and over $C_q$ gives $2\pi b=0$. So $a=b=0$: if $a[\omega_p]+b[\omega_q]=0$ in $H^1(M)$ then $a=b=0$, and the two classes are linearly independent. $\blacksquare$

@quiz self
prompt: Let $F\colon M\to(N,h)$ be a smooth map into a Riemannian manifold. Prove that $F^*h$, defined by $(F^*h)_p(v,w)=h_{F(p)}(dF_pv,dF_pw)$, is a Riemannian metric on $M$ if and only if $F$ is an immersion.
hint: Symmetry and smoothness are automatic. Look at positive definiteness: $(F^*h)_p(v,v)=|dF_pv|^2$.
solution: $F^*h$ is a smooth symmetric bilinear form on each $T_pM$: in charts its coefficients $(F^*h)_{ij}=\sum_{a,b}h_{ab}(F)\,\partial_iF^a\,\partial_jF^b$ are smooth, and bilinearity and symmetry follow from those of $h_{F(p)}$ and linearity of $dF_p$. It is positive semidefinite since $(F^*h)_p(v,v)=|dF_pv|^2_h\ge0$. If $F$ is an immersion and $v\ne0$, then $dF_pv\ne0$ and so $|dF_pv|^2>0$: positive definite. Conversely, if $F^*h$ is positive definite and $dF_pv=0$ then $0=|dF_pv|^2=(F^*h)_p(v,v)$ forces $v=0$, so $dF_p$ is injective for every $p$, that is, $F$ is an immersion. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Notes that smoothness, symmetry and bilinearity of $F^*h$ are automatic.
rubric: Identifies $(F^*h)_p(v,v)=|dF_pv|^2$ and shows positive definiteness when $dF_p$ is injective.
rubric: Proves the converse by showing a kernel vector of $dF_p$ would violate positive definiteness.
