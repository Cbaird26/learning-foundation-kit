@article
A circle is compact and $\mathbb{R}$ is not, so no single homeomorphism identifies the circle with an interval. Yet every small arc of the circle looks like an interval, and calculus on the circle (a function of the angle, a vector field, a differential form) should make sense. A manifold is a space that is locally a copy of $\mathbb{R}^n$, with enough structure on the overlaps to differentiate.

### Definitions

A **topological manifold** of dimension $n$ is a topological space $M$ that is

1. **Hausdorff**: distinct points have disjoint open neighborhoods;
2. **second countable**: the topology has a countable basis;
3. **locally Euclidean of dimension $n$**: every point has an open neighborhood homeomorphic to an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$.

A **chart** is a pair $(U, \varphi)$ with $U \subseteq M$ open and $\varphi\colon U \to \varphi(U) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ a homeomorphism onto an open set. Two charts $(U,\varphi)$ and $(V,\psi)$ are **smoothly compatible** if the **transition map** $\psi \circ \varphi^{-1}\colon \varphi(U \cap V) \to \psi(U \cap V)$ and its inverse $\varphi \circ \psi^{-1}$ are both $C^\infty$ (when $U \cap V = \emptyset$ the condition is vacuous). A **smooth atlas** is a family of pairwise compatible charts whose domains cover $M$. A **smooth structure** is a maximal smooth atlas, and a **smooth manifold** is a topological manifold with a smooth structure.

**Lemma 1.** Every smooth atlas $\mathcal{A}$ on a topological manifold $M$ is contained in a unique maximal smooth atlas, namely the set $\overline{\mathcal{A}}$ of all charts compatible with every chart of $\mathcal{A}$.

*Proof.* Clearly $\mathcal{A} \subseteq \overline{\mathcal{A}}$, so $\overline{\mathcal{A}}$ covers $M$. Let $(V,\psi)$ and $(W,\chi)$ be in $\overline{\mathcal{A}}$ and $p \in V \cap W$. Choose $(U,\varphi) \in \mathcal{A}$ with $p \in U$. On the open set $\psi(U \cap V \cap W)$ we have $\chi \circ \psi^{-1} = (\chi \circ \varphi^{-1}) \circ (\varphi \circ \psi^{-1})$, a composition of smooth maps, so $\chi\circ\psi^{-1}$ is smooth near $\psi(p)$; the same argument applies to $\psi \circ \chi^{-1}$. Hence $\overline{\mathcal{A}}$ is a smooth atlas. If $\mathcal{B} \supseteq \mathcal{A}$ is any smooth atlas, each chart of $\mathcal{B}$ is compatible with all of $\mathcal{A}$, so $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \overline{\mathcal{A}}$: the atlas $\overline{\mathcal{A}}$ is maximal and contains every smooth atlas that contains $\mathcal{A}$, which gives uniqueness. $\blacksquare$

So to give a smooth structure it suffices to exhibit one smooth atlas.

### The sphere

Let $S^n = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^{n+1} : |x| = 1\}$ with the subspace topology; it is Hausdorff and second countable because $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ is. Write $x = (x', x_{n+1})$ and define on $U_N = S^n \setminus \{N\}$, $N = (0, \dots, 0, 1)$, and $U_S = S^n \setminus \{S\}$ the **stereographic projections**
$$\sigma_N(x) = \frac{x'}{1 - x_{n+1}}, \qquad \sigma_S(x) = \frac{x'}{1 + x_{n+1}} .$$
Each is a homeomorphism onto $\mathbb{R}^n$ with $\sigma_N^{-1}(u) = \dfrac{(2u,\; |u|^2 - 1)}{|u|^2 + 1}$. The charts cover $S^n$. For $u \ne 0$, put $x = \sigma_N^{-1}(u)$: then $1 + x_{n+1} = \frac{2|u|^2}{|u|^2+1}$, so
$$\sigma_S \circ \sigma_N^{-1}(u) = \frac{2u/(|u|^2+1)}{2|u|^2/(|u|^2+1)} = \frac{u}{|u|^2},$$
smooth on $\mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{0\} = \sigma_N(U_N \cap U_S)$, and it is its own inverse. **Conclusion: $S^n$ is a smooth manifold of dimension $n$.** The identity can be tested exactly in Python:

```python
from fractions import Fraction

def sigma_N_inv(u):
    s = sum(t * t for t in u)
    return [2 * t / (s + 1) for t in u] + [(s - 1) / (s + 1)]

def sigma_S(x):
    return [t / (1 + x[-1]) for t in x[:-1]]

u = [Fraction(1, 2), Fraction(-3), Fraction(2, 5)]
s = sum(t * t for t in u)
print(sigma_S(sigma_N_inv(u)) == [t / s for t in u])
```

This prints `True`. One point is a check, not a proof; the algebra above is the proof.

### Projective space and non-examples

$\mathbb{RP}^n$ is the set of lines through $0$ in $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$, that is, $(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\})/(x \sim \lambda x)$ with the quotient topology. Its standard charts are in the first worked example; Hausdorffness and second countability are proved in the next lesson.

Each axiom matters. The **line with two origins** (two copies of $\mathbb{R}$ glued along $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$) is locally Euclidean and second countable but not Hausdorff, and limits of sequences are not unique. The **long line** is Hausdorff and locally Euclidean but not second countable. The union of the two coordinate axes in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is not locally Euclidean at the origin: removing the origin from a small connected neighborhood leaves four components, while removing a point from an interval leaves two. The closed disc is a manifold *with boundary*, not a manifold.

### Smooth structures are extra data

The single chart $(\mathbb{R}, x \mapsto x^3)$ is a smooth atlas on $\mathbb{R}$, but it is not compatible with the standard chart: $x \mapsto x^{1/3}$ is not smooth at $0$. So it defines a different smooth structure, although the two smooth manifolds are diffeomorphic (via $x \mapsto x^{1/3}$). Milnor (1956) found smooth structures on the topological $7$-sphere that are not diffeomorphic to the standard one, so the extra data can matter.
@example
title: Stereographic charts of the circle
problem: For $S^1$ with $\sigma_N(x,y) = \dfrac{x}{1-y}$ and $\sigma_S(x,y) = \dfrac{x}{1+y}$, compute the transition map $\sigma_S \circ \sigma_N^{-1}$ and state where it is defined.
step: Domains: $\sigma_N$ is defined on $S^1\setminus\{(0,1)\}$ and $\sigma_S$ on $S^1\setminus\{(0,-1)\}$; the overlap is $S^1\setminus\{(0,\pm1)\}$, and $\sigma_N$ maps it to $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$ (the points $(0,-1)$ and $(0,1)$ correspond to $t = 0$ and $t = \infty$).
step: Invert $\sigma_N$: if $t = x/(1-y)$ and $x^2+y^2=1$, then $x^2 = (1-y)(1+y)$, so $t^2 = (1+y)/(1-y)$, giving $y = \dfrac{t^2-1}{t^2+1}$ and $x = t(1-y) = \dfrac{2t}{t^2+1}$.
step: Apply $\sigma_S$: $1 + y = \dfrac{2t^2}{t^2+1}$, so $\sigma_S(x,y) = \dfrac{2t/(t^2+1)}{2t^2/(t^2+1)} = \dfrac{1}{t}$.
step: The transition map is $t \mapsto 1/t$ on $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$. It is smooth, and it is its own inverse, so the two charts are smoothly compatible.
answer: $\sigma_S \circ \sigma_N^{-1}(t) = 1/t$ on $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$.
@example
title: The standard atlas of real projective space
problem: Let $U_i = \{[x_0 : \dots : x_n] : x_i \ne 0\}$ and $\psi_i([x]) = \left(\frac{x_0}{x_i}, \dots, \widehat{\frac{x_i}{x_i}}, \dots, \frac{x_n}{x_i}\right) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ (the hat means the entry is omitted). Show that the transition maps are smooth.
step: Well defined: the quotients $x_j/x_i$ do not change if $x$ is replaced by $\lambda x$. The sets $U_i$ are open because $\{x_i \ne 0\}$ is open and saturated, and they cover $\mathbb{RP}^n$ because a nonzero vector has some nonzero coordinate.
step: Inverse of $\psi_i$: $\psi_i^{-1}(u_1,\dots,u_n)$ is the line through the vector with entries $u_1,\dots,u_n$ placed in the slots other than $i$ and a $1$ in slot $i$. Both $\psi_i$ and this inverse are continuous for the quotient topology, so $\psi_i$ is a homeomorphism onto $\mathbb{R}^n$.
step: Transition for $n = 2$, $i = 0$, $j = 1$: $\psi_0^{-1}(u,v) = [1 : u : v]$, which lies in $U_1$ exactly when $u \ne 0$, and $\psi_1[1:u:v] = (1/u,\; v/u)$. This is smooth on $\{u \ne 0\} = \psi_0(U_0 \cap U_1)$.
step: In general $\psi_j \circ \psi_i^{-1}$ divides the entries of the vector by its $j$-th entry, which is a rational map with nonvanishing denominator on the overlap. The inverse is the transition in the other direction, so all transitions are smooth, and the $n+1$ charts form a smooth atlas.
answer: The charts $(U_i, \psi_i)$ form a smooth atlas; for $n = 2$ the transition from $\psi_0$ to $\psi_1$ is $(u,v)\mapsto(1/u,\,v/u)$.
@mistakes
- Dropping the Hausdorff or second-countability axiom because the "locally Euclidean" part feels like the whole definition: the line with two origins and the long line show these axioms are independent, and partitions of unity (next lesson) need them.
- Checking only $\psi\circ\varphi^{-1}$ and forgetting $\varphi\circ\psi^{-1}$: a homeomorphism that is smooth with a non-smooth inverse, like $x \mapsto x^3$, gives an incompatible chart.
- Treating "homeomorphic" as "diffeomorphic", or an atlas as a smooth structure: different atlases can generate the same maximal atlas, and one topological manifold can carry several smooth structures.
- Thinking a compact manifold such as $S^n$ can be covered by a single chart: a chart is a homeomorphism onto an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$, and compact spaces do not map homeomorphically onto non-compact sets.

```python
from fractions import Fraction


def rp_transition(i, j, u):
    """Chart change psi_j o psi_i^{-1} on real projective space."""
    x = list(u[:i]) + [Fraction(1)] + list(u[i:])
    if x[j] == 0:
        raise ValueError("point is not in the chart U_j")
    return [x[k] / x[j] for k in range(len(x)) if k != j]
```

@practice self
prompt: Let $X$ be the quotient of $\mathbb{R} \times \{0,1\}$ by the relation $(t,0) \sim (t,1)$ for $t \ne 0$ (the line with two origins), with the quotient topology. Prove that $X$ is locally Euclidean of dimension $1$ and second countable, but not Hausdorff.
hint: Let $\pi$ be the quotient map; show the images $U_i = \pi(\mathbb{R}\times\{i\})$ are open and that $\pi$ restricted to each copy of $\mathbb{R}$ is a homeomorphism onto $U_i$.
hint: For non-Hausdorffness, take neighborhoods of the two origins $p_0 = [0,0]$ and $p_1 = [0,1]$ and look at points $t$ near $0$.
solution: Let $\pi\colon \mathbb{R}\times\{0,1\} \to X$ be the quotient map and $p_i = \pi(0,i)$. For $i \in \{0,1\}$ and an open set $V \subseteq \mathbb{R}$, the preimage of $\pi(V \times \{i\})$ is $V \times \{i\} \cup (V \setminus \{0\}) \times \{1-i\}$, which is open; hence $\pi(V\times\{i\})$ is open in $X$. Taking $V = \mathbb{R}$ shows $U_i = \pi(\mathbb{R}\times\{i\})$ is open, and $U_0 \cup U_1 = X$. The map $\pi_i\colon \mathbb{R} \to U_i$, $t \mapsto \pi(t,i)$, is continuous and bijective (the only identifications are between the two copies, never within one copy), and open by what was just shown, hence a homeomorphism. So $X$ is locally Euclidean of dimension $1$. Second countable: take the images under $\pi_i$ of intervals with rational endpoints for $i = 0, 1$; this countable family is a basis of each $U_i$, and the $U_i$ are open and cover $X$, so it is a basis of $X$. Not Hausdorff: suppose $W_0 \ni p_0$ and $W_1 \ni p_1$ are open. Then $\pi^{-1}(W_0)$ and $\pi^{-1}(W_1)$ are open in $\mathbb{R}\times\{0,1\}$ and contain $(0,0)$ and $(0,1)$, so there is $\varepsilon > 0$ with $(\varepsilon', 0) \in \pi^{-1}(W_0)$ and $(\varepsilon', 1) \in \pi^{-1}(W_1)$ for $\varepsilon' = \varepsilon/2$. But $\pi(\varepsilon',0) = \pi(\varepsilon', 1)$ lies in $W_0 \cap W_1$, so no disjoint neighborhoods exist. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Shows $\pi$ restricted to each copy of $\mathbb{R}$ is a homeomorphism onto an open set $U_i$ (continuity, bijectivity and openness all addressed).
rubric: Gives a countable basis, using that the two open sets $U_0$, $U_1$ cover $X$.
rubric: Proves non-Hausdorffness by finding a common point of any two neighborhoods of the two origins, using openness of the preimages.

@quiz self
prompt: Let $M$ be a smooth manifold of dimension $n$ with smooth structure $\mathcal{A}$ and $W \subseteq M$ an open subset. Prove that $W$, with the subspace topology, is a smooth manifold of dimension $n$ with a natural smooth atlas.
hint: Check the three topological axioms for the subspace topology, then restrict charts.
solution: Topology: a subspace of a Hausdorff space is Hausdorff. If $\mathcal{B}$ is a countable basis of $M$, then $\{B \cap W : B \in \mathcal{B}\}$ is a countable basis of $W$. Charts: for $p \in W$ choose $(U,\varphi) \in \mathcal{A}$ with $p \in U$. Then $U \cap W$ is open, and $\varphi|_{U \cap W}$ is a homeomorphism onto $\varphi(U \cap W)$, which is open in $\varphi(U)$ (the image of an open set under a homeomorphism) and hence open in $\mathbb{R}^n$. So $W$ is locally Euclidean of dimension $n$ and the restrictions $(U \cap W, \varphi|_{U\cap W})$ cover $W$. Smoothness: the transition map between two restricted charts is the restriction of a transition map of $\mathcal{A}$ to an open subset of its domain, and restrictions of smooth maps to open sets are smooth; the same holds for the inverse. So the restricted charts form a smooth atlas on $W$. $\blacksquare$

rubric: Shows the restricted chart images are open in $\mathbb{R}^n$ (uses openness of $W$).
rubric: Observes that transition maps of restricted charts are restrictions of the original smooth transition maps.
