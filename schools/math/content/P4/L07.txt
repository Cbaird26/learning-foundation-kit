@article
Take a river's current: at each point the water has a velocity. A particle dropped in follows a curve whose velocity at each time is the current at its position. On a manifold the current is a vector field, the path is an integral curve, and moving every particle for time $t$ gives a map of the manifold to itself. This is the flow.

### Vector fields and integral curves

A **vector field** on $M$ is a map $X$ assigning to each $p$ a tangent vector $X_p\in T_pM$. It is **smooth** if in every chart its components are smooth: $X=\sum_i X^i\,\partial/\partial x^i$ with $X^i\in C^\infty$. Equivalent: $Xf\colon p\mapsto X_pf$ is smooth for every smooth $f$, so a smooth vector field is a derivation $X\colon C^\infty(M)\to C^\infty(M)$ (the Leibniz rule $X(fg) = fXg+gXf$).

An **integral curve** of $X$ is a smooth curve $\gamma\colon J\to M$ on an interval $J$ with $\gamma'(t) = X_{\gamma(t)}$ for all $t\in J$. In a chart this is the autonomous ODE $\dot x^i = X^i(x)$.

**Theorem 1 (local existence, uniqueness and smooth dependence, from ODE theory; proof not given here).** For every $p\in M$ there are a neighborhood $U\ni p$, an $\varepsilon>0$ and a smooth map $\theta\colon(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\times U\to M$ such that for each $q\in U$ the curve $t\mapsto\theta(t,q)$ is an integral curve starting at $q$, and any two integral curves in a chart with the same value at some time coincide near that time.

**Theorem 2 (global uniqueness).** Let $M$ be Hausdorff and $\gamma_1,\gamma_2\colon J\to M$ integral curves of $X$ on an interval $J$ with $\gamma_1(t_0)=\gamma_2(t_0)$ for some $t_0\in J$. Then $\gamma_1=\gamma_2$ on $J$.

*Proof.* Let $E=\{t\in J:\gamma_1(t)=\gamma_2(t)\}$, nonempty. $E$ is closed in $J$: the set where two continuous maps into a Hausdorff space agree is closed. $E$ is open: if $t_1\in E$, both curves are integral curves through the same point, so they agree near $t_1$ by Theorem 1. $J$ is connected, so $E=J$. $\blacksquare$

The Hausdorff axiom is used: on the line with two origins, $\partial_x$ has integral curves $t\mapsto[t,0]$ and $t\mapsto[t,1]$, which agree for $t\neq0$ and differ at $t = 0$.

### The flow

By Theorem 2, for each $p$ there is a **maximal integral curve** $\theta^{(p)}\colon J_p\to M$ with $\theta^{(p)}(0)=p$ (the union of all integral curves from $p$), where $J_p$ is an open interval. Let $\mathcal{D}=\{(t,p) : t\in J_p\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}\times M$ and $\theta(t,p)=\theta^{(p)}(t)=:\theta_t(p)$.

**Theorem 3 (fundamental theorem on flows).** $\mathcal{D}$ is open, $\theta\colon\mathcal{D}\to M$ is smooth, and: if $(t,p)\in\mathcal{D}$ and $(s,\theta_t(p))\in\mathcal{D}$, then $(s+t,p)\in\mathcal{D}$ and $\theta_s(\theta_t(p))=\theta_{s+t}(p)$.

Openness and smoothness come from Theorem 1 by a standard continuation argument (not reproduced). The **group law** follows from uniqueness: $s\mapsto\theta_{t+s}(p)$ is an integral curve starting at $\theta_t(p)$, so it is a restriction of the maximal curve $\theta^{(\theta_t(p))}$, which gives the identity and the domain statement. If $\mathcal{D}=\mathbb{R}\times M$, the field is **complete**, and $\{\theta_t\}$ is a one-parameter group of diffeomorphisms ($\theta_{-t}=\theta_t^{-1}$).

### Completeness

Not every field is complete. On $\mathbb{R}$, $e^x\partial_x$ has the solution $x(t)=-\log(e^{-x_0}-t)$, which blows up at $t=e^{-x_0}$. Removing a point also destroys completeness: $\partial_x$ on $\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{0\}$ runs into the origin in finite time.

**Theorem 4.** A smooth vector field with compact support on a manifold is complete. In particular every vector field on a compact manifold is complete.

*Proof.* Let $K=\operatorname{supp}X$, compact, and note $X=0$ outside $K$. By Theorem 1 and compactness, finitely many local flows cover $K$, so there is $\varepsilon>0$ such that $\theta$ is defined on $(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\times K$. Let $p\in M$ and let $b=\sup J_p$; suppose $b<\infty$. Pick $t_0\in J_p$ with $t_0>b-\varepsilon$ and $t_0 > 0$. If $q=\theta_{t_0}(p)\notin K$, then $X_q=0$, the constant curve at $q$ is an integral curve, so by uniqueness $\theta_t(p)=q$ for all $t \ge t_0$ in $J_p$, and the constant curve extends the maximal one past $b$: contradiction. If $q\in K$, the curve $s\mapsto\theta_s(q)$, $|s|<\varepsilon$, is an integral curve; the concatenation $\theta_t(p)$ for $t\le t_0$ and $\theta_{t-t_0}(q)$ for $t_0 \le t<t_0+\varepsilon$ is an integral curve (they agree at $t_0$ with matching velocity, and by the group law the two descriptions agree on the overlap) defined past $b$: contradiction. The same argument handles the lower end. $\blacksquare$

### Flows by computation

A numerical method approximates the flow; for the logistic field $X=x(1-x)\partial_x$ (exact flow $\theta_t(x_0)=\big(1+(1/x_0-1)e^{-t}\big)^{-1}$) a Runge–Kutta step of order four agrees with the exact flow to seven digits:

```python
from math import exp


def rk4_step(f, x, h):
    k1 = f(x)
    k2 = f(x + h / 2 * k1)
    k3 = f(x + h / 2 * k2)
    k4 = f(x + h * k3)
    return x + h / 6 * (k1 + 2 * k2 + 2 * k3 + k4)


f = lambda x: x * (1 - x)
x = 0.2
for _ in range(10):
    x = rk4_step(f, x, 0.1)
exact = 1 / (1 + (1 / 0.2 - 1) * exp(-1))
print(abs(x - exact) < 1e-6)
```

This prints `True` (the difference is about $4\times10^{-8}$). Numerical agreement is evidence about a solution, not an existence theorem.
@example
title: A flow that escapes in finite time
problem: Compute the maximal flow of $X=x^2\,\partial_x$ on $\mathbb{R}$ and its domain $\mathcal{D}$.
step: The ODE is $\dot x = x^2$ with $x(0)=x_0$. If $x_0=0$ the solution is $x\equiv0$, defined for all $t$.
step: If $x_0\ne0$, separate variables: $\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(-\dfrac1x\right)=1$, so $-\dfrac1{x(t)}=t-\dfrac1{x_0}$, giving $x(t)=\dfrac{x_0}{1-tx_0}$.
step: This solution is defined as long as $1-tx_0\ne0$ on an interval containing $0$: for $x_0>0$ on $J=(-\infty,1/x_0)$; for $x_0<0$ on $J=(1/x_0,\infty)$.
step: So $\mathcal{D}=\{(t,x) : tx<1\}$, an open set, and $\theta_t(x)=x/(1-tx)$ there. The group law: $\theta_s(\theta_t(x))=\dfrac{x/(1-tx)}{1-sx/(1-tx)}=\dfrac{x}{1-tx-sx}=\theta_{s+t}(x)$.
answer: $\theta_t(x)=\dfrac{x}{1-tx}$ on $\mathcal{D}=\{(t,x):tx<1\}$; the field is not complete.
@example
title: The flow of a linear vector field
problem: Find the flow of $X=(x+y)\,\partial_x+y\,\partial_y$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$.
step: In coordinates $\dot{\mathbf{x}}=A\mathbf{x}$ with $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{x}=(x,y)^\top$. The solution with $\mathbf{x}(0)=\mathbf{x}_0$ is $e^{tA}\mathbf{x}_0$ (the series converges for all $t$ and differentiates termwise).
step: Write $A=I+N$ with $N=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$. Since $I$ commutes with $N$ and $N^2=0$: $e^{tA}=e^{t}e^{tN}=e^t(I+tN)=e^t\begin{pmatrix}1&t\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$.
step: Therefore $\theta_t(x,y)=e^t\,(x+ty,\ y)$, defined for all $(t,x,y)$, so $X$ is complete.
step: Check: $\dfrac{d}{dt}\theta_t(x,y)\big|_{t=0}=(x+y,\ y)=X_{(x,y)}$, and the second coordinate $e^ty$ satisfies $\dot y=y$.
answer: $\theta_t(x,y)=e^t(x+ty,\ y)$ for all $t$.
@mistakes
- Assuming every vector field is complete: $x^2\partial_x$ and $e^x\partial_x$ on $\mathbb{R}$ are not, and compactness (or compact support) is the usual hypothesis that rescues completeness.
- Writing $\theta_s\circ\theta_t=\theta_{s+t}$ without checking domains: for an incomplete field the identity holds only where the left side is defined.
- Forgetting the Hausdorff property in the uniqueness theorem: without it, integral curves through a point need not be unique on an interval.
- Confusing the flow $\theta_t$ (a map $M\to M$ for fixed $t$) with an integral curve $t\mapsto\theta_t(p)$ (a curve for fixed $p$).

model:
```python
from fractions import Fraction


def flow_nilpotent(A, x, t):
    """exp(tA) x for a nilpotent integer matrix A, in exact arithmetic."""
    n = len(x)
    t = Fraction(t)
    term = [Fraction(v) for v in x]
    result = term[:]
    for k in range(1, n + 1):
        term = [sum(A[i][j] * term[j] for j in range(n)) * t / k for i in range(n)]
        result = [r + s for r, s in zip(result, term)]
    return result
```
hint: Keep a running term $\frac{t^k}{k!}A^k\mathbf{x}$ and obtain the next one by applying $A$, multiplying by $t$ and dividing by $k$.
solution: The recursion $\text{term}_k=\frac tk A\,\text{term}_{k-1}$ builds $\frac{t^k}{k!}A^k\mathbf{x}$ with exact factorials; dropping the division by $k$ gives $t^kA^k\mathbf{x}$, which the tests with $t=2,3$ catch. Since $A$ is nilpotent, $A^k=0$ for $k\ge n$, so $n$ iterations suffice. The group law $\theta_s\circ\theta_t=\theta_{s+t}$ is one of the tests; it is a consequence of $e^{sA}e^{tA}=e^{(s+t)A}$, not a proof of it.
@practice self
prompt: (Flow-box theorem.) Let $X$ be a smooth vector field on an $n$-manifold $M$ with $X_p\ne0$. Prove that there are coordinates $(y^1,\dots,y^n)$ on a neighborhood of $p$ in which $X=\partial/\partial y^1$.
hint: Start with a chart in which $p=0$ and $X_0=\partial_1|_0$, and define $\Psi(t,x_2,\dots,x_n)=\theta_t(0,x_2,\dots,x_n)$.
hint: Compute $d\Psi_0$ and apply the inverse function theorem; then use the group law.
solution: Choose a chart centered at $p$ (so $p=0$); after a linear change of coordinates, $X_0=\partial/\partial x^1|_0$. Let $\theta$ be the flow of $X$ (Theorem 3). For $(t,x')=(t,x_2,\dots,x_n)$ near $0$, define $\Psi(t,x')=\theta_t(0,x')$; this is smooth by Theorem 3 and defined near $0$ since $\mathcal{D}$ is open. At the origin, $d\Psi_0(\partial_t)=\frac{d}{dt}\theta_t(0)|_{t=0}=X_0=\partial_1$, and $d\Psi_0(\partial_{x_j})=\partial_j$ for $j\ge2$ because $\theta_0=\mathrm{id}$ so $\Psi(0,x')=(0,x')$. Hence $d\Psi_0$ is the identity matrix, and by the inverse function theorem $\Psi$ is a diffeomorphism from a neighborhood of $0$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ onto a neighborhood of $p$; its inverse gives coordinates $y=(t,x')$. In them, the curve $s\mapsto\Psi(t+s,x')=\theta_s(\theta_t(0,x'))$ (by the group law) is the integral curve of $X$ through $\Psi(t,x')$, and in the $y$-coordinates it is $s\mapsto(t+s,x')$ with velocity $\partial/\partial y^1$. Thus $X=\partial/\partial y^1$ on the neighborhood. $\blacksquare$

@quiz self
prompt: Let $X$ be a complete smooth vector field with flow $\theta$. Prove that each $\theta_t\colon M\to M$ is a diffeomorphism with inverse $\theta_{-t}$.
hint: Use the group law and $\theta_0=\mathrm{id}$, and the smoothness of $\theta$ on $\mathbb{R}\times M$.
solution: Since $X$ is complete, $\mathcal{D}=\mathbb{R}\times M$, so every composition of the maps $\theta_t$ is defined and the group law holds everywhere: $\theta_s\circ\theta_t=\theta_{s+t}$. Also $\theta_0(p)=p$ because the integral curve starts at $p$. Therefore $\theta_{-t}\circ\theta_t=\theta_0=\mathrm{id}_M$ and $\theta_t\circ\theta_{-t}=\mathrm{id}_M$, so $\theta_t$ is a bijection with inverse $\theta_{-t}$. Each $\theta_t$ is smooth, being the restriction of the smooth map $\theta\colon\mathbb{R}\times M\to M$ to the slice $\{t\}\times M$ (composition with the smooth inclusion $p\mapsto(t,p)$), and the same holds for $\theta_{-t}$. So $\theta_t$ is a diffeomorphism. $\blacksquare$
