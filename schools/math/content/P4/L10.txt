@article
In $\mathbb{R}^3$ the operators gradient, curl and divergence satisfy $\operatorname{curl}\operatorname{grad}=0$ and $\operatorname{div}\operatorname{curl}=0$. On a manifold they are all the single operator $d$ with $d^2=0$, and the fundamental theorem of calculus, Green's theorem and the divergence theorem will all be one statement, Stokes's theorem. First we need $d$ and a way to integrate forms.

### The exterior derivative

For $\omega=\sum_I\omega_I\,dx^I$ on a coordinate domain define $d\omega=\sum_Id\omega_I\wedge dx^I$, where $d\omega_I=\sum_j\partial_j\omega_I\,dx^j$.

**Theorem 1.** Let $M$ be a smooth manifold. (a) $d\colon\Omega^k(M)\to\Omega^{k+1}(M)$ is well defined (independent of the chart) and linear. (b) $d(\alpha\wedge\beta)=d\alpha\wedge\beta+(-1)^k\alpha\wedge d\beta$ for $\alpha\in\Omega^k$. (c) $d\circ d=0$. (d) $F^*d\omega=d(F^*\omega)$ for every smooth $F\colon M\to N$ and $\omega\in\Omega^k(N)$.

*Proof.* We work in coordinates. (c): for $\omega=f\,dx^I$, $d\omega=\sum_j\partial_jf\,dx^j\wedge dx^I$ and $d^2\omega=\sum_{i,j}\partial_i\partial_jf\,dx^i\wedge dx^j\wedge dx^I=0$ because $\partial_i\partial_jf$ is symmetric in $(i,j)$ while $dx^i\wedge dx^j$ is antisymmetric. (b): by bilinearity take $\alpha=f\,dx^I$, $\beta=g\,dx^J$; then $d(fg\,dx^I\wedge dx^J)=(g\,df+f\,dg)\wedge dx^I\wedge dx^J=d\alpha\wedge\beta+f\,dg\wedge dx^I\wedge dx^J$, and $dg\wedge dx^I=(-1)^kdx^I\wedge dg$ gives $(-1)^k\alpha\wedge d\beta$. (d) for functions is the chain rule: $F^*(dh)=d(h\circ F)$. For $\omega=h\,dy^{i_1}\wedge\dots\wedge dy^{i_k}$ we have $F^*\omega=(h\circ F)\,d(y^{i_1}\circ F)\wedge\dots\wedge d(y^{i_k}\circ F)$, and by (b) and (c) (applied on $M$) $d(F^*\omega)=d(h\circ F)\wedge d(y^{i_1}\circ F)\wedge\dots$ because each $d(d(y^{i}\circ F))=0$; this equals $F^*(dh)\wedge F^*dy^{i_1}\wedge\dots=F^*d\omega$. Finally, applying (d) to the coordinate change $F=\mathrm{id}$ between two charts shows the two coordinate definitions of $d$ agree, proving (a). $\blacksquare$

A form is **closed** if $d\omega=0$ and **exact** if $\omega=d\eta$; by (c) exact forms are closed. In $\mathbb{R}^3$: $df=\nabla f\cdot d\mathbf{x}$, $d(P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz)$ corresponds to the curl, and $d(A\,dy\wedge dz+B\,dz\wedge dx+C\,dx\wedge dy)=(\partial_xA+\partial_yB+\partial_zC)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz$ to the divergence.

### Orientation

A smooth atlas is **oriented** if all transition maps have positive Jacobian determinant; $M$ is **orientable** if it has an oriented atlas, and an **orientation** is a maximal oriented atlas.

**Proposition 2.** $M^n$ is orientable if and only if there is a nowhere-vanishing $n$-form on $M$.

*Proof.* ($\Rightarrow$) Take an oriented atlas $\{(U_\alpha,x_\alpha)\}$ and a subordinate partition of unity $\{\rho_\alpha\}$; set $\omega=\sum_\alpha\rho_\alpha\,dx_\alpha^1\wedge\dots\wedge dx_\alpha^n$. On overlaps $dx_\alpha^1\wedge\dots\wedge dx^n_\alpha=\det(\partial x_\alpha/\partial x_\beta)\,dx_\beta^1\wedge\dots\wedge dx_\beta^n$ with positive determinant (Theorem 3 of the previous lesson), so at each point $\omega$ is a sum of nonnegative multiples of one nonzero form, with at least one positive coefficient. ($\Leftarrow$) Given $\omega$, cover $M$ by connected chart domains; on each, $\omega=f\,dx^1\wedge\dots\wedge dx^n$ with $f$ nowhere zero, hence of constant sign; replace $x^1$ by $-x^1$ if $f<0$. In the new charts $f>0$, and on overlaps $f_\alpha\,dx_\alpha=f_\beta\,dx_\beta$ forces $\det(\partial x_\beta/\partial x_\alpha)=f_\alpha/f_\beta>0$. $\blacksquare$

**Spheres.** On $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ let $\Omega=\sum_i(-1)^{i-1}x_i\,dx_1\wedge\dots\widehat{dx_i}\dots\wedge dx_{n+1}$. At $p\in S^n$ one has $\Omega_p(v_1,\dots,v_n)=\det(p,v_1,\dots,v_n)$ (expansion along the first column), which is nonzero exactly when $v_1,\dots,v_n$ is a basis of $p^\perp=T_pS^n$. So $\Omega|_{S^n}$ is nowhere vanishing and $S^n$ is orientable.

**Projective spaces.** The antipodal map $a(x)=-x$ satisfies $a^*\Omega=(-1)^{n+1}\Omega$ (since $\det(-p,-v_1,\dots,-v_n)=(-1)^{n+1}\det(p,v_1,\dots,v_n)$). If $n$ is odd, $\Omega|_{S^n}$ is $a$-invariant and descends to a nowhere-vanishing $n$-form on $\mathbb{RP}^n$, which is orientable. If $n$ is even and $\mathbb{RP}^n$ had a nowhere-vanishing $n$-form $\omega'$, then $\pi^*\omega'=f\,\Omega$ with $f$ continuous and nowhere zero; as $\pi\circ a=\pi$, $a^*\pi^*\omega'=\pi^*\omega'$ gives $(f\circ a)(-1)^{n+1}\Omega=f\Omega$, that is $f\circ a=-f$. Since $S^n$ is connected, $f$ takes both signs and so vanishes somewhere: contradiction. **$\mathbb{RP}^n$ is orientable if and only if $n$ is odd.**

### Integration

Let $M$ be an oriented $n$-manifold and $\omega$ a compactly supported $n$-form. If $\operatorname{supp}\omega$ lies in one oriented chart $(U,\varphi)$, define $\int_M\omega=\int_{\varphi(U)}(\varphi^{-1})^*\omega$, where for $(\varphi^{-1})^*\omega=g\,dx^1\wedge\dots\wedge dx^n$ the right side is the Lebesgue integral $\int g\,dx$. This does not depend on the chart: for another oriented chart $\psi$ the change of variables formula (with positive Jacobian, equal to the absolute value) and Theorem 3 of the previous lesson give the same value. In general choose an oriented atlas $\{U_\alpha\}$ and a partition of unity $\{\rho_\alpha\}$ and put $\int_M\omega=\sum_\alpha\int_M\rho_\alpha\omega$ (a finite sum, by compact support and local finiteness); independence of choices follows from the one-chart case by comparing two partitions of unity. The integral is linear, reverses sign when the orientation is reversed, and satisfies $\int_MF^*\omega=\pm\int_N\omega$ for a diffeomorphism $F\colon M\to N$ (sign $+$ if $F$ preserves orientation, $-$ if it reverses it).

The integral of a $k$-form over a $k$-dimensional oriented submanifold means the integral of its pullback by the inclusion. For a parametrization $c\colon[a,b]\to M$ of an oriented curve, $\int_c\omega=\int_a^b\omega(c'(t))\,dt$.
@example
title: Integrating a 1-form over a circle
problem: Let $S$ be the circle of radius $2$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$ oriented counterclockwise. Compute $\int_S(x\,dy+3y\,dx)$.
step: Parametrize $c(t)=(2\cos t,\ 2\sin t)$, $0\le t\le2\pi$, which traverses $S$ counterclockwise. Then $dx=-2\sin t\,dt$ and $dy=2\cos t\,dt$ along $c$.
step: Pull back the form: $x\,dy+3y\,dx=2\cos t\cdot2\cos t\,dt+3\cdot2\sin t\cdot(-2\sin t)\,dt=(4\cos^2t-12\sin^2t)\,dt$.
step: Integrate: $\int_0^{2\pi}4\cos^2t\,dt=4\pi$ and $\int_0^{2\pi}12\sin^2t\,dt=12\pi$, so the integral is $4\pi-12\pi=-8\pi$.
step: Sanity check: reversing the orientation changes the sign, and the closed form $x\,dy-y\,dx$ gives $\int_0^{2\pi}4\,dt=8\pi$; the given form has $d(x\,dy+3y\,dx)=(1-3)\,dx\wedge dy\ne0$, so it is not closed, hence not exact.
answer: $-8\pi$
@example
title: Integrating $z\,dx\wedge dy$ over the unit sphere
problem: Orient $S^2$ by the outward normal. Compute $\int_{S^2}z\,dx\wedge dy$.
step: Split $S^2$ into the open upper and lower hemispheres (the equator has measure zero). On the upper hemisphere $z=\sqrt{1-x^2-y^2}$ and $(x,y)$ is an oriented chart, because the outward normal has positive $z$-component there and $dx\wedge dy$ is positive on the oriented basis.
step: Upper hemisphere: $\int_Dz\,dx\,dy=\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^1\sqrt{1-r^2}\,r\,dr\,d\theta=2\pi\cdot\tfrac13=\tfrac{2\pi}3$.
step: Lower hemisphere: here $z=-\sqrt{1-x^2-y^2}$, but the outward normal points down, so $(x,y)$ is an orientation-reversing chart and $\int=-\int_Dz\,dx\,dy=-\int_D(-\sqrt{1-r^2})\,dx\,dy=\tfrac{2\pi}3$.
step: Add: $\tfrac{2\pi}3+\tfrac{2\pi}3=\tfrac{4\pi}3$, the volume of the unit ball (which Stokes's theorem will explain).
answer: $\dfrac{4\pi}{3}$
@mistakes
- Using $d(\alpha\wedge\beta)=d\alpha\wedge\beta+\alpha\wedge d\beta$ for forms of odd degree: the sign $(-1)^k$ is essential.
- Forgetting orientation when integrating over a hemisphere or a curve: the same form integrates to opposite values over the two orientations.
- Believing $\mathbb{RP}^n$ is non-orientable for all $n$: it is orientable for odd $n$, and $\mathbb{RP}^2$ is the first non-orientable case.
- Integrating a form without compact support, or integrating a $k$-form over an $n$-manifold with $k<n$: the integral requires a compactly supported form of top degree on an oriented manifold of the same dimension.

@practice self
prompt: Prove that a connected orientable manifold $M$ of dimension $n\ge1$ has exactly two orientations, where an orientation is an equivalence class of nowhere-vanishing $n$-forms, two forms being equivalent if one is a positive function times the other.
hint: Compare two nowhere-vanishing $n$-forms by their ratio.
hint: A continuous nowhere-vanishing function on a connected space has constant sign.
solution: Let $\omega$ be a nowhere-vanishing $n$-form (it exists by Proposition 2). For any other nowhere-vanishing $n$-form $\omega'$, at each $p$ the line $\Lambda^nT_p^*M$ is one-dimensional, so $\omega'_p=f(p)\omega_p$ for a unique $f(p)\ne0$. The function $f$ is smooth: in a chart $\omega=g\,dx^1\wedge\dots\wedge dx^n$ and $\omega'=g'\,dx^1\wedge\dots\wedge dx^n$ with $g,g'$ smooth and $g\neq0$, and $f=g'/g$. Since $M$ is connected and $f$ is continuous and nowhere zero, either $f>0$ everywhere or $f<0$ everywhere. Thus $\omega'$ is equivalent to $\omega$ or to $-\omega$. The forms $\omega$ and $-\omega$ are not equivalent: if $-\omega=h\omega$ with $h>0$ then, as $\omega$ is nowhere zero, $h=-1$, a contradiction. Hence there are exactly two equivalence classes, represented by $\omega$ and $-\omega$. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Writes $\omega'=f\omega$ and shows the ratio $f$ is smooth and nowhere zero.
rubric: Uses connectedness to conclude $f$ has constant sign, giving at most two classes.
rubric: Shows $\omega$ and $-\omega$ are inequivalent, so there are exactly two.

@quiz self
prompt: Let $M$ be the open Möbius band, the quotient of $\mathbb{R}\times(-1,1)$ by the group generated by $g(x,y)=(x+1,-y)$. Prove that $M$ is not orientable.
hint: A nowhere-vanishing 2-form on $M$ pulls back to a $g$-invariant 2-form on the strip. Compute $g^*(dx\wedge dy)$.
solution: Let $q\colon\mathbb{R}\times(-1,1)\to M$ be the quotient map, a local diffeomorphism with $q\circ g=q$. Suppose $\omega$ is a nowhere-vanishing 2-form on $M$. Then $q^*\omega=f\,dx\wedge dy$ for a smooth, nowhere-zero function $f$, and $g^*q^*\omega=(q\circ g)^*\omega=q^*\omega$. Since $g^*(dx\wedge dy)=d(x+1)\wedge d(-y)=-dx\wedge dy$, we get $g^*(f\,dx\wedge dy)=-(f\circ g)\,dx\wedge dy$, so $f(g(x,y))=-f(x,y)$. Take $y=0$: $f(x+1,0)=-f(x,0)$. Then the continuous function $x\mapsto f(x,0)$ on $[0,1]$ has opposite signs at $0$ and $1$ (they are $f(0,0)$ and $-f(0,0)\ne0$), so it vanishes somewhere by the intermediate value theorem, contradicting that $f$ is nowhere zero. Hence no nowhere-vanishing 2-form exists, and by Proposition 2 $M$ is not orientable. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Pulls the putative form back to an invariant form $f\,dx\wedge dy$ on the strip using $q\circ g=q$.
rubric: Computes $g^*(dx\wedge dy)=-dx\wedge dy$ and deduces $f\circ g=-f$.
rubric: Restricts to $y=0$ and uses the intermediate value theorem to reach the contradiction.
