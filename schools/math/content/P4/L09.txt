@article
The tangent spaces $T_pM$ form a family indexed by $M$, and the objects of calculus on manifolds (vector fields, covector fields, forms) are smooth choices of an element of each. A vector bundle packages such a family and says what "smooth choice" means.

### Vector bundles

A **(real) vector bundle of rank $k$** over $M$ is a smooth manifold $E$ with a smooth surjection $\pi\colon E\to M$ such that each fiber $E_p=\pi^{-1}(p)$ is a $k$-dimensional real vector space and every point of $M$ has an open neighborhood $U$ with a diffeomorphism $\Phi\colon\pi^{-1}(U)\to U\times\mathbb{R}^k$ (a **local trivialization**) that commutes with the projections and is linear on each fiber. For two trivializations the map $\Phi_\alpha\circ\Phi_\beta^{-1}(p,v)=(p,g_{\alpha\beta}(p)v)$ defines **transition functions** $g_{\alpha\beta}\colon U_\alpha\cap U_\beta\to GL(k,\mathbb{R})$, smooth and satisfying the cocycle condition $g_{\alpha\beta}g_{\beta\gamma}=g_{\alpha\gamma}$. A **section** is a smooth $s\colon M\to E$ with $\pi\circ s=\mathrm{id}$. A bundle is **trivial** if it has a global trivialization $E\cong M\times\mathbb{R}^k$.

Examples. The trivial bundle $M\times\mathbb{R}^k$. The tangent bundle $TM=\bigsqcup_pT_pM$: a chart $(U,x)$ gives the trivialization $\sum v^i\partial_i|_p\mapsto(p,v)$, with transition functions the Jacobian matrices of the chart changes. The **tautological line bundle** $L\to\mathbb{RP}^n$ with $L_{[x]}$ the line $[x]\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$.

**Proposition 1.** The tautological line bundle $L$ over $\mathbb{RP}^n$ ($n\ge1$) is not trivial.

*Proof.* A trivial line bundle has a nowhere-vanishing section (the image of the constant $1$), so it suffices to show every section $s$ of $L$ has a zero. A section assigns to $[x]$ a vector $s([x])\in[x]$, and the function $\lambda\colon S^n\to\mathbb{R}$ defined by $s([x])=\lambda(x)\,x$ for $x\in S^n$ is continuous. Because $[-x]=[x]$, we have $\lambda(-x)(-x)=\lambda(x)x$, so $\lambda(-x)=-\lambda(x)$. If $\lambda(x_0)>0$ then $\lambda(-x_0)<0$; $S^n$ is connected for $n\ge1$, so the intermediate value theorem produces a zero of $\lambda$, hence of $s$. $\blacksquare$

### Covectors and the exterior algebra

The **cotangent bundle** $T^*M$ has fiber $T^*_pM=(T_pM)^*$, with the dual basis $dx^1|_p,\dots,dx^n|_p$ to $\partial_i|_p$ and transition functions the inverse transposes of those of $TM$. A smooth section is a **1-form**; for $f\in C^\infty(M)$ the **differential** $df$ is the 1-form with $df(X)=Xf$, equal to $\sum_i\partial_if\,dx^i$ in coordinates.

For a finite-dimensional vector space $V$ with $\dim V=n$ let $\Lambda^k(V^*)$ be the space of **alternating** $k$-linear forms on $V$ (linear in each slot, changing sign when two arguments are exchanged). For $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_k\in V^*$ define $\alpha_1\wedge\dots\wedge\alpha_k(v_1,\dots,v_k)=\det\big(\alpha_i(v_j)\big)$, which is alternating because $\det$ is. If $e^1,\dots,e^n$ is a basis of $V^*$ dual to $e_1,\dots,e_n$ and $I=(i_1<\dots<i_k)$, put $e^I=e^{i_1}\wedge\dots\wedge e^{i_k}$.

**Theorem 2.** The $e^I$ with $I$ strictly increasing form a basis of $\Lambda^k(V^*)$; hence $\dim\Lambda^k(V^*)=\binom nk$, and $\Lambda^k(V^*)=0$ for $k>n$.

*Proof.* Evaluate on basis vectors: $e^I(e_{j_1},\dots,e_{j_k})=\det(e^{i_a}(e_{j_b}))$ equals $1$ if $J=I$ (identity matrix), and $0$ if $J$ is another increasing tuple (some row of zeros). An alternating form $\omega$ is determined by its values on tuples $(e_{j_1},\dots,e_{j_k})$ with $j_1<\dots<j_k$: tuples with a repeat give $0$, and any other tuple is a permutation of an increasing one, changing the value by the sign. Therefore $\omega=\sum_I\omega(e_I)\,e^I$ (both sides agree on all increasing tuples), and the $e^I$ are linearly independent by the first computation. $\blacksquare$

The **wedge product** $\Lambda^k\times\Lambda^l\to\Lambda^{k+l}$ is the bilinear extension of $e^I\wedge e^J=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\,e^{K}$, where $K$ is the increasing rearrangement of the concatenation $(I,J)$ by a permutation $\sigma$, and the product is $0$ if $I$ and $J$ share an index. Both $(e^I\wedge e^J)\wedge e^L$ and $e^I\wedge(e^J\wedge e^L)$ equal the sign of the sorting permutation of $(I,J,L)$ times the sorted basis form, so the product is associative. It is **graded commutative**: $\alpha\wedge\beta=(-1)^{kl}\beta\wedge\alpha$ for $\alpha \in\Lambda^k$, $\beta\in\Lambda^l$ (moving one 1-factor past $k$ others costs $(-1)^k$ and there are $l$ factors). In particular $\alpha\wedge\alpha=0$ for a 1-form.

### Differential forms and pullback

A **differential $k$-form** is a smooth section of $\Lambda^kT^*M$; locally $\omega=\sum_I\omega_I\,dx^I$ with smooth coefficients. Let $\Omega^k(M)$ be the space of $k$-forms. For smooth $F\colon M\to N$ the **pullback** $F^*\omega\in\Omega^k(M)$ of $\omega\in\Omega^k(N)$ is $(F^*\omega)_p(v_1,\dots,v_k)=\omega_{F(p)}(dF_pv_1,\dots,dF_pv_k)$. It is linear, satisfies $F^*(\alpha\wedge\beta)=F^*\alpha\wedge F^*\beta$ and $(G\circ F)^*=F^*G^*$, and $F^*(g)=g\circ F$ for functions.

**Theorem 3.** If $F\colon U\to V$ is smooth between open subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$ and $\omega=g\,dy^1\wedge\dots\wedge dy^n$, then $F^*\omega=(g\circ F)\,\det(DF)\,dx^1\wedge\dots\wedge dx^n$.

*Proof.* $F^*dy^i=d(y^i\circ F)=\sum_j\frac{\partial F^i}{\partial x^j}dx^j=:\beta_i$. Then $F^*\omega=(g\circ F)\,\beta_1\wedge\dots\wedge\beta_n$, and evaluating on $(\partial_1,\dots,\partial_n)$ gives $\det\big(\beta_i(\partial_j)\big)=\det\big(\partial F^i/\partial x^j\big)$. A top-degree form is determined by its value on $(\partial_1,\dots,\partial_n)$. $\blacksquare$

### Wedge products by machine

Store a form with constant coefficients as a dict from strictly increasing index tuples to coefficients, e.g. $dx^0\wedge dx^2\mapsto\{(0,2):1\}$ and the empty tuple for a 0-form. The key primitive is sorting with a sign:

```python
def sort_sign(seq):
    """Return (sign, sorted tuple), or (0, None) if an index repeats."""
    seq = list(seq)
    if len(set(seq)) < len(seq):
        return 0, None
    sign = 1
    for i in range(len(seq)):
        for j in range(len(seq) - 1 - i):
            if seq[j] > seq[j + 1]:
                seq[j], seq[j + 1] = seq[j + 1], seq[j]
                sign = -sign
    return sign, tuple(seq)


print(sort_sign((2, 0, 1)), sort_sign((1, 1)))
```

This prints `(1, (0, 1, 2)) (0, None)`: the cycle $(2,0,1)$ is an even permutation. The practice asks for the wedge product built on this.
@example
title: A wedge product of 1-forms
problem: In $\mathbb{R}^3$ compute $(2\,dx-dy+3\,dz)\wedge(dx+4\,dz)$.
step: Expand bilinearly: $2dx\wedge dx+8\,dx\wedge dz-dy\wedge dx-4\,dy\wedge dz+3\,dz\wedge dx+12\,dz\wedge dz$.
step: Use $\alpha\wedge\alpha=0$ and antisymmetry: $dx\wedge dx=dz\wedge dz=0$, $-dy\wedge dx=dx\wedge dy$, $3\,dz\wedge dx=-3\,dx\wedge dz$.
step: Collect: $dx\wedge dy+(8-3)\,dx\wedge dz-4\,dy\wedge dz$.
step: Check the coefficient of $dx\wedge dz$ by the determinant formula: $\det\begin{pmatrix}2&3\\1&4\end{pmatrix}=8-3=5$.
answer: $dx\wedge dy+5\,dx\wedge dz-4\,dy\wedge dz$.
@example
title: Pullback of an area form by polar coordinates
problem: Let $F(r,\theta)=(r\cos\theta,\ r\sin\theta)$. Compute $F^*(dx\wedge dy)$ and $F^*(x\,dx\wedge dy)$.
step: Here $x\circ F=r\cos\theta$, $y\circ F=r\sin\theta$, so $F^*dx=\cos\theta\,dr-r\sin\theta\,d\theta$ and $F^*dy=\sin\theta\,dr+r\cos\theta\,d\theta$.
step: Wedge: $F^*(dx\wedge dy)=(\cos\theta\cdot r\cos\theta-(-r\sin\theta)\sin\theta)\,dr\wedge d\theta=r\,dr\wedge d\theta$, which is the same as $\det DF=r$ from Theorem 3.
step: For $F^*(x\,dx\wedge dy)$ multiply by $x\circ F=r\cos\theta$: the result is $r^2\cos\theta\,dr\wedge d\theta$.
answer: $F^*(dx\wedge dy)=r\,dr\wedge d\theta$ and $F^*(x\,dx\wedge dy)=r^2\cos\theta\,dr\wedge d\theta$.
@mistakes
- Treating $\alpha\wedge\beta$ as commutative: it is graded commutative, and $\alpha\wedge\beta=-\beta\wedge\alpha$ for 1-forms, but $\omega\wedge\eta=\eta\wedge\omega$ when one of them has even degree.
- Forgetting the Jacobian determinant (or taking its absolute value) when pulling back a top form: the pullback of a form is a pullback of a multilinear function and keeps the sign.
- Believing $\omega\wedge\omega=0$ for every form: it holds for odd-degree forms, but $(dx\wedge dy+dz\wedge dw)^2=2\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz\wedge dw\ne0$ in $\mathbb{R}^4$.
- Confusing a vector bundle with its total space as a product: transition functions can be nontrivial (the tautological line bundle), and then no global trivialization exists.

model:
```python
from fractions import Fraction


def _sort_sign(seq):
    seq = list(seq)
    if len(set(seq)) < len(seq):
        return 0, None
    sign = 1
    for i in range(len(seq)):
        for j in range(len(seq) - 1 - i):
            if seq[j] > seq[j + 1]:
                seq[j], seq[j + 1] = seq[j + 1], seq[j]
                sign = -sign
    return sign, tuple(seq)


def wedge(a, b):
    """Wedge product of two forms with constant coefficients."""
    out = {}
    for I, c in a.items():
        for J, d in b.items():
            sign, K = _sort_sign(I + J)
            if sign:
                out[K] = out.get(K, 0) + sign * Fraction(c) * Fraction(d)
    return {k: v for k, v in out.items() if v != 0}
```
hint: For every pair of keys $(I,J)$, concatenate the tuples, sort with the sign of the permutation, skip repeats, and add the product of coefficients.
hint: Terms with the same sorted key must be added together, and keys whose total is zero must be dropped (the product of $(dx+dy)$ and $(dx-dy)$ tests this).
solution: The sign of a permutation is $(-1)^{\text{number of adjacent swaps}}$ in a bubble sort. Accumulating into a dict handles several pairs landing on the same basis form, and dropping zero values makes $\alpha\wedge\alpha=\{\}$. Exact `Fraction`s keep the cancellation exact. This computes wedge products at one point (constant coefficients); for forms with function coefficients one would multiply the coefficient polynomials as well.

prompt: Prove that a vector bundle $\pi\colon E\to M$ of rank $k$ is trivial if and only if it has $k$ sections $s_1,\dots,s_k$ that are linearly independent in every fiber.
hint: For the converse define $\Psi\colon M\times\mathbb{R}^k\to E$ by $\Psi(p,a)=\sum_ia_is_i(p)$ and check smoothness of $\Psi$ and of its inverse in a local trivialization.
solution: ($\Rightarrow$) A trivialization $\Phi\colon E\to M\times\mathbb{R}^k$ gives sections $s_i(p)=\Phi^{-1}(p,e_i)$, smooth as compositions of smooth maps, and linearly independent in each fiber because $\Phi$ is a linear isomorphism on each fiber. ($\Leftarrow$) Define $\Psi\colon M\times\mathbb{R}^k\to E$, $\Psi(p,a)=\sum_ia_is_i(p)$. In a local trivialization $\Phi_U\colon\pi^{-1}(U)\to U\times\mathbb{R}^k$ write $\Phi_U(s_i(p))=(p,S_i(p))$ with $S_i\colon U\to\mathbb{R}^k$ smooth; let $S(p)$ be the matrix with columns $S_i(p)$, smooth in $p$ and invertible because the $s_i(p)$ are independent. Then $\Phi_U\circ\Psi(p,a)=(p,S(p)a)$ is smooth, so $\Psi$ is smooth. It is a bijection linear on fibers and fiber-preserving. Its inverse is, in the trivialization, $(p,b)\mapsto(p,S(p)^{-1}b)$, smooth because matrix inversion is smooth on $GL(k,\mathbb{R})$. So $\Psi$ is a diffeomorphism covering the identity, linear on fibers, and $\Psi^{-1}$ is a global trivialization. $\blacksquare$
rubric: For the forward direction, constructs the sections from a trivialization and notes fiberwise independence.
rubric: For the converse, defines $\Psi$ and expresses it in a local trivialization as $(p,a)\mapsto(p,S(p)a)$ with $S(p)$ invertible.
rubric: Shows smoothness of both $\Psi$ and $\Psi^{-1}$ (using smooth matrix inversion) and that $\Psi$ is linear on fibers.

@quiz self
prompt: Prove that if $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_k$ and $\beta_1,\dots,\beta_l$ are 1-forms, then $(\alpha_1\wedge\dots\wedge\alpha_k)\wedge(\beta_1\wedge\dots\wedge\beta_l)=(-1)^{kl}(\beta_1\wedge\dots\wedge\beta_l)\wedge(\alpha_1\wedge\dots\wedge\alpha_k)$.
hint: The wedge of 1-forms is multilinear and changes sign when two factors are swapped. Count the transpositions needed to move the $\beta$'s to the front.
solution: The wedge of 1-forms $\gamma_1\wedge\dots\wedge\gamma_m$ is alternating in the $\gamma_i$ (swapping two factors swaps two rows of the determinant $\det(\gamma_i(v_j))$, which changes its sign). Start with $\alpha_1\wedge\dots\wedge\alpha_k\wedge\beta_1\wedge\dots\wedge\beta_l$. Moving $\beta_1$ to the front passes it through the $k$ factors $\alpha_k,\dots,\alpha_1$, each exchange contributing a factor $-1$, for a total of $(-1)^k$. Doing the same for $\beta_2,\dots,\beta_l$ in turn (each passes the $k$ $\alpha$'s, since the earlier $\beta$'s are already in front) gives $l$ groups of $k$ exchanges. Hence the sign is $(-1)^{kl}$ and the result is $\beta_1\wedge\dots\wedge\beta_l\wedge\alpha_1\wedge\dots\wedge\alpha_k$. $\blacksquare$
rubric: States that the wedge of 1-forms is alternating (a sign change under each exchange).
rubric: Counts $k$ exchanges for each of the $l$ factors to get $(-1)^{kl}$.
rubric: Arranges the exchanges so the order of the $\beta$'s and of the $\alpha$'s among themselves is preserved.
