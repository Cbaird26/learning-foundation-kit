@article
The fundamental theorem of calculus says $\int_a^bf'(x)\,dx=f(b)-f(a)$: the integral of a derivative over an interval is computed from the boundary of the interval. Stokes's theorem says the same for every $\omega$ and every manifold with boundary: integrating $d\omega$ over $M$ equals integrating $\omega$ over $\partial M$.

### Manifolds with boundary

Let $\mathbb{H}^n=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^n:x_n\ge0\}$. A **smooth manifold with boundary** is a second countable Hausdorff space locally homeomorphic to open subsets of $\mathbb{H}^n$, with smoothly compatible charts (a map on a subset of $\mathbb{H}^n$ is smooth if it extends smoothly to an open set of $\mathbb{R}^n$). The **boundary** $\partial M$ is the set of points mapped to $\{x_n=0\}$ by some chart (this is chart independent by the inverse function theorem). It is an embedded $(n-1)$-manifold without boundary; the points of $M\setminus\partial M$ form the interior. The closed disc is a manifold with boundary the circle; $[a,b]$ has boundary $\{a,b\}$.

If $M$ is oriented, $\partial M$ gets the **induced (Stokes) orientation**: a basis $(v_1,\dots,v_{n-1})$ of $T_p\partial M$ is positive if $(N,v_1,\dots,v_{n-1})$ is a positive basis of $T_pM$ for an outward-pointing vector $N$. For $\mathbb{H}^n$ with its standard orientation, $N=-e_n$ and $\det(-e_n,e_1,\dots,e_{n-1})=(-1)^n$, so $\partial\mathbb{H}^n=\mathbb{R}^{n-1}$ carries $(-1)^n$ times the standard orientation. For $n=1$, a boundary point $p$ is a $0$-manifold and its orientation is a sign $\pm1$ ($+$ at the right end $b$ of $[a,b]$, $-$ at the left end $a$), and $\int_p f=\pm f(p)$.

### The theorem

**Theorem (Stokes).** Let $M$ be an oriented smooth $n$-manifold with boundary ($n\ge1$), $\partial M$ carry the induced orientation, and $\omega$ a smooth $(n-1)$-form on $M$ with compact support. Then
$$\int_Md\omega=\int_{\partial M}\iota^*\omega,\qquad \iota\colon\partial M\hookrightarrow M,$$
and the right side is $0$ if $\partial M=\emptyset$.

*Proof.* **Step 1: $M=\mathbb{H}^n$.** Write $\omega=\sum_{i=1}^n\omega_i\,dx^1\wedge\dots\wedge\widehat{dx^i}\wedge\dots\wedge dx^n$ with $\omega_i$ smooth and compactly supported. Then $d\omega=\sum_i(-1)^{i-1}\partial_i\omega_i\,dx^1\wedge\dots\wedge dx^n$. For $i<n$, Fubini lets us integrate in $x_i$ first over $\mathbb{R}$: $\int_{-\infty}^\infty\partial_i\omega_i\,dx_i=0$ by compact support. For $i=n$: $\int_0^\infty\partial_n\omega_n\,dx_n=-\omega_n(x',0)$, so $\int_{\mathbb{H}^n}d\omega=(-1)^{n-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}(-\omega_n(x',0))\,dx'=(-1)^n\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\omega_n(x',0)\,dx'$. On the boundary $x_n=0$, $dx^n$ pulls back to $0$, so $\iota^*\omega=\omega_n(x',0)\,dx^1\wedge\dots\wedge dx^{n-1}$, and with the orientation $(-1)^n\cdot$standard of $\partial\mathbb{H}^n$ we get $\int_{\partial\mathbb{H}^n}\omega=(-1)^n\int\omega_n(x',0)\,dx'$. The two sides agree. The same computation with $\mathbb{R}^n$ (no boundary term) gives $\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}d\omega=0$.

**Step 2: $\operatorname{supp}\omega$ in one oriented chart.** If $\varphi\colon U\to\varphi(U)$ is an oriented chart with $\varphi(U)$ open in $\mathbb{H}^n$ or $\mathbb{R}^n$, then $\int_Md\omega=\int_{\varphi(U)}(\varphi^{-1})^*d\omega=\int d\big((\varphi^{-1})^*\omega\big)$ by naturality of $d$, which equals the boundary integral by Step 1; and the induced orientation of $\partial M$ was defined so that $\varphi$ carries it to that of $\partial\mathbb{H}^n$.

**Step 3: general.** Let $\{\rho_\alpha\}$ be a partition of unity subordinate to an oriented atlas. Only finitely many $\rho_\alpha\omega$ are nonzero ($\operatorname{supp}\omega$ is compact), and $\omega=\sum\rho_\alpha\omega$, hence $d\omega=\sum d(\rho_\alpha\omega)$ and by Step 2, $\int_Md\omega=\sum_\alpha\int_Md(\rho_\alpha\omega)=\sum_\alpha\int_{\partial M}\rho_\alpha\omega=\int_{\partial M}\omega$. $\blacksquare$

### Special cases and sharpness

* $n=1$, $M=[a,b]$, $\omega=f$: $\int_a^bf'\,dx=f(b)-f(a)$.
* $n=2$ (**Green's theorem**): for a compact region $D\subset\mathbb{R}^2$ with smooth boundary, $\oint_{\partial D}P\,dx+Q\,dy=\iint_D(\partial_xQ-\partial_yP)\,dx\,dy$, boundary oriented counterclockwise (outward normal first).
* $n=3$ (**divergence theorem**): for $\omega=A\,dy\wedge dz+B\,dz\wedge dx+C\,dx\wedge dy$, $\iint_{\partial\Omega}\omega=\iiint_\Omega(\partial_xA+\partial_yB+\partial_zC)\,dV$.
* $\int_Md\omega=0$ if $M$ is closed (compact, no boundary). Hence **a volume form on a compact oriented closed manifold is never exact** (quiz), which leads to de Rham cohomology.

**Compact support is essential.** On $M=\mathbb{R}$ (no boundary), $\omega=\arctan x$ has $\int d\omega=\pi$ but $\partial M=\emptyset$. On the closed disc minus its center, $\omega=\dfrac{-y\,dx+x\,dy}{x^2+y^2}$ is closed, so $\int_Md\omega=0$, while $\int_{\partial M}\omega=2\pi$: the manifold is not compact and $\omega$ does not have compact support. Smoothness on all of $M$ is likewise needed.

### A numerical check

Python can test Green's theorem on the unit square with the midpoint rule on $n$ subintervals per edge (error $O(1/n^2)$), comparing the counterclockwise boundary integral with the area integral:

```python
from math import cos, e, exp, sin


def boundary_square(P, Q, n):
    """Counterclockwise integral of P dx + Q dy over the unit square."""
    h = 1.0 / n
    total = 0.0
    for k in range(n):
        s = (k + 0.5) * h
        total += P(s, 0.0) * h   # bottom edge, left to right
        total += Q(1.0, s) * h   # right edge, upward
        total -= P(s, 1.0) * h   # top edge, right to left
        total -= Q(0.0, s) * h   # left edge, downward
    return total


def area_integral(f, n):
    """Midpoint rule for the integral of f over the unit square."""
    h = 1.0 / n
    return sum(f((i + 0.5) * h, (j + 0.5) * h)
               for i in range(n) for j in range(n)) * h * h


P = lambda x, y: sin(y)
Q = lambda x, y: x * exp(y)
b = boundary_square(P, Q, 100)
a = area_integral(lambda x, y: exp(y) - cos(y), 100)
print(abs(b - a) < 1e-3, abs(b - (e - 1 - sin(1))) < 1e-3)
```

This prints `True True`: the boundary integral and the area integral of $\partial_xQ-\partial_yP=e^y-\cos y$ agree within $10^{-3}$, and both are within $10^{-3}$ of the exact value $e-1-\sin1\approx0.8768$. A grid check at one resolution is a sanity test, not a proof; the proof is Steps 1 to 3.
@example
title: Area of an ellipse by Stokes
problem: Let $D=\{x^2/4+y^2\le1\}$. Use Stokes's theorem to compute $\oint_{\partial D}(-y\,dx+x\,dy)$, counterclockwise.
step: Let $\omega=-y\,dx+x\,dy$. Then $d\omega=dx\wedge dy+dx\wedge dy=2\,dx\wedge dy$.
step: $D$ is a compact region with smooth boundary, oriented by $dx\wedge dy$, and $\partial D$ gets the counterclockwise orientation. Stokes gives $\oint_{\partial D}\omega=\iint_D2\,dx\,dy=2\operatorname{area}(D)$.
step: The ellipse with semi-axes $2$ and $1$ has area $\pi\cdot2\cdot1=2\pi$ (use the substitution $x=2u$, $dx\,dy=2\,du\,dy$ on the unit disc).
step: Therefore the integral is $2\cdot2\pi=4\pi$. A direct check with $c(t)=(2\cos t,\sin t)$: $-y\,dx+x\,dy=(\sin^2t\cdot2+2\cos^2t)\,dt=2\,dt$, and $\int_0^{2\pi}2\,dt=4\pi$.
answer: $4\pi$
@example
title: A closed form that is not exact, and Stokes on the annulus
problem: Let $\omega=\dfrac{-y\,dx+x\,dy}{x^2+y^2}$ on $\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{0\}$. Show $d\omega=0$, compute $\oint_{|z|=1}\omega$ and $\oint_{|z|=2}\omega$ (counterclockwise), and explain why Stokes's theorem does not force them to be zero.
step: With $P=-y/(x^2+y^2)$ and $Q=x/(x^2+y^2)$: $\partial_xQ=\dfrac{y^2-x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}=\partial_yP$, so $d\omega=0$ away from the origin.
step: On the circle of radius $r$, $c(t)=(r\cos t,r\sin t)$: $-y\,dx+x\,dy=r^2\,dt$ and $x^2+y^2=r^2$, so $\omega=dt$ and the integral is $\int_0^{2\pi}dt=2\pi$ for every $r$, in particular for $r=1$ and $r=2$.
step: Apply Stokes to the compact annulus $A=\{1\le r\le2\}$: its boundary is the outer circle counterclockwise and the inner circle clockwise (outward normal first), so $0=\int_Ad\omega=2\pi-2\pi$. This is consistent.
step: There is no contradiction with a single circle: the disc $\{r\le1\}$ is not contained in the domain of $\omega$ (the origin is missing). If $\omega$ were $df$ on $\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{0\}$, then $\oint\omega=\oint df=0$ by Stokes on the circle (a closed manifold), but it is $2\pi$; so $\omega$ is not exact.
answer: $d\omega=0$, both circle integrals equal $2\pi$, and $\omega$ is closed but not exact.
@mistakes
- Using the wrong boundary orientation: with outward normal first, the boundary of a planar region is traversed counterclockwise, and the inner boundary circles of an annulus are traversed clockwise.
- Applying Stokes to a form that is singular inside the region, such as $d\theta$ on a disc containing the origin: the form must be smooth on all of $M$.
- Forgetting compact support or compactness of $M$: $\int_{\mathbb{R}}d(\arctan x)=\pi\ne0$.
- Mixing up degrees: $\omega$ has degree $n-1$ so that $d\omega$ has degree $n$ and can be integrated over $M^n$.

model:
```python
import math


def circle_integral(P, Q, cx, cy, R, n):
    """Midpoint-rule line integral of P dx + Q dy over a circle."""
    h = 2 * math.pi / n
    total = 0.0
    for k in range(n):
        t = (k + 0.5) * h
        x = cx + R * math.cos(t)
        y = cy + R * math.sin(t)
        dx = -R * math.sin(t) * h
        dy = R * math.cos(t) * h
        total += P(x, y) * dx + Q(x, y) * dy
    return total
```
hint: At each step evaluate $P$ and $Q$ at the midpoint and multiply by the components of the displacement.
hint: A sign error in $dx$ reverses the orientation of the $P\,dx$ part; the test with $P=y$ detects it.
solution: Parametrizing $c(t)=(c_x+R\cos t,\ c_y+R\sin t)$ gives $c'(t)=(-R\sin t,\ R\cos t)$, so $\oint P\,dx+Q\,dy=\int_0^{2\pi}(P\,x'+Q\,y')\,dt$, approximated by the midpoint rule. For trigonometric polynomial integrands the rule with $n$ larger than the degree is exact up to rounding, which is why the tolerance is small. By Green's theorem the tests have known values: $2\cdot$area for $-y\,dx+x\,dy$, and $-\pi$ for $y\,dx$ (since $\oint y\,dx=-\text{area}$). A numerical agreement checks the implementation, it does not prove Stokes's theorem.

prompt: Prove that there is no smooth map $r\colon B^n\to S^{n-1}$ from the closed unit ball to its boundary sphere ($n\ge2$) with $r(x)=x$ for all $x\in S^{n-1}$ (a smooth retraction).
hint: Pull back a volume form $\mu$ of $S^{n-1}$ by $r$, and apply Stokes's theorem on $B^n$.
hint: Compute $d(r^*\mu)$, and use $\int_{S^{n-1}}\mu\ne0$.
solution: Suppose $r$ is a smooth retraction. Let $\mu$ be a nowhere-vanishing $(n-1)$-form on the orientable manifold $S^{n-1}$ (orientable by the lesson on orientation), positively oriented, so that $\int_{S^{n-1}}\mu>0$ (the integrand is positive in every oriented chart). Let $\iota\colon S^{n-1}\hookrightarrow B^n$ be the inclusion; $r\circ\iota=\mathrm{id}$, so $\iota^*r^*\mu=\mu$. The form $r^*\mu$ is a smooth $(n-1)$-form on the compact oriented manifold with boundary $B^n$, with $\partial B^n=S^{n-1}$. Moreover $d(r^*\mu)=r^*(d\mu)=0$, because $d\mu$ is an $n$-form on the $(n-1)$-dimensional manifold $S^{n-1}$ and so vanishes. By Stokes's theorem, $0=\int_{B^n}d(r^*\mu)=\int_{S^{n-1}}\iota^*r^*\mu=\int_{S^{n-1}}\mu>0$, a contradiction. Hence no smooth retraction exists. $\blacksquare$

prompt: Let $M$ be a compact oriented $n$-manifold without boundary, $n\ge1$, and $\omega$ a nowhere-vanishing $n$-form that is positive with respect to the orientation. Prove that $\omega$ is not exact.
hint: Compare $\int_M\omega$ with $\int_Md\eta$ for a putative primitive $\eta$.
solution: In every oriented chart $\omega=f\,dx^1\wedge\dots\wedge dx^n$ with $f>0$, and $\omega$ has compact support because $M$ is compact. If $\operatorname{supp}$ is covered by finitely many charts and we use a partition of unity $\{\rho_\alpha\}$, then $\int_M\omega=\sum_\alpha\int\rho_\alpha f_\alpha\,dx$ is a sum of nonnegative terms, at least one of which is positive (some $\rho_\alpha$ is positive on a nonempty open set), hence $\int_M\omega>0$. If $\omega=d\eta$ for some smooth $(n-1)$-form $\eta$, then $\eta$ has compact support (as $M$ is compact) and Stokes's theorem gives $\int_M\omega=\int_{\partial M}\eta=0$ because $\partial M=\emptyset$. This is a contradiction, so $\omega$ is not exact. $\blacksquare$
