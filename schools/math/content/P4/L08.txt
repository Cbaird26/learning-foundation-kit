@article
Two vector fields $X$ and $Y$ each generate a flow. If you flow along $X$ for time $s$, then along $Y$ for time $t$, do you arrive where you would have by doing $Y$ then $X$? Usually not, and the failure, to second order, is measured by the Lie bracket $[X,Y]$.

### The bracket

Think of a vector field as a derivation $X\colon C^\infty(M)\to C^\infty(M)$. The composite $XY$ is not a derivation (it has second-order terms), but the commutator is. The **Lie bracket** is $[X,Y]f=X(Yf)-Y(Xf)$.

**Proposition 1.** $[X,Y]$ is a smooth vector field, and in a chart
$$[X,Y]=\sum_j\Big(\sum_i X^i\frac{\partial Y^j}{\partial x^i}-Y^i\frac{\partial X^j}{\partial x^i}\Big)\frac{\partial}{\partial x^j}.$$

*Proof.* Linearity is clear. For the Leibniz rule: $[X,Y](fg)=X(fYg+gYf)-Y(fXg+gXf)=(Xf)(Yg)+f\,XYg+(Xg)(Yf)+g\,XYf-(Yf)(Xg)-f\,YXg-(Yg)(Xf)-g\,YXf=f[X,Y]g+g[X,Y]f$. A derivation at each point is determined by its values on the coordinate functions (Theorem 1 of the lesson on tangent vectors), and $[X,Y]x^j=X(Y^j)-Y(X^j)$. $\blacksquare$

**Proposition 2.** For smooth vector fields $X,Y,Z$ and function $f$: (a) $[X,Y]=-[Y,X]$, and bilinearity over $\mathbb{R}$; (b) $[X,fY]=f[X,Y]+(Xf)Y$; (c) **Jacobi identity**: $[X,[Y,Z]]+[Y,[Z,X]]+[Z,[X,Y]]=0$.

*Proof.* (a) is clear from the definition. (b): $[X,fY]g=X(fYg)-fY(Xg)=(Xf)Yg+fXYg-fYXg$. (c): expanding, $[X,[Y,Z]]=XYZ-XZY-YZX+ZYX$ (as operators), and the cyclic sum has twelve terms in which each of the six orderings $XYZ, XZY, YXZ, YZX, ZXY, ZYX$ appears once with each sign, so they cancel. $\blacksquare$

Consequently the bracket is **not** $C^\infty(M)$-bilinear: $[fX,Y]=f[X,Y]-(Yf)X$.

**Naturality.** If $F\colon M\to N$ is a diffeomorphism and $F_*X$ is the field $(F_*X)_{F(p)}=dF_p(X_p)$, then $(F_*X)f\circ F=X(f\circ F)$, hence $(F_*[X,Y])f\circ F=[X,Y](f\circ F)=X(Y(f\circ F))-Y(X(f\circ F))=[F_*X,F_*Y]f\circ F$, that is, $F_*[X,Y]=[F_*X,F_*Y]$.

**Theorem 3 (stated without proof).** $[X,Y]=\frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}(\theta_{-t})_*Y$, with $\theta$ the flow of $X$ (the Lie derivative $\mathcal{L}_XY$). Consequently, for complete $X, Y$ the flows commute, $\theta_s\circ\varphi_t=\varphi_t\circ\theta_s$ for all $s,t$ (with $\varphi$ the flow of $Y$), if and only if $[X,Y]=0$. The "only if" direction is immediate from the formula; "if" needs an ODE argument. For example $\partial_x$ and $\partial_y$ commute.

### Lie groups

A **Lie group** is a smooth manifold $G$ with a group structure whose multiplication and inversion are smooth. Examples: $(\mathbb{R}^n,+)$, $S^1$, the open subset $GL(n,\mathbb{R})\subseteq M_n(\mathbb{R})$ where $\det\neq0$, and $SL(n,\mathbb{R})$ and $O(n)$ (regular level sets by the previous lessons, with multiplication and inversion smooth because they are restrictions of smooth maps to embedded submanifolds). Left translation $L_g(h)=gh$ is a diffeomorphism. A vector field is **left-invariant** if $(L_g)_*X=X$ for all $g$.

**Theorem 4.** The map $X\mapsto X_e$ is a linear isomorphism from the left-invariant vector fields onto $T_eG$; left-invariant fields are smooth and complete, and the bracket of two left-invariant fields is left-invariant. Hence $\mathfrak{g}=T_eG$ is a Lie algebra under $[v,w]=[X_v,X_w]_e$.

*Proof.* Given $v\in T_eG$ define $X_g=d(L_g)_e v$; invariance follows from $L_{h}\circ L_g=L_{hg}$ and the chain rule, and any left-invariant field has $X_g=d(L_g)_eX_e$, so the map is bijective. Smoothness: $(Xf)(g)=v\big(h\mapsto f(gh)\big)$, and $(g,h)\mapsto f(gh)$ is smooth on $G\times G$, so differentiating in $h$ in the direction $v$ gives a smooth function of $g$. Completeness: let $\gamma\colon(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to G$ be the integral curve with $\gamma(0)=e$; by invariance $L_g\circ\gamma$ is an integral curve from $g$ for every $g$, so $\theta$ is defined on $(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\times G$ with uniform $\varepsilon$, and the group law extends $\gamma$ to all of $\mathbb{R}$. The bracket: by naturality $(L_g)_*[X,Y]=[(L_g)_*X,(L_g)_*Y]=[X,Y]$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 5.** For $G=GL(n,\mathbb{R})$, $\mathfrak{g}=M_n(\mathbb{R})$ with bracket $[A,B]=AB-BA$.

*Proof.* $G$ is open in $M_n(\mathbb{R})$ with coordinates $g_{kl}$. Since $L_g$ is linear, the left-invariant field with $X_e=A$ is $X_A|_g=gA$, that is, $X_A(g_{kl})=(gA)_{kl}=\sum_mg_{km}A_{ml}$. Then
$$[X_A,X_B]g_{kl}=X_A\Big(\sum_mg_{km}B_{ml}\Big)-X_B\Big(\sum_mg_{km}A_{ml}\Big)=(gAB)_{kl}-(gBA)_{kl}=(g[A,B])_{kl},$$
so $[X_A,X_B]=X_{[A,B]}$. $\blacksquare$

The integral curve of $X_A$ from $I$ is $t\mapsto e^{tA}$, since $\frac{d}{dt}e^{tA}=e^{tA}A$. Beware the sign: for the *linear* fields $\dot{\mathbf{x}}=A\mathbf{x}$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$ the bracket is $BA-AB$ (practice).

### Brackets by machine

Polynomial vector fields are stored as lists of polynomials, each a dict from exponent tuples to `Fraction` coefficients. Differentiating a polynomial is the only new primitive:

```python
from fractions import Fraction


def diff_poly(P, k):
    """Partial derivative of P with respect to variable k."""
    out = {}
    for exps, c in P.items():
        if exps[k] > 0:
            e = list(exps)
            e[k] -= 1
            out[tuple(e)] = out.get(tuple(e), 0) + Fraction(c) * exps[k]
    return out


print(diff_poly({(3, 1): 2, (0, 5): 1}, 1))
```

The output is `{(3, 0): Fraction(2, 1), (0, 4): Fraction(5, 1)}`. A bracket then needs products and sums of polynomials, which the practice asks you to write. An exact computation proves the bracket of those two specific fields; a pattern across examples would not prove an identity.
@example
title: A bracket in two ways
problem: Compute $[x\partial_y,\ y\partial_x]$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$.
step: With $X=x\partial_y$ ($X^x=0$, $X^y=x$) and $Y=y\partial_x$ ($Y^x=y$, $Y^y=0$), the formula gives $[X,Y]^j=X(Y^j)-Y(X^j)$.
step: $j=x$: $X(y)-Y(0)=x\cdot1-0=x$. $j=y$: $X(0)-Y(x)=0-y\cdot1=-y$.
step: So $[X,Y]=x\partial_x-y\partial_y$.
step: Check on $f=x^2y$: $Yf=y\cdot2xy=2xy^2$, $X(Yf)=x\cdot4xy=4x^2y$; $Xf=x\cdot x^2=x^3$, $Y(Xf)=y\cdot3x^2=3x^2y$; difference $x^2y$, and $(x\partial_x-y\partial_y)(x^2y)=2x^2y-x^2y=x^2y$. The two agree.
answer: $[x\partial_y,\,y\partial_x]=x\partial_x-y\partial_y$.
@example
title: Brackets of the rotation fields on $\mathbb{R}^3$
problem: Let $R_1=z\partial_y-y\partial_z$, $R_2=x\partial_z-z\partial_x$, $R_3=y\partial_x-x\partial_y$ (infinitesimal rotations about the three axes). Compute $[R_1,R_2]$.
step: Components: $R_1=(0,\ z,\ -y)$, $R_2=(-z,\ 0,\ x)$ in the order $(\partial_x,\partial_y,\partial_z)$.
step: $[R_1,R_2]^j=R_1(R_2^j)-R_2(R_1^j)$. For $j=x$: $R_1(-z)=-(-y)=y$ and $R_2(0)=0$, giving $y$.
step: For $j=y$: $R_1(0)=0$ and $R_2(z)=x$, so $0-x=-x$. For $j=z$: $R_1(x)=0$ and $R_2(-y)=0$, giving $0$.
step: Hence $[R_1,R_2]=y\partial_x-x\partial_y=R_3$, and cyclically $[R_2,R_3]=R_1$, $[R_3,R_1]=R_2$: the Lie algebra of $SO(3)$ in this basis.
answer: $[R_1,R_2]=R_3$.
@mistakes
- Using $XY$ instead of $XY-YX$: the composite of two vector fields is a second-order operator, not a vector field.
- Dropping the second term of the coordinate formula, or getting its sign wrong: $[X,Y]^j=X(Y^j)-Y(X^j)$, in that order.
- Treating the bracket as $C^\infty(M)$-bilinear: $[fX,Y]=f[X,Y]-(Yf)X$.
- Mixing up the two sign conventions: the left-invariant fields on $GL(n,\mathbb{R})$ give $AB-BA$, but the linear vector fields $\dot{\mathbf{x}}=A\mathbf{x}$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$ give $BA-AB$.

model:
```python
from fractions import Fraction


def _diff(P, k):
    out = {}
    for exps, c in P.items():
        if exps[k] > 0:
            e = list(exps)
            e[k] -= 1
            out[tuple(e)] = out.get(tuple(e), 0) + Fraction(c) * exps[k]
    return out


def _mul(P, Q):
    out = {}
    for e1, c1 in P.items():
        for e2, c2 in Q.items():
            e = tuple(a + b for a, b in zip(e1, e2))
            out[e] = out.get(e, 0) + Fraction(c1) * Fraction(c2)
    return out


def bracket(X, Y):
    """Components of the Lie bracket of two polynomial vector fields."""
    n = len(X)
    result = []
    for j in range(n):
        total = {}
        for i in range(n):
            for e, c in _mul(X[i], _diff(Y[j], i)).items():
                total[e] = total.get(e, 0) + c
            for e, c in _mul(Y[i], _diff(X[j], i)).items():
                total[e] = total.get(e, 0) - c
        result.append({e: c for e, c in total.items() if c != 0})
    return result
```
hint: Write helpers for the derivative of a polynomial, the product of two polynomials and addition into a dict, then loop over $j$ and $i$.
hint: Remember to drop coefficients that cancel to zero before returning.
solution: For each component $j$ the formula is a sum over $i$ of two polynomial products, one added and one subtracted. Exact `Fraction` arithmetic makes the cancellation exact, which is why zero coefficients can be removed safely; the test `bracket(X, X) == [{}, {}]` checks both antisymmetry and that canceled terms are dropped. Reversing the order of the two terms flips the sign, which the tests for `bracket(X, Y)` and `bracket(Y, X)` catch.

prompt: On $\mathbb{R}^n$ let $X_A=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}x_j\,\partial_i$ and $X_B=\sum_{i,j}B_{ij}x_j\,\partial_i$ be the linear vector fields $\dot{\mathbf{x}}=A\mathbf{x}$ and $\dot{\mathbf{x}}=B\mathbf{x}$. Prove that $[X_A,X_B]=X_{BA-AB}$, and explain why this sign differs from $[X_A,X_B]=X_{AB-BA}$ for left-invariant fields on $GL(n,\mathbb{R})$.
hint: Use $[X,Y]^j=X(Y^j)-Y(X^j)$ with $X^j=(A\mathbf{x})_j$ and $Y^j=(B\mathbf{x})_j$.
solution: The components are $X_A^i=(A\mathbf{x})_i=\sum_kA_{ik}x_k$ and $X_B^j=(B\mathbf{x})_j=\sum_lB_{jl}x_l$. Then $X_A(X_B^j)=\sum_iX_A^i\,\partial_i(X_B^j)=\sum_iX_A^iB_{ji}=\sum_iB_{ji}(A\mathbf{x})_i=(BA\mathbf{x})_j$, and similarly $X_B(X_A^j)=(AB\mathbf{x})_j$. By the coordinate formula $[X_A,X_B]^j=(BA\mathbf{x})_j-(AB\mathbf{x})_j=((BA-AB)\mathbf{x})_j$, so $[X_A,X_B]=X_{BA-AB}$. The sign differs because of where the matrix sits. On $GL(n)$ the field $X_B$ has components $(gB)_{km}$, with $B$ multiplying the variable $g$ from the right, so applying $X_A$ replaces $g$ by $gA$ and gives $gAB$. For the linear fields the components are $(B\mathbf{x})_j$, with $B$ multiplying the variable from the left, so applying $X_A$ replaces $\mathbf{x}$ by $A\mathbf{x}$ and gives $B(A\mathbf{x})$, reversing the order. $\blacksquare$

@quiz self
prompt: Prove that for smooth vector fields $X,Y$ and smooth functions $f,g$ one has $[fX,gY]=fg[X,Y]+f(Xg)\,Y-g(Yf)\,X$.
hint: Apply both sides to a function $h$ and expand with the Leibniz rule.
solution: Let $h$ be smooth. Then $[fX,gY]h=fX(gYh)-gY(fXh)$. By the Leibniz rule $X(gYh)=(Xg)(Yh)+g\,XYh$ and $Y(fXh)=(Yf)(Xh)+f\,YXh$. So $[fX,gY]h=f(Xg)Yh+fg\,XYh-g(Yf)Xh-gf\,YXh=fg\,(XYh-YXh)+f(Xg)\,Yh-g(Yf)\,Xh$. This is the value on $h$ of $fg[X,Y]+f(Xg)Y-g(Yf)X$. Since two vector fields that agree as operators on all smooth functions are equal, the identity follows. $\blacksquare$
