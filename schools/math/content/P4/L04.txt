@article
Does the map $t \mapsto (t^2, t^3)$ from $\mathbb{R}$ to $\mathbb{R}^2$ draw a smooth curve? It is smooth and injective, and it is even a homeomorphism onto its image, a cusp. But the cusp is not a smooth submanifold, and the reason is that the differential $(2t, 3t^2)$ vanishes at $t = 0$. The rank of the differential decides what a smooth map looks like locally.

### Definitions

Let $F\colon M\to N$ be smooth, $\dim M = m$, $\dim N = n$. The **rank** of $F$ at $p$ is the rank of the linear map $dF_p$ (the rank of its Jacobian matrix in any charts). $F$ is an **immersion** if $dF_p$ is injective for every $p$ (so $m\le n$), a **submersion** if every $dF_p$ is surjective (so $m \ge n$), and a **local diffeomorphism** if every $dF_p$ is an isomorphism. An **embedding** is an immersion that is also a homeomorphism onto its image $F(M)$, with the subspace topology of $N$.

**Theorem (inverse function theorem on manifolds).** If $F\colon M\to N$ is smooth and $dF_p$ is an isomorphism, then there are open neighborhoods $U\ni p$ and $V \ni F(p)$ such that $F|_U\colon U\to V$ is a diffeomorphism.

This is the Euclidean inverse function theorem from analysis (assumed), applied to a coordinate representation; charts carry the conclusion back to $M$ because the rank of $dF_p$ does not depend on the charts.

### The local forms

**Theorem 1 (local immersion theorem).** If $dF_p$ is injective, there are charts $(U,\varphi)$ at $p$ and $(V,\psi)$ at $F(p)$ with $F(U)\subseteq V$ and $\psi\circ F\circ\varphi^{-1}(x) = (x, 0)\in\mathbb{R}^n$.

*Proof.* Work with $F\colon W\to\mathbb{R}^n$, $W\subseteq\mathbb{R}^m$ open, $F(0)=0$. Permuting coordinates of $\mathbb{R}^n$ we may assume the first $m$ rows of the Jacobian at $0$ form an invertible $m\times m$ block $A$ (an injective $n\times m$ matrix has $m$ independent rows). Write $F = (F', F'')$ and define $G\colon W\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}\to\mathbb{R}^n$ by $G(x,y) = (F'(x),\, F''(x) + y)$. Its Jacobian at $0$ is $\begin{pmatrix}A & 0\\ B & I\end{pmatrix}$, invertible. By the inverse function theorem $G$ is a diffeomorphism from a neighborhood of $0$ onto a neighborhood of $0$, and $G(x,0) = F(x)$. Use $G^{-1}$ as the chart near $F(p)$: then $G^{-1}\circ F(x) = (x,0)$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 2 (local submersion theorem).** If $dF_p$ is surjective, there are charts with $\psi\circ F\circ\varphi^{-1}(x_1,\dots,x_m) = (x_1,\dots,x_n)$.

*Proof.* Again $F\colon W\to\mathbb{R}^n$, $F(0)=0$, and after permuting the coordinates of $\mathbb{R}^m$ the first $n$ columns of the Jacobian form an invertible block $A$. Define $H(x) = (F(x),\, x_{n+1},\dots,x_m)$. Its Jacobian at $0$ is $\begin{pmatrix}A & B\\ 0 & I\end{pmatrix}$, invertible, so $H$ is a diffeomorphism near $0$, and $F = \pi\circ H$ with $\pi$ the projection to the first $n$ coordinates. Using $H$ as the chart on the source gives $F\circ H^{-1} = \pi$. $\blacksquare$

**Constant rank theorem (stated without proof; it is proved by applying the inverse function theorem to both source and target).** If $\operatorname{rank}dF_q = r$ for all $q$ in a neighborhood of $p$, there are charts in which $F(x_1,\dots,x_m) = (x_1,\dots,x_r,0,\dots,0)$. The *constant* rank near $p$ is essential: $F(x)=x^2$ has rank $0$ at $x=0$ but rank $1$ nearby, and no such charts exist.

### Consequences

**Corollary 1.** A submersion is an open map. Indeed, if $W\subseteq M$ is open and $p\in W$, Theorem 2 gives a neighborhood $U\subseteq W$ of $p$ on which $F$ is $\pi\circ H$ with $H$ a diffeomorphism onto an open set and $\pi$ open, so $F(U)$ is open; $F(W)$ is a union of such sets.

**Corollary 2.** If $F$ is an immersion, every $p$ has a neighborhood $U$ such that $F|_U$ is an embedding (by Theorem 1, $F|_U$ is a homeomorphism onto the slice $\{y=0\}$).

**Corollary 3.** An injective immersion $F\colon M\to N$ with $M$ compact is an embedding: $F$ is a continuous bijection onto $F(M)$, closed because compact sets map to compact, hence closed, sets in the Hausdorff space $N$; a closed continuous bijection is a homeomorphism onto its image.

### Why "injective immersion" is not "embedding"

The map $\gamma(t) = (\sin 2t,\; \sin t)$ on $(-\pi,\pi)$ is an injective immersion into $\mathbb{R}^2$ whose image is a figure eight (second example). A line $t\mapsto(e^{it}, e^{i\alpha t})$ in the torus $S^1\times S^1$ with $\alpha$ irrational is another: injective, immersive, dense image, and the subspace topology on the image is not the topology of $\mathbb{R}$. The cusp above is a homeomorphism onto its image but fails to be an immersion at one point. All three conditions (immersion, injective, homeomorphism onto the image) are independent requirements on an embedding.

### Deciding by computation

To decide where a polynomial map is an immersion, compute the rank of its Jacobian: the rank is $m$ exactly when some $m\times m$ minor is nonzero. For $F(u,v)=(uv, v, u^2)$ see the first example. Exact rational arithmetic matters: a floating-point rank test cannot distinguish a tiny nonzero minor from zero. Row reduction over `Fraction`s gives the exact rank, as in the code item below; but only an algebraic argument proves that the rank drops at *no other* point.
@example
title: Where is a polynomial map an immersion?
problem: Let $F\colon\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^3$, $F(u,v) = (uv,\; v,\; u^2)$. Find the points where $F$ is not an immersion, and decide whether $F$ is injective.
step: The Jacobian is $J = \begin{pmatrix} v & u \\ 0 & 1 \\ 2u & 0\end{pmatrix}$, with columns $c_1 = (v, 0, 2u)$ and $c_2 = (u, 1, 0)$.
step: $dF$ is injective exactly when the columns are linearly independent. Suppose $\alpha c_1 + \beta c_2 = 0$. The second row gives $\beta = 0$, and then $\alpha(v, 2u) = (0,0)$.
step: If $(u,v)\ne(0,0)$ then $\alpha=0$, so the columns are independent. At $(0,0)$, $c_1 = 0$ and the rank is $1$. So $F$ fails to be an immersion at the origin only, and its restriction to $\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{0\}$ is an immersion.
step: Injectivity: $F(u,0) = (0,0,u^2) = F(-u,0)$, so $F$ is not injective; it is the Whitney umbrella, with a line of double points on the $z$-axis.
answer: $F$ is an immersion except at $(0,0)$, where the rank is $1$, and $F$ is not injective.
@example
title: An injective immersion that is not an embedding
problem: Show that $\gamma\colon(-\pi,\pi)\to\mathbb{R}^2$, $\gamma(t) = (\sin2t,\sin t)$, is an injective immersion but not an embedding.
step: Immersion: $\gamma'(t) = (2\cos2t,\cos t)$. If $\cos t = 0$ then $t = \pm\pi/2$ and $\cos 2t = \cos(\pm\pi) = -1$, so the first component is $-2\ne0$. Hence $\gamma'(t)\ne0$ for all $t$.
step: Injective: suppose $\gamma(s)=\gamma(t)$. Then $\sin s = \sin t$, so $t = s$ or $t = \pi - s$ (mod $2\pi$, within $(-\pi,\pi)$). If $t = \pi - s \ne s$, then $\sin2t = \sin(2\pi - 2s) = -\sin 2s$, so $\sin 2s = -\sin 2s$ and $\sin 2s = 0$, giving $s \in\{0, \pm\pi/2\}$; then $t=\pi-s\in\{\pi, \pm\pi/2\}$. Only $s = t = \pm\pi/2$ lies in the interval, so $t=s$.
step: Not an embedding: let $t_k = \pi - 1/k$. Then $\gamma(t_k) = (-\sin(2/k),\;\sin(1/k)) \to (0,0)=\gamma(0)$. If $\gamma$ were a homeomorphism onto its image, $t_k = \gamma^{-1}(\gamma(t_k))$ would converge to $\gamma^{-1}(0,0) = 0$, but $t_k\to\pi\notin(-\pi,\pi)$.
answer: $\gamma$ is an injective immersion whose inverse on the image is not continuous at the origin, so it is not an embedding.
@mistakes
- Believing that "injective immersion" means embedding: the figure-eight and the irrational line on the torus are injective immersions whose images carry a coarser topology than the domain.
- Using a floating-point rank (for example from a tolerance on singular values) to decide an immersion: a rank drop at an isolated point may be missed by sampling; decide with an exact minor.
- Applying the constant rank theorem when the rank is only constant at the point: the rank must be constant on a neighborhood.
- Checking only that the image is smooth-looking: the cusp $y^2=x^3$ and the nodal curve are images of smooth maps, but the first is not an immersion and the second is not an embedding.

```python
from fractions import Fraction


def _rank(rows):
    """Exact rank of a matrix by Gaussian elimination."""
    a = [[Fraction(v) for v in row] for row in rows]
    rank = 0
    ncols = len(a[0]) if a else 0
    for col in range(ncols):
        pivot = None
        for r in range(rank, len(a)):
            if a[r][col] != 0:
                pivot = r
                break
        if pivot is None:
            continue
        a[rank], a[pivot] = a[pivot], a[rank]
        for r in range(rank + 1, len(a)):
            factor = a[r][col] / a[rank][col]
            a[r] = [x - factor * y for x, y in zip(a[r], a[rank])]
        rank += 1
    return rank


def non_immersion_points(J, pts):
    """Points where the Jacobian has rank below its number of columns."""
    bad = []
    for p in pts:
        rows = J(p)
        if _rank(rows) < len(rows[0]):
            bad.append(p)
    return bad
```
hint: Compute the rank by Gaussian elimination, moving on to the next column when no row at or below the current one has a nonzero entry there.
hint: The first row of a Jacobian may be zero (a test does this), so search the rows below for a pivot instead of assuming the diagonal entry works.
solution: The map is an immersion at $p$ exactly when the $n\times m$ Jacobian has rank $m$. Gaussian elimination over the rationals counts pivots exactly. A column without a pivot lowers the rank, and the swap handles zero rows or zero leading entries. Evaluating at sample points finds failures there; it does not prove the rank is full elsewhere, which needs an algebraic argument as in the umbrella example.

prompt: Prove: if $F\colon M\to N$ is an injective immersion and a proper map (the preimage of every compact set is compact), then $F$ is an embedding. (You may use that manifolds are Hausdorff and first countable.)
hint: A continuous bijection onto its image is a homeomorphism if it is a closed map. Show that $F$ maps closed sets to closed sets using a sequence argument.
solution: Since $F$ is an immersion by hypothesis, it remains to show $F\colon M\to F(M)$ is a homeomorphism for the subspace topology. $F$ is a continuous bijection onto $F(M)$, so it suffices to show it is a closed map into $N$ (a closed set of $M$ then maps to a set closed in $N$, hence closed in $F(M)$). Let $C\subseteq M$ be closed and let $y\in N$ be in the closure of $F(C)$. Because $N$ is first countable there are $x_k\in C$ with $F(x_k)\to y$. The set $K = \{F(x_k) : k\}\cup\{y\}$ is compact, so $F^{-1}(K)$ is compact by properness, and it contains all the $x_k$. A compact subset of a first countable Hausdorff space is sequentially compact, so a subsequence $x_{k_j}\to x\in F^{-1}(K)$. Since $C$ is closed, $x\in C$, and by continuity $F(x) = \lim F(x_{k_j}) = y$. Therefore $y\in F(C)$ and $F(C)$ is closed. A closed continuous bijection $M\to F(M)$ is a homeomorphism, since its inverse is continuous exactly when images of closed sets are closed. $\blacksquare$

@quiz self
prompt: Prove that a submersion $F\colon M\to N$ is an open map, and give an example of a smooth map between manifolds that is not a submersion and is not open.
hint: Use the local submersion theorem to see that near each point $F$ is a projection.
solution: Let $W\subseteq M$ be open and $p\in W$. By the local submersion theorem there are charts $(U,\varphi)$ at $p$ and $(V,\psi)$ at $F(p)$ with $F(U)\subseteq V$ and $\psi\circ F\circ\varphi^{-1} = \pi$, where $\pi\colon\mathbb{R}^m\to\mathbb{R}^n$ keeps the first $n$ coordinates. The set $\varphi(U\cap W)$ is open in $\mathbb{R}^m$, and $\pi$ is an open map (the image of a ball of radius $r$ about a point is the ball of radius $r$ about its image in $\mathbb{R}^n$), so $\pi(\varphi(U\cap W))$ is open in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Therefore $F(U\cap W) = \psi^{-1}(\pi(\varphi(U\cap W)))$ is open in $N$ and contains $F(p)$. Hence $F(W)$ is a union of open sets, so it is open. For the example take $F\colon\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}$, $F(x,y)=x^2$: $dF = (2x, 0)$ vanishes on the line $x=0$, so $F$ is not a submersion, and the image of $\mathbb{R}^2$ is $[0,\infty)$, which is not open in $\mathbb{R}$, so $F$ is not open. $\blacksquare$
