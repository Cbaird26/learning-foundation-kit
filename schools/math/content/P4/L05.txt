@article
The sphere is the solution set $x_1^2 + \dots + x_{n+1}^2 = 1$ of one equation, and the group $O(n)$ is the solution set of the $n(n+1)/2$ independent equations $A^\top A = I$. When is a solution set of equations a manifold, and of what dimension? The answer is the implicit function theorem in the language of manifolds.

### Embedded submanifolds

A subset $S\subseteq M$ of an $n$-manifold is an **embedded submanifold of dimension $k$** if every $p\in S$ lies in the domain $U$ of a chart $\varphi\colon U\to\mathbb{R}^n$ of $M$ such that
$$\varphi(U\cap S) = \varphi(U)\cap\big(\mathbb{R}^k\times\{0\}\big)$$
(a **slice chart**). With the subspace topology, $S$ is Hausdorff and second countable, and the maps $x\mapsto(x_1,\dots,x_k)$ obtained from slice charts are charts of $S$ whose transition maps are restrictions of transition maps of $M$ to $\mathbb{R}^k\times\{0\}$, hence smooth. So $S$ is a $k$-manifold and the inclusion $S\hookrightarrow M$ is an embedding (in a slice chart it is $x\mapsto(x,0)$). The converse also holds: the image of an embedding is an embedded submanifold; this follows from the local immersion theorem (stated here, not reproved). The codimension is $n-k$.

### The regular value theorem

For smooth $F\colon M\to N$, a point $p$ is **regular** if $dF_p$ is surjective and **critical** otherwise. A point $q\in N$ is a **regular value** if every point of $F^{-1}(q)$ is regular (so every $q\notin F(M)$ is a regular value, vacuously), and a **critical value** otherwise.

**Theorem (regular value theorem).** Let $F\colon M\to N$ be smooth, $\dim M = m$, $\dim N = n$, and let $q\in N$ be a regular value of $F$. Then $F^{-1}(q)$ is empty or an embedded submanifold of $M$ of dimension $m-n$, and $T_p F^{-1}(q) = \ker dF_p$ for every $p\in F^{-1}(q)$ (as a subspace of $T_pM$ via the differential of the inclusion).

*Proof.* Let $p \in S = F^{-1}(q)$. Since $dF_p$ is surjective, the local submersion theorem gives charts $(U,\varphi)$ at $p$ and $(V,\psi)$ at $q$ with $F(U)\subseteq V$ and $\psi\circ F\circ\varphi^{-1}(x) = (x_1,\dots,x_n)$. Compose $\psi$ with a translation so that $\psi(q)=0$. Then for $x \in \varphi(U)$ we have $F(\varphi^{-1}(x)) = q$ exactly when $(x_1,\dots,x_n)=0$. Hence $\varphi(U\cap S) = \varphi(U)\cap(\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{m-n})$. After swapping the two blocks of coordinates, this is a slice chart of dimension $m-n$. For the tangent space let $\iota\colon S\hookrightarrow M$ be the inclusion. Since $F\circ\iota$ is constant, $dF_p\circ d\iota_p = 0$, so $d\iota_p(T_pS)\subseteq\ker dF_p$. Both spaces have dimension $m-n$ ($d\iota_p$ is injective and $dF_p$ is onto), so they are equal. $\blacksquare$

Every embedded submanifold is locally of this form (in a slice chart it is the common zero set of $n-k$ coordinate functions): a local defining function. The next lesson uses this to study transversality.

### What the hypotheses say

* **Regular is sufficient, not necessary.** For $f(x)=x^3$, the value $0$ is critical ($f'(0)=0$), yet $f^{-1}(0)=\{0\}$ is a $0$-dimensional submanifold of $\mathbb{R}$. What fails is the guarantee, not always the conclusion.
* **Critical points in the fiber matter.** For $f(x,y)=xy$ the value $0$ is critical, and $f^{-1}(0)$ is the union of the two axes, not a submanifold. For $f(x,y)=x^2-y^2$ likewise (two crossing lines).
* **Critical points away from the fiber are harmless.** Only critical points lying in $F^{-1}(q)$ matter, so a function can have critical points and still have many regular values: the next lesson shows that almost every value is regular.

### An algebraic recipe

To show a level set $\{f = c\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$ is a submanifold: (1) compute $\nabla f$ and solve $\nabla f = 0$ for all critical points; (2) compute $f$ at each critical point; (3) if none of these values equals $c$, the theorem applies. Python can hunt for critical points on a grid, which suggests where to look, but only the algebra in step (1) shows there are no others. For matrix groups, $\mathbb{R}^n$ is a space of matrices and one computes $dF_A(B)$ by expanding $F(A+tB)$ to first order in $t$.
@example
title: The orthogonal group is a submanifold
problem: Show that $O(n) = \{A\in M_n(\mathbb{R}) : A^\top A = I\}$ is an embedded submanifold of $M_n(\mathbb{R})\cong\mathbb{R}^{n^2}$ of dimension $n(n-1)/2$, with $T_IO(n)$ the skew-symmetric matrices.
step: Let $\mathrm{Sym}_n$ be the space of symmetric $n\times n$ matrices, of dimension $n(n+1)/2$, and $F\colon M_n(\mathbb{R})\to\mathrm{Sym}_n$, $F(A) = A^\top A$ (symmetric, so $F$ lands in $\mathrm{Sym}_n$). Then $O(n) = F^{-1}(I)$.
step: Differentiate: $F(A+tB) = A^\top A + t(A^\top B + B^\top A) + t^2B^\top B$, so $dF_A(B) = A^\top B + B^\top A$.
step: Surjectivity at $A\in O(n)$: given $S\in\mathrm{Sym}_n$, take $B = \tfrac12AS$. Then $A^\top B + B^\top A = \tfrac12A^\top AS + \tfrac12S^\top A^\top A = \tfrac12S+\tfrac12S = S$ because $A^\top A=I$ and $S^\top=S$. So $I$ is a regular value of $F$.
step: By the regular value theorem $O(n)$ is an embedded submanifold of dimension $n^2 - n(n+1)/2 = n(n-1)/2$, and $T_IO(n) = \ker dF_I = \{B : B + B^\top = 0\}$.
answer: $O(n)$ is a submanifold of dimension $n(n-1)/2$ with $T_IO(n) = \{B : B^\top = -B\}$.
@example
title: When a level set of $xyz$ is a surface
problem: For which $c$ is $\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^3 : xyz = c\}$ guaranteed by the regular value theorem to be a $2$-dimensional submanifold?
step: Let $f(x,y,z) = xyz$. Then $\nabla f = (yz,\ xz,\ xy)$.
step: Critical points: $\nabla f=0$ means $yz = xz = xy = 0$. If two of the coordinates were nonzero, say $x, y\ne0$, then $xy\ne0$; so at most one coordinate is nonzero, that is, at least two coordinates vanish.
step: At such points $f = xyz = 0$. So the only critical value is $0$.
step: Hence every $c\ne0$ is a regular value and $\{xyz = c\}$ is a $2$-dimensional embedded submanifold (it has four components, one for each sign pattern of $(x,y,z)$ with product of the sign of $c$). For $c=0$ the theorem gives no information; in fact $\{xyz=0\}$ is the union of the three coordinate planes, and near a point of a coordinate axis it is a line times a cross, which is not locally Euclidean (not proved here).
answer: For every $c\neq0$; the only critical value of $xyz$ is $0$.
@mistakes
- Checking surjectivity of $dF$ only at one point (say the identity) and concluding $q$ is a regular value: surjectivity is needed at every point of the level set, so one needs an argument valid for all $A$ with $F(A)=q$.
- Looking for critical points of $F$ everywhere and discarding $q$ when some point away from $F^{-1}(q)$ is critical: only critical points lying in the fiber matter.
- Concluding that a level set which is not covered by the theorem fails to be a submanifold: the theorem is a sufficient condition ($x\mapsto x^3$ at $0$).
- Forgetting that the target is $\mathrm{Sym}_n$ and not $M_n(\mathbb{R})$ for $A\mapsto A^\top A$: with target $M_n(\mathbb{R})$ the differential is never surjective (for $n\ge2$) and the count of equations is wrong.

model:
```python
def ddet(A, B):
    """Derivative of the 3x3 determinant at A in the direction B."""
    total = 0
    for i in range(3):
        for j in range(3):
            r = [x for x in range(3) if x != i]
            c = [x for x in range(3) if x != j]
            minor = A[r[0]][c[0]] * A[r[1]][c[1]] - A[r[0]][c[1]] * A[r[1]][c[0]]
            total += (-1) ** (i + j) * minor * B[i][j]
    return total
```
hint: Delete row $i$ and column $j$, take the $2\times2$ determinant, attach the sign $(-1)^{i+j}$ and multiply by $B_{ij}$.
hint: Do not use $\det(A)\operatorname{tr}(A^{-1}B)$: it needs $A$ invertible.
solution: The determinant is linear in each entry, so the derivative in direction $B$ is $\sum_{ij}\frac{\partial\det}{\partial a_{ij}}(A)\,B_{ij}$, and $\partial\det/\partial a_{ij}$ is the cofactor $(-1)^{i+j}M_{ij}$. The formula $\det(A)\operatorname{tr}(A^{-1}B)$ agrees with it when $A$ is invertible but fails for singular $A$, which the tests include. The singular case matters: the cofactor matrix of a rank $2$ matrix is nonzero, and that is why $d\det_A\ne0$ there.

prompt: Prove that $SL(n,\mathbb{R}) = \{A\in M_n(\mathbb{R}) : \det A = 1\}$ is an embedded submanifold of $M_n(\mathbb{R})$ of dimension $n^2-1$, and that $T_ISL(n,\mathbb{R})$ is the space of matrices with trace $0$.
hint: Compute $d\det_A(B)$ for invertible $A$ by writing $\det(A+tB) = \det A\cdot\det(I+tA^{-1}B)$.
hint: To show surjectivity of a linear map to $\mathbb{R}$ it is enough to find one $B$ with $d\det_A(B)\ne0$.
solution: Let $f = \det\colon M_n(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}$, a polynomial in the entries, hence smooth. For $A$ invertible and any $B$, $\det(A+tB) = \det(A)\det(I+tC)$ with $C = A^{-1}B$. In the Leibniz expansion of $\det(I+tC) = \sum_\sigma\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_i(\delta_{i\sigma(i)}+tC_{i\sigma(i)})$, every permutation other than the identity has at least two indices with $\sigma(i)\ne i$, so its product contains a factor $t^2$; the identity term is $\prod_i(1+tC_{ii}) = 1 + t\operatorname{tr}C+O(t^2)$. Hence $\det(I+tC) = 1+t\operatorname{tr}C+O(t^2)$ and $d f_A(B) = \det(A)\operatorname{tr}(A^{-1}B)$. If $\det A = 1$, choose $B = A$: $df_A(A) = \operatorname{tr}(I) = n\ne0$, so the linear map $df_A\colon M_n(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}$ is surjective. Thus $1$ is a regular value, and by the regular value theorem $SL(n,\mathbb{R}) = f^{-1}(1)$ is an embedded submanifold of dimension $n^2-1$, with $T_I = \ker df_I = \{B : \operatorname{tr}B = 0\}$ since $df_I(B)=\operatorname{tr}B$. $\blacksquare$

solution: Let $\Omega = \{(x,y,z) : x^2+y^2>0\}$, an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^3$ and so a $3$-manifold, and let $g\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}$ be the given function, smooth there since $\rho = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ is smooth for $\rho > 0$.
