@article
In a symmetric cipher, sender and receiver share a secret key. In 1976 Diffie and Hellman proposed *public-key* cryptography: a receiver publishes a key that lets anyone encrypt, while only the receiver can decrypt. Rivest, Shamir and Adleman gave the first practical realization in 1977. The scheme rests on an asymmetry that you now have the tools to understand: multiplying two primes is easy, and modular powers are fast (week 5), yet no fast way to undo the product is known.

**Safety note.** Everything in this course uses *toy* keys, a few digits long, so that you can follow every number. Such keys offer no security at all. Real systems use keys of thousands of bits, vetted libraries and padding schemes, and you should never write your own cryptography to protect real data.

### Key generation, encryption, decryption

1. Choose two distinct primes $p$ and $q$ and set $n = pq$ and $\varphi(n) = (p-1)(q-1)$.
2. Choose an exponent $e$ with $1 < e < \varphi(n)$ and $\gcd(e, \varphi(n)) = 1$.
3. Compute $d$ with $ed \equiv 1 \pmod{\varphi(n)}$ and $1 \le d < \varphi(n)$ (week 3).

The **public key** is $(n, e)$; the **private key** is $d$ (together with $p$ and $q$). A message is an integer $m$ with $0 \le m < n$. Encryption is $c = m^e \bmod n$ and decryption is $m' = c^d \bmod n$. Both are computed with `pow(m, e, n)`.

```python
def rsa_keygen(p, q, e):
    """Return (n, e, d) for distinct primes p, q and gcd(e, (p-1)(q-1)) = 1."""
    phi = (p - 1) * (q - 1)
    d = 1
    while e * d % phi != 1:       # slow but clear; week 3 gave a fast inverse
        d += 1
    return p * q, e, d


n, e, d = rsa_keygen(83, 127, 11)
m = 4321
c = pow(m, e, n)
print(n, d, c, pow(c, d, n))
```

This prints `10541 6575 10015 4321`: with $p = 83$, $q = 127$ we get $\varphi(n) = 82 \cdot 126 = 10332$, $d = 6575$ (check: $11 \cdot 6575 = 72325 = 7 \cdot 10332 + 1$), the message $4321$ encrypts to $10015$, and decryption returns $4321$.

### Why decryption works

**Theorem 1 (correctness of RSA).** Let $p \ne q$ be primes, $n = pq$, and let $e, d$ be positive integers with $ed \equiv 1 \pmod{(p-1)(q-1)}$. Then $m^{ed} \equiv m \pmod n$ for **every** integer $m$.

*Proof.* Since $ed \equiv 1 \pmod{(p-1)(q-1)}$ and $ed \ge 1$, we can write $ed = 1 + k(p-1)(q-1)$ with an integer $k \ge 0$. First modulo $p$. If $p \mid m$, both $m^{ed}$ and $m$ are $\equiv 0 \pmod p$ (the exponent $ed$ is at least $1$). If $p \nmid m$, Fermat's little theorem gives $m^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$, so
$$m^{ed} = m \cdot \left(m^{p-1}\right)^{k(q-1)} \equiv m \cdot 1 = m \pmod p.$$
The same argument with $q$ in place of $p$ gives $m^{ed} \equiv m \pmod q$. Hence $p$ and $q$ both divide $m^{ed} - m$, and since $\gcd(p, q) = 1$ the product $n = pq$ divides it (week 4). $\blacksquare$

The hypotheses matter. If $p = q$, then $\varphi(p^2) = p(p-1) \ne (p-1)^2$ and the proof breaks: with $p = q = 11$, $e = 7$ and $d = 43$ (so $ed = 301 \equiv 1 \pmod{100}$), the message $m = 11$ gives $11^{301} \equiv 0 \not\equiv 11 \pmod{121}$. If $\gcd(e, \varphi(n)) > 1$ no inverse $d$ exists at all. Note also that the theorem covers messages with $\gcd(m, n) > 1$, although such messages are very rare for large $n$.

### Knowing $\varphi(n)$ is knowing the factors

If an attacker learns $\varphi(n)$, then $p + q = n - \varphi(n) + 1$ and $pq = n$, so $p$ and $q$ are the roots of $x^2 - (n - \varphi(n) + 1)x + n = 0$. Computing $d$ from $\varphi(n)$ is then one modular inverse. Conversely, factoring $n$ gives $\varphi(n)$. So anyone who can factor $n$ can break the *textbook* scheme. No efficient classical factoring algorithm is known for large $n$, but there is no proof that breaking RSA requires factoring. Next week looks at factoring methods and why keys with close primes or small primes are weak.

### What the toy leaves out

Textbook RSA is deterministic: the same message always gives the same ciphertext, so an eavesdropper can test guesses. It is also **malleable**: $c \cdot s^e \bmod n$ is an encryption of $m s \bmod n$ (a practice problem). Real systems apply a randomized padding scheme before encrypting and use different protocols for signatures. Key sizes are chosen so that factoring is out of reach; records and recommendations change, so consult current standards rather than a textbook.

@example
title: A toy key by hand
problem: Take $p = 11$, $q = 23$, $e = 7$. Find $n$, $\varphi(n)$ and $d$, and encrypt and decrypt $m = 100$.
step: $n = 11 \cdot 23 = 253$ and $\varphi(n) = 10 \cdot 22 = 220$. Check $\gcd(7, 220) = 1$ since $220 = 31 \cdot 7 + 3$ and $\gcd(7, 3) = 1$.
step: Find $d$: $220 = 31 \cdot 7 + 3$, $7 = 2 \cdot 3 + 1$, so $1 = 7 - 2 \cdot 3 = 7 - 2(220 - 31 \cdot 7) = 63 \cdot 7 - 2 \cdot 220$. Thus $d = 63$.
step: Encrypt: $100^7 \bmod 253$. First $100^2 = 10000 = 39 \cdot 253 + 133$, so $100^2 \equiv 133$. Then $100^4 \equiv 133^2 = 17689 = 69 \cdot 253 + 232$, so $100^4 \equiv 232$.
step: Multiply: $100^7 = 100^4 \cdot 100^2 \cdot 100 \equiv 232 \cdot 133 \cdot 100$. Here $232 \cdot 133 = 30856 = 121 \cdot 253 + 243$, and $243 \cdot 100 = 24300 = 96 \cdot 253 + 12$. So the ciphertext is $c = 12$.
step: Decrypt: $12^{63} \bmod 253$ is $100$ (by repeated squaring, or by computer: `pow(12, 63, 253)`), as Theorem 1 predicts.
answer: $n = 253$, $\varphi(n) = 220$, $d = 63$; the ciphertext of $100$ is $12$ and decryption returns $100$.

@example
title: Decryption works even when the message shares a factor with $n$
problem: Let $p = 3$, $q = 5$, $e = 3$. Show that $m = 6$ encrypts and decrypts correctly.
step: $n = 15$ and $\varphi(n) = 8$. Since $3 \cdot 3 = 9 \equiv 1 \pmod 8$, we may take $d = 3$.
step: Encrypt: $c = 6^3 = 216 = 14 \cdot 15 + 6$, so $c = 6$.
step: Decrypt: $c^d = 6^3 \equiv 6$ again. Here $\gcd(6, 15) = 3 > 1$, so Euler's theorem does not apply to $m$.
step: Theorem 1 still works: modulo $3$ both $m$ and $m^{ed}$ are $0$, and modulo $5$ Fermat's theorem applies since $5 \nmid 6$.
answer: $6$ is its own ciphertext for this toy key, and decryption recovers it.

@mistakes
- Using $\varphi(n) = n - 1$ for composite $n$. For $n = pq$ it is $(p-1)(q-1)$.
- Taking $d \equiv e^{-1} \pmod n$. The inverse is modulo $\varphi(n)$ because the exponent lives modulo $\varphi(n)$ (week 5).
- Encrypting a message $m \ge n$: it is reduced modulo $n$, so it cannot be recovered. Split long messages into blocks below $n$.
- Treating a toy demonstration as a secure design. Small keys are broken instantly and textbook RSA lacks padding.

model:
```python
def encrypt_text(text, e, n):
    """Encrypt an even-length uppercase string two letters per block."""
    blocks = []
    for i in range(0, len(text), 2):
        x = ord(text[i]) - ord("A")
        y = ord(text[i + 1]) - ord("A")
        blocks.append(pow(26 * x + y, e, n))
    return blocks


def decrypt_text(blocks, d, n):
    """Inverse of encrypt_text."""
    letters = []
    for c in blocks:
        m = pow(c, d, n)
        letters.append(chr(ord("A") + m // 26))
        letters.append(chr(ord("A") + m % 26))
    return "".join(letters)
```
hint: A block is $26x + y$ with $0 \le x, y \le 25$, so `divmod(block, 26)` recovers the two letters.
solution: Since $26 \cdot 25 + 25 = 675 < n$, every block is a valid message, and Theorem 1 gives $c^d \equiv m \pmod n$, so decrypting returns the block exactly. Writing the block as $26x + y$ is unique because $0 \le y < 26$.

model:
```python
def decrypt_crt(c, d, p, q):
    """RSA decryption with the Chinese remainder theorem."""
    mp = pow(c, d % (p - 1), p)
    mq = pow(c, d % (q - 1), q)
    # q_inv = inverse of q modulo p, by brute force on a tiny modulus
    qinv = next(t for t in range(1, p) if q * t % p == 1) if p > 1 else 0
    h = qinv * (mp - mq) % p
    return mq + h * q
```
hint: Fermat's theorem lets you reduce the exponent modulo $p - 1$ and $q - 1$. To combine, write $m = m_q + hq$ and solve for $h$ modulo $p$.
solution: Modulo $p$, if $p \nmid c$ then $c^{p-1} \equiv 1$, so $c^d \equiv c^{d \bmod (p-1)}$; if $p \mid c$ both sides are $0$ because $d \bmod (p-1) \ge 1$ ($d$ is invertible modulo $p-1$, so it is not a multiple of $p - 1$ when $p > 2$). The same holds for $q$. By Theorem 1, $c^d \bmod pq$ is the unique number below $pq$ that is $m_p$ modulo $p$ and $m_q$ modulo $q$. Writing it as $m_q + hq$ gives $h \equiv q^{-1}(m_p - m_q) \pmod p$ with $0 \le h < p$, so $m < pq$. Two exponentiations with half-size numbers are cheaper than one full-size one.

choice*: Decryption recovers $m$ even when $\gcd(m, n) > 1$, for $0 \le m < n$.
choice*: Anyone who knows $n$ and $\varphi(n)$ can compute $p$ and $q$.
choice*: Anyone who factors $n$ can compute the private exponent $d$ from $e$.

solution: Recovering $m$ when $\gcd(m, n) > 1$ is Theorem 1. Knowing $n$ and $\varphi(n)$ gives $p$ and $q$ as the roots of $x^2 - (n - \varphi(n) + 1)x + n$. model:
```python
from math import isqrt


def factor_from_phi(n, phi_n):
    """Recover p < q with p*q = n and (p-1)*(q-1) = phi_n, or None."""
    s = n - phi_n + 1              # p + q
    disc = s * s - 4 * n           # (q - p)**2
    if disc <= 0:
        return None
    t = isqrt(disc)
    if t * t != disc or (s - t) % 2 != 0:
        return None
    p, q = (s - t) // 2, (s + t) // 2
    if p < 2 or p * q != n:
        return None
    return p, q
```
hint: $p$ and $q$ are the roots of $x^2 - sx + n$ with $s = n - \varphi_n + 1$; the discriminant is $(q - p)^2$.
solution: From $(p-1)(q-1) = pq - (p+q) + 1 = n - s + 1$ we get $s = p + q = n - \varphi_n + 1$. Then $(q-p)^2 = s^2 - 4n$, so $q - p = \sqrt{s^2 - 4n}$ must be a positive integer for a valid pair, and $p, q = (s \mp t)/2$. When $n = p^2$ is a square the discriminant is $0$, and the problem asks for $p < q$, so it returns `None` (this is why the case $p = q$ is excluded from RSA).
