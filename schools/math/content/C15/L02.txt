@article
Is there a number system where $6$ factors into primes in two different ways? In $\mathbb{Z}$ there is not, and the reason is deeper than it looks. Write $360 = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 \cdot 5$: you could multiply these in any order, but could another list of primes give $360$? This week proves that the answer is no, and then computes with the factorization.

A **prime** is an integer $p \ge 2$ whose only positive divisors are $1$ and $p$. An integer $n \ge 2$ that is not prime is **composite**.

### Euclid's lemma

**Theorem 1 (Euclid's lemma).** Let $p$ be a prime and $a, b$ integers. If $p \mid ab$, then $p \mid a$ or $p \mid b$.

*Proof.* Suppose $p \nmid a$. The only positive divisors of $p$ are $1$ and $p$, and $p \nmid a$, so $\gcd(p, a) = 1$. By Bézout's identity (week 1) there are integers $x, y$ with $px + ay = 1$. Multiply by $b$: $b = pbx + aby$. Now $p \mid pbx$ and $p \mid ab$, so $p \mid b$. $\blacksquare$

By induction on the number of factors, if a prime divides $a_1 a_2 \cdots a_k$ then it divides some $a_i$. The hypothesis "$p$ is prime" matters: $6 \mid 4 \cdot 9$ but $6$ divides neither factor.

### The fundamental theorem of arithmetic

**Theorem 2.** Every integer $n \ge 2$ is a product of primes, and this product is unique apart from the order of the factors.

*Proof of existence (strong induction on $n$).* Let $n \ge 2$ and assume every integer $m$ with $2 \le m < n$ is a product of primes. If $n$ is prime, it is a product of one prime. Otherwise $n = ab$ with $1 < a, b < n$, and by the hypothesis $a$ and $b$ are products of primes; concatenating the two lists gives $n$.

*Proof of uniqueness (induction on the number $k$ of primes in one factorization).* Suppose $p_1 \cdots p_k = q_1 \cdots q_l$ with all $p_i, q_j$ prime; we show $k = l$ and the lists agree after reordering. Since $p_1$ divides the right side, Theorem 1 (extended to several factors) gives $p_1 \mid q_j$ for some $j$; as $q_j$ is prime and $p_1 \ge 2$, $p_1 = q_j$. Cancel it from both sides. If $k = 1$ the left side is now $1$, so no primes remain on the right, and $l = 1$. If $k > 1$ the remaining products are equal and the left has $k - 1$ primes, so the induction hypothesis finishes the proof. $\blacksquare$

Uniqueness uses Euclid's lemma, and it can fail in other rings. In the ring $\{a + b\sqrt{-5} : a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ one has $6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5})$, and all four factors cannot be split further (a check with the norm $a^2 + 5b^2$, which we omit here). Refer to your abstract algebra course for such rings.

**Counting divisors.** If $n = p_1^{e_1} \cdots p_r^{e_r}$ with distinct primes, then by uniqueness the positive divisors of $n$ are exactly $p_1^{f_1} \cdots p_r^{f_r}$ with $0 \le f_i \le e_i$. So $n$ has $(e_1 + 1) \cdots (e_r + 1)$ positive divisors. For $360 = 2^3 3^2 5$ this gives $4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 = 24$. Likewise $\gcd$ takes the smaller exponent of each prime and $\operatorname{lcm}$ the larger.

### There are infinitely many primes

**Theorem 3 (Euclid).** There are infinitely many primes.

*Proof.* Suppose $p_1, \dots, p_k$ were all of them, and let $N = p_1 \cdots p_k + 1 \ge 2$. By Theorem 2, $N$ has a prime factor $p$, so $p = p_i$ for some $i$. Then $p$ divides both $N$ and $p_1 \cdots p_k$, hence their difference $1$, which is absurd. $\blacksquare$

The proof does not say that $N$ is prime, only that it has a prime factor outside the list.

### Finding primes: the sieve and trial division

**Lemma 4.** A composite integer $n$ has a prime divisor $p$ with $p \le \sqrt{n}$.

*Proof.* Write $n = ab$ with $1 < a \le b < n$. Then $a^2 \le ab = n$, so $a \le \sqrt{n}$. Any prime factor of $a$ divides $n$ and is at most $a$. $\blacksquare$

The **sieve of Eratosthenes** lists the primes up to $N$ by crossing out multiples. Starting at $i^2$ is enough: a multiple $ik$ with $k < i$ has a smaller prime factor and was already crossed out (Lemma 4).

```python
def sieve(n):
    """Return the list of primes <= n."""
    is_prime = [True] * (n + 1)
    is_prime[0:2] = [False] * min(2, n + 1)
    for i in range(2, int(n ** 0.5) + 1):
        if is_prime[i]:
            for j in range(i * i, n + 1, i):
                is_prime[j] = False
    return [k for k in range(n + 1) if is_prime[k]]


def factorize(n):
    """Return {prime: exponent} for an integer n >= 2 by trial division."""
    result = {}
    d = 2
    while d * d <= n:
        while n % d == 0:
            result[d] = result.get(d, 0) + 1
            n //= d
        d += 1
    if n > 1:
        result[n] = result.get(n, 0) + 1
    return result


print(sieve(50))
print(factorize(360))
```

The first line prints the fifteen primes $2, 3, \dots, 47$ and the second prints `{2: 3, 3: 2, 5: 1}`. Why does `factorize` return only primes? When the loop tries $d$, all smaller factors have been divided out, so the smallest divisor above $1$ of the remaining $n$ is prime (a divisor $d'$ of $d$ with $1 < d' < d$ would divide $n$ and would have been removed earlier). When the loop stops, $d^2 > n$, and by Lemma 4 the remaining $n > 1$ has no prime factor at most $\sqrt{n}$, so it is itself prime.

@example
title: The number $p_1 \cdots p_k + 1$ need not be prime
problem: Let $N = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdot 11 \cdot 13 + 1$. Find its prime factors and say what this shows about Euclid's proof.
step: Compute $N = 30030 + 1 = 30031$.
step: By Lemma 4 we only test primes up to $\sqrt{30031} < 174$. None of $2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13$ divides $N$ (each leaves remainder $1$).
step: Testing the next primes, $17, 19, \dots, 59$, we find $30031 = 59 \cdot 509$, and $509$ is prime since no prime up to $22$ divides it.
step: So $N$ is composite, but its prime factors $59$ and $509$ are both missing from the list $2, \dots, 13$, exactly as the proof predicts.
answer: $30031 = 59 \cdot 509$; Euclid's argument produces a new prime factor, not necessarily a new prime of the form $N$.

@mistakes
- Believing $p_1 \cdots p_k + 1$ is always prime. It is composite for the first six primes ($30031 = 59 \cdot 509$).
- Applying Euclid's lemma to a composite modulus: $12 \mid 3 \cdot 4$, yet $12$ divides neither factor. The lemma needs a prime.
- Testing divisors all the way up to $n - 1$. Testing every $d \le \sqrt{n}$ is enough, and so is testing only the primes up to $\sqrt{n}$.
- Counting divisors as the sum of the exponents $+1$ instead of the product $\prod (e_i + 1)$.

```python
def spf_table(n):
    """Smallest prime factor of every k <= n (0 for k = 0, 1)."""
    s = [0] * (n + 1)
    for i in range(2, n + 1):
        if s[i] == 0:          # i has not been marked, so it is prime
            s[i] = i
            for j in range(i * i, n + 1, i):
                if s[j] == 0:
                    s[j] = i
    return s
```

prompt: Here is a recursive extended Euclidean algorithm for integers $a \ge 0$, $b \ge 0$, not both zero:

`def eg(a, b): ` if `b == 0` return `(a, 1, 0)`; otherwise let `(g, x, y) = eg(b, a % b)` and return `(g, y, x - (a // b) * y)`.

Prove by strong induction on $b$ that `eg(a, b)` returns $(g, x, y)$ with $g = \gcd(a, b)$ and $ax + by = g$.
hint: In the inductive step apply the hypothesis to the pair $(b, a \bmod b)$, whose second entry is smaller than $b$, and substitute $a \bmod b = a - \lfloor a/b \rfloor b$.
solution: Induct on $b \ge 0$, for all $a \ge 0$. Base case $b = 0$: then $a > 0$ and the call returns $(a, 1, 0)$; indeed $\gcd(a, 0) = a$ and $a \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 = a$. Inductive step: let $b > 0$ and assume the claim for all pairs whose second entry is less than $b$. Put $r = a \bmod b = a - qb$ with $q = \lfloor a/b \rfloor$ and $0 \le r < b$. The returned triple is $(g, y, x - qy)$ and $a y + b(x - q y) = ay + bx - bqy = bx + (a - qb)y = bx + ry = g$. So the claim holds for $(a, b)$, and by strong induction for all pairs. The recursion terminates because the second entry strictly decreases.

```python
def factorial_exponents(n):
    """Exponent of each prime p <= n in n!, by Legendre's formula."""
    result = {}
    for p in range(2, n + 1):
        if all(p % d for d in range(2, int(p ** 0.5) + 1)):
            e, power = 0, p
            while power <= n:
                e += n // power
                power *= p
            result[p] = e
    return result
```
hint: Find the primes up to $n$, then add $\lfloor n/p \rfloor + \lfloor n/p^2 \rfloor + \cdots$ until the power of $p$ exceeds $n$.
solution: Among $1, \dots, n$ exactly $\lfloor n/p \rfloor$ numbers are multiples of $p$, $\lfloor n/p^2 \rfloor$ are multiples of $p^2$, and so on; a number divisible by exactly $p^j$ is counted $j$ times in the sum, which is its contribution to the exponent. The loop stops when $p^k > n$ because all further terms are $0$.

model:
```python
def primes_in_range(lo, hi):
    """Primes in [lo, hi] by a segmented sieve."""
    root = int(hi ** 0.5)
    while (root + 1) * (root + 1) <= hi:
        root += 1
    while root * root > hi:
        root -= 1
    base = [p for p in range(2, root + 1)
            if all(p % d for d in range(2, int(p ** 0.5) + 1))]
    flags = [True] * (hi - lo + 1)
    for p in base:
        start = max(p * p, ((lo + p - 1) // p) * p)
        for m in range(start, hi + 1, p):
            flags[m - lo] = False
    return [lo + i for i, ok in enumerate(flags) if ok and lo + i >= 2]
```
hint: For each base prime $p$, the first multiple to cross out is the larger of $p^2$ and the smallest multiple of $p$ that is at least $lo$.
solution: By Lemma 4 every composite in $[lo, hi]$ has a prime factor at most $\sqrt{hi}$, so crossing out the multiples of those primes leaves exactly the primes. Starting at $p^2$ (or later) keeps $p$ itself when $p$ lies in the range. The final filter removes $1$.

@quiz self
prompt: Prove that there are infinitely many primes $p$ with $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, that is, primes leaving remainder $3$ on division by $4$.
hint: Suppose $3 = p_1, \dots, p_k$ are all of them and consider $N = 4p_1 \cdots p_k - 1$. A product of integers that leave remainder $1$ on division by $4$ again leaves remainder $1$.
solution: Suppose the primes of the form $4m + 3$ are exactly $p_1 = 3, p_2, \dots, p_k$ (there is at least one, $3$). Let $N = 4p_1 \cdots p_k - 1$. Then $N$ is odd and leaves remainder $3$ on division by $4$. Factor $N$ into primes (Theorem 2); all are odd. If all of them left remainder $1$ on division by $4$, so would their product, because $(4a + 1)(4b + 1) = 4(4ab + a + b) + 1$; but $N$ leaves remainder $3$. So some prime factor $p$ of $N$ leaves remainder $3$, hence $p = p_i$ for some $i$. Then $p$ divides $4p_1 \cdots p_k$ and $N$, so $p \mid 1$, a contradiction.
rubric: Builds $N = 4p_1 \cdots p_k - 1$ and notes it leaves remainder $3$ on division by $4$.
rubric: Shows that a product of numbers $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$ is $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$, so some prime factor is $\equiv 3 \pmod 4$.
rubric: Derives the contradiction $p \mid 1$ from $p \mid N$ and $p \mid 4p_1 \cdots p_k$.
