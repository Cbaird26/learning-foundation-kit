@article
The circle has $\pi_1 = \mathbb{Z}$, one generator and no relations. What is the fundamental group of two circles glued at a point? A loop can go around the first circle, then the second, then the first backward. There is no reason the order should not matter, and van Kampen's theorem says it does: the group is the free group on two generators. More generally, if a space is a union of two pieces whose fundamental groups you know, the theorem computes the whole group. It is the main tool of the first half of the course, and every exam problem of the form "compute the fundamental group of this space" is solved by cutting the space into two pieces and applying it.

### Free products

Let $G_\alpha$ be groups. Their **free product** $*_\alpha G_\alpha$ is the set of **reduced words** $g_1 g_2 \cdots g_k$, where each $g_i \ne 1$ lies in some $G_{\alpha_i}$ and consecutive letters lie in different factors, with multiplication by concatenation followed by combining and canceling adjacent letters from the same factor. Its universal property: homomorphisms $\varphi_\alpha\colon G_\alpha \to H$ extend uniquely to a homomorphism $*_\alpha G_\alpha \to H$. Standard facts: every element has exactly one reduced form (the normal form theorem, stated here without proof); the free product of $n$ copies of $\mathbb{Z}$ is the free group $F_n$; the abelianization of $G * H$ is $G^{ab} \times H^{ab}$ (both satisfy the same universal property for maps to abelian groups); if $G$ and $H$ are nontrivial, $G * H$ is not abelian, since $gh$ and $hg$ have different reduced forms for $g \in G$ and $h \in H$ nontrivial.

### The theorem

**Theorem (van Kampen).** Let $X = U \cup V$ where $U$, $V$ are open and path-connected, $U \cap V$ is path-connected and contains the basepoint $x_0$. Let $i\colon \pi_1(U \cap V) \to \pi_1(U)$, $j\colon \pi_1(U \cap V) \to \pi_1(V)$ and the maps to $\pi_1(X)$ be induced by inclusions, all at $x_0$. Then the homomorphism
$$\Phi\colon \pi_1(U, x_0) * \pi_1(V, x_0) \to \pi_1(X, x_0)$$
is surjective, and its kernel is the normal subgroup $N$ generated by all $i(\omega)\,j(\omega)^{-1}$ for $\omega \in \pi_1(U \cap V, x_0)$. Hence
$$\pi_1(X, x_0) \cong \big(\pi_1(U) * \pi_1(V)\big) / N.$$

The general version, for any open cover $\{A_\alpha\}$ by path-connected sets containing $x_0$ whose pairwise intersections are path-connected, gives surjectivity, and the kernel statement holds if also every triple intersection is path-connected. For two sets there are no further conditions.

*Proof of surjectivity.* This is Proposition 2 of Lesson 2: every loop at $x_0$ is a product of loops in $U$ or $V$, so lies in the image of $\Phi$. $\blacksquare$

*Outline of the kernel (this is an outline, not a complete proof).* A relation $i(\omega)j(\omega)^{-1}$ lies in $N$ and is killed because $\omega$ is a loop in $U \cap V$, so it represents the same class of $X$ either way; this shows $N \subseteq \ker\Phi$. For the converse, suppose a product of loops in $U$ and in $V$ is null-homotopic in $X$ by a homotopy $H\colon I \times I \to X$. Subdivide the square into small rectangles each mapped into $U$ or $V$, join the grid vertices to $x_0$ by paths in $U \cap V$ or in $U$ or $V$ as needed, and read the product of the boundary loops of the rectangles. Moving across a rectangle changes the factorization by an equivalence in the free product that is either a reduction inside $\pi_1(U)$ or $\pi_1(V)$, or a replacement of a loop of $U \cap V$ viewed in $U$ by the same loop viewed in $V$, which is a relation in $N$. See Hatcher for the details.

### The hypotheses are sharp

**Open sets.** The proof uses a Lebesgue number, so the sets must be open (or have open neighborhoods that deformation retract to them). **Path-connected intersection.** Cover $S^1$ by two open arcs $U, V$ meeting in two arcs. Then $\pi_1(U) * \pi_1(V) = 1$ and $\pi_1(S^1) = \mathbb{Z}$, so the pushout formula fails and even surjectivity fails: the intersection has two components, and a loop around the circle needs both. Without the connectedness of $U \cap V$ one needs the groupoid version of the theorem.

### Consequences

**Wedge sums.** If $x \in X$ and $y \in Y$ have open neighborhoods that deformation retract to them, then $\pi_1(X \vee Y) \cong \pi_1(X) * \pi_1(Y)$ for path-connected $X, Y$: take $U = X \cup (\text{neighborhood of } y)$ and $V$ similarly. So a wedge of $n$ circles has $\pi_1 = F_n$. **Graphs.** A connected finite graph with $V$ vertices and $E$ edges has a spanning tree with $V - 1$ edges; collapsing it (a homotopy equivalence) leaves a wedge of $E - V + 1$ circles, so $\pi_1$ is free of rank $E - V + 1$. **Punctured plane.** $\pi_1(\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{k \text{ points}\})$ is free of rank $k$ (Example 1).

@example
title: The plane with k punctures
problem: Prove by induction on $k \ge 1$ that $X_k = \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{(1, 0), (2, 0), \dots, (k, 0)\}$ has $\pi_1(X_k)$ free of rank $k$, given the case $k = 1$ (a punctured plane, which is homotopy equivalent to $S^1$).
step: For $k \ge 2$ let $U = X_k \cap \{x < k - \tfrac12 + \tfrac14\}$ and $V = X_k \cap \{x > k - \tfrac12 - \tfrac14\}$. Both are open and path-connected: $U$ is a half-plane missing $k - 1$ points and $V$ a half-plane missing one point.
step: Their intersection is the open strip $\{k - \tfrac34 < x < k - \tfrac14\}$, which contains no puncture, since the punctures have integer coordinates $\le k - 1$ or equal to $k$. It is convex, so path-connected and simply connected. Choose $x_0$ in the strip.
step: $U \cong X_{k-1}$ up to homeomorphism (a half-plane with $k-1$ punctures is homeomorphic to the plane with $k - 1$ punctures), so $\pi_1(U)$ is free of rank $k - 1$ by induction, and $\pi_1(V) \cong \mathbb{Z}$.
step: Since $\pi_1(U \cap V) = 1$, the normal subgroup $N$ is trivial, and van Kampen gives $\pi_1(X_k) \cong F_{k-1} * \mathbb{Z} = F_k$.
answer: $\pi_1(X_k)$ is free of rank $k$.

@example
title: The torus from a punctured torus and a disk
problem: Compute $\pi_1(T^2)$, writing $T^2$ as the unit square with opposite sides identified: $a$ is the bottom edge, $b$ the left edge.
step: Let $B$ be a small open disk around the center of the square, and $A = T^2 \setminus \{\text{center}\}$. Both are open; $A \cup B = T^2$.
step: Radial projection from the center deformation retracts $A$ onto the image of the boundary of the square, a wedge $S^1 \vee S^1$ with loops $a$ and $b$. So $\pi_1(A)$ is free on $a, b$. Also $\pi_1(B) = 1$.
step: $A \cap B$ is a punctured open disk, path-connected with $\pi_1 = \mathbb{Z}$, generated by a small circle $\omega$ around the center.
step: Under the radial deformation retraction, $\omega$ is homotopic in $A$ to the boundary of the square, the loop $aba^{-1}b^{-1}$. So $i(\omega) = aba^{-1}b^{-1}$ and $j(\omega) = 1$.
step: By van Kampen, $\pi_1(T^2) \cong F(a, b) / \langle\langle aba^{-1}b^{-1} \rangle\rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}^2$, the free group modulo its commutator relation, which is the free abelian group of rank $2$.
answer: $\pi_1(T^2) \cong \mathbb{Z}^2$, in agreement with the product formula of Lesson 2.

@mistakes
- Using closed halves of a space as $U$ and $V$: the theorem needs open sets, or closed sets thickened to open neighborhoods that deformation retract onto them.
- Forgetting to check that $U \cap V$ is path-connected: two open arcs covering $S^1$ give the wrong answer $\pi_1 = 1$.
- Computing $\pi_1(X \vee Y)$ as a free product when the wedge point has no neighborhood deformation retracting to it: the wedge of infinitely many shrinking circles (the Hawaiian earring) is not a free group.
- Writing the answer for $\pi_1(U) * \pi_1(V)$ and omitting the quotient by $N$: when $U \cap V$ is not simply connected, the relations $i(\omega) = j(\omega)$ matter, as in the torus.

@practice num
prompt: Let $X = A \vee B$ where $A$ is a space with $\pi_1(A) \cong \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$, $B$ is a space with $\pi_1(B) \cong \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, and the wedge point has a neighborhood in each deformation retracting to it. Find the order of the abelianization of $\pi_1(X)$.
answer: 24
check: 4 * 6
hint: Van Kampen identifies $\pi_1(X)$ with a free product.
hint: A homomorphism from a free product to an abelian group is the same as a pair of homomorphisms from the factors.
solution: By van Kampen $\pi_1(X) \cong \mathbb{Z}/4 * \mathbb{Z}/6$. A homomorphism from this to an abelian group $M$ is a pair of homomorphisms $\mathbb{Z}/4 \to M$ and $\mathbb{Z}/6 \to M$, which is the same as a homomorphism $\mathbb{Z}/4 \times \mathbb{Z}/6 \to M$. So the abelianization is $\mathbb{Z}/4 \times \mathbb{Z}/6$, of order $24$. The free product itself is infinite; its abelianization is finite.

@practice self
prompt: Prove that the wedge of two circles $S^1 \vee S^1$ and the torus $T^2$ are not homotopy equivalent, using the fact that $\pi_1(S^1 \vee S^1)$ is the free group $F(a, b)$ on two generators and $\pi_1(T^2) \cong \mathbb{Z}^2$. You must prove that $F(a, b)$ is not abelian without invoking the normal form theorem.
hint: Homotopy equivalences induce isomorphisms of fundamental groups.
hint: To show two elements do not commute, map the free group onto a nonabelian group by the universal property.
solution: Suppose $S^1 \vee S^1 \simeq T^2$. By Lesson 1, their fundamental groups are isomorphic. But $\mathbb{Z}^2$ is abelian, so it suffices to show $F(a, b)$ is not. By the universal property of the free group there is a homomorphism $\varphi\colon F(a, b) \to S_3$ with $\varphi(a) = (1\ 2)$ and $\varphi(b) = (1\ 3)$. Then $\varphi(ab) = (1\ 2)(1\ 3)$ and $\varphi(ba) = (1\ 3)(1\ 2)$. Composing right to left, $(1\ 2)(1\ 3)$ sends $1 \to 3 \to 3$, $3 \to 1 \to 2$, $2 \to 2 \to 1$, so it is the cycle $(1\ 3\ 2)$, while $(1\ 3)(1\ 2)$ sends $1 \to 2 \to 2$, $2 \to 1 \to 3$, $3 \to 3 \to 1$, so it is $(1\ 2\ 3)$. These differ, so $ab \ne ba$ in $F(a, b)$. Thus $F(a, b)$ is not isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}^2$, and the spaces are not homotopy equivalent. $\blacksquare$ (Both spaces have $H_1 \cong \mathbb{Z}^2$, so abelian invariants such as $H_1$ do not separate them; $H_2$ does.)
rubric: Reduces to a statement about fundamental groups using that homotopy equivalence induces an isomorphism.
rubric: Gives an explicit homomorphism to a nonabelian group and computes the images of $ab$ and $ba$ correctly.
rubric: Concludes that $F(a, b)$ is nonabelian and hence not isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}^2$.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which of these statements about free products are true?
choice*: $\mathbb{Z} * \mathbb{Z}$ is not abelian.
choice*: $\mathbb{Z}/2 * \mathbb{Z}/3$ is infinite.
choice*: The abelianization of $\mathbb{Z} * \mathbb{Z}$ is $\mathbb{Z}^2$.
choice: $\mathbb{Z}/2 * \mathbb{Z}/2$ is finite.
choice: The free product of two nontrivial groups is always abelian if both factors are abelian.
hint: Write down long reduced words alternating between the two factors.
solution: The words $(ab)^k$ with $a, b$ the generators of the two factors are reduced and distinct for $\mathbb{Z}/2 * \mathbb{Z}/2$ and $\mathbb{Z}/2 * \mathbb{Z}/3$, so both groups are infinite. In $\mathbb{Z} * \mathbb{Z}$ the reduced words $ab$ and $ba$ differ, and the abelianization is $\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$ by the universal property. A free product of nontrivial groups is never abelian, even when the factors are.
