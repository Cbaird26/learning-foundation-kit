@article
Singular homology is defined for every space, but you cannot compute with infinitely many simplices. A CW complex gives one chain group per cell, a finite computation. The theorem of this lesson says that the finite computation gives the right answer. It is the most used computational tool in algebraic topology and the way almost every exam problem on the homology of a specific space is solved.

### Homology of the skeleta

Let $X$ be a finite CW complex with skeleta $X^0 \subseteq X^1 \subseteq \cdots \subseteq X^N = X$. We use the standard fact that $(X^n, X^{n-1})$ is a good pair, with $X^n / X^{n-1}$ a wedge of $c_n$ spheres $S^n$, one for each $n$-cell.

**Lemma 1.** (a) $H_k(X^n, X^{n-1})$ is free abelian of rank $c_n$ for $k = n$ and zero for $k \ne n$. (b) $H_k(X^n) = 0$ for $k > n$. (c) $H_k(X^n) \to H_k(X)$ is an isomorphism for $k < n$.

*Proof.* (a) Good pair: $H_k(X^n, X^{n-1}) \cong \tilde H_k(\bigvee_{c_n} S^n)$, which is $\mathbb{Z}^{c_n}$ in degree $n$ and zero otherwise by the wedge formula (Lesson 9) and Theorem 4 of that lesson. (b) Induct on $n$, starting from $H_k(X^0) = 0$ for $k > 0$. In the exact sequence $H_{k+1}(X^n, X^{n-1}) \to H_k(X^{n-1}) \to H_k(X^n) \to H_k(X^n, X^{n-1})$ with $k > n$, both outer terms vanish by (a), so $H_k(X^n) \cong H_k(X^{n-1})$, and this is $0$ by induction. (c) In $H_{k+1}(X^{n+1}, X^n) \to H_k(X^n) \to H_k(X^{n+1}) \to H_k(X^{n+1}, X^n)$ with $k < n$, both outer terms vanish by (a), so $H_k(X^n) \cong H_k(X^{n+1})$ for $k < n$; iterate up to $X^N = X$. $\blacksquare$

### The cellular chain complex

Put $C_n^{CW} = H_n(X^n, X^{n-1})$, free of rank $c_n$ with a basis given by the $n$-cells, and $d_n = j_{n-1}\partial_n\colon C_n^{CW} \to C_{n-1}^{CW}$, where $\partial_n\colon H_n(X^n, X^{n-1}) \to H_{n-1}(X^{n-1})$ is the connecting map and $j_{n-1}\colon H_{n-1}(X^{n-1}) \to H_{n-1}(X^{n-1}, X^{n-2})$ is induced by inclusion. Then $d_{n-1}d_n = j_{n-2}\partial_{n-1}j_{n-1}\partial_n = 0$ because $\partial_{n-1}j_{n-1} = 0$ (consecutive maps in the exact sequence of the pair $(X^{n-1}, X^{n-2})$).

**Theorem 2 (cellular homology).** For a finite CW complex, $H_n(X) \cong \ker d_n / \operatorname{im} d_{n+1}$.

*Proof.* By Lemma 1(c) and the exact sequence of $(X^{n+1}, X^n)$, using $H_n(X^{n+1}, X^n) = 0$, we have $H_n(X) \cong H_n(X^{n+1}) \cong H_n(X^n)/\operatorname{im}\partial_{n+1}$. Next, $H_n(X^{n-1}) = 0$ by Lemma 1(b), so the exact sequence of $(X^n, X^{n-1})$ shows that $j_n\colon H_n(X^n) \to H_n(X^n, X^{n-1})$ is injective, with image $\ker\partial_n$. Also $j_{n-1}$ is injective by the same argument one degree lower, so $\ker d_n = \ker\partial_n$. Thus $j_n$ identifies $H_n(X^n)$ with $\ker d_n$ and $\operatorname{im}\partial_{n+1}$ with $j_n(\operatorname{im}\partial_{n+1}) = \operatorname{im} d_{n+1}$. Hence $H_n(X) \cong \ker d_n/\operatorname{im} d_{n+1}$. $\blacksquare$

**Cellular boundary formula** (stated without proof): $d_n(e^n_\alpha) = \sum_\beta d_{\alpha\beta}\, e^{n-1}_\beta$ where $d_{\alpha\beta}$ is the degree (Lesson 12) of the composite $S^{n-1} \xrightarrow{\varphi_\alpha} X^{n-1} \to X^{n-1}/(X^{n-1} \setminus e^{n-1}_\beta) \cong S^{n-1}$. For $n = 1$ it is the usual boundary of an edge; for a $2$-cell attached to a wedge of circles it is the vector of exponent sums of the attaching word, as in Lesson 10.

### Examples and a program

* $S^n$ ($n \ge 1$): cells in dimensions $0$ and $n$ only, so all $d$ vanish and $H_* = \mathbb{Z}$ in degrees $0, n$.
* $\mathbb{CP}^n$ has cells in dimensions $0, 2, \dots, 2n$ (a standard fact), no cells in adjacent dimensions, so all $d = 0$ and $H_k = \mathbb{Z}$ for even $k \le 2n$ and $0$ for odd $k$.
* $\mathbb{RP}^n$ has one cell in each dimension up to $n$, with $d_k = 1 + (-1)^k$ (the attaching map is the double cover $S^{k-1} \to \mathbb{RP}^{k-1}$, of degree $1 + (-1)^k$ onto the top cell; the antipodal degree is computed in Lesson 12). So the chain complex is $\mathbb{Z} \xleftarrow{0} \mathbb{Z} \xleftarrow{2} \mathbb{Z} \xleftarrow{0} \mathbb{Z} \xleftarrow{2} \cdots$, and $H_k(\mathbb{RP}^n) = \mathbb{Z}/2$ for $k$ odd with $k < n$, $0$ for $k$ even with $0 < k \le n$, $H_0 = \mathbb{Z}$, and $H_n = \mathbb{Z}$ for $n$ odd, $0$ for $n$ even.

The following program computes Betti numbers from cell counts and boundary matrices:

```python
from fractions import Fraction


def rank(matrix):
    """Rank over the rationals by Gaussian elimination."""
    rows = [[Fraction(x) for x in row] for row in matrix]
    r = 0
    for c in range(len(rows[0]) if rows else 0):
        pivot = next((i for i in range(r, len(rows)) if rows[i][c] != 0), None)
        if pivot is None:
            continue
        rows[r], rows[pivot] = rows[pivot], rows[r]
        for i in range(len(rows)):
            if i != r and rows[i][c] != 0:
                f = rows[i][c] / rows[r][c]
                rows[i] = [a - f * b for a, b in zip(rows[i], rows[r])]
        r += 1
    return r


def cellular_betti(cells, d):
    """cells[k] = number of k-cells; d[k] = matrix of d_k (cells[k-1] rows)."""
    ranks = [0] + [rank(d[k]) for k in range(1, len(cells))] + [0]
    return [cells[k] - ranks[k] - ranks[k + 1] for k in range(len(cells))]


# The genus 2 surface: 1 vertex, 4 edges, 1 face, all d = 0.
print(cellular_betti([1, 4, 1], {1: [[0, 0, 0, 0]], 2: [[0], [0], [0], [0]]}))
# Connected sum of three projective planes: d_2 = (2, 2, 2).
print(cellular_betti([1, 3, 1], {1: [[0, 0, 0]], 2: [[2], [2], [2]]}))
```

This prints `[1, 4, 1]` and `[1, 2, 0]`. The second says $b_1(N_3) = 2$, and the cellular complex also records the torsion $\mathbb{Z}/2$, which the rank computation does not see.

### The Euler characteristic

**Theorem 3.** For a finite CW complex, $\chi(X) = \sum_n (-1)^n c_n = \sum_n (-1)^n \operatorname{rank} H_n(X)$. In particular $\chi$ does not depend on the cell structure and is a homotopy invariant.

*Proof.* Apply rank–nullity to the cellular complex over $\mathbb{Q}$ exactly as in Lesson 7: $b_n = c_n - \operatorname{rank} d_n - \operatorname{rank} d_{n+1}$ by Theorem 2, and the alternating sum telescopes. $\blacksquare$

So $\chi(S^n) = 1 + (-1)^n$, $\chi(\Sigma_g) = 2 - 2g$, $\chi(N_h) = 2 - h$, $\chi(\mathbb{RP}^{n}) = 1$ for $n$ even and $0$ for $n$ odd. **Covers.** A covering space of a CW complex is a CW complex whose cells are the lifts of the cells (a standard fact, using that cells are simply connected so they lift); thus an $n$-sheeted cover of a finite complex has $\chi(\tilde X) = n\chi(X)$. This is how the genus of a covering surface is computed in practice.

@example
title: The homology of a real projective space
problem: Compute $H_k(\mathbb{RP}^5)$ for all $k$.
step: $\mathbb{RP}^5$ has one cell in each dimension $0, \dots, 5$ with $d_k = 1 + (-1)^k$ for $k \ge 1$: $d_1 = 0$, $d_2 = 2$, $d_3 = 0$, $d_4 = 2$, $d_5 = 0$.
step: Degree $0$: $d_1 = 0$, so $H_0 = \mathbb{Z}$.
step: Degree $1$: $\ker d_1 = \mathbb{Z}$ and $\operatorname{im} d_2 = 2\mathbb{Z}$, so $H_1 = \mathbb{Z}/2$. Degree $2$: $d_2$ is injective, so $\ker d_2 = 0$ and $H_2 = 0$. Degree $3$: $\ker d_3 = \mathbb{Z}$ and $\operatorname{im} d_4 = 2\mathbb{Z}$, so $H_3 = \mathbb{Z}/2$. Degree $4$: $\ker d_4 = 0$, so $H_4 = 0$.
step: Degree $5$: $\ker d_5 = \mathbb{Z}$ and there are no $6$-cells, so $H_5 = \mathbb{Z}$.
answer: $H_*(\mathbb{RP}^5) = (\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}/2, 0, \mathbb{Z}/2, 0, \mathbb{Z})$. Check: counting cells, $\chi = 1 - 1 + 1 - 1 + 1 - 1 = 0$, and counting Betti numbers, $b_0 - b_5 = 1 - 1 = 0$.

@example
title: A cover of a surface
problem: A closed orientable surface $\Sigma_2$ of genus $2$ has a connected $3$-sheeted cover $\tilde\Sigma$ that is a closed orientable surface. Find its genus.
step: $\chi(\Sigma_2) = 2 - 2 \cdot 2 = -2$.
step: For a finite $n$-sheeted covering, $\chi(\tilde\Sigma) = n\,\chi(\Sigma_2) = 3 \cdot (-2) = -6$.
step: A closed orientable surface of genus $g$ has $\chi = 2 - 2g$, so $2 - 2g = -6$ gives $g = 4$.
answer: The cover has genus $4$.

@mistakes
- Treating $d_n$ as the boundary of the cell's attaching map in the space and not as a degree: for $\mathbb{RP}^2$ the $2$-cell has $d_2 = 2$, not $1$ (its attaching word is $a^2$).
- Using Euler characteristic to conclude homeomorphism or homotopy equivalence: $T^2$ and the Klein bottle both have $\chi = 0$ yet differ in $H_1$.
- Computing $H_n$ as $\ker d_n$ divided by nothing when $d_{n+1}$ is nonzero, or reading $\ker d_n$ as $\mathbb{Z}$ when $d_n \ne 0$: if $d_n \ne 0$ on a cyclic chain group $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$, the kernel is $0$.
- Forgetting that cellular homology needs the cells to form a CW complex: attaching maps must go into the lower skeleton.

model:
```python
def cyclic_homology(ds):
    """Homology of a chain complex with Z in each degree."""
    n_top = len(ds)
    d = [0] + list(ds) + [0]            # d[n] : C_n -> C_{n-1}
    result = []
    for n in range(n_top + 1):
        if d[n] != 0:                    # kernel of d_n is zero
            result.append((0, 1))
        elif d[n + 1] == 0:              # kernel Z, nothing killed
            result.append((1, 1))
        else:                            # Z modulo |d_{n+1}| Z
            result.append((0, abs(d[n + 1])))
    return result
```
hint: The kernel of multiplication by an integer on $\mathbb{Z}$ is $\mathbb{Z}$ if the integer is $0$ and $0$ otherwise.
hint: Divide into three cases for $H_n = \ker d_n / \operatorname{im} d_{n+1}$: $d_n \ne 0$; $d_n = 0 = d_{n+1}$; $d_n = 0 \ne d_{n+1}$. Remember $d_0 = d_{N+1} = 0$.
solution: In degree $n$ the cycles are $\ker d_n$, equal to $\mathbb{Z}$ if $d_n = 0$ and $0$ otherwise, and the boundaries are $d_{n+1}\mathbb{Z}$. If $d_n \ne 0$ then $H_n = 0$. hint: Use the fact that a finite $n$-sheeted covering of a finite CW complex is a finite CW complex with $n$ lifts of each cell.

solution: Give $\Sigma_h$ a finite CW structure with $c_k$ cells of dimension $k$. A covering space of a CW complex is a CW complex whose $k$-cells are the lifts of the $k$-cells: each open cell is simply connected, so the characteristic map lifts (lifting criterion), and each cell has exactly $n$ lifts, one through each point of the fiber over a point of the cell. Hence the cover has $nc_k$ cells of dimension $k$, and Theorem 3 gives $\chi(\Sigma_g) = \sum (-1)^k nc_k = n\chi(\Sigma_h)$. Since $\chi(\Sigma_g) = 2 - 2g$ and $\chi(\Sigma_h) = 2 - 2h$, we get $2 - 2g = n(2 - 2h)$, that is $g - 1 = n(h - 1)$. For $g = 2$ and $h = 3$ this reads $1 = 2n$, impossible for an integer $n$. For $h = 1$ it reads $g - 1 = 0$, so $g = 1$. $\blacksquare$
