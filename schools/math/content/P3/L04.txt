@article
How do you compute the fundamental group of a surface? Cut it open along a few loops until you get a polygon, then remember which edges were glued. The polygon is a disk, so it contributes nothing but a single relation. This lesson turns that picture into a theorem.

### Cell complexes

A **CW complex** is built in stages. Start with a discrete set $X^0$ of **$0$-cells**. Given $X^{n-1}$, form $X^n$ by taking a disjoint union of closed balls $D^n_\alpha$ and gluing each boundary sphere $S^{n-1} = \partial D^n_\alpha$ to $X^{n-1}$ by a continuous **attaching map** $\varphi_\alpha\colon S^{n-1} \to X^{n-1}$. The space is $X = \bigcup_n X^n$ with the weak topology (a set is closed if it meets each closed cell in a closed set). A finite complex needs no further condition. Examples:

| space | cells |
|---|---|
| $S^n$ | one $0$-cell and one $n$-cell, attached by the constant map |
| $\mathbb{RP}^n$ | one cell in each dimension $0, \dots, n$; the $k$-cell is attached by the double cover $S^{k-1} \to \mathbb{RP}^{k-1}$ |
| torus $T^2$ | one vertex, edges $a, b$, one $2$-cell attached along $aba^{-1}b^{-1}$ |
| orientable surface $\Sigma_g$, $g \ge 1$ | one vertex, edges $a_1, b_1, \dots, a_g, b_g$, one $2$-cell attached along $[a_1, b_1] \cdots [a_g, b_g]$ |
| nonorientable surface $N_h$, $h \ge 1$ | one vertex, edges $a_1, \dots, a_h$, one $2$-cell attached along $a_1^2 a_2^2 \cdots a_h^2$ |

Here $[a, b] = aba^{-1}b^{-1}$, and a letter with exponent $-1$ means the edge traversed backward. The torus is $\Sigma_1$, the projective plane is $N_1$, and the Klein bottle is $N_2$, which is also given by the word $aba^{-1}b$.

### Attaching cells

**Theorem 1.** Let $X$ be path-connected with basepoint $x_0$, and let $Y$ be obtained from $X$ by attaching $2$-cells $e_\alpha$ along maps $\varphi_\alpha\colon S^1 \to X$. For each $\alpha$ choose a path $\gamma_\alpha$ in $X$ from $x_0$ to $\varphi_\alpha(1)$, and let $N$ be the normal subgroup of $\pi_1(X, x_0)$ generated by the classes $[\gamma_\alpha \cdot \varphi_\alpha \cdot \bar\gamma_\alpha]$. Then the inclusion induces a surjection $\pi_1(X) \to \pi_1(Y)$ with kernel $N$. If instead cells of dimension $n \ge 3$ are attached, the inclusion induces an isomorphism $\pi_1(X) \to \pi_1(Y)$.

*Proof for one cell.* Let $Y = X \cup_\varphi D^n$, with $n \ge 2$, let $z$ be the center of the cell, $U = Y \setminus \{z\}$ and $V$ the open cell. Both are open and path-connected. Radial deformation of the punctured ball shows that $U$ deformation retracts onto $X$. $V$ is an open ball, hence contractible. The intersection $U \cap V$ is a punctured open ball, homotopy equivalent to $S^{n-1}$, which is path-connected for $n \ge 2$; its fundamental group is $\mathbb{Z}$ for $n = 2$ and trivial for $n \ge 3$ (Lesson 2). Choose a basepoint in $U \cap V$ and transport it to $X$ along a radial path; this is where the path $\gamma$ enters. By van Kampen, $\pi_1(Y) = \pi_1(U)/N'$ where $N'$ is the normal closure of the image of $\pi_1(U \cap V)$ in $\pi_1(U)$. For $n = 2$ the generator of $\pi_1(U \cap V)$, a small circle around $z$, is homotopic in $U$ to the attaching loop $\varphi$ pushed into $X$, giving $N$. For $n \ge 3$ the group $\pi_1(U \cap V)$ is trivial, so $N'$ is trivial. $\blacksquare$

For several cells attach them one at a time (finitely many) or apply van Kampen to the family $U$ and the open cells (an outline, as the argument needs the general version of the theorem for the triple intersections). Two consequences matter for exams. **First**, $\pi_1$ of a path-connected CW complex depends only on its $2$-skeleton. **Second**, if $X$ has one $0$-cell, $1$-cells $x_1, \dots, x_n$ and $2$-cells attached along words $w_\alpha$ in the letters $x_i^{\pm 1}$, then
$$\pi_1(X) \cong \langle x_1, \dots, x_n \mid w_\alpha \text{ for all } \alpha \rangle.$$
For example $\pi_1(\Sigma_g) = \langle a_1, b_1, \dots \mid [a_1, b_1] \cdots [a_g, b_g] \rangle$, $\pi_1(\mathbb{RP}^2) = \langle a \mid a^2 \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}/2$, and $\pi_1(\mathbb{RP}^n) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2$ for $n \ge 2$ since its $2$-skeleton is $\mathbb{RP}^2$.

### Abelianization

The **abelianization** $G^{ab} = G / [G, G]$ of a presentation group is the abelian group on the same generators with the same relators written additively, that is $\mathbb{Z}^n / \text{(row space of the exponent-sum matrix)}$. The row of a relator lists the total exponent of each generator in it. The rank of $G^{ab}$ is $n$ minus the rank of this matrix over $\mathbb{Q}$, and the torsion is read from its Smith normal form.

```python
def free_reduce(word):
    """Free reduction of a word: a list of nonzero integers, -k meaning x_k^-1."""
    out = []
    for letter in word:
        if out and out[-1] == -letter:
            out.pop()
        else:
            out.append(letter)
    return out


def exponent_matrix(n, relators):
    """Row r lists the exponent sums of x_1, ..., x_n in the r-th relator."""
    return [[sum(1 if s > 0 else -1 for s in w if abs(s) == g)
             for g in range(1, n + 1)] for w in relators]


print(free_reduce([1, 2, -2, -1, 3]))
print(exponent_matrix(2, [[1, 2, -1, -2]]))
```

This prints `[3]` and `[[0, 0]]`: the commutator has exponent sum zero in each letter, so $\langle a, b \mid [a, b]\rangle$ has abelianization $\mathbb{Z}^2$, and the matrix of the relator is zero. Checking a presentation by computer proves nothing about an infinite group; it computes the abelianization, which is a finite computation.

**A useful trick for exams.** To show a group is nonabelian, or that two presentations give different groups, map onto something familiar. The surface group $\pi_1(\Sigma_g)$ maps onto the free group $F_g$ by $a_i \mapsto x_i$, $b_i \mapsto 1$, since the relator goes to $\prod [x_i, 1] = 1$; so it is nonabelian for $g \ge 2$, even though its abelianization is $\mathbb{Z}^{2g}$.

@example
title: Reading the group of a surface from its polygon
problem: Let $S$ be the quotient of a hexagon by identifying its edges according to the word $a\,a\,b\,b\,c\,c$ (all edges oriented around the boundary). Assuming the quotient has one vertex, find $\pi_1(S)$, its abelianization, and identify the surface.
step: The cell structure has one vertex, three edges $a, b, c$ and one $2$-cell attached along $a^2b^2c^2$, which is the standard cell structure of $N_3$.
step: By Theorem 1 and the presentation consequence, $\pi_1(S) = \langle a, b, c \mid a^2 b^2 c^2 \rangle$.
step: The exponent-sum matrix has one row, $(2, 2, 2)$. Over $\mathbb{Q}$ its rank is $1$, so the abelianization has rank $3 - 1 = 2$.
step: The row is $2 \cdot (1, 1, 1)$, and $(1, 1, 1)$ is primitive, so it extends to a basis of $\mathbb{Z}^3$, for instance $(1,1,1), (0,1,0), (0,0,1)$. In that basis the relation subgroup is spanned by twice the first basis vector, so the quotient is $\mathbb{Z}/2 \oplus \mathbb{Z}^2$.
step: Euler characteristic check: $1 - 3 + 1 = -1 = 2 - 3$, as for $N_3$, the connected sum of three projective planes.
answer: $\pi_1(S) = \langle a, b, c \mid a^2b^2c^2\rangle$ with abelianization $\mathbb{Z}^2 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2$.

@example
title: Attaching a $2$-cell to a circle with a degree-$n$ map
problem: Let $Y$ be obtained from $S^1$ by attaching a $2$-cell along the map $z \mapsto z^n$ (with $n \ge 1$; for $n = 2$ this is $\mathbb{RP}^2$). Compute $\pi_1(Y)$.
step: Take $X = S^1$ with one vertex and $\pi_1(X) = \mathbb{Z}$ generated by $a$, the loop that goes once around.
step: The attaching loop $\varphi(z) = z^n$ is $a^n$; here $\varphi(1) = 1$ is the basepoint, so the path $\gamma$ is constant and no conjugation is needed.
step: By Theorem 1, $N$ is the normal closure of $a^n$ in $\mathbb{Z}$, namely $n\mathbb{Z}$.
step: Hence $\pi_1(Y) = \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. For $n = 2$ this is $\mathbb{RP}^2$, for $n = 1$ the disk.
answer: $\pi_1(Y) \cong \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$.

@mistakes
- Using the subgroup generated by the attaching words instead of its normal closure: the kernel of $\pi_1(X) \to \pi_1(Y)$ is normal in $\pi_1(X)$.
- Ignoring the connecting path $\gamma_\alpha$ when the attaching map does not pass through the basepoint: the relation is the conjugate $\gamma_\alpha \varphi_\alpha \bar\gamma_\alpha$.
- Claiming that attaching a $3$-cell does nothing: it does not change $\pi_1$, but it does change homology (attaching a $3$-cell to $S^2 \vee S^2$ along a degree-one map onto one sphere kills that sphere's $H_2$ class).
- Reading "$\mathbb{Z}/2 \oplus \mathbb{Z}$" off a one-relator group by counting relators: use the exponent-sum matrix, since $\langle a, b \mid a^2b^2 \rangle$ has abelianization $\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2$, not $\mathbb{Z}/2$.

@practice ms
prompt: Let $X$ be a path-connected CW complex. Which of these operations leave $\pi_1(X)$ unchanged (up to isomorphism, for every such $X$)?
choice*: Attaching a $3$-cell along any map $S^2 \to X$.
choice*: Attaching a $5$-cell along any map $S^4 \to X$.
choice*: Attaching a $2$-cell along a loop that is null-homotopic in $X$.
choice: Attaching a $2$-cell along any loop in $X$.
choice: Attaching a $1$-cell whose two endpoints are both in $X$.
hint: Compute $N$ for each case, or compare with a wedge with a circle.
solution: Cells of dimension $n \ge 3$ do not change $\pi_1$ (Theorem 1). A $2$-cell attached along a null-homotopic loop has $N = 1$. Attaching a $2$-cell along an essential loop kills a nontrivial normal subgroup. Attaching an arc between two points of $X$ adds a free generator: the new space is homotopy equivalent to $X \vee S^1$ and has $\pi_1 = \pi_1(X) * \mathbb{Z}$.

model:
```python
from fractions import Fraction


def abelian_rank(n, relators):
    """Rank of the abelianization of <x_1..x_n | relators>."""
    rows = []
    for word in relators:
        row = [Fraction(0)] * n
        for letter in word:
            row[abs(letter) - 1] += 1 if letter > 0 else -1
        rows.append(row)
    rank = 0
    for col in range(n):
        pivot = next((r for r in range(rank, len(rows))
                      if rows[r][col] != 0), None)
        if pivot is None:
            continue
        rows[rank], rows[pivot] = rows[pivot], rows[rank]
        for r in range(len(rows)):
            if r != rank and rows[r][col] != 0:
                factor = rows[r][col] / rows[rank][col]
                rows[r] = [a - factor * b for a, b in zip(rows[r], rows[rank])]
        rank += 1
    return n - rank
```
hint: Build the exponent-sum rows first, then row-reduce over the rationals and count pivots.
hint: Two different relators can give dependent rows, so do not just subtract the number of relators.
solution: The abelianization is $\mathbb{Z}^n$ modulo the integer row space, so its rank is $n$ minus the rank of the matrix over $\mathbb{Q}$. Rank is computed by Gaussian elimination with exact fractions. Counting relators instead of rank fails when relators are dependent, and the cyclic example $x_1x_2,\ x_2x_3,\ x_3x_1$ shows that rank over $\mathbb{Q}$ cannot see the torsion $\mathbb{Z}/2$ that is present.

hint: Write each group as a presentation and compute its abelianization.
hint: Homotopy equivalent spaces have isomorphic fundamental groups, hence isomorphic abelianizations.
solution: By Theorem 1, $\pi_1(T^2) = \langle a, b \mid aba^{-1}b^{-1} \rangle$ and $\pi_1(K) = \langle a, b \mid abab^{-1} \rangle$. The exponent-sum row of the first relator is $(0, 0)$ and of the second is $(2, 0)$. So $\pi_1(T^2)^{ab} = \mathbb{Z}^2$ and $\pi_1(K)^{ab} = \mathbb{Z}^2 / \langle (2, 0) \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}/2 \oplus \mathbb{Z}$. These abelian groups are not isomorphic: the second has a nonzero element of finite order and the first is torsion-free. A homotopy equivalence induces an isomorphism of fundamental groups (Lesson 1), hence of their abelianizations, so $T^2 \not\simeq K$. $\blacksquare$

@practice self
prompt: Show that $\pi_1(\Sigma_2)$, the fundamental group of the closed orientable surface of genus $2$, is not abelian, even though its abelianization is $\mathbb{Z}^4$.
hint: Find a surjection onto a free group by killing generators.
hint: Check that the relator $[a_1, b_1][a_2, b_2]$ maps to the identity.
solution: By Theorem 1, $\pi_1(\Sigma_2) = \langle a_1, b_1, a_2, b_2 \mid [a_1, b_1][a_2, b_2] \rangle$. Define a homomorphism from the free group on $a_1, b_1, a_2, b_2$ to the free group $F(x, y)$ by $a_1 \mapsto x$, $b_1 \mapsto 1$, $a_2 \mapsto y$, $b_2 \mapsto 1$. The relator maps to $[x, 1][y, 1] = 1$, so the map factors through a surjection $\pi_1(\Sigma_2) \to F(x, y)$. If $\pi_1(\Sigma_2)$ were abelian, its image $F(x, y)$ would be abelian, but $F(x,y)$ is not: by Lesson 3 it maps onto $S_3$ by $x \mapsto (1\ 2)$, $y \mapsto (1\ 3)$, where $xy \ne yx$. The abelianization is the row-space quotient: the single relator has exponent sums $(0, 0, 0, 0)$, so $\pi_1(\Sigma_2)^{ab} = \mathbb{Z}^4$. $\blacksquare$
