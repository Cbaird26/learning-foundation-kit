@article
Simplicial homology required a triangulation, and the answer might depend on the choice. Singular homology has no such choice: use all continuous maps of simplices into the space. The chain groups are enormous (uncountable for most spaces), but the definition is functorial in the space at no cost, and homotopy invariance becomes a clean algebraic argument.

### Singular chains

Let $\Delta^n = [e_0, \dots, e_n]$ be the standard $n$-simplex. A **singular $n$-simplex** in $X$ is a continuous map $\sigma\colon \Delta^n \to X$. The group $C_n(X)$ is the free abelian group on all singular $n$-simplices, and
$$\partial\sigma = \sum_{i=0}^{n} (-1)^i\, \sigma|_{[e_0, \dots, \widehat{e_i}, \dots, e_n]},$$
where the restriction to the $i$-th face is identified with a singular $(n-1)$-simplex via the order-preserving linear map from $\Delta^{n-1}$. The cancellation argument of Lesson 7 shows $\partial\partial = 0$. Then $H_n(X) = \ker\partial_n / \operatorname{im}\partial_{n+1}$. A continuous $f\colon X \to Y$ gives $f_\#\sigma = f \circ \sigma$, and $\partial f_\# = f_\#\partial$ (a **chain map**), so $f$ induces $f_*\colon H_n(X) \to H_n(Y)$ with $(gf)_* = g_* f_*$ and $\mathrm{id}_* = \mathrm{id}$.

**Proposition 1.** (a) If $X$ has path components $X_\alpha$, then $H_n(X) \cong \bigoplus_\alpha H_n(X_\alpha)$. (b) For path-connected $X$, $H_0(X) \cong \mathbb{Z}$. (c) For a point, $H_0 \cong \mathbb{Z}$ and $H_n = 0$ for $n \ge 1$.

*Proof.* (a) The image of a simplex $\Delta^n$ is path-connected, so lies in one $X_\alpha$; hence $C_n(X) = \bigoplus C_n(X_\alpha)$ compatibly with $\partial$. (b) For a $1$-simplex, $\partial\sigma = \sigma(e_1) - \sigma(e_0)$. Let $\varepsilon\colon C_0(X) \to \mathbb{Z}$ add up the coefficients. Then $\varepsilon\partial = 0$, so $\varepsilon$ descends to $H_0(X) \to \mathbb{Z}$, which is onto. If $\varepsilon(\sum n_i x_i) = 0$, choose paths $\gamma_i$ from a fixed point $x_*$ to $x_i$; then $\sum n_i x_i = \sum n_i (x_i - x_*) = \partial(\sum n_i \gamma_i)$, a boundary. So $\varepsilon$ is injective on $H_0$. (c) For the point, $C_n = \mathbb{Z}$ with a single simplex $\sigma_n$ and $\partial\sigma_n = \sum_{i=0}^n (-1)^i \sigma_{n-1}$, which is $\sigma_{n-1}$ for $n$ even and $0$ for $n$ odd. So $\partial_n$ is an isomorphism for $n \ge 2$ even and zero for $n$ odd, and $H_n = 0$ for $n \ge 1$, $H_0 = \mathbb{Z}$. $\blacksquare$

### Homology and the fundamental group

A loop $f\colon I \to X$ is a singular $1$-simplex with $\partial f = 0$, hence a cycle, so $[f] \in H_1(X)$.

**Theorem 2 (Hurewicz, degree one).** For path-connected $X$ the map $h\colon \pi_1(X, x_0) \to H_1(X)$, $[f] \mapsto [f]$, is a well-defined surjective homomorphism whose kernel is the commutator subgroup. Hence $H_1(X) \cong \pi_1(X, x_0)^{ab}$. (Stated here without proof; Hatcher proves it by showing path homotopy and products behave as homology classes should, and then constructing the surjectivity by subdividing cycles into loops.)

Consequences: $H_1(S^1) \cong \mathbb{Z}$, $H_1(T^2) \cong \mathbb{Z}^2$, $H_1$ of a wedge of $n$ circles is $\mathbb{Z}^n$, and $H_1$ of the plane with $k$ punctures is $\mathbb{Z}^k$. **Homology is weaker than $\pi_1$:** the groups $H_1$ cannot tell apart $T^2$ from a wedge of two circles with a sphere attached, although their fundamental groups $\mathbb{Z}^2$ and $F_2$ differ.

### Homotopy invariance

**Lemma 3.** Chain maps $\varphi, \psi\colon C_\bullet \to D_\bullet$ are **chain homotopic** if there are $P\colon C_n \to D_{n+1}$ with $\partial P + P\partial = \psi - \varphi$. Then $\varphi_* = \psi_*$ on homology.

*Proof.* Let $z \in C_n$ be a cycle. Then $\psi(z) - \varphi(z) = \partial P z + P\partial z = \partial(Pz)$, a boundary, so $[\psi z] = [\varphi z]$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 4 (homotopy invariance).** If $f \simeq g\colon X \to Y$, then $f_* = g_*\colon H_n(X) \to H_n(Y)$ for all $n$.

*Proof (the algebra complete, the geometric identity outlined).* Let $F\colon X \times I \to Y$ be a homotopy from $f$ to $g$. For $\sigma\colon \Delta^n \to X$, the prism $\Delta^n \times I$ is subdivided into $n + 1$ simplices $[e_0, \dots, e_i, e_i', \dots, e_n']$, where $e_j$ are the vertices at level $0$ and $e_j'$ at level $1$. Define the **prism operator**
$$P(\sigma) = \sum_{i=0}^{n} (-1)^i\, F \circ (\sigma \times \mathrm{id})\big|_{[e_0, \dots, e_i, e_i', \dots, e_n']} \in C_{n+1}(Y).$$
A computation in which the interior faces of the prism cancel in pairs (not reproduced here; see Hatcher) gives $\partial P = g_\# - f_\# - P\partial$. This says $f_\#$ and $g_\#$ are chain homotopic, and Lemma 3 gives $f_* = g_*$. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary 5.** A homotopy equivalence induces isomorphisms on all $H_n$. Contractible spaces have $H_n = 0$ for $n \ge 1$ and $H_0 = \mathbb{Z}$, the homology of a point. In particular $H_n(\mathbb{R}^k) = 0$ for $n \ge 1$.

*Proof.* If $gf \simeq \mathrm{id}_X$ and $fg \simeq \mathrm{id}_Y$, then $g_* f_* = \mathrm{id}$ and $f_* g_* = \mathrm{id}$ by Theorem 4 and functoriality. $\blacksquare$

The isomorphism of simplicial and singular homology for a simplicial complex $K$ and $|K|$ is a standard theorem, stated here without proof (Hatcher proves it for $\Delta$-complexes by induction over skeleta, using the long exact sequence of Lesson 9, the five lemma and excision). Granting it, the computations of Lesson 7 are computations of singular homology. Two different spaces can share all their homology groups without being homotopy equivalent, so homology is an invariant and never a complete one.

@example
title: Path components of the topologist's sine curve with its limit point
problem: Let $S = \{(x, \sin(1/x)) : 0 < x \le 1\} \cup \{(0, 0)\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ and $O = (0, 0)$. Show that $H_0(S) \cong \mathbb{Z}^2$, although $S$ is connected.
step: Let $G = \{(x, \sin(1/x)) : 0 < x \le 1\}$. It is the continuous image of $(0, 1]$, so it is path-connected.
step: Claim: no path in $S$ joins $O$ to a point of $G$. Suppose $\gamma\colon I \to S$ is continuous with $\gamma(0) = O$ and $\gamma(1) \in G$. The set $\gamma^{-1}(O)$ is closed and not all of $I$, so $a = \max \gamma^{-1}(O) < 1$, and $\gamma(t) \in G$ for $t > a$.
step: Write $\gamma(t) = (x(t), \sin(1/x(t)))$ for $t > a$, with $x$ continuous and positive. Continuity of $\gamma$ at $a$ forces $x(t) \to 0$ and $\sin(1/x(t)) \to 0$ as $t \to a^+$.
step: By the intermediate value theorem $x$ takes every value in $(0, x(t_1)]$ on $(a, t_1]$ for any $t_1 > a$, including values $1/(\pi/2 + 2\pi k)$ for all large $k$, where $\sin(1/x) = 1$. This contradicts $\sin(1/x(t)) \to 0$.
step: So the path components are $G$ and $\{O\}$, and Proposition 1 gives $H_0(S) \cong \mathbb{Z}^2$. Yet $G \subseteq S \subseteq \overline{G}$ and the closure of a connected set is connected, so $S$ is connected: $H_0$ counts path components.
answer: $H_0(S) \cong \mathbb{Z}^2$, one generator for $G$ and one for the point $O$.

@example
title: Homology of the punctured plane
problem: Compute $H_0$ and $H_1$ of $\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}$.
step: The space deformation retracts onto $S^1$ (Lesson 1), so it is homotopy equivalent to $S^1$, and homology is a homotopy invariant (Corollary 5).
step: $S^1$ is path-connected, so $H_0 \cong \mathbb{Z}$ by Proposition 1.
step: $\pi_1(S^1) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ is abelian, so Theorem 2 gives $H_1(S^1) \cong \mathbb{Z}^{ab} = \mathbb{Z}$, generated by the class of the loop going once around.
answer: $H_0 \cong \mathbb{Z}$ and $H_1 \cong \mathbb{Z}$.

@mistakes
- Believing $H_1(X) = \pi_1(X)$: the Hurewicz map kills commutators, so $H_1(X) = \pi_1(X)^{ab}$, and a nonabelian $\pi_1$ loses information.
- Using homotopy invariance for maps that are only homotopic after moving the basepoint: for homology there is no basepoint and no conjugation, so any homotopy gives $f_* = g_*$.
- Forgetting that $H_0$ counts path components, not connected components: for the topologist's sine curve the two differ.
- Thinking a chain homotopy must be unique or a chain map: $P$ is just a family of homomorphisms $C_n \to D_{n+1}$, usually not commuting with $\partial$.

answer: 8
check: 2 ** 3
hint: Each coordinate has a sign; each choice of signs gives an open octant.
solution: A point of $X$ has all three coordinates nonzero, so $X$ is the disjoint union of the $2^3 = 8$ open octants. Each octant is convex, hence path-connected, and they are open and disjoint, so they are the path components. By Proposition 1, $H_0(X) \cong \mathbb{Z}^8$, of rank $8$.

@practice self
prompt: Let $\varphi, \psi\colon C_\bullet \to D_\bullet$ be chain homotopic chain maps. Prove that $\varphi_* = \psi_*$ on homology. Then deduce that if $f\colon X \to Y$ is a homotopy equivalence, with homotopy inverse $g$, then $f_*$ is an isomorphism on every $H_n$, using homotopy invariance of the induced maps.
hint: A chain homotopy is $P$ with $\partial P + P\partial = \psi - \varphi$. Apply both sides to a cycle.
hint: For the second part use functoriality of $f \mapsto f_*$ on the two composites $gf$ and $fg$.
solution: Let $P\colon C_n \to D_{n+1}$ satisfy $\partial P + P\partial = \psi - \varphi$. If $z \in C_n$ is a cycle, then $\partial z = 0$ and so $\psi(z) - \varphi(z) = \partial(Pz) + P(\partial z) = \partial(Pz)$. Thus $\psi(z)$ and $\varphi(z)$ differ by a boundary, and $[\psi z] = [\varphi z]$ in $H_n(D)$, which is $\psi_* = \varphi_*$. For the second part, let $gf \simeq \mathrm{id}_X$ and $fg \simeq \mathrm{id}_Y$. By homotopy invariance (Theorem 4) and functoriality, $g_* f_* = (gf)_* = (\mathrm{id}_X)_* = \mathrm{id}_{H_n(X)}$ and $f_* g_* = (fg)_* = \mathrm{id}_{H_n(Y)}$. So $f_*$ has a two-sided inverse $g_*$ and is an isomorphism. $\blacksquare$

rubric: States that the argument works degree by degree and conclude equality in homology, not equality of chains.

@practice self
prompt: Use the Hurewicz isomorphism $H_1 \cong \pi_1^{ab}$ and the computation of $\pi_1$ for punctured planes (Lesson 3) to prove that $\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{k \text{ points}\}$ and $\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{l \text{ points}\}$ are homotopy equivalent if and only if $k = l$ (for $k, l \ge 0$).
hint: If the numbers of punctures agree the spaces are homeomorphic. For the converse compute $H_1$ and its rank.
hint: A free abelian group $\mathbb{Z}^k$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}^l$ only if $k = l$; tensor with $\mathbb{Q}$ or reduce mod $2$ to see it.
solution: If $k = l$, the two spaces are homeomorphic: any two $k$-element subsets of the plane are carried to each other by a homeomorphism of the plane (a standard fact), hence so are their complements. Conversely suppose $X_k \simeq X_l$. By Lesson 3, $\pi_1(X_k)$ is free of rank $k$ (for $k = 0$ it is trivial). The abelianization of a free group $F_k$ is $\mathbb{Z}^k$ (the universal property of the free group: homomorphisms $F_k \to A$ to an abelian group are $k$-tuples of elements of $A$, as are homomorphisms $\mathbb{Z}^k \to A$). By Theorem 2, $H_1(X_k) \cong \mathbb{Z}^k$, and likewise $H_1(X_l) \cong \mathbb{Z}^l$. By Corollary 5 a homotopy equivalence induces an isomorphism $\mathbb{Z}^k \cong \mathbb{Z}^l$. Tensoring with $\mathbb{Q}$ gives $\mathbb{Q}^k \cong \mathbb{Q}^l$, and dimension of a vector space is well defined, so $k = l$. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Explains the easy direction by a homeomorphism.
rubric: Computes $H_1(X_k) \cong \mathbb{Z}^k$ by combining the free fundamental group with Hurewicz.
rubric: Uses invariance of $H_1$ under homotopy equivalence and the well-definedness of the rank of a free abelian group.

@quiz mc
prompt: Which statement is correct?
choice*: If $f \simeq g\colon X \to Y$ by a homotopy that moves a basepoint around a loop, then still $f_* = g_*$ on singular homology.
choice: If $H_n(X) \cong H_n(Y)$ for every $n$ then $X$ and $Y$ are homotopy equivalent.
choice: $H_1(X) \cong \pi_1(X)$ for every path-connected space $X$.
choice: Singular homology of a compact space is finitely generated in every degree because compact spaces have finitely many simplices.
hint: Homology has no basepoint. What happens to the conjugation in the $\pi_1$ statement after abelianizing?
solution: Homotopy invariance of singular homology (Theorem 4) holds for any homotopy, with no basepoint conditions; on $\pi_1$ the basepoint moving causes conjugation, which disappears in the abelianization. The other statements are false: $T^2$ and a wedge $S^1 \vee S^1 \vee S^2$ share homology, $H_1$ is the abelianization of $\pi_1$, and the chain groups $C_n(X)$ are free on all continuous maps $\Delta^n \to X$, which is an infinite set already for $X = [0, 1]$.
