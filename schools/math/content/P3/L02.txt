@article
Walk once around a circle, then twice, then backward once. Each walk is a loop, and the number of times you went around, counted with sign, appears to be the only thing that cannot be undone by wiggling the walk. This lesson proves that this is exactly the whole story for the circle, and then uses the same idea of cutting a loop into short pieces to show that spheres of dimension at least two have no loops that cannot be shrunk.

### The circle

Let $p\colon \mathbb{R} \to S^1$ be $p(s) = e^{2\pi i s}$. You met the following lifting properties in the earlier course; Lesson 5 proves them for every covering space, so we use them here.

**Lifting properties.** (a) For every path $f\colon I \to S^1$ and every $r \in \mathbb{R}$ with $p(r) = f(0)$ there is a unique path $\tilde f\colon I \to \mathbb{R}$ with $p\tilde f = f$ and $\tilde f(0) = r$. (b) For every homotopy $F\colon I \times I \to S^1$ and every $r$ with $p(r) = F(0, 0)$ there is a unique lift $\tilde F$ with $\tilde F(0, 0) = r$.

**Theorem 1.** The map $\Phi\colon \pi_1(S^1, 1) \to \mathbb{Z}$, $\Phi[f] = \tilde f(1)$ where $\tilde f$ is the lift with $\tilde f(0) = 0$, is a well-defined isomorphism.

*Proof.* The endpoint $\tilde f(1)$ lies in $p^{-1}(1) = \mathbb{Z}$. If $F$ is a path homotopy from $f_0$ to $f_1$, then the lift $\tilde F$ with $\tilde F(0,0) = 0$ has $t \mapsto \tilde F(0, t)$ and $t \mapsto \tilde F(1, t)$ lifting the constant paths, hence constant by uniqueness, so $\tilde f_0(1) = \tilde F(1, 0) = \tilde F(1, 1) = \tilde f_1(1)$. For a homomorphism, let $\tilde f$ and $\tilde g$ be lifts starting at $0$ with $\tilde f(1) = m$; then $\tilde f \cdot (m + \tilde g)$ is the lift of $f \cdot g$ starting at $0$, ending at $m + \tilde g(1)$. Surjective: the path $s \mapsto ns$ lifts $e^{2\pi i n s}$. Injective: if $\tilde f(1) = 0$, then $\tilde f$ is a loop in $\mathbb{R}$, homotopic to the constant by the straight-line homotopy $(1-t)\tilde f(s)$, and composing with $p$ gives $f \simeq_p c$. $\blacksquare$

The integer $\Phi[f]$ is the **winding number** of $f$. Winding number is also defined, and is invariant under free homotopy, for loops in $\mathbb{C} \setminus \{0\}$: use $z/|z|$.

### Decomposing loops

**Proposition 2.** Let $X = U \cup V$ with $U, V$ open and path-connected, $x_0 \in U \cap V$, and $U \cap V$ path-connected. Then every loop at $x_0$ in $X$ is path homotopic to a product of loops at $x_0$, each lying in $U$ or in $V$. In particular, if $U$ and $V$ are simply connected, so is $X$.

*Proof.* Let $f\colon I \to X$ be a loop. The sets $f^{-1}(U)$ and $f^{-1}(V)$ form an open cover of the compact metric space $I$; by the Lebesgue number lemma there is a partition $0 = s_0 < \dots < s_m = 1$ with each $f([s_{i-1}, s_i])$ inside $U$ or inside $V$. Merging adjacent intervals that go into the same set, we may assume consecutive intervals alternate, so $f(s_i) \in U \cap V$ for $0 < i < m$. Choose paths $g_i$ in $U \cap V$ from $x_0$ to $f(s_i)$, with $g_0, g_m$ constant. Let $f_i$ be $f$ restricted to $[s_{i-1}, s_i]$, reparametrized to $I$. Then
$$f \simeq_p (f_1 \cdot \bar g_1) \cdot (g_1 \cdot f_2 \cdot \bar g_2) \cdots (g_{m-1} \cdot f_m),$$
by inserting $\bar g_i \cdot g_i \simeq_p c$ (Lesson 1). Each factor $g_{i-1} f_i \bar g_i$ is a loop at $x_0$ in $U$ or in $V$. If $U, V$ are simply connected, each factor is null-homotopic, hence so is $f$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 3.** For $n \ge 2$ the sphere $S^n$ is simply connected.

*Proof.* Let $N, S$ be the poles, $U = S^n \setminus \{N\}$, $V = S^n \setminus \{S\}$. Stereographic projection makes each homeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^n$, which is contractible. The intersection $U \cap V \cong \mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{0\}$ is path-connected exactly when $n \ge 2$. Apply Proposition 2. $\blacksquare$

**For $n = 1$ the hypothesis fails and so does the conclusion**: $S^1 \setminus \{\pm 1\}$ has two components, and $\pi_1(S^1) \cong \mathbb{Z}$.

### Products and applications

If $X, Y$ are path-connected, then $\pi_1(X \times Y, (x_0, y_0)) \cong \pi_1(X, x_0) \times \pi_1(Y, y_0)$ via $[f] \mapsto ([p_X f], [p_Y f])$: a loop in the product is a pair of loops, a path homotopy in the product is a pair of path homotopies. So $\pi_1(T^2) \cong \mathbb{Z}^2$ for the torus $T^2 = S^1 \times S^1$.

Applications that appear on qualifying exams: there is no retraction $D^2 \to S^1$ (Lesson 1: it would make $\mathbb{Z} = \pi_1(S^1)$ inject into $\pi_1(D^2) = 1$); the fundamental theorem of algebra; and $\mathbb{R}^2 \not\cong \mathbb{R}^n$ for $n \ge 3$ (both are practice problems below). A typical strategy: find a loop in a complement, compute its winding number, and compare.

### Winding numbers by computer

For a closed polygon through nonzero complex numbers, add the argument of each ratio of consecutive vertices, each in $(-\pi, \pi)$:

```python
import math


def winding_number(zs):
    """Winding number about 0 of the closed polygon through zs."""
    total = 0.0
    for k in range(len(zs)):
        ratio = zs[(k + 1) % len(zs)] / zs[k]
        total += math.atan2(ratio.imag, ratio.real)
    return round(total / (2 * math.pi))


print(winding_number([1 + 0j, 1j, -1 + 0j, -1j]))
print(winding_number([1 + 0j, -1j, -1 + 0j, 1j]))
```

This prints `1` and `-1`. The sum computes the lift, so this is exactly Theorem 1. It is correct only when no edge passes through $0$. If you sample a loop too coarsely, the code answers a different question: the four points $1, -i, -1, i$ also sample the loop $e^{6\pi i s}$ (winding number $3$) at $s = 0, \tfrac14, \tfrac12, \tfrac34$, but the code returns $-1$. Computation checks examples and never replaces the proof.

@example
title: Reading a class off a lift
problem: Find the class in $\pi_1(S^1, 1) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ of the loop $f(s) = e^{2\pi i (2s + \frac{1}{4}\sin 2\pi s)}$.
step: Guess a lift: $\tilde f(s) = 2s + \tfrac14 \sin 2\pi s$ is continuous and $p\tilde f = f$.
step: Check the starting point: $\tilde f(0) = 0$, which is the lift required by Theorem 1.
step: By uniqueness of lifts, this is the lift of $f$ starting at $0$. Compute the endpoint: $\tilde f(1) = 2 + \tfrac14 \sin 2\pi = 2$.
step: By Theorem 1 the class of $f$ is $2$. As a check, $\sin 2\pi s$ is a bounded perturbation, so $f$ is homotopic (via $2s + \tfrac{t}{4}\sin 2\pi s$) to $e^{4\pi i s}$.
answer: The class is $2$, the loop winds twice around the circle.

@example
title: A sphere with a point removed from each side
problem: Show that $S^2 = U \cup V$ with $U = S^2 \setminus \{N\}$, $V = S^2 \setminus \{S\}$ satisfies the hypotheses of Proposition 2 and conclude $\pi_1(S^2) = 1$.
step: Both sets are open, being complements of points in a Hausdorff space.
step: Stereographic projection from $N$ is a homeomorphism $U \to \mathbb{R}^2$ sending $S$ to $0$ and $V \cap U$ onto $\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}$.
step: $\mathbb{R}^2$ is contractible, so $U$ is path-connected and simply connected; the same for $V$ by symmetry (project from $S$).
step: $\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}$ is path-connected, so is $U \cap V$; pick a basepoint $x_0$ on the equator, which lies in $U \cap V$.
step: Proposition 2 now applies: $S^2$ is simply connected.
answer: $\pi_1(S^2) = 1$. The same proof gives $\pi_1(S^n) = 1$ for all $n \ge 2$.

@mistakes
- Applying Proposition 2 to $S^1$ with two open arcs: the intersection of two arcs covering the circle has two components, so the proposition does not apply, and indeed $\pi_1(S^1) \ne 1$.
- Using closed sets in the decomposition: the proof needs open sets so that the Lebesgue number lemma gives a partition into pieces mapped into one set.
- Computing a winding number by counting the sampled points that lie above the real axis: only the sum of the argument increments (the lift) is a homotopy invariant, and it needs fine enough sampling.
- Assuming that two loops with the same winding number around two different punctures are homotopic in the plane minus two points: that group is free on two generators, not $\mathbb{Z}^2$ (Lesson 3).

model:
```python
def winding_pair(points, p, q):
    """Winding numbers of a closed polygon about p and q (ray crossings)."""
    def winding(center):
        cx, cy = center
        total = 0
        for k in range(len(points)):
            x1, y1 = points[k]
            x2, y2 = points[(k + 1) % len(points)]
            if (y1 > cy) != (y2 > cy):
                x_cross = x1 + (cy - y1) * (x2 - x1) / (y2 - y1)
                if x_cross > cx:
                    total += 1 if y2 > y1 else -1
        return total

    return (winding(p), winding(q))
```
hint: An edge crosses the horizontal line through the point exactly when one endpoint is above it and the other is not.
hint: Compute the $x$-coordinate where the edge meets that line, and count the crossing only if it lies to the right of the point.
solution: A closed curve winds around a point as many times, counted with sign, as it crosses a fixed ray from the point, upward crossings on the right-hand side counting $+1$ for a counterclockwise loop. The helper handles one center; the answer applies it to both points. Counting without the sign, or counting crossings of the whole line instead of the ray, fails on the bowtie, whose two lobes have opposite orientation.

@practice self
prompt: Prove the fundamental theorem of algebra: every nonconstant polynomial $q(z) = z^n + a_{n-1}z^{n-1} + \dots + a_0$ with complex coefficients and $n \ge 1$ has a root in $\mathbb{C}$. Use the winding number of loops in $\mathbb{C} \setminus \{0\}$ and the fact that it is invariant under (free) homotopy of loops.
hint: Suppose $q$ has no root. For $r > 0$ consider the loop $t \mapsto q(re^{2\pi i t})$ and let $r$ vary from $0$.
hint: For $r$ large compare $q(re^{2\pi i t})$ with $r^n e^{2\pi i n t}$ along a straight-line family and bound the lower-order terms.
solution: Suppose $q$ has no root. For $r \ge 0$ let $\gamma_r(t) = q(re^{2\pi i t})$ for $t \in I$, a loop in $\mathbb{C} \setminus \{0\}$. The map $(t, r) \mapsto \gamma_r(t)$ is continuous, so the winding number $w(\gamma_r)$ does not depend on $r$ (it is an integer varying continuously, being invariant under homotopy of loops: lift the homotopy to $\mathbb{R}$ and compare the integers $\tilde\gamma_r(1) - \tilde\gamma_r(0)$, which vary continuously and are integers). At $r = 0$ the loop is constant, so $w(\gamma_r) = 0$ for all $r$. Now fix $r > \max(1, \sum_j |a_j|)$ and set $q_s(z) = z^n + s\sum_{j<n} a_j z^j$ for $s \in [0, 1]$. On $|z| = r$, $|s\sum_{j<n} a_j z^j| \le \sum_j |a_j| r^{j} \le (\sum_j |a_j|) r^{n-1} < r^n = |z^n|$, so $q_s(z) \ne 0$ on $|z| = r$. Thus the loops $t \mapsto q_s(re^{2\pi i t})$ are a homotopy in $\mathbb{C} \setminus \{0\}$ from $\gamma_r$ to $t \mapsto r^n e^{2\pi i n t}$, whose winding number is $n$. So $0 = w(\gamma_r) = n \ne 0$, a contradiction. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Defines the loops $\gamma_r$ and justifies that their winding number is constant in $r$ and equal to $0$ at $r = 0$.
rubric: Gives a homotopy for large $r$ between $\gamma_r$ and $r^n e^{2\pi i n t}$ and proves with an explicit estimate that it avoids $0$.
rubric: Computes the winding number $n \ne 0$ of the comparison loop and states the contradiction.

solution: Two open arcs covering the circle overlap in two disjoint arcs, so the intersection is not connected. The arcs themselves are open and path-connected (each is homeomorphic to an interval, contractible), and a basepoint can be chosen in either overlap, but no single path in the intersection joins the two overlaps, so the decomposition of loops fails; indeed a loop around the circle cannot be written as a product of loops in a single arc.
