@article
Professional writing is writing that gets something done: a request granted, a meeting set, a question answered. Your reader is busy, may skim, and will judge your competence by how easy you make their task. The skills are the same ones you have practiced all course, applied to a short form: know your audience and purpose, be specific, and revise.

### Anatomy of an effective email

1. **Subject line**: specific and informative. "Question about shadowing opportunities, Dr. Rao's clinic" beats "Hello" or "Question."
2. **Greeting**: use the person's name and title ("Dear Dr. Rao," or "Hello Ms. Lee,"). If unsure, be more formal and check the spelling.
3. **Opening line**: say who you are in one line (if needed) and why you are writing.
4. **Body**: give necessary background in a few short sentences. One email, one main request. Use a short list if you have several items.
5. **Clear request and deadline**: say what you want and by when ("Could you let me know by Friday, March 7, whether a thirty-minute meeting is possible?").
6. **Closing**: thank the reader and sign with your full name (and a role or program if useful). "Sincerely," or "Best regards," works.

### Tone and formality

Match tone to the relationship: more formal with a teacher, employer or stranger; friendlier but still polite with a classmate. Avoid slang, all caps, excessive exclamation points and emoji in formal contexts. Be direct without sounding demanding: "Could you..." or "Would you be able to..." is more effective than "I need you to...". Make it easy to say yes by offering specific times or options.

### Concision and scannability

Lead with the purpose. Keep paragraphs to two to four sentences. Cut apologies and filler ("I know you are very busy, but..."). Use bullets for lists of items or questions. Put the key information (date, time, action) where it can be found at a glance. If you refer to an attachment, name it and say what it is.

### Special cases

- **Following up**: wait a reasonable time (a few business days), then send a polite reminder that restates the request briefly.
- **Declining or giving bad news**: be clear and courteous; offer an alternative if you can.
- **Reply-all**: use it only when everyone needs the information.
- **Confidentiality**: do not put sensitive personal information in an email unless it is necessary and the channel is approved for it. In health settings, never include identifiable patient information in ordinary email; follow the institution's privacy rules.
- **Permanence**: an email can be forwarded. Write nothing you would not want the reader to show others.

### Other professional documents

A **résumé** lists education, experience, skills and activities, with bullet points that begin with strong verbs and give results ("Organized a food drive that collected 450 pounds of food"). A **cover letter** selects two or three relevant strengths and ties them to the specific role. A **thank-you note** after an interview or a favor is short, specific and prompt.

### Proofread

Check names, dates, attachments and the spelling of the recipient's name. Read the email aloud once. Reread the subject line. Only then send.

@example
title: Revising a vague request
problem: Revise this email to a teacher. Subject: "Help." Body: "hey, i was gonna ask if you could maybe look at my essay sometime, thanks."
step: Write a specific subject line: "Request for feedback on my research essay by Friday."
step: Use a proper greeting and name yourself: "Dear Ms. Park, I am in your fourth-period English class."
step: State the request and the context with a deadline: "Could you give me feedback on my draft this week? The final version is due on Monday, April 7."
step: Make it easy and polite: offer a time ("I can come by after school on Wednesday or Thursday") and thank the reader; close with your full name.

@mistakes
- Using a vague or empty subject line, or none.
- Burying the request in a long paragraph, or making several unrelated requests in one email.
- Using slang or a demanding tone with someone who has authority over you.
- Sending sensitive or identifiable information in ordinary email.
