@article
A personal essay or statement answers a quiet question every reader has: *Who is this person, and what would they bring to our community?* Admissions readers may read hundreds of essays, so yours must be clear, specific and genuinely yours. It is not a résumé in paragraph form. It is a short piece of reflective storytelling.

### Choose a focus

The best topics are small and concrete: a recurring chore, a failed experiment, a conversation, a place you know well. The topic does not need to be dramatic. A well-observed afternoon repairing a bicycle can say more than a vague account of "a life-changing mission trip." Ask:

- What do I know in unusual detail?
- When did I change my mind or learn something?
- What do I do when no one is grading me?

Read the **prompt** carefully and answer it. Many applications offer several prompts and a limit (for example, 650 words); respect both.

### Show, then reflect

**Telling**: "I am a hard-working and caring person." **Showing**: "At 5:40 each morning I measured flour for forty loaves while my aunt read orders aloud, and I learned to hear when the oven's hum changed." Specific sensory detail and action let readers draw the conclusion themselves. But a scene alone is not enough. **Reflection** is where you say what the moment meant, what you understood later and how it changed what you do. A common weakness is all scene with no insight, or all insight with no scene.

### A flexible structure

1. **Opening**: start in the middle of a moment (a line of dialogue, an action, an image), not with a dictionary definition or a sweeping claim.
2. **Development**: a few concrete scenes or beats, with the tension or problem clear.
3. **Turning point**: a moment of surprise, failure, decision or realization.
4. **Reflection and ending**: what you learned and how it points forward. Avoid a tidy moral that repeats the opening; end on an image or a specific next step.

Not every essay fits this pattern, but readers should always sense an arc: a change from one way of seeing to another.

### Voice and honesty

Write the way a thoughtful version of you speaks. Avoid a thesaurus voice ("I endeavored to ameliorate the situation"). Humor is fine if it is natural. Write about your own experience and growth, not about being the hero of someone else's hardship. Be truthful: invented achievements are easy to spot and cost trust.

### Clichés to avoid

The sports victory that "taught me teamwork," the volunteer trip that "opened my eyes," the broad quote that opens the essay, and the list of adjectives. These topics are not forbidden, but they need unusual detail and honest reflection to avoid sounding generic.

### Revising

Cut anything that does not serve the story. Read it aloud. Check that each paragraph has a job. Make sure the word count is within the limit. Ask a trusted reader: *What did you learn about me?* If the answer is not what you meant, revise. Proofread last: a careless typo distracts from a careful essay.

@example
title: From a generic opening to a specific one
problem: Revise this opening for a personal essay: "I have always been passionate about helping people, and volunteering at the hospital taught me the value of compassion."
step: Diagnose. The sentence tells traits ("passionate," "compassion") and offers no scene, so any applicant could have written it.
step: Find the specific moment. Perhaps a patient asked for help with a crossword and the writer realized how much waiting felt like loneliness.
step: Start in the scene: "Seven letters, 'a feeling of being alone,' Mr. Alvarez asked me, tapping his crossword with a pencil stub."
step: Plan the reflection: the writer later realizes that a simple, unhurried conversation is a form of care, and that moment shapes how the writer wants to listen.

@mistakes
- Opening with a broad statement or a quotation instead of a moment.
- Listing achievements already in the application, rather than revealing something new.
- Ending with a moral that merely repeats what the scenes already showed.
- Ignoring the word limit or the prompt.
