@article
**Geography** is the study of places, the features of Earth's surface, and the ways people and environments affect each other. Geographers split their work into **physical geography** (landforms, climate, rivers, oceans) and **human geography** (where people live, what languages they speak, how they trade and move).

### Parts of a map

Most maps share a few tools. The **title** says what the map shows. The **compass rose** shows directions. The **legend** (or key) explains the symbols and colors. The **scale** links distance on the map to distance on the ground. Different maps answer different questions: a **physical map** shows mountains and rivers, a **political map** shows borders and cities, and a **thematic map** shows one topic, such as rainfall or population.

### The global grid

Mapmakers use imaginary lines to give every place an "address."

- **Latitude** lines run east-west and measure how far north or south a place is from the **equator** (0°). They run from 0° to 90° north (the North Pole) and 90° south (the South Pole).
- **Longitude** lines (meridians) run north-south and measure how far east or west a place is from the **prime meridian** (0°), which passes through Greenwich, England. They run from 0° to 180° east and 180° west.

A place is written latitude first, then longitude. For example, 34° S, 18° E means 34 degrees south of the equator and 18 degrees east of the prime meridian. The equator divides Earth into the **Northern** and **Southern Hemispheres**; the prime meridian and the 180° meridian divide it into the **Eastern** and **Western Hemispheres**.

One degree of latitude is about 111 km everywhere on Earth, so you can estimate north-south distance: multiply the difference in degrees by 111 km.

### Scale

A scale such as "1 cm = 50 km" means every centimeter on the map stands for 50 kilometers on the ground. To find real distance, multiply the map distance by the scale.

### Time zones

Earth turns through 360° in 24 hours, so it turns 15° each hour. Places 15° of longitude apart differ by about one hour of local solar time. Countries use **time zones** so that clocks in a region agree; their boundaries often follow political borders rather than straight meridians.

### Maps distort

Earth is a sphere, and a flat map cannot show a sphere without stretching or squeezing something. Every **map projection** is a trade-off: it may keep shapes right but distort area, or keep area right but distort shape. A globe is the only fully accurate model.

@example
title: Distance along a meridian
problem: City P is at 15° S, 40° E and City Q is at 25° N, 40° E. About how far apart are they, and which direction is Q from P?
step: The longitudes match (40° E), so the cities lie on the same meridian and only latitude differs.
step: P is south of the equator and Q is north, so the angle between them is the sum: $15 + 25 = 40$ degrees.
step: Multiply by about 111 km per degree: $40 \times 111 = 4440$ km.
step: Q has the larger northern latitude, so Q is north of P. The distance is about 4,440 km.

@mistakes
- Writing longitude before latitude, or forgetting to give N/S and E/W, so the place is ambiguous.
- Mixing up which lines are which: lines of latitude run east-west, but they measure north-south position.
- Using the scale backwards (dividing map distance by the scale instead of multiplying).
- Assuming a flat map shows true sizes of countries near the poles.
