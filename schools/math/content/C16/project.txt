## Week-by-week key ideas

1. **Counting principles.** Sum, product and bijection rules; $n!$, $\binom{n}{k}$; arrangements with repeats $\frac{n!}{\prod n_i!}$; stars and bars $\binom{n+k-1}{k-1}$; ranking permutations by the factorial number system.
2. **Binomial coefficients.** Pascal's identity, the binomial theorem, Vandermonde, hockey stick, reflection for lattice paths; prove identities by double counting.
3. **Inclusion–exclusion.** $|\bigcup A_i|=\sum(-1)^{|S|+1}|\bigcap_S A_i|$; $D_n=n!\sum(-1)^k/k!$; surjections $\sum(-1)^j\binom kj(k-j)^n$; rook numbers.
4. **Pigeonhole and Ramsey.** Generalized pigeonhole, $R(s,t)\le R(s-1,t)+R(s,t-1)$, $R(3,3)=6$, counting lower bound, Goodman's formula.
5. **Recurrences.** Characteristic roots (distinct, repeated), particular solutions, companion matrices.
6. **Generating functions.** Formal series, inverses, partial fractions, products for counting, Euler's partition identity.
7. **Graphs.** Handshake lemma, Havel–Hakimi, isomorphism invariants, $(A^k)_{ij}$ counts walks.
8. **Trees.** Connected with $n-1$ edges, cut property and Kruskal, Prüfer codes and $n^{n-2}$, matrix-tree theorem.
9. **Euler and Hamilton.** Even degrees and connectivity; Dirac's condition; bridges lie on no cycle.
10. **Matchings.** Berge, Hall, König, Kuhn's algorithm.
11. **Coloring and planarity.** Odd cycles, $\chi\le\Delta+1$, deletion–contraction, Euler's formula, $E\le3V-6$, $E\le2V-4$.
12. **Flows.** Weak duality, residual networks, max-flow min-cut, integrality, Edmonds–Karp.

## Common errors

- Dividing by the wrong factorials or forgetting a shift in stars and bars.
- Applying $\sum(-1)^k\binom nk=0$ at $n=0$.
- Treating $R(s,t)$ recursion as equality, or a degree sequence as determining a graph.
- Forgetting the initial values or the root multiplicity in recurrences.
- Dropping connectivity in Euler's theorem; treating Dirac's condition as necessary.
- Using a maximal matching as maximum; forgetting backward residual edges in flows.

prompt: The wheel $W_5$ has a hub vertex joined to each vertex of a $5$-cycle (so $6$ vertices and $10$ edges). How many spanning trees does it have?
answer: 121
check: int(run("from fractions import Fraction\nn = 6\nedges = [(i, (i + 1) % 5) for i in range(5)] + [(i, 5) for i in range(5)]\nL = [[Fraction(0)] * n for _ in range(n)]\nfor u, v in edges:\n    L[u][u] += 1\n    L[v][v] += 1\n    L[u][v] -= 1\n    L[v][u] -= 1\nM = [r[:n - 1] for r in L[:n - 1]]\nm = n - 1\ndet = Fraction(1)\nfor c in range(m):\n    p = next(r for r in range(c, m) if M[r][c] != 0)\n    if p != c:\n        M[c], M[p] = M[p], M[c]\n        det = -det\n    det *= M[c][c]\n    for r in range(c + 1, m):\n        f = M[r][c] / M[c][c]\n        M[r] = [x - f * y for x, y in zip(M[r], M[c])]\nprint(int(det))"))
hint: Use the matrix-tree theorem: delete one row and column of the Laplacian and take the determinant.
hint: Deleting the hub's row and column leaves a $5 \times 5$ circulant matrix with $3$ on the diagonal and $-1$ for cycle neighbors.
solution: The Laplacian minor obtained by deleting the hub has $3$ on the diagonal and $-1$ between neighbors on the $5$-cycle. Its determinant, computed by exact elimination, is $121$, so by the matrix-tree theorem there are $121$ spanning trees. (For wheels this equals a Lucas number minus $2$.)

model:
```python
def walks_between(n, edges, u, v, k):
    """Number of walks of length k from u to v."""
    neighbors = [[] for _ in range(n)]
    for a, b in edges:
        neighbors[a].append(b)
        neighbors[b].append(a)
    counts = [0] * n
    counts[u] = 1
    for _ in range(k):
        new = [0] * n
        for x in range(n):
            for y in neighbors[x]:
                new[y] += counts[x]
        counts = new
    return counts[v]
```
hint: `counts[x]` is the number of walks of the current length from `u` to `x`; one step sends each count to the neighbors.
solution: By the walk theorem the answer is $(A^k)_{uv}$. Multiplying the row vector $e_u$ by $A$ a total of $k$ times computes that entry with exact integers in $O(k(n+m))$ steps. For $K_4$ the closed walks of length $k$ at a vertex number $(3^k + 3(-1)^k)/4$, which gives the last test, and the counts satisfy a linear recurrence in $k$ whose roots are the eigenvalues of $A$.

@exam self
prompt: Let $G$ be a $k$-regular bipartite graph with $k \ge 1$. Prove that the edges of $G$ can be partitioned into $k$ perfect matchings.
hint: Induct on $k$; use that a regular bipartite graph has a perfect matching.
hint: Delete a perfect matching and check what regularity remains.
solution: Induct on $k$. By Hall's theorem, a $k$-regular bipartite graph with $k \ge 1$ has a perfect matching: counting edges gives $|X| = |Y|$, and for $S \subseteq X$ the $k|S|$ edges leaving $S$ end in $N(S)$, which receives at most $k|N(S)|$ edges, so $|N(S)| \ge |S|$. For $k = 1$ the graph itself is a perfect matching. For $k \ge 2$, choose a perfect matching $M$; removing its edges lowers every degree by exactly $1$, leaving a $(k-1)$-regular bipartite graph with $k - 1 \ge 1$. By induction its edges split into $k - 1$ perfect matchings, which together with $M$ give $k$ perfect matchings partitioning the edges of $G$.
rubric: Proves or correctly cites Hall's theorem to obtain a perfect matching, checking the hypothesis for regular bipartite graphs.
rubric: Removes the matching and observes the remaining graph is $(k-1)$-regular and bipartite.
rubric: Completes the induction with the base case $k = 1$.
