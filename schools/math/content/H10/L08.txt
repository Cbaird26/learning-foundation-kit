@article
Words are the writer's tools, and a large, flexible vocabulary lets you understand harder texts and express exact ideas.

### Word parts

Many English words are built from **roots**, **prefixes** and **suffixes**, often from Greek and Latin.

- **Prefixes**: *un-* (not), *re-* (again), *pre-* (before), *anti-* (against), *mis-* (wrong), *sub-* (under).
- **Roots**: *bene* (good), *mal* (bad), *dict* (say), *scrib/script* (write), *spect* (look), *port* (carry), *chron* (time), *graph* (write).
- **Suffixes**: *-ness* (state of), *-able* (able to be), *-logy* (study of), *-tion* (act of).

So *benevolent* ("wishing good"), *malicious* ("full of ill will"), and *inscription* ("something written in") become decodable. Use word parts as clues, not proof; check with a dictionary because meanings drift.

### Context clues

When you meet an unknown word, use the text around it:

- **Definition**: "The *taciturn*, or silent, guest said little."
- **Synonym or restatement**, **antonym** or contrast ("unlike his *garrulous* brother, he said almost nothing"), and **example**.
- **Inference** from the whole situation.

### Denotation, connotation and register

**Denotation** is the dictionary meaning; **connotation** is the emotional or cultural shading. *Thrifty*, *frugal* and *cheap* all mean careful with money, but *thrifty* is positive and *cheap* is negative. **Register** is the level of formality: "kids" in conversation, "children" in a report.

### Precision

Choose the most specific word: "walked" is vague; *strolled*, *trudged* and *marched* each tell more. Precision often replaces an adjective plus a vague word with one strong word ("very tired" becomes *exhausted*). Beware of misusing impressive-sounding words: a thesaurus lists related words, but only some fit your meaning and tone.

### Concision

Cut redundancy ("past history," "end result," "completely eliminate"), filler ("in order to" becomes "to"; "there are many people who" becomes "many people"), and repeated ideas. Prefer verbs to noun forms: "make a decision" becomes "decide."

### Figurative and idiomatic language

Idioms ("break the ice") cannot be decoded literally. Recognize them from context and use them sparingly in formal writing.

### Revision recipe

1. Underline vague words (*thing*, *good*, *a lot*, *very*).
2. Replace each with a precise word, checking connotation.
3. Cut repetition and filler.
4. Read aloud to check tone and rhythm.

@example
title: Revising for precision and concision
problem: Revise: "In my opinion, I think that the final end result of the game was really very bad for our team, due to the fact that we made a lot of mistakes."
step: Remove filler and redundancy: delete "in my opinion, I think that" and "final end."
step: Replace vague words: "really very bad" becomes "disastrous."
step: Replace the long phrase: "due to the fact that" becomes "because."
step: Result: "The result of the game was disastrous because we made many mistakes." (less than half the words, more exact)

@mistakes
- Choosing a thesaurus synonym with the wrong connotation.
- Assuming a word part always has one meaning (for example, "in-" can mean "in" or "not").
- Adding adverbs (very, really) instead of choosing a stronger word.
- Using slang or idioms in formal writing without thinking about register.
