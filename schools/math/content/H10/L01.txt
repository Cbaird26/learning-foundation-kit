@article
**Close reading** means reading slowly enough to notice what a writer chose to include and how those choices produce meaning. A story is not only what happens. It is also the way the writer tells it.

### The elements to track

- **Plot** is the sequence of events: exposition, rising action, climax, falling action and resolution. The **conflict** drives it, whether it is person against person, against nature, against society or against themselves.
- **Characterization** is how we learn who someone is: through what they say, do and think, how others react to them, and what the narrator says directly. Characters who change over the story are **dynamic**; those who do not are **static**.
- **Setting** is time and place, but also mood. A cold, empty kitchen at dawn suggests something different from a crowded kitchen at noon.
- **Point of view** is who tells the story. In **first person** a character says "I" and we see only what that character knows. In **third-person limited** the narrator stays close to one character's thoughts. In **third-person omniscient** the narrator can enter any mind.
- **Tone** is the narrator's or writer's attitude toward the subject (wry, tender, bitter). It comes from **diction** (word choice) and **syntax** (sentence structure).

### From detail to theme

A **theme** is a statement about life that the story suggests. It is not a single word. "Loneliness" is a **topic**; "People sometimes hide their loneliness behind busy routines" is a theme. Reach a theme by moving from evidence to claim:

1. Notice a detail (a repeated object, a shift in tone, a gap between what a character says and does).
2. Ask what it does: what does it reveal or suggest?
3. Connect several such details into a pattern.
4. State the pattern as a sentence that could apply beyond this story.

### Annotating

Mark the text as you read. Circle unfamiliar words, underline striking images, put a star beside turning points, and write short margin notes such as "mood shifts here" or "contradicts earlier claim." Annotations are most useful when they ask questions: *Why this word? Why does the narrator skip this?*

### Supporting a claim

A claim without evidence is only an opinion. Pair every interpretation with a short quotation or specific detail, then explain how the detail supports the claim. Good readers separate three things: what the text **says** (summary), what it **shows** (evidence) and what it **means** (interpretation).

@example
title: From detail to theme
problem: Read this original passage and state a theme. "Marta wiped the counter again, though it was already clean. The phone sat beside the sugar bowl. She had told her brother she would call first; he had told her the same. The kettle clicked off, and she poured the water over a single teabag."
step: Notice details. Marta cleans something already clean. The phone sits untouched. Both siblings made the same promise. She makes tea for one.
step: Ask what the details do. The pointless cleaning and the untouched phone show nervous avoidance; "the same" promise shows that both are waiting for the other.
step: Connect the pattern: stubbornness on both sides keeps the call from happening, and Marta fills the time with small tasks.
step: State a theme as a sentence: Pride can keep people apart even when each of them wants to reconnect.

@mistakes
- Stating a topic ("friendship," "pride") instead of a full-sentence theme.
- Summarizing the plot when asked for analysis; retelling is not the same as explaining meaning.
- Assuming the narrator is the author, or that a first-person narrator is always reliable.
- Quoting a line without saying what it proves.
