@project
title: A literary analysis paper on a public-domain work
overview: You will write a literary analysis paper of about 1,000 words on a public-domain novel or play, such as Frankenstein, Pride and Prejudice, Great Expectations, Macbeth or Romeo and Juliet. The paper must have an arguable thesis, embedded quotations with analysis, and citations in MLA style. You will also give a short, five-minute explanation of your argument.
goal: Argue an interpretation of a whole work through close reading of selected passages.
goal: Embed, explain and cite quotations accurately.
goal: Revise for clear structure, concise and precise style, and correct grammar.
step: Choose a public-domain novel or play you can read in full (a free edition is fine) and read it with annotations for conflict, imagery, repeated motifs, and shifts in tone.
step: List three to five patterns or puzzles you noticed, and pick the one that interests you most; write a draft thesis in the form "By [technique], the author shows [meaning]."
step: Collect six to eight short quotations that support your thesis, noting page, chapter or act, scene and line for each; sort them into three groups, each becoming a body paragraph.
step: Write an outline with introduction, three body paragraphs (claim, evidence, analysis, link) and a conclusion; add a counterargument or complication paragraph if you can.
step: Draft the paper (about 1,000 words) in present tense, embedding quotations with signal phrases and following each with analysis longer than the quotation.
step: Revise in three passes: structure and thesis, then concision and precision of word choice, then grammar and punctuation; read the paper aloud.
step: Format the citations (parenthetical page or act-scene-line references and a Works Cited entry) and check each quotation against the text.
step: Prepare and rehearse a five-minute talk summarizing your thesis, your three points and why the interpretation matters.
deliverable: The finished paper of about 1,000 words with a Works Cited entry.
deliverable: Your annotated copy of at least two passages (photographs or photocopies, or typed notes).
deliverable: An outline or notes card for the five-minute talk.
rubric: The thesis is arguable, specific, and names a technique and a meaning.
rubric: Every body paragraph has a clear claim, a well-chosen embedded quotation and analysis that explains how the words create meaning.
rubric: The organization is logical, with transitions and a conclusion that explains significance.
rubric: Quotations are accurate and cited; the paper uses present tense and avoids plot summary.
rubric: Sentences are concise, precise and correct, with few errors in grammar and punctuation.
tutor: Here is my thesis. Is it arguable, and how could I make it more specific?
tutor: Here is a quotation I plan to use; what words and devices could I analyze in it?
tutor: What is the strongest counterargument to my thesis, and how could I answer it?

@review
### Week by week

- **Week 1, reading fiction closely.** Track plot, character, setting, point of view and tone. A theme is a full-sentence claim about life supported by a pattern of details.
- **Week 2, poetry.** Identify the speaker and situation, imagery, figures (simile, metaphor, personification), sound devices, meter (iambic pentameter has five iambs) and the turn. Always link technique to effect.
- **Week 3, drama.** Know dialogue, soliloquy, aside, stage directions and dramatic irony; analyze conflict as want plus obstacle; paraphrase Shakespeare to the punctuation, not the line end.
- **Week 4, literary nonfiction.** Find the central idea and purpose; in memoir separate the younger self from the older narrator; summarize versus analyze.
- **Week 5, rhetoric.** Ethos is credibility, pathos is emotion and logos is logic; analyze the rhetorical situation; recognize devices and fallacies such as straw man and false dilemma.
- **Week 6, sources.** Primary, secondary and tertiary sources; authority, accuracy, purpose, currency and corroboration; correlation is not causation.
- **Week 7, grammar and style.** Fix fragments, run-ons and comma splices; agreement, apostrophes, parallelism, active voice and concision.
- **Week 8, vocabulary.** Roots, prefixes, suffixes and context clues; denotation, connotation and register; precision and concision.
- **Week 9, literary analysis essays.** An arguable thesis, body paragraphs with claim, evidence, analysis and link; embed quotations and write in present tense.
- **Week 10, argument.** Claims of fact, value and policy; reasons, evidence, counterargument and rebuttal.
- **Week 11, research.** Narrow a research question, evaluate sources, paraphrase honestly and cite.
- **Week 12, speaking and listening.** One main message, a clear structure, rehearsed delivery and active listening.

### Self-check

- I can state a theme as a full sentence and support it with evidence.
- I can name a device and explain its effect.
- I can label ethos, pathos and logos and name common fallacies.
- I can judge a source using several criteria.
- I can fix a comma splice and a fragment.
- I can write a thesis and a body paragraph with embedded quotations.

### Common errors

Confusing topic and theme; naming devices without effects; summarizing plot instead of analyzing; treating correlation as causation; using only a comma between independent clauses; leaving quotations unexplained; and changing a few words and calling it a paraphrase.

@exam self
prompt: Read this original paragraph from a draft. "Many people like dogs. Dogs are good. Due to the fact that dogs are loyal, they make good pets, they are very friendly." Identify two problems with the writing (such as a comma splice, vague words, filler or an unsupported claim) and write a revised version of two or three sentences that states a claim with one piece of evidence you could cite.
hint: Look for a comma splice, vague words like "good," and filler such as "due to the fact that"; then add a specific reason.
solution: Problems: (1) a comma splice ("they make good pets, they are very friendly"); (2) vague and filler language ("good," "due to the fact that"), plus an unsupported claim about loyalty. Revision: Dogs make strong companions because they are loyal and sociable. A study that tracks pet owners over time could show whether owners report less loneliness than non-owners; that evidence would turn a general opinion into a supported claim.
rubric: Correctly identifies at least two problems.
rubric: The revision fixes those problems and is concise and precise.
rubric: States a claim and names an appropriate kind of evidence.
