@article
Theorem 2 of Week 5 splits the interpolation error into a part that depends on the function, $f^{(n+1)}(\xi)/(n+1)!$, and a part that depends only on the nodes, $\omega(x) = \prod (x - x_j)$. We cannot change $f$, but we can choose the nodes. Which $n + 1$ points in $[-1, 1]$ make $\max|\omega|$ as small as possible?

### Chebyshev polynomials

The **Chebyshev polynomial** of degree $n$ is $T_n(x) = \cos(n \arccos x)$ for $x \in [-1, 1]$. With $x = \cos\theta$, $T_n(x) = \cos n\theta$, and the identity $\cos((n+1)\theta) + \cos((n-1)\theta) = 2\cos\theta\cos n\theta$ gives the **three-term recurrence**
$$T_0 = 1, \quad T_1 = x, \quad T_{n+1}(x) = 2x\,T_n(x) - T_{n-1}(x).$$
By induction $T_n$ is a polynomial of degree $n$ with leading coefficient $2^{n-1}$ (for $n \ge 1$). On $[-1, 1]$ we have $|T_n| \le 1$, and $T_n$ attains $\pm 1$ at the $n + 1$ points $\cos(k\pi/n)$, $k = 0, \dots, n$, with alternating signs $(-1)^k$. Its zeros are $\cos\bigl((2k+1)\pi/(2n)\bigr)$, $k = 0, \dots, n-1$.

**Chebyshev points** for degree $n$ interpolation (that is, $n + 1$ nodes) are the zeros of $T_{n+1}$:
$$x_k = \cos\frac{(2k+1)\pi}{2(n+1)}, \qquad k = 0, \dots, n.$$
They are the projections onto the $x$-axis of equally spaced points on the unit circle, so they cluster near $\pm 1$.

### Why they are optimal

**Theorem 1.** Among all monic polynomials $q$ of degree $m \ge 1$, the polynomial $2^{1-m}T_m$ has the smallest value of $\max_{[-1,1]}|q|$, namely $2^{1-m}$.

*Proof.* Put $\tilde T = 2^{1-m}T_m$, which is monic with $\max|\tilde T| = 2^{1-m}$ and takes the values $\pm 2^{1-m}$ alternately at the $m + 1$ points $\cos(k\pi/m)$. Suppose a monic $q$ of degree $m$ had $\max|q| < 2^{1-m}$. Then $r = \tilde T - q$ has degree at most $m - 1$ (the leading terms cancel), and at the points $\cos(k\pi/m)$ the sign of $r$ equals the sign of $\tilde T$, which alternates. So $r$ changes sign $m$ times and has at least $m$ zeros by the intermediate value theorem. A nonzero polynomial of degree at most $m - 1$ has at most $m - 1$ zeros, so $r \equiv 0$, i.e. $q = \tilde T$, contradicting $\max|q| < 2^{1-m}$. $\blacksquare$

For nodes equal to the zeros of $T_{n+1}$ we have $\omega = 2^{-n}T_{n+1}$, so $\max|\omega| = 2^{-n}$, the smallest possible. With Theorem 2 of Week 5:
$$\max_{[-1,1]}|f - p| \le \frac{\max|f^{(n+1)}|}{(n+1)!\,2^{n}}.$$
For $f(x) = 1/(1 + 25x^2)$ the maximum interpolation error at Chebyshev points is:

| degree $n$ | 4 | 6 | 10 | 14 | 20 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| max error | 0.40 | 0.26 | 0.109 | 0.047 | 0.0153 |

It decreases steadily, geometrically with ratio about $1/1.22$ per degree (stated without proof), where equispaced nodes gave $59.8$ at $n = 20$.

### Piecewise methods: splines

Another cure for the Runge phenomenon is to keep the degree low and shrink the pieces. **Piecewise linear interpolation** joins the data points by straight lines.

**Theorem 2.** Let $f \in C^2[a, b]$, let $a = x_0 < x_1 < \dots < x_N = b$ with $x_{i+1} - x_i \le h$, and let $s$ be the piecewise linear interpolant. Then $\max_{[a,b]}|f - s| \le \dfrac{h^2}{8}\max_{[a,b]}|f''|$.

*Proof.* On $[x_i, x_{i+1}]$ the function $s$ is the degree-one interpolant. Theorem 2 of Week 5 with $n = 1$ gives $f(x) - s(x) = \tfrac12 f''(\xi)(x - x_i)(x - x_{i+1})$. The quadratic $|(x - x_i)(x - x_{i+1})|$ is largest at the midpoint, where it equals $(h_i/2)^2 \le h^2/4$. Hence $|f - s| \le \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{h^2}{4}\max|f''|$. $\blacksquare$

The error is $O(h^2)$ however many pieces there are, and it does not suffer from the Runge phenomenon, but $s$ has corners. A **cubic spline** is a function that is a cubic polynomial on each subinterval and is twice continuously differentiable ($C^2$) on $[a, b]$. With $N$ intervals there are $4N$ coefficients. Interpolation at both ends of every piece gives $2N$ conditions, and continuity of $s'$ and $s''$ at the $N - 1$ interior nodes gives $2(N - 1)$ more: $4N - 2$ in all, leaving two free. Two end conditions, for example $s''(a) = s''(b) = 0$ (the **natural spline**), make the spline unique. For $f \in C^4$, a mesh whose largest spacing is at most a fixed multiple of its smallest, and a suitable end condition (for example, prescribing $s'(a)$ and $s'(b)$ from $f'$), the error is $O(h^4)$ (stated without proof); the natural condition can spoil the order near the ends.

```python
import math


def chebyshev_nodes(n):
    """The n + 1 zeros of T_{n+1}, in decreasing order."""
    return [math.cos((2 * k + 1) * math.pi / (2 * (n + 1))) for k in range(n + 1)]


nodes = chebyshev_nodes(2)
print(all(abs(4 * x**3 - 3 * x) < 1e-12 for x in nodes))   # True
```

The last line checks that these three nodes are zeros of $T_3(x) = 4x^3 - 3x$. Such a numerical check supports the formula; the proof is the identity $\cos 3\theta = 4\cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta$.

@example
title: An error bound from Chebyshev points
problem: Let $p$ interpolate $f(x) = \sin(2x)$ on $[-1, 1]$ at the four zeros of $T_4$ (degree $n = 3$). Bound $\max|f - p|$.
step: Derivatives: $f^{(4)}(x) = 16 \sin 2x$, so $\max_{[-1,1]}|f^{(4)}| \le 16$.
step: For Chebyshev points $\max|\omega| = 2^{-n} = 2^{-3} = 1/8$.
step: The bound is $\dfrac{16}{4!} \cdot \dfrac18 = \dfrac{16}{192} = \dfrac{1}{12} \approx 0.083$.
step: A bound is not an equality: the actual maximum error is smaller (about $0.027$), but the bound needs no knowledge of the interpolant.
answer: $\max|f - p| \le 1/12$.

@example
title: Piecewise linear interpolation of the cosine
problem: How many equal pieces on $[0, \pi]$ guarantee a piecewise linear interpolant of $\cos x$ with error at most $10^{-4}$?
step: $\max|f''| = \max|\cos x| = 1$, so Theorem 2 gives error $\le h^2/8$ with $h = \pi/N$.
step: Require $\pi^2/(8N^2) \le 10^{-4}$, i.e. $N^2 \ge \pi^2 \cdot 10^4/8 \approx 12337$.
step: $\sqrt{12337} \approx 111.07$, so $N = 112$ pieces suffice.
answer: $N = 112$.

@mistakes
- Using $T_n$ outside $[-1, 1]$ via $\cos(n \arccos x)$: the formula needs $|x| \le 1$; the recurrence defines the polynomial everywhere and grows rapidly outside.
- Mixing up $n$ and $n + 1$: degree-$n$ interpolation uses the $n + 1$ zeros of $T_{n+1}$, and the bound has $(n+1)!\,2^n$.
- Concluding that Chebyshev interpolation is good for every function: the error bound needs $f \in C^{n+1}$ and derivatives that do not grow too fast; for $|x|$ convergence is slow.
- Using a piecewise linear fit where a smooth interpolant is needed: it has $O(h^2)$ error and a discontinuous derivative.

```python
def pw_linear(xs, ys, t):
    """Piecewise linear interpolation at t."""
    i = 0
    while i < len(xs) - 2 and t > xs[i + 1]:
        i += 1
    slope = (ys[i + 1] - ys[i]) / (xs[i + 1] - xs[i])
    return ys[i] + slope * (t - xs[i])
```
hint: Find the piece $[x_i, x_{i+1}]$ containing `t`, never letting `i + 1` run past the last index, then use the line through its two endpoints.
solution: The loop moves to the next piece while `t` lies beyond the right end of the current one, but stops at the last piece so that `t == xs[-1]` is still handled by the final line. The uneven spacing means the slope must use the actual node differences.

```python
import math


def cheb_interp_max_error(f, n, M):
    """Max error of Chebyshev-point interpolation on [-1, 1], on a grid."""
    xs = [math.cos((2 * k + 1) * math.pi / (2 * (n + 1))) for k in range(n + 1)]
    ys = [f(x) for x in xs]

    def p(t):
        total = 0.0
        for j in range(n + 1):
            term = ys[j]
            for k in range(n + 1):
                if k != j:
                    term *= (t - xs[k]) / (xs[j] - xs[k])
            total += term
        return total

    worst = 0.0
    for j in range(M + 1):
        t = -1 + 2 * j / M
        worst = max(worst, abs(f(t) - p(t)))
    return worst
```
hint: Reuse the Lagrange evaluation from Week 5, but with the Chebyshev nodes; the test grid stays equispaced.
solution: The nodes change, nothing else. For $1/(1 + 16x^2)$ equispaced interpolation of degree $10$ has error above $1$ (Runge), while Chebyshev nodes give about $0.059$, and increasing $n$ keeps lowering the error. The last test is the bound of Theorem 1 with $\max|f^{(6)}| \le e$ for $e^x$ on $[-1, 1]$; a grid maximum is below the true maximum, which is below the bound.

```python
def cheb_T(n, x):
    """Chebyshev polynomial T_n(x) by the three-term recurrence."""
    if n == 0:
        return 1.0
    prev, cur = 1.0, x
    for _ in range(n - 1):
        prev, cur = cur, 2 * x * cur - prev
    return cur
```
hint: Keep two variables for $T_{k-1}$ and $T_k$; treat $n = 0$ separately, since the loop starts from $T_1$.
solution: The recurrence follows from $\cos((k+1)\theta) + \cos((k-1)\theta) = 2\cos\theta\cos k\theta$ and it defines the polynomial for every real $x$: for example $T_3(2) = 4 \cdot 8 - 3 \cdot 2 = 26$, outside $[-1, 1]$ where the cosine formula does not apply.
