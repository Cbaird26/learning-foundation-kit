@article
To solve $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ you learned elimination: subtract multiples of row $1$ from the rows below to clear column $1$, then continue. Recording the multiples turns the method into a factorization that can solve many right-hand sides cheaply and can be analyzed.

### Elimination as factorization

Suppose $A$ is $n \times n$. Step $k$ subtracts $l_{ik} = a^{(k)}_{ik}/a^{(k)}_{kk}$ times row $k$ from row $i > k$, for $i = k+1, \dots, n$. The end result is an upper triangular matrix $U$, and the multipliers fill a **unit lower triangular** matrix $L$ (ones on the diagonal, $l_{ik}$ below it) with $A = LU$. To solve $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$: solve $L\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{b}$ by **forward substitution**, then $U\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}$ by **back substitution**. Each substitution costs $O(n^2)$, and the factorization $O(n^3)$: step $k$ performs $(n-k)^2$ multiply–subtract updates, so the total is
$$\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(n-k)^2 = \frac{(n-1)n(2n-1)}{6} \approx \frac{n^3}{3}$$
multiply–subtract pairs, about $\tfrac23 n^3$ floating-point operations.

**Theorem 1 (LU without pivoting).** Let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix whose leading principal submatrices $A_{[1..k]}$ (the top-left $k \times k$ blocks) are nonsingular for $k = 1, \dots, n - 1$. Then $A = LU$ with $L$ unit lower triangular and $U$ upper triangular.

*Proof.* Induction on $n$. For $n = 1$, take $L = (1)$, $U = A$. For $n \ge 2$ write $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & r^\top \\ c & B \end{pmatrix}$ with $a = a_{11} \ne 0$ (the case $k = 1$). One step of elimination gives
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ c/a & I \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} a & r^\top \\ 0 & S \end{pmatrix}, \qquad S = B - \tfrac1a\,c\,r^\top.$$
Row operations of the form "subtract a multiple of row $1$ from a lower row" do not change the determinant of any leading principal submatrix, so the determinant of the leading $(k+1) \times (k+1)$ block of $A$ equals $a$ times the determinant of the leading $k \times k$ block of $S$. For $k + 1 \le n - 1$ these are nonzero, so $S$ ($(n-1) \times (n-1)$) satisfies the hypothesis, and $S = L'U'$ by induction. Then $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ c/a & L' \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} a & r^\top \\ 0 & U' \end{pmatrix}$. $\blacksquare$

The hypothesis is needed: the permutation matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ is nonsingular but has $a_{11} = 0$, and no $LU$ factorization exists, since $l_{21}u_{11} = 1$ would force $u_{11} \ne 0$ while $u_{11} = a_{11} = 0$. (A singular matrix such as $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ can have an $LU$ factorization: its hypothesis holds.)

### Pivoting

Even when $LU$ exists, a small pivot is dangerous. Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 10^{-20} & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $\mathbf b = (1, 2)^\top$, with exact solution $x_1 \approx 1$, $x_2 \approx 1$. Without pivoting $l_{21} = 10^{20}$ and $u_{22} = 1 - 10^{20} \approx -10^{20}$ in floating point (the $1$ is lost), $y_2 = 2 - 10^{20} \approx -10^{20}$, so $x_2 = 1$ and $x_1 = (1 - x_2)/10^{-20} = 0$: the first component is completely wrong although the problem is well conditioned. **Partial pivoting** swaps the row with the largest $|a_{ik}|$ ($i \ge k$) into the pivot position before each step, producing $PA = LU$ with $P$ a permutation matrix and all multipliers $|l_{ik}| \le 1$.

**Theorem 2.** For every nonsingular $A$, partial pivoting runs to completion and gives $PA = LU$ with $|l_{ik}| \le 1$.

*Proof.* Suppose step $k$ has started: the matrix so far has the form $\begin{pmatrix} U_{11} & * \\ 0 & S \end{pmatrix}$ with $U_{11}$ upper triangular with nonzero diagonal ($k - 1$ steps done), and its determinant equals $\pm\det A \ne 0$, since elimination steps keep it and swaps only change the sign. If the first column of $S$ were zero, the determinant would be $\det U_{11}\det S = 0$, a contradiction. So some entry in column $k$ on or below the diagonal is nonzero, the largest one is a valid pivot, and it makes every multiplier at most $1$ in absolute value. $\blacksquare$

The multipliers being at most $1$ does not make partial pivoting perfect: the entries of $U$ can in principle grow like $2^{n-1}$, but this almost never happens in practice (stated without proof).

```python
from fractions import Fraction


def lu_nopivot(A):
    """Return (L, U) for A = LU; entries are Fractions; no row swaps."""
    n = len(A)
    U = [[Fraction(x) for x in row] for row in A]
    L = [[Fraction(int(i == j)) for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)]
    for k in range(n - 1):
        for i in range(k + 1, n):
            L[i][k] = U[i][k] / U[k][k]
            for j in range(k, n):
                U[i][j] -= L[i][k] * U[k][j]
    return L, U


L, U = lu_nopivot([[4, 3], [6, 3]])
print(L[1][0], U[1][1])      # 3/2 -3/2
```

Using `Fraction` the factorization is exact, so $LU = A$ holds exactly; in floats you would compare with a tolerance.

@example
title: Solving a tridiagonal system
problem: Solve $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$ with $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 2\end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf b = (1, 0, 1)^\top$ by $LU$.
step: Step $1$: $l_{21} = -1/2$; row $2$ becomes $(0, 3/2, -1)$. Row $3$ is unchanged, $l_{31} = 0$.
step: Step $2$: $l_{32} = (-1)/(3/2) = -2/3$; the $(3,3)$ entry becomes $2 - (-2/3)(-1) = 4/3$. So $U = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3/2 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 4/3\end{pmatrix}$, and $\det A = 2 \cdot \tfrac32 \cdot \tfrac43 = 4$.
step: Forward substitution $L\mathbf y = \mathbf b$: $y_1 = 1$, $y_2 = 0 + \tfrac12 \cdot 1 = \tfrac12$, $y_3 = 1 + \tfrac23 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac43$.
step: Back substitution $U\mathbf x = \mathbf y$: $x_3 = (4/3)/(4/3) = 1$, $x_2 = (\tfrac12 + x_3)/\tfrac32 = 1$, $x_1 = (1 + x_2)/2 = 1$.
answer: $\mathbf x = (1, 1, 1)^\top$ and $\det A = 4$.

@example
title: Partial pivoting on a 3 by 3 matrix
problem: Compute $PA = LU$ for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & -1 \\ 3 & 1 & 2 \\ 2 & -3 & 6\end{pmatrix}$ and find $\det A$.
step: Column $1$: the largest entry is $3$ (row $2$). Swap rows $1$ and $2$: rows are now $(3, 1, 2)$, $(1, 2, -1)$, $(2, -3, 6)$. Multipliers $1/3$ and $2/3$ give rows $(0, 5/3, -5/3)$ and $(0, -11/3, 14/3)$.
step: Column $2$: compare $5/3$ and $-11/3$; the larger in absolute value is $-11/3$, so swap the last two rows. The multiplier is $(5/3)/(-11/3) = -5/11$ and the $(3,3)$ entry becomes $-5/3 - (-5/11)(14/3) = 5/11$.
step: $U = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & -11/3 & 14/3 \\ 0 & 0 & 5/11\end{pmatrix}$ and $L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2/3 & 1 & 0 \\ 1/3 & -5/11 & 1\end{pmatrix}$, where $P$ puts the original rows in the order $2, 3, 1$ (row $2$ of $A$ first, then row $3$, then row $1$).
step: Two swaps give $\det P = +1$, so $\det A = 3 \cdot (-\tfrac{11}{3}) \cdot \tfrac{5}{11} = -5$; direct expansion confirms $-5$.
answer: $\det A = -5$, with $U$ and $L$ as above.

@mistakes
- Assuming that a nonsingular matrix always has an $LU$ factorization: a zero pivot (here $a_{11} = 0$) can stop the process; row swaps fix this.
- Pivoting only when a pivot is exactly zero: a tiny nonzero pivot gives huge multipliers and destroys accuracy, so always pivot on the largest entry.
- Forgetting to swap the already computed multipliers in $L$ together with the rows of $U$ when exchanging rows.
- Losing the sign of the determinant: each row swap multiplies $\det$ by $-1$, so $\det A = (-1)^{\text{swaps}}\prod u_{kk}$.

model:
```python
from fractions import Fraction


def lu_solve(L, U, b):
    """Solve L U x = b by forward and back substitution (exact)."""
    n = len(b)
    y = [Fraction(0)] * n
    for i in range(n):
        s = Fraction(b[i])
        for j in range(i):
            s -= L[i][j] * y[j]
        y[i] = s / L[i][i]
    x = [Fraction(0)] * n
    for i in range(n - 1, -1, -1):
        s = y[i]
        for j in range(i + 1, n):
            s -= U[i][j] * x[j]
        x[i] = s / U[i][i]
    return x
```
hint: Forward substitution runs over rows $0, 1, \dots$ and divides by `L[i][i]`; back substitution runs over rows $n-1, \dots, 0$ and divides by `U[i][i]`.
solution: Each unknown is found from one equation after the later (back) or earlier (forward) unknowns are known: $y_i = (b_i - \sum_{j<i} l_{ij}y_j)/l_{ii}$ and $x_i = (y_i - \sum_{j>i} u_{ij}x_j)/u_{ii}$. Dividing by the diagonal entries (not assuming ones) keeps the function correct for general triangular factors. The cost is $O(n^2)$.

```python
from fractions import Fraction


def lu_pivot(A):
    """PA = LU with partial pivoting, in exact arithmetic."""
    n = len(A)
    U = [[Fraction(x) for x in row] for row in A]
    L = [[Fraction(0)] * n for _ in range(n)]
    perm = list(range(n))
    for k in range(n):
        p = k
        for i in range(k + 1, n):
            if abs(U[i][k]) > abs(U[p][k]):
                p = i
        if U[p][k] == 0:
            raise ValueError("singular matrix")
        if p != k:
            U[k], U[p] = U[p], U[k]
            L[k], L[p] = L[p], L[k]
            perm[k], perm[p] = perm[p], perm[k]
        for i in range(k + 1, n):
            L[i][k] = U[i][k] / U[k][k]
            for j in range(k, n):
                U[i][j] -= L[i][k] * U[k][j]
    for i in range(n):
        L[i][i] = Fraction(1)
    return perm, L, U
```
hint: Find the row of largest absolute value in column `k` among rows `k..n-1`, swap it up (in `U`, `L` and `perm`), then eliminate below.
solution: The swap must include the multipliers already stored in `L`, otherwise `L` no longer matches the rows of `U`. Strict inequality in the search picks the smallest index on ties. With exact fractions, `matmul(L, U)` equals the permuted matrix exactly, as the test checks. Theorem 2 guarantees that the pivot candidate is nonzero for a nonsingular matrix, so `ValueError` signals a singular input.

model:
```python
from fractions import Fraction


def det_pivot(A):
    """Determinant by elimination with partial pivoting."""
    n = len(A)
    M = [[Fraction(x) for x in row] for row in A]
    sign = 1
    result = Fraction(1)
    for k in range(n):
        p = k
        for i in range(k + 1, n):
            if abs(M[i][k]) > abs(M[p][k]):
                p = i
        if M[p][k] == 0:
            return Fraction(0)
        if p != k:
            M[k], M[p] = M[p], M[k]
            sign = -sign
        result *= M[k][k]
        for i in range(k + 1, n):
            factor = M[i][k] / M[k][k]
            for j in range(k, n):
                M[i][j] -= factor * M[k][j]
    return sign * result
```
hint: Keep a sign variable that flips at each row swap, multiply the pivots together, and return `Fraction(0)` as soon as no nonzero pivot exists.
solution: Elimination without swaps keeps the determinant; each swap multiplies it by $-1$; the determinant of the final upper triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal. A column of zeros at or below the diagonal means the matrix is singular, so the determinant is $0$. For the first test the single swap gives $-1$.
