def midpoint(h, a, b, m=20000):
    """Midpoint-rule approximation of the integral of h over [a, b]."""
    w = (b - a) / m

@review
**Weeks 1–2: measures.** A σ-algebra is closed under complements and countable unions; a measure is countably additive on it. Know the four basic properties (monotone, countably subadditive, continuous from below, continuous from above when $\mu(A_1) < \infty$) and the first Borel–Cantelli lemma. Lebesgue outer measure is built from covers; Carathéodory's criterion makes the measurable sets a σ-algebra on which $\lambda^*$ is a complete measure; regularity sandwiches measurable sets between compact and open sets. Keep three examples ready: the Cantor set (null, uncountable), a fat Cantor set (positive measure, empty interior) and a Vitali set (not measurable).

**Weeks 3–4: functions and the integral.** Measurable means $\{f > a\}$ is measurable; sums, products, sups and limits preserve measurability. Every $f \ge 0$ is an increasing limit of simple functions $\min(n, 2^{-n}\lfloor 2^n f \rfloor)$. The integral is defined for simple functions, then by supremum, then by $f^+ - f^-$. Chebyshev: $\mu(f \ge t) \le \frac{1}{t}\int f$. $\sin x / x$ is not in $L^1$.

**Weeks 5–6: convergence theorems.** Monotone convergence: $0 \le f_n \uparrow f$ gives $\int f_n \uparrow \int f$. Fatou: $\int \liminf f_n \le \liminf \int f_n$. Dominated convergence: $f_n \to f$ a.e. and $|f_n| \le g \in L^1$ give $\int |f_n - f| \to 0$. Differentiation under the integral sign needs an integrable bound on the derivative. Counterexamples: $n\mathbf{1}_{(0,1/n)}$ (concentrating), $\mathbf{1}_{[n, n+1]}$ (escaping), $\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}_{[0,n]}$ (spreading).

**Weeks 7–8: modes and products.** $L^p \Rightarrow$ in measure $\Rightarrow$ a.e. subsequence (Riesz); on finite measure, a.e. $\Rightarrow$ in measure and Egorov gives uniform convergence off a small set. The typewriter sequence converges in $L^p$ but nowhere. Tonelli (nonnegative, σ-finite) and Fubini ($\int |f| < \infty$) allow the order of integration to be swapped; the diagonal with counting measure and $\frac{x^2 - y^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2}$ show both hypotheses are needed.

**Weeks 9–10: $L^p$.** Young, Hölder ($\|fg\|_1 \le \|f\|_p \|g\|_q$), Minkowski, completeness (Riesz–Fischer via a rapidly Cauchy subsequence and Fatou). $C_c$ and simple functions are dense for $p < \infty$; translation is continuous in $L^p$; $L^\infty$ is not separable.

**Weeks 11–12: derivatives.** Hahn and Jordan decompositions; Lebesgue decomposition; Radon–Nikodym for σ-finite measures with $\nu \ll \mu$; the maximal inequality $\lambda(Mf > t) \le 3\|f\|_1 / t$; Lebesgue differentiation $A_r f \to f$ a.e.; the Cantor function shows that absolute continuity is needed in the fundamental theorem of calculus.

@exam self
prompt: Let $(X, \mathcal{A}, \mu)$ be a measure space, $f_n, f$ measurable, $f_n \to f$ in measure, and suppose there is $g \in L^1(\mu)$ with $|f_n| \le g$ almost everywhere for every $n$. Prove that $f \in L^1(\mu)$ and $\int |f_n - f| \, d\mu \to 0$.
hint: Argue by contradiction, extracting a subsequence with $\int |f_{n_k} - f| \ge \varepsilon$, then use Riesz's theorem to pass to an a.e. convergent further subsequence.
hint: Show $|f| \le g$ a.e. from the a.e. convergent subsequence, then apply the dominated convergence theorem to that subsequence.
solution: By Riesz's theorem some subsequence $f_{n_k} \to f$ almost everywhere; since $|f_{n_k}| \le g$ a.e., the limit satisfies $|f| \le g$ a.e., so $f \in L^1(\mu)$. Now suppose $\int |f_n - f| \, d\mu \not\to 0$. Then there are $\varepsilon > 0$ and a subsequence $(f_{m_k})$ with $\int |f_{m_k} - f| \ge \varepsilon$ for all $k$. This subsequence still converges to $f$ in measure, so by Riesz's theorem it has a further subsequence $(f_{m_{k_j}})$ converging to $f$ a.e. Along it $|f_{m_{k_j}} - f| \le 2g$ a.e. and $|f_{m_{k_j}} - f| \to 0$ a.e., so the dominated convergence theorem gives $\int |f_{m_{k_j}} - f| \to 0$, contradicting the lower bound $\varepsilon$. Hence $\int |f_n - f| \to 0$. $\blacksquare$

rubric: Applies dominated convergence with dominating function $2g$ and concludes by the subsequence argument that the whole sequence converges in $L^1$.

hint: Write $n x^{n-1} dx = d(x^n)$ and integrate by parts: the integral equals $\cos 1 + \int_0^1 x^n \sin x \, dx$.
solution: Integration by parts gives $\int_0^1 \cos x \, d(x^n) = \cos 1 + \int_0^1 x^n \sin x \, dx$. The last integral tends to $0$ by dominated convergence ($|x^n \sin x| \le 1$ and $x^n \to 0$ for $x < 1$). So the limit is $\cos 1 \approx 0.5403$. Note that no dominating function exists for the integrand $n x^{n-1}\cos x$ itself.
