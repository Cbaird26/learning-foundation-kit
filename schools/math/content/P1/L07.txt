@article
A sequence of functions can fail to converge in three different senses, and an exam question usually asks which of them survive. Let $(X, \mathcal{A}, \mu)$ be a measure space and $f_n, f\colon X \to \mathbb{R}$ measurable. We say that $f_n \to f$

- **almost everywhere** (a.e.) if $\mu(\{x : f_n(x) \not\to f(x)\}) = 0$;
- **in measure** if for every $\varepsilon > 0$, $\mu(\{|f_n - f| > \varepsilon\}) \to 0$;
- **in $L^p$** ($1 \le p < \infty$) if $\|f_n - f\|_p = \left(\int |f_n - f|^p \, d\mu\right)^{1/p} \to 0$.

### The implications that always hold

**Theorem 1.** $L^p$ convergence implies convergence in measure.

*Proof.* By Chebyshev's inequality applied to $|f_n - f|^p$, $\mu(\{|f_n - f| > \varepsilon\}) \le \varepsilon^{-p} \int |f_n - f|^p \to 0$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 2 (Riesz).** If $f_n \to f$ in measure, then some subsequence $f_{n_k} \to f$ almost everywhere.

*Proof.* Choose $n_1 < n_2 < \cdots$ with $\mu(E_k) < 2^{-k}$, where $E_k = \{|f_{n_k} - f| > 2^{-k}\}$. Since $\sum_k \mu(E_k) < \infty$, the first Borel–Cantelli lemma gives $\mu(\limsup E_k) = 0$. If $x \notin \limsup E_k$, then $x \in E_k$ for only finitely many $k$, so $|f_{n_k}(x) - f(x)| \le 2^{-k}$ for all large $k$, and $f_{n_k}(x) \to f(x)$. $\blacksquare$

In particular, $L^p$ convergence gives an a.e. convergent subsequence.

### Finite measure: Egorov's theorem

**Theorem 3 (Egorov).** Let $\mu(X) < \infty$, and let $f_n, f$ be measurable and real-valued with $f_n \to f$ almost everywhere. Then for every $\delta > 0$ there is a measurable $E$ with $\mu(E) < \delta$ such that $f_n \to f$ uniformly on $X \setminus E$.

*Proof.* Discard a null set. For $k, n \ge 1$ let $E_n^k = \bigcup_{m \ge n} \{|f_m - f| \ge 1/k\}$. For fixed $k$ the sets decrease in $n$, and $\bigcap_n E_n^k = \emptyset$ because $f_m(x) \to f(x)$ for each $x$. Since $\mu(X) < \infty$, continuity from above gives $\mu(E_n^k) \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$. Choose $n_k$ with $\mu(E_{n_k}^k) < \delta 2^{-k}$ and let $E = \bigcup_k E_{n_k}^k$, so $\mu(E) < \delta$. If $x \notin E$ and $m \ge n_k$ then $|f_m(x) - f(x)| < 1/k$; $n_k$ does not depend on $x$, so the convergence is uniform on $X \setminus E$. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary 4.** If $\mu(X) < \infty$, a.e. convergence implies convergence in measure. (Given $\varepsilon$ and $\delta$, take $E$ from Egorov's theorem; for large $n$, $\{|f_n - f| > \varepsilon\} \subseteq E$.)

### The counterexamples

The converses fail, and each failure has a standard witness. All are on Lebesgue measure.

| Sequence | a.e. | in measure | in $L^1$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| typewriter (below) | nowhere | yes | yes |
| $n \mathbf{1}_{(0, 1/n)}$ on $(0,1)$ | yes | yes | no ($\int = 1$) |
| $\mathbf{1}_{[n, n+1]}$ on $\mathbb{R}$ | yes | no | no |

The **typewriter sequence** enumerates the dyadic intervals: for $n = 2^k + j$ with $0 \le j < 2^k$, let $f_n = \mathbf{1}_{[j 2^{-k}, (j+1) 2^{-k}]}$ on $[0,1]$. Then $\int |f_n|^p = 2^{-k} \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$, so $f_n \to 0$ in every $L^p$, hence in measure. But for each $x \in [0,1]$ and each level $k$ some interval of level $k$ contains $x$, so $f_n(x) = 1$ for infinitely many $n$ while $f_n(x) = 0$ for infinitely many $n$: the sequence converges at no point. Riesz's theorem is sharp: the subsequence $f_{2^k}$ converges a.e. (to $0$ except at $0$).

The sequence $\mathbf{1}_{[n,n+1]}$ shows that on an infinite measure space, a.e. convergence does not imply convergence in measure, so Corollary 4 needs finite measure. The sequence $n \mathbf{1}_{(0,1/n)}$ shows that a.e. and in-measure convergence do not imply $L^1$ convergence even on a finite space; the missing ingredient is domination or uniform integrability (Lesson 6).

```python
def typewriter(n):
    """Interval (a, b) of the n-th typewriter function, n >= 1."""
    k = n.bit_length() - 1
    j = n - 2 ** k
    return j / 2 ** k, (j + 1) / 2 ** k


x = 0.3
hits = [n for n in range(1, 2 ** 10)
        if typewriter(n)[0] <= x <= typewriter(n)[1]]
print(len(hits), hits[:6])
```

This prints `10 [1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 41]`: the point $0.3$ is hit exactly once on each of the $10$ levels, so the values $f_n(0.3)$ equal $1$ infinitely often. Evidence for one point; the argument above is the proof for all points.

@example
title: Typewriter analysis of convergence modes
problem: For the typewriter sequence $f_n$ on $[0,1]$ decide whether $f_n \to 0$ a.e., in measure and in $L^2$, and find a subsequence converging a.e.
step: In $L^2$: for $n = 2^k + j$, $\|f_n\|_2^2 = \lambda([j2^{-k}, (j+1)2^{-k}]) = 2^{-k}$, and $k \to \infty$ as $n \to \infty$. So $\|f_n\|_2 \to 0$.
step: In measure: for $0 < \varepsilon < 1$, $\{|f_n| > \varepsilon\}$ is the interval of length $2^{-k}$, so its measure tends to $0$ (also implied by Theorem 1).
step: Not a.e.: every $x \in [0,1]$ lies in at least one level-$k$ interval for each $k$, so $f_n(x) = 1$ infinitely often, while $f_n(x) = 0$ infinitely often (each level has $2^k$ intervals and at most two of them contain $x$, so most functions of each level vanish at $x$). So $f_n(x)$ has no limit.
step: Subsequence: $f_{2^k} = \mathbf{1}_{[0, 2^{-k}]}$ and for $x > 0$ this is $0$ once $2^{-k} < x$, so $f_{2^k} \to 0$ everywhere except $x = 0$, hence a.e.
answer: $f_n \to 0$ in $L^2$ and in measure, but nowhere pointwise; $(f_{2^k})$ converges to $0$ a.e.

@example
title: Egorov's theorem for a power sequence
problem: Let $f_n(x) = x^n$ on $[0,1]$. For $\delta \in (0, 1)$ find a set $E$ with $\lambda(E) < \delta$ off which the convergence $f_n \to 0$ is uniform, and show the convergence is not uniform on $[0, 1)$.
step: The pointwise limit is $0$ on $[0, 1)$, so $f_n \to 0$ almost everywhere on the finite measure space $[0,1]$ and Egorov's theorem applies.
step: Take $E = (1 - \delta/2, 1]$, so $\lambda(E) = \delta/2 < \delta$. On $[0, 1 - \delta/2]$, $\sup |x^n| = (1 - \delta/2)^n \to 0$, so the convergence is uniform.
step: On $[0, 1)$ we have $\sup_{x < 1} x^n = 1$ for every $n$, so $\sup |f_n - 0| \not\to 0$ and the convergence is not uniform there; one really must remove a set near $1$.
answer: $E = (1 - \delta/2, 1]$ works; the convergence is uniform off $E$ but not on all of $[0,1)$.

@mistakes
- Believing a.e. convergence implies convergence in measure in general: it needs $\mu(X) < \infty$ (or domination); $\mathbf{1}_{[n,n+1]}$ is the counterexample.
- Believing convergence in measure implies a.e. convergence: only a subsequence converges a.e., and the typewriter sequence shows the full sequence may converge nowhere.
- Applying Egorov's theorem on an infinite measure space: it needs $\mu(X) < \infty$, and it gives uniform convergence only off a small set, not everywhere.
- Confusing "$L^p$-norm tends to zero" with "$f_n \to 0$ pointwise": the first never implies the second for the full sequence.

model:
```python
from fractions import Fraction


def interval(n):
    """Support interval of the n-th sliding bump."""
    k = 1
    while n > k:
        n -= k
        k += 1
    j = n - 1
    return Fraction(j, k), Fraction(j + 1, k)


def hits(x, N):
    """Number of n <= N whose bump contains x."""
    count = 0
    for n in range(1, N + 1):
        a, b = interval(n)
        if a <= x <= b:
            count += 1
    return count
```
hint: Subtract the block sizes $1, 2, 3, \dots$ from $n$ until $n$ fits inside the current block; the remainder gives $j$.
hint: Compare using `Fraction` arithmetic so that endpoints are exact.
solution: Block $k$ contains $k$ indices, so peeling off blocks of size $1, 2, \dots$ locates the block $k$ and the position $j$ within it. The width $1/k \to 0$, so $\|f_n\|_p^p = 1/k \to 0$ and $f_n \to 0$ in every $L^p$; but each $x$ lies in at least one interval of every block, so $f_n(x) = 1$ infinitely often. Counting hits for a few $x$ and $N$ is evidence of the pattern; the argument above is the proof.

@practice self
prompt: Let $(X, \mathcal{A}, \mu)$ be a finite measure space and $f_n, f\colon X \to \mathbb{R}$ measurable. Prove that $f_n \to f$ in measure if and only if every subsequence of $(f_n)$ has a further subsequence that converges to $f$ almost everywhere.
hint: For the forward direction apply Riesz's theorem to the subsequence.
hint: For the converse argue by contradiction: if convergence in measure fails, extract a subsequence staying $\delta$-far, then use Corollary 4 on a further a.e. convergent subsequence.
solution: ($\Rightarrow$) A subsequence of a sequence converging in measure converges in measure, so by Riesz's theorem it has a further subsequence converging a.e. ($\Leftarrow$) Suppose $f_n \not\to f$ in measure. Then there are $\varepsilon, \delta > 0$ and a subsequence $(f_{n_k})$ with $\mu(\{|f_{n_k} - f| > \varepsilon\}) \ge \delta$ for all $k$. By hypothesis a further subsequence $(g_i)$ converges to $f$ a.e. Since $\mu(X) < \infty$, a.e. convergence implies convergence in measure (Corollary 4), so $\mu(\{|g_i - f| > \varepsilon\}) \to 0$, contradicting the lower bound $\delta$ for every term of $(g_i)$. $\blacksquare$ Corollary 4 is proved from Egorov's theorem, or directly: $\mu(\{|g_i - f| > \varepsilon\}) \le \mu(\bigcup_{m \ge i} \{|g_m - f| > \varepsilon\})$, and these sets decrease to a subset of the null set where $g_m \not\to f$, so by continuity from above (using $\mu(X) < \infty$) the measures tend to $0$.
rubric: Forward direction: observes that subsequences inherit convergence in measure and applies Riesz's subsequence theorem.
rubric: Converse: argues by contradiction, extracting a subsequence with $\mu(\{|f_{n_k} - f| > \varepsilon\}) \ge \delta$.
rubric: Uses finiteness of $\mu$ to turn a.e. convergence of the further subsequence into convergence in measure, and states the contradiction.

hint: Fix $x$ and look at $f_n(x)$ for large $n$; then compute $\lambda(\{f_n > 1/2\})$.
solution: For fixed $x$, $f_n(x) = 0$ once $n > x$, so $f_n \to 0$ pointwise. But $\lambda(\{|f_n| > 1/2\}) = 1$ and $\int f_n = 1$ for every $n$, so there is no convergence in measure or in $L^1$. This is why a.e. convergence implies convergence in measure only on finite measure spaces.
