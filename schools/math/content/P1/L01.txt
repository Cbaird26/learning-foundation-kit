@article
Riemann integration breaks on limits: the indicator of the rationals in $[0,1]$ is a pointwise limit of Riemann-integrable functions, yet it is not Riemann integrable. The cure is a notion of "size" for sets that is stable under countable operations. The ideal would be a function $\lambda$ defined on **all** subsets of $\mathbb{R}$ with $\lambda([a,b]) = b - a$, invariant under translation, and countably additive. No such function exists (Lesson 2 gives the argument). So we must give up the domain: we assign sizes only to the sets in a well-chosen family.

### σ-algebras

**Definition.** Let $X$ be a set. A collection $\mathcal{A}$ of subsets of $X$ is a **σ-algebra** if (i) $X \in \mathcal{A}$; (ii) $A \in \mathcal{A}$ implies $X \setminus A \in \mathcal{A}$; (iii) if $A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal{A}$ then $\bigcup_n A_n \in \mathcal{A}$. The pair $(X, \mathcal{A})$ is a **measurable space**. De Morgan's laws give closure under countable intersections and differences, and $\emptyset \in \mathcal{A}$. Closure under finite unions alone defines an **algebra**; the countable condition is what makes limits work.

For any family $\mathcal{E}$ of subsets of $X$, the intersection of all σ-algebras containing $\mathcal{E}$ is a σ-algebra, the smallest one containing $\mathcal{E}$: the **σ-algebra generated by** $\mathcal{E}$, written $\sigma(\mathcal{E})$. The **Borel σ-algebra** $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$ is generated by the open sets. It is also generated by the half-lines $(a, \infty)$: every open set is a countable union of open intervals, $(a,b) = (a,\infty) \setminus [b,\infty)$, and $[b, \infty) = \bigcap_n (b - 1/n, \infty)$.

### Measures

**Definition.** A **measure** on $(X, \mathcal{A})$ is a function $\mu\colon \mathcal{A} \to [0, \infty]$ with $\mu(\emptyset) = 0$ and **countable additivity**: for pairwise disjoint $A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal{A}$, $\mu\left(\bigsqcup_n A_n\right) = \sum_n \mu(A_n)$. Standard examples: counting measure; the Dirac measure $\delta_x(A) = 1$ if $x \in A$ and $0$ otherwise; and Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}$ (constructed in Lesson 2). A set $N$ with $\mu(N) = 0$ is **null**; a measure is **complete** if every subset of a null set is measurable.

**Theorem 1.** Let $(X, \mathcal{A}, \mu)$ be a measure space.
(a) If $A \subseteq B$ are in $\mathcal{A}$ then $\mu(A) \le \mu(B)$.
(b) For $A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal{A}$, $\mu(\bigcup_n A_n) \le \sum_n \mu(A_n)$.
(c) If $A_1 \subseteq A_2 \subseteq \cdots$ in $\mathcal{A}$ and $A = \bigcup_n A_n$, then $\mu(A) = \lim_n \mu(A_n)$.
(d) If $A_1 \supseteq A_2 \supseteq \cdots$ in $\mathcal{A}$, $\mu(A_1) < \infty$ and $A = \bigcap_n A_n$, then $\mu(A) = \lim_n \mu(A_n)$.

*Proof.* (a) $B = A \sqcup (B \setminus A)$, so $\mu(B) = \mu(A) + \mu(B \setminus A) \ge \mu(A)$.
(b) Put $B_1 = A_1$ and $B_n = A_n \setminus \bigcup_{j<n} A_j$. The $B_n$ are measurable, pairwise disjoint, $B_n \subseteq A_n$, and $\bigcup B_n = \bigcup A_n$. Then $\mu(\bigcup A_n) = \sum \mu(B_n) \le \sum \mu(A_n)$ by (a).
(c) Put $D_1 = A_1$ and $D_n = A_n \setminus A_{n-1}$. These are disjoint with $A_N = \bigsqcup_{n \le N} D_n$ and $A = \bigsqcup_n D_n$. So $\mu(A) = \sum_n \mu(D_n) = \lim_N \sum_{n \le N} \mu(D_n) = \lim_N \mu(A_N)$.
(d) The sets $A_1 \setminus A_n$ increase to $A_1 \setminus A$. By (c), $\mu(A_1 \setminus A) = \lim_n \mu(A_1 \setminus A_n)$. Since $\mu(A_1) < \infty$, $\mu(A_1 \setminus A_n) = \mu(A_1) - \mu(A_n)$ and $\mu(A_1 \setminus A) = \mu(A_1) - \mu(A)$. Subtracting the finite number $\mu(A_1)$ gives $\mu(A) = \lim_n \mu(A_n)$. $\blacksquare$

**The finiteness hypothesis in (d) is needed.** On $\mathbb{R}$ with Lebesgue measure, $A_n = [n, \infty)$ decreases to $\emptyset$, but $\mu(A_n) = \infty$ for every $n$. The proof breaks exactly where it subtracts $\infty - \infty$.

### Uniqueness

**Theorem 2 (uniqueness of measures).** Let $\mathcal{P}$ be a **π-system** (closed under finite intersections) on $X$, and let $\mu, \nu$ be measures on $\sigma(\mathcal{P})$ that agree on $\mathcal{P}$. If there are $E_1, E_2, \dots \in \mathcal{P}$ with $\bigcup_n E_n = X$ and $\mu(E_n) = \nu(E_n) < \infty$, then $\mu = \nu$ on $\sigma(\mathcal{P})$.

This is proved with Dynkin's π–λ theorem, which you should know how to cite. It shows, for instance, that two Borel measures on $\mathbb{R}$ that are finite on bounded intervals and agree on every interval $(a, b]$ are equal.

### Completion

If $\mu$ is not complete, its **completion** adds all subsets of null sets: $\overline{\mathcal{A}}$ consists of sets $A \cup M$ with $A \in \mathcal{A}$ and $M$ contained in a null set of $\mathcal{A}$, with $\overline{\mu}(A \cup M) = \mu(A)$. This is well defined. Lebesgue measure is the completion of Borel measure, as Lesson 2 will show.

@example
title: The countable–cocountable measure
problem: Let $X$ be an uncountable set and $\mathcal{A} = \{A \subseteq X : A \text{ or } X \setminus A \text{ is countable}\}$. Define $\mu(A) = 0$ if $A$ is countable and $\mu(A) = 1$ if $X \setminus A$ is countable. Show that $\mathcal{A}$ is a σ-algebra and that $\mu$ is a measure.
step: Well defined: no set is both countable and cocountable, since then $X$ would be a union of two countable sets, hence countable. So $\mu$ takes exactly one value on each $A \in \mathcal{A}$.
step: σ-algebra: $X$ is cocountable. Complements swap "countable" and "cocountable". For $A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal{A}$, if all are countable then $\bigcup A_n$ is a countable union of countable sets, hence countable. If some $A_m$ is cocountable, then $X \setminus \bigcup A_n \subseteq X \setminus A_m$ is countable, so the union is cocountable.
step: Measure: $\mu(\emptyset) = 0$. Let $A_n$ be pairwise disjoint in $\mathcal{A}$. At most one $A_n$ can be cocountable, since two disjoint cocountable sets would give $X = (X \setminus A_i) \cup (X \setminus A_j)$ countable.
step: If none is cocountable, the union is countable and both sides equal $0$. If $A_m$ is cocountable, the union is cocountable, so $\mu(\bigsqcup A_n) = 1$, and the right side is $1 + 0 + 0 + \cdots = 1$.
answer: $\mathcal{A}$ is a σ-algebra and $\mu$ is a measure; singletons are null but $\mu(X) = 1$.

@example
title: Continuity from above at a point
problem: Let $\lambda$ be Lebesgue measure and $A_n = [0, 1/n]$ for $n \ge 1$. Find $\bigcap_n A_n$ and verify Theorem 1(d) directly.
step: The intersection is $\{0\}$: $0 \in A_n$ for all $n$, and if $x > 0$ then $x > 1/n$ for large $n$, so $x \notin A_n$.
step: Check the hypotheses: the sets decrease and $\lambda(A_1) = 1 < \infty$, so Theorem 1(d) applies.
step: Compute both sides: $\lambda(A_n) = 1/n \to 0$ and $\lambda(\{0\}) = 0$, so $\lim \lambda(A_n) = \lambda(\bigcap A_n)$, as the theorem predicts.
answer: $\bigcap_n A_n = \{0\}$ and both quantities equal $0$.

@mistakes
- Using continuity from above without $\mu(A_1) < \infty$: the sets $[n, \infty)$ have infinite Lebesgue measure each but empty intersection.
- Asserting that a countable union of σ-algebra members stays in a mere algebra: the finite-or-cofinite sets on $\mathbb{N}$ form an algebra, but the union of the singletons $\{2\}, \{4\}, \{6\}, \dots$ is neither finite nor cofinite.
- Writing $\mu(B \setminus A) = \mu(B) - \mu(A)$ without $\mu(A) < \infty$: with counting measure on $\mathbb{N}$ and $A = \mathbb{N} \setminus \{1\}$, both $\mu(A)$ and $\mu(\mathbb{N})$ are $\infty$ while $\mu(\mathbb{N} \setminus A) = 1$.
- Adding $\mu(A \cup B) = \mu(A) + \mu(B)$ for sets that overlap: additivity requires disjointness.

@practice num
prompt: Let $X = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$ and let $\mathcal{A}$ be the σ-algebra generated by the two sets $\{1, 2\}$ and $\{2, 3\}$. How many sets does $\mathcal{A}$ contain?
answer: 16
check: 2 ** len(set(tuple(x in A for A in ([1, 2], [2, 3])) for x in range(1, 6)))
hint: A σ-algebra on a finite set is determined by its atoms, the minimal nonempty members. Find which points cannot be separated by the two generators.
solution: The generators separate the points into the atoms $\{1\}$, $\{2\}$, $\{3\}$ and $\{4, 5\}$ (points $4$ and $5$ lie in neither set). Every member of $\mathcal{A}$ is a union of atoms and every such union is in $\mathcal{A}$, so $|\mathcal{A}| = 2^4 = 16$.

@practice self
prompt: (First Borel–Cantelli lemma.) Let $(X, \mathcal{A}, \mu)$ be a measure space and $E_1, E_2, \dots \in \mathcal{A}$ with $\sum_n \mu(E_n) < \infty$. Prove that $\mu(\limsup_n E_n) = 0$, where $\limsup_n E_n = \bigcap_{N \ge 1} \bigcup_{n \ge N} E_n$.
hint: Write $\limsup E_n$ as a decreasing intersection of the tails $T_N = \bigcup_{n \ge N} E_n$.
hint: Bound $\mu(T_N)$ by countable subadditivity, and let $N \to \infty$ using the convergence of the series.
solution: Let $T_N = \bigcup_{n \ge N} E_n \in \mathcal{A}$ and $L = \limsup E_n = \bigcap_N T_N \in \mathcal{A}$. Since $L \subseteq T_N$, monotonicity and countable subadditivity give $\mu(L) \le \mu(T_N) \le \sum_{n \ge N} \mu(E_n)$ for every $N$. The right side is the tail of a convergent series of nonnegative terms, so it tends to $0$ as $N \to \infty$. Hence $\mu(L) \le \inf_N \sum_{n \ge N} \mu(E_n) = 0$. $\blacksquare$ (No finiteness of $\mu(X)$ is needed, because only subadditivity is used.)
rubric: Writes $\limsup E_n$ as $\bigcap_N \bigcup_{n \ge N} E_n$ and notes that it is measurable.
rubric: Applies monotonicity and countable subadditivity to bound $\mu(\limsup E_n)$ by a tail sum.
rubric: Concludes that tails of a convergent series tend to $0$; does not assume independence or finiteness of $\mu(X)$.

@practice self
prompt: Let $(X, \mathcal{A})$ be a measurable space and let $\mu\colon \mathcal{A} \to [0, \infty)$ be finitely additive with $\mu(\emptyset) = 0$. Suppose $\mu(B_n) \to 0$ whenever $B_1 \supseteq B_2 \supseteq \cdots$ are in $\mathcal{A}$ with $\bigcap_n B_n = \emptyset$. Prove that $\mu$ is countably additive.
hint: For disjoint $A_n$ with union $A$, look at the remainders $B_N = A \setminus \bigcup_{n \le N} A_n$.
hint: Use finite additivity to write $\mu(A) = \sum_{n \le N} \mu(A_n) + \mu(B_N)$ and show $B_N$ decreases to the empty set.
solution: Let $A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal{A}$ be pairwise disjoint with union $A \in \mathcal{A}$, and set $B_N = A \setminus \bigcup_{n \le N} A_n \in \mathcal{A}$. The sets $B_N$ decrease. Their intersection is empty: a point of $A$ lies in some $A_m$ and so is not in $B_N$ for $N \ge m$. Since $A$ is the disjoint union of $A_1, \dots, A_N, B_N$, finite additivity gives $\mu(A) = \sum_{n \le N} \mu(A_n) + \mu(B_N)$. By hypothesis $\mu(B_N) \to 0$, so $\sum_{n \le N} \mu(A_n) \to \mu(A)$, i.e. $\mu(A) = \sum_n \mu(A_n)$. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Defines the remainder sets $B_N$ and proves they decrease with empty intersection.
rubric: Uses finite additivity to split $\mu(A)$ into a partial sum plus $\mu(B_N)$.
rubric: Passes to the limit using the continuity hypothesis at $\emptyset$.

prompt: Let $(X, \mathcal{A}, \mu)$ be a measure space. Which of these statements are true?

choice*: If $A, B$ are measurable and $\mu(A \cap B) < \infty$, then $\mu(A \cup B) = \mu(A) + \mu(B) - \mu(A \cap B)$.
