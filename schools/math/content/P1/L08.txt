@article
Calculus says that a double integral over a rectangle may be computed in either order. For the Lebesgue integral the right statement is two theorems: Tonelli for nonnegative functions, Fubini for integrable ones. Let $(X, \mathcal{A}, \mu)$ and $(Y, \mathcal{B}, \nu)$ be measure spaces. The **product σ-algebra** $\mathcal{A} \otimes \mathcal{B}$ is generated by the **measurable rectangles** $A \times B$. For $E \subseteq X \times Y$ write $E_x = \{y : (x,y) \in E\}$ and $E^y = \{x : (x, y) \in E\}$ for sections.

**Lemma 1.** If $E \in \mathcal{A} \otimes \mathcal{B}$ then $E_x \in \mathcal{B}$ for every $x$; if $f$ is $\mathcal{A} \otimes \mathcal{B}$-measurable then each section $f(x, \cdot)$ is $\mathcal{B}$-measurable.

*Proof.* The sets $E$ all of whose sections $E_x$ are in $\mathcal{B}$ contain the rectangles ($(A \times B)_x$ is $B$ or $\emptyset$) and form a σ-algebra, since sections commute with complements and countable unions. For $f$ apply this to $\{f > a\}$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 2 (Tonelli).** Let $\mu, \nu$ be σ-finite and $f\colon X \times Y \to [0, \infty]$ be $\mathcal{A} \otimes \mathcal{B}$-measurable. Then $x \mapsto \int f(x, y) \, d\nu(y)$ is $\mathcal{A}$-measurable, $y \mapsto \int f(x, y) \, d\mu(x)$ is $\mathcal{B}$-measurable, and
$$\int_X \left( \int_Y f \, d\nu \right) d\mu = \int_Y \left( \int_X f \, d\mu \right) d\nu = \int f \, d(\mu \times \nu),$$
where $\mu \times \nu$ is the unique measure on $\mathcal{A} \otimes \mathcal{B}$ with $(\mu \times \nu)(A \times B) = \mu(A) \nu(B)$, given by $(\mu \times \nu)(E) = \int \nu(E_x) \, d\mu(x)$.

*Proof.* *Step 1: finite measures, indicators.* Let $\mathcal{D}$ be the family of $E \in \mathcal{A} \otimes \mathcal{B}$ for which $x \mapsto \nu(E_x)$ and $y \mapsto \mu(E^y)$ are measurable and $\int \nu(E_x) \, d\mu = \int \mu(E^y) \, d\nu$. Rectangles lie in $\mathcal{D}$ (both sides equal $\mu(A)\nu(B)$) and form a π-system. $\mathcal{D}$ is a λ-system: it contains $X \times Y$; if $E \subseteq F$ are in $\mathcal{D}$ then $\nu((F \setminus E)_x) = \nu(F_x) - \nu(E_x)$ (finite measures) so $F \setminus E \in \mathcal{D}$; and for increasing unions use monotone convergence. By Dynkin's π–λ theorem $\mathcal{D} = \mathcal{A} \otimes \mathcal{B}$. The common value $(\mu \times \nu)(E)$ is countably additive by termwise integration, and unique by Lesson 1's uniqueness theorem.
*Step 2: σ-finite.* Write $X = \bigcup X_n$ and $Y = \bigcup Y_n$ with increasing sets of finite measure. Apply Step 1 to the restrictions to $X_n \times Y_n$ and let $n \to \infty$ using monotone convergence.
*Step 3: functions.* Both sides are additive in $f$ and respect increasing limits (monotone convergence applied to the inner and outer integrals), and they agree on indicators of measurable sets by Step 2, so they agree on simple functions and then on all nonnegative measurable $f$ (take $s_n \uparrow f$). $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 3 (Fubini).** Let $\mu, \nu$ be σ-finite and $f$ be $\mathcal{A} \otimes \mathcal{B}$-measurable with $\int |f| \, d(\mu \times \nu) < \infty$. Then $f(x, \cdot) \in L^1(\nu)$ for $\mu$-a.e. $x$, the a.e.-defined function $x \mapsto \int f(x, y) \, d\nu(y)$ is in $L^1(\mu)$, and $\int f \, d(\mu \times \nu) = \int \left( \int f \, d\nu \right) d\mu$; the same holds with the roles of $x$ and $y$ exchanged.

*Proof.* By Tonelli applied to $|f|$, $\int_X \int_Y |f| \, d\nu \, d\mu < \infty$, so the inner integral is finite for a.e. $x$. Apply Tonelli to $f^+$ and $f^-$ and subtract the two finite identities. $\blacksquare$

**Working rule.** To swap the order, first apply Tonelli to $|f|$: if the iterated integral of $|f|$ is finite (in either order), Fubini justifies the swap. If $f \ge 0$ no check is needed beyond σ-finiteness. Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}^2$ is the completion of the product of Lebesgue measures, and Fubini holds for Lebesgue-measurable functions with "for a.e. $x$" sections.

### Counterexamples

**σ-finiteness is needed.** Let $\mu$ be Lebesgue measure and $\nu$ counting measure on $[0,1]$, and $D = \{(x,x)\}$ the diagonal (a closed, hence Borel, set). Then $\int \int \mathbf{1}_D \, d\nu \, d\mu = \int 1 \, d\mu = 1$ but $\int \int \mathbf{1}_D \, d\mu \, d\nu = \int 0 \, d\nu = 0$. Counting measure on $[0,1]$ is not σ-finite.

**Absolute integrability is needed.** The signed function in the second example has two different iterated integrals.

```python
import math


def midpoint(h, a, b, m=100000):
    """Midpoint-rule approximation of the integral of h over [a, b]."""
    w = (b - a) / m
    return sum(h(a + (i + 0.5) * w) for i in range(m)) * w


def inner_dy(x):
    """Exact integral over y in (0,1) via the antiderivative y/(x^2+y^2)."""
    return 1 / (1 + x * x)


def inner_dx(y):
    """Exact integral over x in (0,1) via the antiderivative -x/(x^2+y^2)."""
    return -1 / (1 + y * y)


print(round(midpoint(inner_dy, 0, 1), 4), round(midpoint(inner_dx, 0, 1), 4))
```

The two iterated integrals of $\frac{x^2 - y^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2}$ over $(0,1)^2$ are $\pi/4 \approx 0.7854$ and $-\pi/4$; the code reproduces both numbers from the exact inner integrals. This is evidence that the order matters; the proof that $|f|$ is not integrable is in the example.

@example
title: Swapping the order in a triangular region
problem: Evaluate $\int_0^1 \int_x^1 \frac{\sin y}{y} \, dy \, dx$.
step: The inner integral has no elementary antiderivative, so change the order. The region is $\{(x, y) : 0 \le x \le y \le 1\}$.
step: Justify the swap: $\frac{\sin y}{y} \ge 0$ on $(0, 1]$, so Tonelli applies with Lebesgue measure on $[0,1]$ (σ-finite) and no integrability check is needed.
step: Swap: for fixed $y \in (0, 1]$, $x$ ranges over $[0, y]$, so the integral equals $\int_0^1 \frac{\sin y}{y} \left( \int_0^y dx \right) dy = \int_0^1 \sin y \, dy$.
step: Evaluate: $\int_0^1 \sin y \, dy = 1 - \cos 1$.
answer: $1 - \cos 1 \approx 0.4597$.

@example
title: Iterated integrals that disagree
problem: Let $f(x,y) = \frac{x^2 - y^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2}$ on $(0,1)^2$. Compute both iterated integrals and explain why Fubini's theorem does not apply.
step: Antiderivative in $y$: $\frac{\partial}{\partial y} \frac{y}{x^2 + y^2} = \frac{x^2 - y^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2}$, so $\int_0^1 f(x,y) \, dy = \frac{1}{1 + x^2}$ and $\int_0^1 \frac{dx}{1 + x^2} = \frac{\pi}{4}$.
step: By antisymmetry $f(y,x) = -f(x,y)$, so integrating over $x$ first gives $-\frac{1}{1 + y^2}$ and then $-\frac{\pi}{4}$.
step: The two iterated integrals differ, so $f \notin L^1((0,1)^2)$; otherwise Fubini would make them equal. Directly, in polar coordinates $f = \cos(2\theta)/r^2$, and the quarter disk $\{r < 1\}$ lies in $(0,1)^2$, so $\int |f| \ge \int_0^{\pi/2} |\cos 2\theta| \, d\theta \int_0^1 r^{-2} \, r \, dr = 1 \cdot \infty$.
answer: The iterated integrals are $\pi/4$ and $-\pi/4$; $|f|$ is not integrable, so Fubini's hypothesis fails.

@mistakes
- Swapping the order for a signed function without checking $\int |f| < \infty$: the example $\frac{x^2 - y^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2}$ gives $\pi/4$ against $-\pi/4$.
- Applying Tonelli with a non-σ-finite measure such as counting measure on an uncountable set.
- Assuming separate measurability suffices: $f$ must be measurable for $\mathcal{A} \otimes \mathcal{B}$, and Borel sets of $\mathbb{R}^2$ are generated by rectangles, but $f(x, \cdot)$ and $f(\cdot, y)$ measurable for all $x, y$ is not enough.
- Forgetting to swap the limits of integration when the region is not a rectangle: describe the region by inequalities first.

model:
```python
def entry(m, n):
    """Entry a_{m,n}: 1 on the diagonal, -1 just below it, else 0."""
    if m == n:
        return 1
    if m == n + 1:
        return -1
    return 0


def iterated(K):
    """Iterated sums of the array in the two orders (truncated)."""
    rows_first = sum(sum(entry(m, n) for n in range(1, K + 2))
                     for m in range(1, K + 1))
    columns_first = sum(sum(entry(m, n) for m in range(1, K + 2))
                        for n in range(1, K + 1))
    return rows_first, columns_first
```
hint: Row $1$ sums to $1$; every later row sums to $1 - 1 = 0$. Each column sums to $0$.
solution: Counting measure on $\mathbb{N}$ is σ-finite, but $\sum_{m,n} |a_{m,n}| = \infty$, so Fubini does not apply, and the two iterated sums are $1$ and $0$. The code shows the pattern for finite truncations that capture whole rows and columns; the two infinite iterated sums differ for the reason just given, which a finite computation only illustrates.

prompt: Let $f, g \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$ (Lebesgue measure). Prove that the integral $(f * g)(x) = \int f(x - y) g(y) \, dy$ converges absolutely for almost every $x$, that $f * g \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$, and that $\|f * g\|_1 \le \|f\|_1 \|g\|_1$.
hint: Replace $f, g$ by Borel functions equal to them a.e., so $(x, y) \mapsto f(x - y) g(y)$ is Borel on $\mathbb{R}^2$.
hint: Apply Tonelli to $|f(x - y)| |g(y)|$, integrating over $x$ first and using translation invariance.
solution: Every Lebesgue measurable function equals a Borel function almost everywhere, and changing $f$ or $g$ on null sets changes $f * g$ at most on a null set of $x$ (for $g$: the integral in $y$ is unchanged; for $f$: $\{(x,y) : f(x-y) \neq \tilde f(x-y)\}$ is null in the plane and Tonelli shows its sections are null for a.e. $x$). So take $f, g$ Borel. Then $F(x, y) = f(x - y) g(y)$ is Borel on $\mathbb{R}^2$ since $(x,y) \mapsto x - y$ is continuous. By Tonelli applied to $|F|$ and translation invariance of Lebesgue measure, $\int \int |f(x-y)| |g(y)| \, dx \, dy = \int |g(y)| \left( \int |f(x - y)| \, dx \right) dy = \|f\|_1 \|g\|_1 < \infty$. So $|F| \in L^1(\mathbb{R}^2)$. By Tonelli again, $x \mapsto \int |F(x, y)| \, dy$ is measurable with integral $\|f\|_1 \|g\|_1$, hence finite for a.e. $x$, so $(f * g)(x)$ is defined for a.e. $x$; by Fubini it is measurable and integrable, and $\|f * g\|_1 \le \int \int |F| \, dy \, dx = \|f\|_1 \|g\|_1$. $\blacksquare$
