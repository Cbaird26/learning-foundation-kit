@article
Lesson 5 produced measures from functions: $\nu(A) = \int_A f \, d\mu$. The Radon–Nikodym theorem says that, under natural hypotheses, **every** measure that vanishes on $\mu$-null sets arises this way. That makes densities the language of absolute continuity.

A **signed measure** on $(X, \mathcal{A})$ is a function $\nu\colon \mathcal{A} \to [-\infty, \infty]$ with $\nu(\emptyset) = 0$, taking at most one of the values $\pm\infty$, that is countably additive on disjoint sequences (with the series convergent, absolutely if the sum is finite). A set $A$ is **positive** for $\nu$ if $\nu(B) \ge 0$ for every measurable $B \subseteq A$, **negative** if $\nu(B) \le 0$ for all such $B$.

**Theorem 1 (Hahn decomposition).** For a signed measure $\nu$ there are disjoint measurable sets $P, N$ with $X = P \cup N$, $P$ positive and $N$ negative. The pair is unique up to sets that are null for $\nu$ in every measurable subset.

**Corollary 2 (Jordan decomposition).** Define $\nu^+(A) = \nu(A \cap P)$ and $\nu^-(A) = -\nu(A \cap N)$. Then $\nu^\pm$ are measures, mutually singular, $\nu = \nu^+ - \nu^-$, and $|\nu| = \nu^+ + \nu^-$ is the **total variation**. Among decompositions $\nu = \alpha - \beta$ into positive measures, $\nu^\pm$ is the smallest.

### Absolute continuity and singularity

For a positive measure $\mu$ and a signed measure $\nu$: $\nu \ll \mu$ ($\nu$ is **absolutely continuous** with respect to $\mu$) if $\mu(A) = 0$ implies $\nu(A) = 0$. We say $\nu \perp \mu$ (**mutually singular**) if there is $N \in \mathcal{A}$ with $\mu(N) = 0$ and $|\nu|(X \setminus N) = 0$. Examples: $\delta_0 \perp \lambda$; $f\,\lambda \ll \lambda$ for every $f \in L^1$; Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}$ is not absolutely continuous with respect to $\delta_0$.

**Theorem 3 (Lebesgue decomposition).** Let $\mu, \nu$ be σ-finite measures on $(X, \mathcal{A})$. There are unique measures $\nu_a \ll \mu$ and $\nu_s \perp \mu$ with $\nu = \nu_a + \nu_s$.

**Theorem 4 (Radon–Nikodym).** Let $\mu$ and $\nu$ be σ-finite measures on $(X, \mathcal{A})$ with $\nu \ll \mu$. Then there is a measurable $f\colon X \to [0, \infty)$ with $\nu(A) = \int_A f \, d\mu$ for all $A \in \mathcal{A}$. The function $f = \frac{d\nu}{d\mu}$, the **Radon–Nikodym derivative**, is unique $\mu$-almost everywhere. It is integrable if and only if $\nu$ is finite.

*Proof (finite $\mu, \nu$).* *Uniqueness.* If $\int_A f \, d\mu = \int_A g \, d\mu < \infty$ for all $A$, take $A = \{f > g\}$: $\int_A (f - g) \, d\mu = 0$ with $f - g > 0$ on $A$, so $\mu(A) = 0$; symmetrically for $\{g > f\}$.
*Existence.* Let $\lambda = \mu + \nu$, a finite measure. For $g \in L^2(\lambda)$, $\left|\int g \, d\nu\right| \le \int |g| \, d\lambda \le \lambda(X)^{1/2} \|g\|_{L^2(\lambda)}$, so $g \mapsto \int g \, d\nu$ is a bounded linear functional on the Hilbert space $L^2(\lambda)$ (complete by Lesson 9). By the Riesz representation theorem there is $h \in L^2(\lambda)$ with $\int g \, d\nu = \int g h \, d\lambda$ for all $g \in L^2(\lambda)$. Taking $g = \mathbf{1}_A$ gives $\nu(A) = \int_A h \, d\lambda$ with $0 \le \nu(A) \le \lambda(A)$, so $0 \le h \le 1$ $\lambda$-a.e. Rewriting with $\lambda = \mu + \nu$: $\int g (1 - h) \, d\nu = \int g h \, d\mu$ for all bounded measurable $g$ (*). Let $Z = \{h = 1\}$. Taking $g = \mathbf{1}_Z$ in (*) gives $0 = \mu(Z)$, so $\nu(Z) = 0$ by $\nu \ll \mu$. On $X \setminus Z$ we have $0 \le h < 1$. Put $g = (1 + h + \cdots + h^n) \mathbf{1}_A$ in (*): $\int_A (1 - h^{n+1}) \, d\nu = \int_A (h + \cdots + h^{n+1}) \, d\mu$. As $n \to \infty$ the left side tends to $\nu(A \setminus Z)$, because $h^{n+1} \to 0$ on $X \setminus Z$ and $\nu(Z) = 0$ (dominated convergence for the bounded integrands), and the right side increases by monotone convergence to $\int_{A \setminus Z} \frac{h}{1 - h} \, d\mu$ (on $Z$ the integrand is $n+1$ times the null set's indicator, contributing $0$). So $\nu(A \setminus Z) = \int_{A \setminus Z} \frac{h}{1-h} \, d\mu$. Since $\nu(Z) = 0 = \mu(Z)$, set $f = \frac{h}{1-h} \mathbf{1}_{X \setminus Z}$: $\nu(A) = \int_A f \, d\mu$. For σ-finite measures, exhaust $X$ by disjoint sets $X_n$ of finite $\mu$- and $\nu$-measure, apply the finite case on each, and sum the densities. $\blacksquare$

**Both hypotheses matter.** Without σ-finiteness the theorem is false: take $\mu$ = counting measure on $[0,1]$ and $\nu$ = Lebesgue measure. Then $\nu \ll \mu$ (the only $\mu$-null set is $\emptyset$), but $\nu(\{x\}) = 0 = \int_{\{x\}} f \, d\mu = f(x)$ forces $f = 0$, contradicting $\nu([0,1]) = 1$. Without $\nu \ll \mu$ there is only the Lebesgue decomposition: $\nu = \delta_0$ against Lebesgue measure has no density. The Cantor measure $\nu_C$ with distribution function the Cantor function $\varphi$ is a third kind: it has no atoms ($\varphi$ is continuous), yet $\nu_C \perp \lambda$ because $\nu_C$ is concentrated on the null set $C$.

**Consequences.** If $\nu = f \mu$ then $\int g \, d\nu = \int g f \, d\mu$ for $g \ge 0$ (Lesson 5). Chain rule: if $\nu \ll \mu \ll \rho$ (σ-finite) then $\frac{d\nu}{d\rho} = \frac{d\nu}{d\mu} \frac{d\mu}{d\rho}$ $\rho$-a.e. In probability, a random variable with density $f$ has law $f \, d\lambda$, and a ratio of densities is a likelihood ratio.

@example
title: Jordan decomposition of a mixed measure
problem: Let $\nu = 2\delta_{1/2} - \lambda|_{[0,1]}$ on the Borel sets of $\mathbb{R}$, i.e. $\nu(A) = 2\mathbf{1}_A(1/2) - \lambda(A \cap [0,1])$. Find a Hahn decomposition, $\nu^\pm$ and $|\nu|(\mathbb{R})$.
step: Is $\nu$ a signed measure? It is a difference of a finite measure and a finite measure, hence takes finite values and is countably additive.
step: Hahn decomposition: take $P = \{1/2\}$ and $N = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{1/2\}$. A subset $B \subseteq P$ is $\emptyset$ or $\{1/2\}$ and $\nu(B) \in \{0, 2 - 0\}$ is $\ge 0$. A subset $B \subseteq N$ has $\nu(B) = -\lambda(B \cap [0,1]) \le 0$.
step: Jordan parts: $\nu^+(A) = \nu(A \cap P) = 2\mathbf{1}_A(1/2)$, i.e. $\nu^+ = 2\delta_{1/2}$; and $\nu^-(A) = -\nu(A \cap N) = \lambda(A \cap [0,1])$, i.e. $\nu^- = \lambda|_{[0,1]}$.
step: Total variation: $|\nu|(\mathbb{R}) = \nu^+(\mathbb{R}) + \nu^-(\mathbb{R}) = 2 + 1 = 3$, while $\nu(\mathbb{R}) = 2 - 1 = 1$.
answer: $\nu^+ = 2\delta_{1/2}$, $\nu^- = \lambda|_{[0,1]}$, $|\nu|(\mathbb{R}) = 3$.

@example
title: Lebesgue decomposition and density
problem: Let $\mu = \lambda$ on $\mathbb{R}$ and $\nu(A) = \int_{A \cap [0,1]} 2x \, dx + 3\delta_{1/2}(A)$. Find the Lebesgue decomposition of $\nu$ with respect to $\mu$ and the Radon–Nikodym derivative of the absolutely continuous part.
step: Both measures are σ-finite ($\nu$ is finite with $\nu(\mathbb{R}) = 1 + 3 = 4$), so Theorem 3 applies.
step: The first term $\nu_a(A) = \int_A 2x \mathbf{1}_{[0,1]}(x) \, dx$ is absolutely continuous: if $\lambda(A) = 0$ the integral is $0$.
step: The second term $\nu_s = 3\delta_{1/2}$ is concentrated on the Lebesgue-null set $\{1/2\}$, so $\nu_s \perp \lambda$. By uniqueness this is the Lebesgue decomposition.
step: The density is $\frac{d\nu_a}{d\lambda}(x) = 2x \mathbf{1}_{[0,1]}(x)$. The full $\nu$ has no density, since $\nu(\{1/2\}) = 3 \ne 0$ while $\lambda(\{1/2\}) = 0$.
answer: $\nu_a = 2x\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]}\,dx$ with $\nu_a(\mathbb{R}) = 1$; $\nu_s = 3\delta_{1/2}$.

@mistakes
- Dropping σ-finiteness: counting measure on an uncountable set is the standard counterexample to existence of a density.
- Confusing "no atoms" with "absolutely continuous": the Cantor measure is atomless and singular.
- Writing $\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}$ for a measure $\nu$ that is not absolutely continuous: only $\nu_a$ has a density.
- Treating $|\nu(A)|$ as $|\nu|(A)$: in general $|\nu(A)| \le |\nu|(A)$, with equality only when $A$ lies, up to null sets, in $P$ or in $N$.

@practice num
prompt: Let $\mu$ be the Poisson distribution with mean $1$ and $\nu$ the Poisson distribution with mean $2$ on $\{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$, that is $\mu(\{n\}) = e^{-1}/n!$ and $\nu(\{n\}) = e^{-2}2^n/n!$. Find $\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(3)$ to three decimal places.
answer: 2.943
tol: 0.001
check: (exp(-2) * 2 ** 3 / factorial(3)) / (exp(-1) / factorial(3))
hint: On a countable set, the Radon–Nikodym derivative at $n$ is the ratio of the point masses.
solution: $\nu \ll \mu$ since every point has positive $\mu$-mass, and $\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(n) = \frac{\nu(\{n\})}{\mu(\{n\})} = e^{-1} 2^n$. At $n = 3$ this is $8/e \approx 2.943$.

@practice num
prompt: Let $\nu(A) = \int_A (x^2 - \tfrac{1}{4}) \, dx$ on $[-1, 1]$. Find $\nu^+([-1, 1])$, the mass of the positive part in the Jordan decomposition.
answer: 1/3
tol: 1e-6
check: sum(quad(lambda x: x * x - 0.25, a, b) for a, b in [(-1, -0.5), (0.5, 1)])
hint: A Hahn decomposition is $P = \{x^2 \ge 1/4\}$ and $N = \{x^2 < 1/4\}$; integrate the density over $P$.
solution: $P = [-1, -1/2] \cup [1/2, 1]$ and $N = (-1/2, 1/2)$. Then $\nu^+([-1,1]) = \int_P (x^2 - 1/4) \, dx = 2 \int_{1/2}^1 (x^2 - 1/4) \, dx = 2\left(\frac{7}{24} - \frac{1}{8}\right) = 2 \cdot \frac{1}{6} = \frac{1}{3}$. (Also $\nu^- = 1/6$, $|\nu| = 1/2$ and $\nu = 1/6$.)

@practice self
prompt: Let $\mu$ and $\nu$ be finite measures on $(X, \mathcal{A})$ with $\nu \ll \mu$. Prove the $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ form of absolute continuity: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $\delta > 0$ such that $\mu(A) < \delta$ implies $\nu(A) < \varepsilon$.
hint: Argue by contradiction and choose sets $A_n$ with $\mu(A_n) < 2^{-n}$ but $\nu(A_n) \ge \varepsilon$.
hint: Look at $B = \limsup A_n$; use the first Borel–Cantelli lemma for $\mu$ and continuity from above for the finite measure $\nu$.
solution: Suppose the claim fails for some $\varepsilon > 0$. Then for each $n$ there is $A_n \in \mathcal{A}$ with $\mu(A_n) < 2^{-n}$ and $\nu(A_n) \ge \varepsilon$. Put $B = \limsup_n A_n = \bigcap_N \bigcup_{n \ge N} A_n$. Since $\sum \mu(A_n) < \infty$, the first Borel–Cantelli lemma gives $\mu(B) = 0$, hence $\nu(B) = 0$ because $\nu \ll \mu$. On the other hand, the sets $C_N = \bigcup_{n \ge N} A_n$ decrease to $B$, and $\nu$ is finite, so continuity from above gives $\nu(B) = \lim_N \nu(C_N) \ge \limsup_N \nu(A_N) \ge \varepsilon > 0$. This contradiction proves the claim. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Sets up the contradiction hypothesis with sets $A_n$ having summable $\mu$-measures and $\nu(A_n) \ge \varepsilon$.
rubric: Applies Borel–Cantelli to conclude $\mu(\limsup A_n) = 0$ and uses $\nu \ll \mu$ to get $\nu(\limsup A_n) = 0$.
rubric: Uses continuity from above (finiteness of $\nu$) to show $\nu(\limsup A_n) \ge \varepsilon$, a contradiction.

@quiz mc
prompt: Let $\nu_C$ be the Cantor measure on $[0,1]$, the measure with $\nu_C([0,x]) = \varphi(x)$ for the Cantor function $\varphi$. Which description relative to Lebesgue measure $\lambda$ is correct?
choice*: $\nu_C$ has no atoms and is mutually singular with $\lambda$.
choice: $\nu_C \ll \lambda$ with density $\mathbf{1}_C$.
choice: $\nu_C$ is purely atomic.
choice: $\nu_C = \lambda$ on $[0,1]$.
hint: Where is $\nu_C$ concentrated, and what is the Lebesgue measure of that set?
solution: $\varphi$ is continuous, so $\nu_C(\{x\}) = 0$ for every $x$. The measure is concentrated on the Cantor set $C$ (because $\varphi$ is constant on each removed interval), and $\lambda(C) = 0$, so $\nu_C \perp \lambda$. A density $\mathbf{1}_C$ would give $\nu_C(C) = \lambda(C) = 0$, but $\nu_C(C) = 1$.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which of these statements are true?
choice*: If $\mu$ and $\nu$ are σ-finite and $\nu \ll \mu$, then $\nu = f\mu$ for some measurable $f \ge 0$ that is unique $\mu$-a.e.
choice*: Every signed measure has a Hahn decomposition into a positive set and a negative set.

@quiz self
prompt: Let $\mu, \nu$ be finite measures on $(X, \mathcal{A})$. Prove that if $\nu = \nu_a + \nu_s = \nu_a' + \nu_s'$ are two decompositions with $\nu_a, \nu_a' \ll \mu$ and $\nu_s, \nu_s' \perp \mu$ (all finite measures), then $\nu_a = \nu_a'$ and $\nu_s = \nu_s'$.
hint: Let $N, N'$ be $\mu$-null sets carrying $\nu_s$ and $\nu_s'$ respectively, and work on $N \cup N'$ and its complement.
solution: Choose $N, N' \in \mathcal{A}$ with $\mu(N) = \mu(N') = 0$, $\nu_s(X \setminus N) = 0$ and $\nu_s'(X \setminus N') = 0$, and put $M = N \cup N'$, so $\mu(M) = 0$. Then $\nu_a(B) = \nu_a'(B) = 0$ for every measurable $B \subseteq M$ (absolute continuity), and $\nu_s(B) = \nu_s'(B) = 0$ for every measurable $B \subseteq X \setminus M$. Let $A \in \mathcal{A}$. Then $\nu_a(A) = \nu_a(A \setminus M) $ and $\nu_a'(A) = \nu_a'(A \setminus M)$. On $A \setminus M$ we have $\nu_a(A \setminus M) = \nu(A \setminus M) = \nu_a'(A \setminus M)$ since the singular parts vanish there. Hence $\nu_a(A) = \nu_a'(A)$. Since all measures are finite, $\nu_s = \nu - \nu_a = \nu - \nu_a' = \nu_s'$. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Finds a single $\mu$-null set $M$ outside which both singular parts vanish.
rubric: Shows both absolutely continuous parts vanish on subsets of $M$ and equal $\nu$ off $M$.
rubric: Concludes equality of the absolutely continuous parts and, by subtraction (finiteness), of the singular parts.
