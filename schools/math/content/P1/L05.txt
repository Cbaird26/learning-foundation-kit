@article
For which limits may we pass the limit through the integral? The Riemann integral needs uniform convergence. The Lebesgue integral needs only monotonicity.

**Theorem 1 (monotone convergence theorem).** Let $(X, \mathcal{A}, \mu)$ be a measure space and $f_n\colon X \to [0, \infty]$ measurable with $f_1 \le f_2 \le \cdots$ pointwise and $f_n \to f$ pointwise. Then $f$ is measurable and $\int f \, d\mu = \lim_n \int f_n \, d\mu$ (in $[0, \infty]$).

*Proof.* $f = \sup_n f_n$ is measurable. Since $f_n \le f_{n+1} \le f$, the sequence $\int f_n$ is nondecreasing and bounded by $\int f$, so $L = \lim \int f_n \le \int f$. For the reverse, fix a simple $s = \sum_i a_i \mathbf{1}_{A_i}$ with $0 \le s \le f$ and a number $c \in (0, 1)$. Put $E_n = \{x : f_n(x) \ge c s(x)\}$, measurable and increasing. They exhaust $X$: if $s(x) = 0$ then $x \in E_1$; if $s(x) > 0$ then $c s(x) < s(x) \le f(x)$, so $f_n(x) > c s(x)$ for large $n$. Now $f_n \ge c s \mathbf{1}_{E_n}$, so
$$\int f_n \ge c \int s \mathbf{1}_{E_n} = c \sum_i a_i \mu(A_i \cap E_n).$$
As $n \to \infty$ continuity from below gives $\mu(A_i \cap E_n) \to \mu(A_i)$, so $L \ge c \int s$. Let $c \uparrow 1$, then take the supremum over $s$: $L \ge \int f$. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary 2.** For measurable $f, g \ge 0$: (a) $\int (f + g) = \int f + \int g$. (b) If $f_n \ge 0$ are measurable then $\int \sum_n f_n = \sum_n \int f_n$. (c) For measurable $f \ge 0$, $\nu(A) = \int_A f \, d\mu = \int f \mathbf{1}_A \, d\mu$ defines a measure on $\mathcal{A}$.

*Proof.* (a) Take simple $s_n \uparrow f$ and $t_n \uparrow g$ (Lesson 3). Then $s_n + t_n \uparrow f + g$, and $\int (s_n + t_n) = \int s_n + \int t_n$ for simple functions, so the monotone convergence theorem gives (a). (b) Apply (a) repeatedly to the partial sums, which increase to $\sum f_n$, then monotone convergence. (c) $\nu(\emptyset) = 0$. For pairwise disjoint $A_k$ with union $A$, $f \mathbf{1}_A = \sum_k f \mathbf{1}_{A_k}$, and (b) gives $\nu(A) = \sum_k \nu(A_k)$. $\blacksquare$

By (a), the integral is linear on $L^1$: apply it to the identity $(f+g)^+ + f^- + g^- = (f+g)^- + f^+ + g^+$.

**Theorem 3 (Fatou's lemma).** If $f_n\colon X \to [0, \infty]$ are measurable then $\int \liminf_n f_n \, d\mu \le \liminf_n \int f_n \, d\mu$.

*Proof.* Let $g_k = \inf_{n \ge k} f_n$, measurable, nondecreasing in $k$, with $g_k \uparrow \liminf f_n$. For $n \ge k$ we have $g_k \le f_n$, so $\int g_k \le \inf_{n \ge k} \int f_n$. Let $k \to \infty$: the left side tends to $\int \liminf f_n$ by monotone convergence, and the right side tends to $\liminf \int f_n$. $\blacksquare$

### What goes wrong without the hypotheses

Fatou's inequality can be **strict**, and the cause is always the same: mass that disappears in the limit.

| Sequence | $\int f_n$ | $\liminf f_n$ | What happens |
|---|---|---|---|
| $f_n = n \mathbf{1}_{(0,1/n)}$ on $(0,1)$ | $1$ | $0$ | mass concentrates at a point |
| $f_n = \mathbf{1}_{[n, n+1]}$ on $\mathbb{R}$ | $1$ | $0$ | mass escapes to infinity |
| $f_n = \frac{1}{n} \mathbf{1}_{[0,n]}$ on $[0, \infty)$ | $1$ | $0$ | mass spreads out (uniform convergence) |

None of these sequences is monotone, so the monotone convergence theorem does not apply. It also fails for **decreasing** sequences without a finiteness hypothesis: $f_n = \mathbf{1}_{[n, \infty)} \downarrow 0$ but $\int f_n = \infty$ for all $n$. With $\int f_1 < \infty$, applying the monotone convergence theorem to $f_1 - f_n$ repairs this.

```python
def f(n, x):
    """f_n = n on the interval (0, 1/n), else 0."""
    return n if 0 < x < 1 / n else 0


for x in [0.3, 0.05, 0.001]:
    print(x, next(n for n in range(1, 10 ** 6) if f(n, x) == 0))
print([n * (1 / n) for n in (1, 10, 100)])
```

The first lines print the index after which $f_n(x) = 0$ for each fixed $x$ (it is about $1/x$), so $f_n \to 0$ pointwise; the last shows $\int f_n = 1$ each time. The code is an illustration; the proof is Fatou's lemma and the definitions.

@example
title: Termwise integration by monotone convergence
problem: Compute $\int_0^1 \frac{\ln(1/x)}{1 - x^2} \, dx$.
step: For $0 \le x < 1$, $\frac{1}{1 - x^2} = \sum_{k \ge 0} x^{2k}$, and $\ln(1/x) \ge 0$. So the integrand equals $\sum_{k \ge 0} g_k(x)$ with $g_k(x) = x^{2k} \ln(1/x) \ge 0$ almost everywhere on $(0,1)$.
step: Nonnegativity lets us use Corollary 2(b) with no domination hypothesis: $\int_0^1 \sum_k g_k = \sum_k \int_0^1 g_k$.
step: Compute each term by parts: $\int_0^1 x^m \ln(1/x) \, dx = \frac{1}{(m+1)^2}$ for $m \ge 0$. With $m = 2k$ this gives $\frac{1}{(2k+1)^2}$.
step: The sum is $\sum_{k \ge 0} (2k+1)^{-2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6} - \frac{1}{4} \cdot \frac{\pi^2}{6} = \frac{\pi^2}{8}$ (all squares minus the even squares).
answer: $\pi^2/8 \approx 1.2337$.

@example
title: Fatou's inequality is strict
problem: Let $f_n = n \mathbf{1}_{(0, 1/n)}$ on $(0, 1)$ with Lebesgue measure. Compare $\int \liminf f_n$ with $\liminf \int f_n$, and explain why the monotone convergence theorem does not apply.
step: For fixed $x \in (0,1)$, $f_n(x) = 0$ once $n \ge 1/x$. So $\lim f_n = 0$ pointwise and $\int \liminf f_n = 0$.
step: $\int f_n = n \cdot \frac{1}{n} = 1$ for every $n$, so $\liminf \int f_n = 1$.
step: Hence $0 = \int \liminf f_n < \liminf \int f_n = 1$: Fatou's lemma holds, strictly.
step: The sequence is not monotone: $f_1 = \mathbf{1}_{(0,1)}$ and $f_2(0.7) = 0 < f_1(0.7) = 1$. So the monotone convergence theorem is not applicable, and its conclusion is false here.
answer: $0 < 1$; the strict inequality is caused by mass concentrating at $0$.

@mistakes
- Applying the monotone convergence theorem to a decreasing sequence: $\mathbf{1}_{[n,\infty)} \downarrow 0$ but the integrals stay $\infty$. A decreasing sequence needs $\int f_1 < \infty$.
- Writing Fatou's lemma backwards: the inequality is $\int \liminf f_n \le \liminf \int f_n$, never the reverse, and it needs $f_n \ge 0$.
- Exchanging sum and integral for a series with signed terms by "monotone convergence": that needs $f_n \ge 0$, or $\sum \int |f_n| < \infty$ (Lesson 6).
- Concluding $\int f_n \to \int f$ from $f_n \to f$ pointwise alone: concentrating and escaping mass are counterexamples.

model:
```python
from fractions import Fraction


def f(n, x):
    """f_n(x) = n^2 on (1/(n+1), 1/n], else 0."""
    return n * n if Fraction(1, n + 1) < x <= Fraction(1, n) else 0


def mass(n):
    """Integral of f_n: n^2 times the length of the interval."""
    return n * n * (Fraction(1, n) - Fraction(1, n + 1))


def support_index(x):
    """The unique n with x in (1/(n+1), 1/n]."""
    return int(1 / x)
```
hint: The intervals $(1/(n+1), 1/n]$ partition $(0, 1]$, so every $x$ lies in exactly one.
hint: For $x$ in the $n$th interval, $1/x \in [n, n+1)$.
solution: The mass is $n^2 (\frac{1}{n} - \frac{1}{n+1}) = \frac{n}{n+1}$, which tends to $1$, while $f_n(x) \ne 0$ for exactly one $n$ per $x$, so $f_n \to 0$ pointwise. This is concentrating mass: $\int \liminf f_n = 0 < 1 = \liminf \int f_n$. The exact-fraction tests avoid the rounding problems that a float comparison of $x \le 1/n$ would cause at the endpoints. The finite checks illustrate the phenomenon; the proof is the pointwise argument.

@practice self
prompt: Let $(X, \mathcal{A}, \mu)$ be a measure space and $f\colon X \to [0, \infty]$ measurable. Define $\nu(A) = \int_A f \, d\mu$. (a) Prove that $\nu$ is a measure. (b) Prove that $\int g \, d\nu = \int g f \, d\mu$ for every measurable $g\colon X \to [0, \infty]$.
hint: For (a), write $f \mathbf{1}_A = \sum_k f \mathbf{1}_{A_k}$ for a disjoint decomposition and use termwise integration of nonnegative series.
hint: For (b), do indicators, then simple functions, then pass to the limit along $s_n \uparrow g$ with the monotone convergence theorem on both sides.
solution: (a) $\nu(\emptyset) = \int 0 = 0$. Let $A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal{A}$ be pairwise disjoint with union $A$. Then $f \mathbf{1}_A = \sum_k f \mathbf{1}_{A_k}$ pointwise, a series of nonnegative measurable functions. By termwise integration (a consequence of additivity and the monotone convergence theorem), $\nu(A) = \sum_k \nu(A_k)$. (b) If $g = \mathbf{1}_B$ then $\int g \, d\nu = \nu(B) = \int f \mathbf{1}_B \, d\mu = \int g f \, d\mu$. For a simple $g = \sum a_i \mathbf{1}_{B_i}$ both sides are linear in $g$ (additivity of the integral for nonnegative functions on both sides), so they agree. For general $g \ge 0$ take simple $s_n \uparrow g$. Then $s_n f \uparrow g f$, and the monotone convergence theorem gives $\int g \, d\nu = \lim \int s_n \, d\nu = \lim \int s_n f \, d\mu = \int g f \, d\mu$. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Part (a) checks $\nu(\emptyset) = 0$ and countable additivity through termwise integration of nonnegative functions.
rubric: Part (b) proceeds indicator, simple function, then general $g$, using linearity at the simple stage.
rubric: Part (b) applies the monotone convergence theorem twice (once for $\nu$ and once for $\mu$) with $s_n f \uparrow g f$.
