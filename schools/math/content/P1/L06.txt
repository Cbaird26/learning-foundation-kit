@article
Monotone convergence needs $f_n \ge 0$ and monotone growth. Most limits in analysis are neither. The replacement hypothesis is a single integrable function that bounds every $|f_n|$.

**Theorem 1 (dominated convergence theorem).** Let $(X, \mathcal{A}, \mu)$ be a measure space, $f_n, f\colon X \to \mathbb{R}$ (or $\mathbb{C}$) measurable, and $g\colon X \to [0, \infty]$ with $\int g \, d\mu < \infty$. Suppose $f_n \to f$ almost everywhere and $|f_n| \le g$ almost everywhere for every $n$. Then $f \in L^1(\mu)$, $\int |f_n - f| \, d\mu \to 0$, and in particular $\int f_n \, d\mu \to \int f \, d\mu$.

*Proof.* Discard a null set outside which $f_n \to f$ and every $|f_n| \le g$. Then $|f| \le g$ there, so $\int |f| \le \int g < \infty$ and $f \in L^1$. The functions $h_n = 2g - |f_n - f|$ satisfy $h_n \ge 2g - 2g = 0$, are measurable and $h_n \to 2g$ pointwise. By Fatou's lemma,
$$\int 2g \le \liminf_n \int (2g - |f_n - f|) = \int 2g - \limsup_n \int |f_n - f|,$$
where we used linearity (all integrals are finite). Subtracting the finite number $\int 2g$ gives $\limsup_n \int |f_n - f| \le 0$. Finally $|\int f_n - \int f| \le \int |f_n - f| \to 0$. $\blacksquare$

**The role of each hypothesis.** The dominating function $g$ must not depend on $n$. In $f_n = n x e^{-n x^2}$ on $(0,1)$ we have $f_n \to 0$ pointwise, yet $\int_0^1 f_n = (1 - e^{-n})/2 \to 1/2$: the supremum $\sup_n f_n(x) \approx 1/(e x)$ (attained near $n = 1/x^2$) is not integrable near $0$, so no dominating function exists. Domination is **sufficient, not necessary**; on a finite measure space the sharp condition is uniform integrability (look up the Vitali convergence theorem; on infinite spaces a tightness condition is also needed). **Bounded convergence** is the special case $\mu(X) < \infty$, $|f_n| \le M$, where $g = M$.

**Corollary 2 (series).** If $f_n$ are measurable and $\sum_n \int |f_n| \, d\mu < \infty$, then $\sum_n f_n$ converges absolutely almost everywhere, is integrable, and $\int \sum_n f_n = \sum_n \int f_n$.

*Proof.* By Corollary 2(b) of Lesson 5, $\int \sum |f_n| = \sum \int |f_n| < \infty$, so $G = \sum |f_n|$ is integrable and finite almost everywhere. The partial sums are dominated by $G$ and converge a.e., so the dominated convergence theorem applies. $\blacksquare$

### Differentiation under the integral sign

**Theorem 3.** Let $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ be an open interval and $f\colon X \times I \to \mathbb{R}$ such that $f(\cdot, t) \in L^1(\mu)$ for every $t \in I$; for almost every $x$ the map $t \mapsto f(x, t)$ is differentiable on $I$; and there is $g \in L^1(\mu)$ with $|\partial_t f(x, t)| \le g(x)$ for all $t \in I$ and almost every $x$. Then $F(t) = \int f(x, t) \, d\mu(x)$ is differentiable on $I$ and $F'(t) = \int \partial_t f(x, t) \, d\mu(x)$.

*Proof.* Fix $t \in I$ and a sequence $h_n \to 0$ with $t + h_n \in I$, $h_n \ne 0$. Put $q_n(x) = \frac{f(x, t + h_n) - f(x, t)}{h_n}$. Then $q_n \to \partial_t f(\cdot, t)$ a.e., the latter is measurable as an a.e. limit of measurable functions, and by the mean value theorem $|q_n(x)| \le \sup_s |\partial_t f(x, s)| \le g(x)$. The dominated convergence theorem gives $\frac{F(t + h_n) - F(t)}{h_n} = \int q_n \to \int \partial_t f(\cdot, t)$. The sequence was arbitrary. $\blacksquare$

The same argument, with continuity in place of differentiability, shows that $t \mapsto \int f(x, t) \, d\mu$ is continuous when $f(x, \cdot)$ is continuous and $|f(x, t)| \le g(x)$.

```python
import math


def midpoint(h, a, b, m=20000):
    """Midpoint-rule approximation of the integral of h over [a, b]."""
    w = (b - a) / m
    return sum(h(a + (i + 0.5) * w) for i in range(m)) * w


for n in (5, 50, 500):
    value = midpoint(lambda x: n * x * math.exp(-n * x * x), 0.0, 1.0)
    print(n, round(value, 4), round((1 - math.exp(-n)) / 2, 4))
```

The two columns agree and approach $1/2$, not $0$. This is evidence that domination is not a technicality; the supremum computation above is the proof.

@example
title: Interchanging a limit and an integral over a half-line
problem: Evaluate $\lim_{n \to \infty} \int_0^\infty \left(1 + \frac{x}{n}\right)^n e^{-2x} \, dx$ and justify the interchange.
step: Pointwise limit: $(1 + x/n)^n \to e^x$ for each $x \ge 0$, so the integrands converge to $e^{x} e^{-2x} = e^{-x}$.
step: Find a dominating function: since $\ln(1 + u) \le u$ for $u > -1$, $(1 + x/n)^n \le e^x$, so $0 \le (1 + x/n)^n e^{-2x} \le e^{-x}$ for all $n$ and $x \ge 0$.
step: The bound $g(x) = e^{-x}$ is integrable on $[0, \infty)$ with $\int g = 1$ and does not depend on $n$, so the dominated convergence theorem applies.
step: Hence the limit equals $\int_0^\infty e^{-x} \, dx = 1$.
answer: The limit is $1$.

@example
title: Differentiating a parameter integral
problem: For $t > 0$ let $F(t) = \int_0^\infty e^{-tx} \frac{\sin x}{x} \, dx$. Show that $F$ is differentiable and compute $F'(t)$.
step: The integrand is $f(x, t) = e^{-tx} \frac{\sin x}{x}$ with $|f| \le e^{-tx}$ (since $|\sin x| \le x$), integrable on $[0, \infty)$ for each $t > 0$.
step: Differentiate in $t$: $\partial_t f = -e^{-tx} \sin x$. Fix $t_0 > 0$ and restrict to $t > t_0$: then $|\partial_t f| \le e^{-t_0 x} =: g(x)$, which is integrable.
step: Theorem 3 applies on $(t_0, \infty)$, so $F'(t) = -\int_0^\infty e^{-tx} \sin x \, dx$.
step: The standard integral $\int_0^\infty e^{-tx} \sin x \, dx = \frac{1}{1 + t^2}$ gives $F'(t) = -\frac{1}{1 + t^2}$. Since $t_0$ was arbitrary this holds for every $t > 0$.
answer: $F'(t) = -\dfrac{1}{1 + t^2}$ for all $t > 0$.

@mistakes
- Choosing a dominating function that depends on $n$: it must be one fixed integrable function. On $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbf{1}_{[n, n+1]} \le 1$, but $1$ is not integrable.
- Believing uniform convergence is enough on an infinite measure space: $\frac{1}{n} \mathbf{1}_{[0, n]} \to 0$ uniformly, yet each integral is $1$.
- Forgetting a.e. in the hypotheses, or forgetting that the limit function must be shown measurable and integrable (it is, by $|f| \le g$).
- Differentiating under the integral over a parameter range that is too large: domination of $\partial_t f$ may hold only on compact subintervals, and that suffices because differentiability is local.

answer: 2
tol: 0.001
check: quad(lambda x: x * x * exp(-x), 0, 60)
hint: The pointwise limit of the integrand is $x^2 e^{-x}$. For $n \ge 4$, $(1 + x/n)^{-n} \le (1 + x/4)^{-4}$, since $(1 + x/n)^n$ increases with $n$.
solution: For $n \ge 4$, $(1 + x/n)^{-n} x^2 \le (1 + x/4)^{-4} x^2$, which is integrable on $[0, \infty)$ since it is $O(x^{-2})$ at infinity, and it does not depend on $n$. Pointwise, $(1 + x/n)^{-n} \to e^{-x}$. By dominated convergence the limit is $\int_0^\infty x^2 e^{-x} \, dx = \Gamma(3) = 2$.

model:
```python
import math


def envelope_integral(N, M):
    """Midpoint-rule integral over (0, 1) of max_{n <= N} n^2 x e^(-n x)."""
    total = 0.0
    for i in range(M):
        x = (i + 0.5) / M
        total += max(n * n * x * math.exp(-n * x) for n in range(1, N + 1))
    return total / M
```
hint: For each midpoint take the maximum over $n = 1, \dots, N$ with a generator expression.
solution: The values grow by roughly $4 \ln 4 / e^2 \approx 0.75$ each time $N$ is multiplied by $4$, matching the analytic envelope $4/(e^2 x)$ on $x \ge 2/N$ (for fixed $x$ the real maximum of $n^2 x e^{-nx}$ is at $n = 2/x$), whose integral grows like $(4/e^2) \ln N$. The numbers suggest that the envelope is not integrable, so no dominating function exists; the proof is the analytic bound $\sup_n f_n(x) \ge \lfloor 2/x \rfloor^2 x e^{-\lfloor 2/x \rfloor x} \ge c/x$ for small $x$, whose integral diverges. Computation is evidence, not proof.

@practice self
prompt: For each of the following, decide whether $\lim \int = \int \lim$ holds, and prove your answer, citing the theorem you use: (a) $f_n(x) = \sqrt{n} \, x^n$ on $[0,1]$ with Lebesgue measure. (b) $\int_0^1 \sum_{n \ge 1} (-1)^n x^n \, dx$ versus $\sum_{n \ge 1} (-1)^n \int_0^1 x^n \, dx$. (c) $f_n = \frac{1}{n^2} \mathbf{1}_{[0, n^2]}$ on $[0, \infty)$ with Lebesgue measure.
hint: For (a) use $x \le e^{-(1 - x)}$ to bound $\sqrt{n} x^n$ by a multiple of $(1 - x)^{-1/2}$.
hint: For (b) the series is not absolutely integrable, so bound the partial sums directly: $\sum_{n=1}^N (-x)^n = -x \frac{1 - (-x)^N}{1 + x}$.
solution: (a) The interchange holds. $x^n \le e^{-n(1-x)}$ because $\ln x \le -(1-x)$, so $\sqrt{n} x^n \le (1-x)^{-1/2} \sqrt{n(1-x)} e^{-n(1-x)} \le C (1-x)^{-1/2}$ with $C = \sup_{u \ge 0} \sqrt{u} e^{-u} = (2e)^{-1/2}$. The function $g(x) = C (1-x)^{-1/2}$ is integrable on $[0,1)$ (integral $2C$), and $\sqrt{n} x^n \to 0$ for each $x \in [0,1)$. By dominated convergence $\int f_n \to 0 = \int \lim f_n$ (indeed $\int f_n = \sqrt{n}/(n+1) \to 0$). (b) Valid, but not by Tonelli: $\sum |(-x)^n| = x/(1-x)$ is not integrable. The partial sums satisfy $|S_N(x)| = x \frac{|1 - (-x)^N|}{1 + x} \le \frac{2x}{1+x} \le 2$ on $[0,1]$ and converge pointwise to $-x/(1+x)$. Constants are integrable on $[0,1]$, so dominated convergence gives $\int_0^1 \sum (-1)^n x^n = \lim_N \sum_{n \le N} (-1)^n \frac{1}{n+1} = \sum_{n \ge 1} \frac{(-1)^n}{n+1}$, and both sides equal $\ln 2 - 1$. (c) The interchange fails: $f_n \to 0$ uniformly (since $0 \le f_n \le 1/n^2$) but $\int f_n = 1$ for every $n$, so $\lim \int f_n = 1 \ne 0$. No integrable dominating function exists: for $x > 0$, $f_n(x) \ne 0$ exactly when $n \ge \sqrt{x}$, so $\sup_n f_n(x) = 1/\lceil \sqrt{x} \rceil^2$, which equals $1/k^2$ on $((k-1)^2, k^2]$, an interval of length $2k - 1$; its integral is $\sum_k (2k-1)/k^2 = \infty$. Lebesgue measure on $[0, \infty)$ is infinite, so uniform convergence does not help.
rubric: Part (a) produces an explicit integrable dominating function (with the inequality $x^n \le e^{-n(1-x)}$ or an equivalent argument) and applies dominated convergence.
rubric: Part (b) recognizes that absolute convergence fails, bounds the partial sums uniformly, and applies dominated convergence to the partial sums.
rubric: Part (c) refutes the interchange by computing $\int f_n = 1$ against the limit $0$, and notes why uniform convergence is irrelevant on infinite measure.

@quiz self
prompt: Let $F(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2} \cos(t x) \, dx$ for $t \in \mathbb{R}$. Prove that $F$ is differentiable and satisfies $F'(t) = -\frac{t}{2} F(t)$. (You may use that improper integrals of the form $\int_{-\infty}^\infty$ of absolutely integrable continuous functions agree with Lebesgue integrals.)
hint: Differentiate under the integral sign; $|\partial_t (e^{-x^2} \cos(tx))| \le |x| e^{-x^2}$ does not depend on $t$.
hint: Integrate by parts on $[-R, R]$ in $\int x e^{-x^2} \sin(tx) \, dx$ with $dv = x e^{-x^2} dx$.
solution: Let $f(x, t) = e^{-x^2} \cos(tx)$. Then $|f| \le e^{-x^2} \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$, $\partial_t f = -x e^{-x^2} \sin(tx)$, and $|\partial_t f| \le |x| e^{-x^2} =: g(x)$ for all $t$, with $g \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$. By Theorem 3, $F$ is differentiable and $F'(t) = -\int x e^{-x^2} \sin(tx) \, dx$. On $[-R, R]$ integrate by parts with $u = \sin(tx)$, $dv = x e^{-x^2} dx$, $v = -\frac{1}{2} e^{-x^2}$: $\int_{-R}^R x e^{-x^2} \sin(tx) dx = \left[-\tfrac{1}{2} e^{-x^2} \sin(tx)\right]_{-R}^R + \frac{t}{2} \int_{-R}^R e^{-x^2} \cos(tx) dx$. The boundary term is $-\frac{1}{2} e^{-R^2} (\sin(tR) + \sin(tR)) \to 0$ as $R \to \infty$, and both integrals converge to the Lebesgue integrals by dominated convergence (or the stated agreement). Hence $\int x e^{-x^2} \sin(tx) dx = \frac{t}{2} F(t)$ and $F'(t) = -\frac{t}{2} F(t)$. $\blacksquare$
