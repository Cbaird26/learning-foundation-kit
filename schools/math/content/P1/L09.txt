@article
For a measure space $(X, \mathcal{A}, \mu)$ and $1 \le p < \infty$ put $\|f\|_p = \left(\int |f|^p \, d\mu\right)^{1/p}$, and $\|f\|_\infty = \operatorname{ess\,sup} |f| = \inf\{M : |f| \le M \text{ a.e.}\}$. The space $L^p(\mu)$ consists of measurable $f$ with $\|f\|_p < \infty$, **identified when equal almost everywhere**; without the identification $\|\cdot\|_p$ would only be a seminorm, since $\|f\|_p = 0$ only gives $f = 0$ a.e. The conjugate exponent of $p \in [1, \infty]$ is $q$ with $\frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{q} = 1$ (so $p = 1$ and $q = \infty$ are conjugate).

**Lemma 1 (Young).** For $a, b \ge 0$ and $1 < p, q < \infty$ with $\frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{q} = 1$: $ab \le \frac{a^p}{p} + \frac{b^q}{q}$.

*Proof.* If $ab = 0$ it is trivial. Otherwise, since $\log$ is concave, $\log\left(\frac{1}{p} a^p + \frac{1}{q} b^q\right) \ge \frac{1}{p} \log a^p + \frac{1}{q} \log b^q = \log(ab)$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 2 (Hölder).** If $1 \le p \le \infty$ with conjugate $q$, $f \in L^p$ and $g \in L^q$, then $fg \in L^1$ and $\|fg\|_1 \le \|f\|_p \|g\|_q$.

*Proof.* The cases $p \in \{1, \infty\}$ are immediate from $|fg| \le \|g\|_\infty |f|$ a.e. Let $1 < p < \infty$. If $\|f\|_p = 0$ or $\|g\|_q = 0$ then $fg = 0$ a.e. Otherwise set $\tilde f = f/\|f\|_p$ and $\tilde g = g/\|g\|_q$ and apply Young pointwise: $|\tilde f \tilde g| \le \frac{|\tilde f|^p}{p} + \frac{|\tilde g|^q}{q}$. Integrating gives $\int |\tilde f \tilde g| \le \frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{q} = 1$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 3 (Minkowski).** For $1 \le p \le \infty$ and $f, g \in L^p$: $\|f + g\|_p \le \|f\|_p + \|g\|_p$.

*Proof.* The cases $p = 1, \infty$ follow from $|f+g| \le |f| + |g|$. Let $1 < p < \infty$ and $q = p/(p-1)$. Since $|f + g|^p \le 2^{p-1}(|f|^p + |g|^p)$, $f + g \in L^p$. Write $|f+g|^p \le |f+g|^{p-1}|f| + |f+g|^{p-1}|g|$. Since $(p-1) q = p$, $\||f+g|^{p-1}\|_q = \|f+g\|_p^{p/q}$. Hölder gives $\|f+g\|_p^p \le \|f+g\|_p^{p/q}(\|f\|_p + \|g\|_p)$. Divide by $\|f+g\|_p^{p/q}$ (if it is $0$ there is nothing to prove) and use $p - p/q = 1$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 4 (Riesz–Fischer: completeness).** For $1 \le p < \infty$, $L^p(\mu)$ is complete: every Cauchy sequence converges in norm.

*Proof.* Let $(f_n)$ be Cauchy. Choose $n_1 < n_2 < \cdots$ with $\|f_{n_{k+1}} - f_{n_k}\|_p < 2^{-k}$ and put $G_K = \sum_{k \le K} |f_{n_{k+1}} - f_{n_k}|$ and $G = \lim_K G_K$. By Minkowski $\|G_K\|_p \le 1$, so by monotone convergence $\int G^p \le 1$ and $G < \infty$ a.e. On that set the series $f_{n_1} + \sum_k (f_{n_{k+1}} - f_{n_k})$ converges absolutely; let $f$ be its sum (and $0$ elsewhere). Then $f_{n_k} \to f$ a.e. and $f$ is measurable. Given $\varepsilon > 0$ take $N$ with $\|f_n - f_m\|_p < \varepsilon$ for $n, m \ge N$. For $n \ge N$ Fatou's lemma along $k \to \infty$ gives $\int |f_n - f|^p \le \liminf_k \int |f_n - f_{n_k}|^p \le \varepsilon^p$. So $f_n - f \in L^p$, hence $f \in L^p$, and $\|f_n - f\|_p \to 0$. $\blacksquare$

$L^\infty$ is also complete (a Cauchy sequence converges uniformly off a single null set). Note that completeness of $L^p$ is where the Lebesgue integral beats the Riemann integral: the Riemann-integrable functions are not complete under $\|\cdot\|_1$.

### Which exponents?

For power functions on $(0,1)$ and on $(1, \infty)$: $x^{-a} \in L^p(0,1)$ iff $ap < 1$, and $x^{-a} \in L^p(1, \infty)$ iff $ap > 1$. No exponent works on all of $(0,\infty)$. On a finite measure space $L^q \subseteq L^p$ for $p < q$; on $\mathbb{N}$ with counting measure $\ell^p \subseteq \ell^q$ for $p < q$; in general there is no inclusion either way.

```python
def holder_check(f, g, p):
    """Return (sum |f g|, ||f||_p * ||g||_q) for finite lists, q conjugate."""
    q = p / (p - 1)
    lhs = sum(abs(a * b) for a, b in zip(f, g))
    rhs = (sum(abs(a) ** p for a in f) ** (1 / p)
           * sum(abs(b) ** q for b in g) ** (1 / q))
    return lhs, rhs


print([round(v, 4) for v in holder_check([1, -2, 3], [2, 0.5, -1], 3)])
print([round(v, 4) for v in holder_check([1, 2, 3], [1, 4, 9], 3)])
```

The first line prints `[6.0, 8.5708]`: strict inequality. The second prints `[36, 36.0]`: equality, because $|g|^q$ is proportional to $|f|^p$ (here $g = f^{p-1}$). Checking vectors is evidence; the proof of Hölder is Young's inequality.

@example
title: A borderline membership in L^p
problem: Let $f(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}\,(1 + |\ln x|)}$ on $(0, 1)$. For which $p \in [1, \infty)$ is $f \in L^p(0,1)$?
step: For $p = 2$: $\int_0^1 f^2 \, dx = \int_0^1 \frac{dx}{x (1 + |\ln x|)^2}$. Substitute $t = -\ln x$, $dx / x = -dt$: the integral is $\int_0^\infty \frac{dt}{(1+t)^2} = 1 < \infty$. So $f \in L^2$.
step: For $1 \le p < 2$: $(0,1)$ has finite measure, so $L^2 \subseteq L^p$ (Hölder applied to $|f|^p \cdot 1$), and $f \in L^p$.
step: For $p > 2$: $f^p = x^{-p/2}(1 + |\ln x|)^{-p}$. Since $x^{-(p/2 - 1)}(1 + |\ln x|)^{-p} \to \infty$ as $x \to 0^+$, we have $f^p \ge 1/x$ near $0$, and $\int_0^\varepsilon dx/x = \infty$. So $f \notin L^p$.
answer: $f \in L^p(0,1)$ exactly for $1 \le p \le 2$; the logarithm rescues the endpoint $p = 2$ but not larger $p$.

@example
title: Equality in Hölder's inequality
problem: Find $\sup\left\{\int_0^1 f g \, dx : \|g\|_2 \le 1\right\}$ for $f(x) = x^{-1/5}$, and a function $g$ attaining it.
step: By Hölder with $p = q = 2$, $\int f g \le \|f\|_2 \|g\|_2 \le \|f\|_2$, so the supremum is at most $\|f\|_2$.
step: Compute $\|f\|_2^2 = \int_0^1 x^{-2/5} dx = \frac{1}{1 - 2/5} = \frac{5}{3}$, so $\|f\|_2 = \sqrt{5/3} \approx 1.2910$.
step: Equality in Hölder needs $g$ proportional to $f$. Take $g = f / \|f\|_2$, which has $\|g\|_2 = 1$ and $\int f g = \|f\|_2^2 / \|f\|_2 = \|f\|_2$.
answer: The supremum is $\sqrt{5/3} \approx 1.2910$, attained at $g = \sqrt{3/5}\, x^{-1/5}$.

@mistakes
- Treating $\|\cdot\|_p$ as a norm on functions instead of equivalence classes: $\|f\|_p = 0$ only forces $f = 0$ almost everywhere.
- Using Minkowski's inequality for $0 < p < 1$: it is reversed there, and $\|\cdot\|_p$ is not a norm.
- Forgetting that $L^q \subseteq L^p$ for $p < q$ needs a finite measure space: $1/x \cdot \mathbf{1}_{[1,\infty)}$ is in $L^2(\mathbb{R})$ but not in $L^1$.
- Writing the conjugate exponent as $q = p - 1$ or $1/p$: it is $q = p/(p-1)$.

model:
```python
def lp_norm(v, w, p):
    """Weighted L^p norm on a finite measure space; p may be infinity."""
    if p == float('inf'):
        return max((abs(a) for a, wt in zip(v, w) if wt > 0), default=0.0)
    total = 0.0
    for a, wt in zip(v, w):
        total += wt * abs(a) ** p
    return total ** (1 / p)
```
hint: For $p = \infty$ ignore the entries whose weight is zero, since they form a null set.
hint: For finite $p$ accumulate $w_i |v_i|^p$ in a loop and take the $1/p$ power at the end.
solution: The essential supremum ignores null points, which is why `[3, 100]` with weights `[1, 0]` has $L^\infty$ norm $3$. The finite-$p$ formula is the definition of the integral of $|v|^p$ against a weighted counting measure. Spot checks of values do not prove Minkowski's inequality; they only illustrate the formula.

prompt: Let $(X, \mathcal{A}, \mu)$ be a measure space with $\mu(X) < \infty$ and let $1 \le p < q \le \infty$. Prove that $L^q(\mu) \subseteq L^p(\mu)$ and $\|f\|_p \le \mu(X)^{1/p - 1/q} \|f\|_q$. Show by examples that neither this inclusion nor its reverse holds in general without finiteness of $\mu$.
hint: For $q < \infty$ apply Hölder to $|f|^p \cdot 1$ with exponents $q/p$ and its conjugate.
hint: For the examples use $\mathbb{R}$ with Lebesgue measure and $\mathbb{N}$ with counting measure.
solution: If $q = \infty$ then $|f|^p \le \|f\|_\infty^p$ a.e., so $\int |f|^p \le \mu(X) \|f\|_\infty^p$ and $\|f\|_p \le \mu(X)^{1/p} \|f\|_\infty$. For $q < \infty$ let $r = q/p > 1$ with conjugate $r' = q/(q - p)$. Hölder gives $\int |f|^p \cdot 1 \, d\mu \le \left(\int |f|^{pr}\right)^{1/r} \mu(X)^{1/r'} = \|f\|_q^p \, \mu(X)^{1 - p/q}$. Taking $p$th roots: $\|f\|_p \le \mu(X)^{1/p - 1/q} \|f\|_q$. So $L^q \subseteq L^p$. Without finiteness: on $\mathbb{R}$, $f(x) = (1 + |x|)^{-1}$ lies in $L^2$ ($\int f^2 = 2$) but not in $L^1$ ($\int f = \infty$), so $L^2 \not\subseteq L^1$. On $(0,1)$, which has finite measure, the reverse fails; on $\mathbb{N}$ with counting measure, $\ell^1 \subseteq \ell^2$ but $(1/n)_n \in \ell^2 \setminus \ell^1$ shows the other inclusion fails there too. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Handles $q = \infty$ separately and gets $\|f\|_p \le \mu(X)^{1/p} \|f\|_\infty$.
rubric: Applies Hölder to $|f|^p \cdot 1$ with the correct exponents $q/p$ and $q/(q-p)$ and tracks the power of $\mu(X)$.
rubric: Gives valid counterexamples on an infinite measure space for the failure of $L^q \subseteq L^p$ and states where finiteness is used.
