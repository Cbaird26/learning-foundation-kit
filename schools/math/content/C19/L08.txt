@article
A population is counted once a year, and next year's count depends only on this year's: $x_{n+1} = f(x_n)$. Small populations grow, crowded ones starve, so a natural model is $f(x) = rx(1 - x)$ with $x$ the population as a fraction of the maximum. Nothing in this formula looks complicated, yet its behavior ranges from a steady value to a perfect two-year cycle to an unpredictable sequence as the single number $r$ increases. This is the **logistic map**, popularized as a model of chaos by May in 1976.

### Orbits, fixed points and cycles

For a map $f\colon I \to I$ the **orbit** of $x_0$ is $x_0, x_1 = f(x_0), x_2 = f(f(x_0)), \dots$, and we write $f^n$ for the $n$-fold composition. A **fixed point** satisfies $f(p) = p$. A point of **period $k$** satisfies $f^k(p) = p$ with $f^j(p) \ne p$ for $0 < j < k$; its orbit $\{p, f(p), \dots, f^{k-1}(p)\}$ is a **$k$-cycle**. The **multiplier** of a $k$-cycle is $(f^k)'(p) = f'(p)\,f'(f(p)) \cdots f'(f^{k-1}(p))$ by the chain rule.

**Theorem 1 (stability of a fixed point).** Let $f$ be continuously differentiable with $f(p) = p$. If $|f'(p)| < 1$, then there is $\delta > 0$ such that $|x_0 - p| < \delta$ implies $x_n \to p$ (the fixed point is **attracting**), indeed with $|x_n - p| \le q^n|x_0 - p|$ for some $q < 1$. If $|f'(p)| > 1$, the orbits starting near $p$ move away from $p$ initially (the point is **repelling**).

*Proof for $|f'(p)| < 1$.* Choose $q$ with $|f'(p)| < q < 1$. By continuity of $f'$ there is $\delta > 0$ with $|f'(x)| \le q$ for $|x - p| < \delta$. By the mean value theorem, for such $x$, $|f(x) - p| = |f(x) - f(p)| \le q|x - p|$. So $f$ maps the interval $(p - \delta, p + \delta)$ into itself and shrinks distances to $p$ by the factor $q$; by induction $|x_n - p| \le q^n|x_0 - p| \to 0$. If $|f'(p)| > 1$ the same argument with $q > 1$ and $|f'(x)| \ge q$ shows $|f(x) - p| \ge q|x - p|$. $\blacksquare$

If $|f'(p)| = 1$ the test is silent: $f(x) = x - x^3$ has $f'(0) = 1$ and $0$ attracts every orbit starting in $(-1, 1)$ (slowly), while $f(x) = x + x^3$ has $f'(0) = 1$ and repels. A cycle is attracting if its multiplier has absolute value less than $1$ (apply the theorem to $f^k$).

### The logistic map

For $0 < r \le 4$, $f_r(x) = rx(1 - x)$ maps $[0, 1]$ into itself, because its maximum on $[0, 1]$ is $f_r(\tfrac12) = r/4 \le 1$. Its fixed points are $x = 0$ and $p = 1 - 1/r$ (for $r > 1$). Since $f_r'(x) = r(1 - 2x)$: $f_r'(0) = r$ and $f_r'(p) = 2 - r$. So $0$ is attracting for $r < 1$; $p$ is attracting for $|2 - r| < 1$, that is, for $1 < r < 3$. At $r = 1$ the two fixed points exchange stability (a transcritical bifurcation, Week 2). At $r = 3$ the multiplier is $-1$: the fixed point loses stability and a $2$-cycle is born, a **period-doubling** (flip) bifurcation.

The $2$-cycle solves $f_r(f_r(x)) = x$, and one checks by expanding that
$$f_r(f_r(x)) - x = -x\,(rx - r + 1)\,\bigl(r^2x^2 - r(r+1)x + (r+1)\bigr).$$
The first two factors give the fixed points $0$ and $p$. The quadratic factor has discriminant $r^2(r+1)^2 - 4r^2(r+1) = r^2(r+1)(r-3)$, so for $r > 3$ it has two real roots
$$x_\pm = \frac{(r + 1) \pm \sqrt{(r+1)(r-3)}}{2r},$$
which form a $2$-cycle with $f_r(x_+) = x_-$. For $r < 3$ the roots are not real and there is no $2$-cycle. For $r = 7/2$ the square root is $\sqrt{(9/2)(1/2)} = 3/2$ and the cycle is the pair of rational numbers $3/7$ and $6/7$.

### Exact and floating-point orbits

Python's `Fraction` computes orbits exactly. A long orbit in floating point is only a pseudo-orbit (each step rounds), but for an attracting cycle the rounding errors are damped and the numerical orbit is trustworthy; Week 10 returns to what happens when orbits are not damped.

```python
from fractions import Fraction


def orbit(f, x0, n):
    """The list [x_0, f(x_0), ..., f^n(x_0)]."""
    xs = [x0]
    for _ in range(n):
        xs.append(f(xs[-1]))
    return xs


r = Fraction(7, 2)
f = lambda x: r * x * (1 - x)
print([str(x) for x in orbit(f, Fraction(3, 7), 4)])
```

This prints `['3/7', '6/7', '3/7', '6/7', '3/7']`, an exact proof by computation that $\{3/7, 6/7\}$ is a $2$-cycle of $f_{7/2}$ (the check is $f(3/7) = 6/7$ and $f(6/7) = 3/7$). That this cycle is *unstable* at $r = 7/2$ follows from its multiplier: the orbit of any nearby floating-point start will not stay on it.

@example
title: The fixed point of the logistic map and its stability
problem: For $r = 2.5$ find the nonzero fixed point of $f(x) = rx(1 - x)$, its multiplier, and the first few iterates from $x_0 = 0.2$ (to four places).
step: The fixed point is $p = 1 - 1/r = 1 - 0.4 = 0.6$.
step: The multiplier is $f'(p) = 2 - r = -0.5$, so $|f'(p)| < 1$ and $p$ is attracting; the negative sign means the iterates alternate on the two sides of $p$.
step: Iterate: $x_1 = 2.5 \cdot 0.2 \cdot 0.8 = 0.4$, $x_2 = 2.5 \cdot 0.4 \cdot 0.6 = 0.6$. The orbit hits $0.6$ exactly because the problem was chosen to land on it; a generic start approaches it with errors that shrink by about the factor $0.5$ each step.
answer: $p = 0.6$ with multiplier $-0.5$; attracting.

@example
title: When does a period-2 orbit exist?
problem: For which $r$ does the logistic map have a $2$-cycle, and what is it for $r = 3.5$?
step: The $2$-cycle points are the roots of $r^2x^2 - r(r+1)x + (r+1) = 0$ that are not fixed points.
step: The discriminant is $r^2(r+1)(r-3)$, which is positive exactly when $r > 3$ (for $r > 0$). So a $2$-cycle exists for $r > 3$ and not for $r < 3$.
step: For $r = 3.5$: $x_\pm = \frac{4.5 \pm \sqrt{4.5 \cdot 0.5}}{7} = \frac{4.5 \pm 1.5}{7}$, which gives $6/7$ and $3/7$.
step: These roots are not fixed points because the fixed points are $0$ and $1 - 1/3.5 = 5/7$.
answer: A $2$-cycle exists exactly for $r > 3$; at $r = 3.5$ it is $\{3/7, 6/7\}$.

@mistakes
- Believing $|f'(p)| = 1$ means neutral stability: the test is inconclusive there, and $x \mapsto x \mp x^3$ show both outcomes.
- Taking the multiplier of a cycle as $f'$ at a single point; it is the product of $f'$ over all points of the cycle.
- Stating that the logistic map has a $2$-cycle for $r < 3$ because floating-point iteration seems to wobble: below $r = 3$ the discriminant is negative and the orbit converges.
- Treating an exact rational orbit as a long-run tool: denominators grow exponentially (they square at every step for $r = 4$), so exact arithmetic is only practical for a few steps.

model:
```python
def attracting_period(r, x0, n_transient, max_period, tol):
    """Period of the cycle the orbit has settled on, or None."""
    x = x0
    for _ in range(n_transient):
        x = r * x * (1 - x)
    y = x
    for p in range(1, max_period + 1):
        y = r * y * (1 - y)
        if abs(y - x) < tol:
            return p
    return None
```
hint: Throw away the transient, then iterate once more from the same point and compare each iterate with the starting value.
solution: After the transient the orbit is within rounding of an attracting cycle, so $f^p(x) \approx x$ exactly when $p$ is a multiple of the period, and the first such `p` is the period. If the orbit is chaotic ($r = 3.9$) no `p` up to $16$ works. The case with `max_period = 3` shows that the answer depends on the search bound; the tolerance and the transient length must both be chosen with care near a bifurcation, where the convergence is slow.

model:
```python
from fractions import Fraction


def denominators(x0, n):
    """Denominators of the exact logistic orbit at r = 4."""
    x = Fraction(x0)
    out = [x.denominator]
    for _ in range(n):
        x = 4 * x * (1 - x)
        out.append(x.denominator)
    return out
```
hint: A `Fraction` is already in lowest terms and exposes `.denominator`; apply $x \mapsto 4x(1-x)$ with `Fraction` arithmetic.
solution: If $x = a/b$ in lowest terms with $b$ odd and $b > 1$, then $4x(1-x) = 4a(b-a)/b^2$, and the numerator shares no factor with $b^2$ (since $\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(b - a, b) = 1$ and $b$ is odd, so coprime to $4$), so the fraction is already in lowest terms and the denominator squares at every step: $3, 3^2, 3^4, 3^8, \dots$. The number of digits therefore doubles each step, which is why exact orbits are limited to a few dozen steps. A point such as $3/4$ is a fixed point, and $1/2 \mapsto 1 \mapsto 0$ lands on the fixed point $0$.

model:
```python
def cobweb(f, x0, n):
    """Vertices of the cobweb path of the orbit of x0 under f."""
    points = [(x0, 0)]
    x = x0
    for _ in range(n):
        y = f(x)
        points.append((x, y))
        points.append((y, y))
        x = y
    return points
```
hint: Compute `y = f(x)` once per step and append `(x, y)` and then `(y, y)`.
solution: The vertical segment goes from the diagonal (or the axis for the first step) to the graph and the horizontal one goes from the graph back to the diagonal, so each step contributes two vertices, and the second one has equal coordinates (it lies on the diagonal). Where a path spirals into the intersection of the graph and the diagonal, the fixed point is attracting; a path that moves away shows a repelling one.
