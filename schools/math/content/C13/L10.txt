@article
The integral $\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{\cos x}{1 + x^2}\,dx$ has no elementary antiderivative. Yet its value is $\pi/e$, and the proof takes three lines once you view the integrand as the real part of a function of a complex variable. The residue theorem turns hard real integrals into finite sums of residues, *provided each piece of the contour is justified*.

### Type 1: trigonometric integrals

For a rational function $R$ of two variables, put $z = e^{i\theta}$. Then $\cos\theta = \frac12(z + z^{-1})$, $\sin\theta = \frac{1}{2i}(z - z^{-1})$ and $d\theta = \dfrac{dz}{iz}$, so
$$\int_0^{2\pi}R(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\,d\theta = \oint_{|z|=1}R\Big(\tfrac{z + z^{-1}}{2}, \tfrac{z - z^{-1}}{2i}\Big)\frac{dz}{iz} = 2\pi\sum_{|a|<1}\operatorname{Res}(\cdot, a),$$
provided the resulting rational function has no poles on the circle. The residue theorem applies because a rational function is holomorphic in a large disk except at finitely many points.

### Type 2: rational functions over $\mathbb{R}$

**Theorem 1.** Let $P, Q$ be polynomials, $Q$ without real zeros, $\deg Q \ge \deg P + 2$. Then
$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\,dx = 2\pi i\sum_{\operatorname{Im}a > 0}\operatorname{Res}\Big(\frac{P}{Q}, a\Big).$$

*Proof.* The integral converges absolutely because $|P/Q| = O(1/x^2)$. Choose $R$ so large that every zero of $Q$ in the upper half-plane lies in $|z| < R$. The closed contour $\Gamma_R$ consists of the segment $[-R, R]$ followed by the semicircle $C_R\colon z = Re^{it}$, $0 \le t \le \pi$. It lies in a disk containing no singularities except the finitely many zeros of $Q$, and its winding number is $1$ about each zero in the upper half-plane and $0$ about the others. By the residue theorem, $\int_{-R}^RP/Q\,dx + \int_{C_R}P/Q\,dz = 2\pi i\sum_{\operatorname{Im}a>0}\operatorname{Res}$. For large $|z|$, $|P(z)| \le c_1|z|^p$ and $|Q(z)| \ge c_2|z|^q$ with $q \ge p + 2$ (as in the proof of the fundamental theorem of algebra), so on $C_R$ we get $|P/Q| \le K/R^2$, and the ML lemma gives $\left|\int_{C_R}\right| \le \frac{K}{R^2}\cdot\pi R \to 0$. Let $R \to \infty$. $\blacksquare$

### Type 3: Fourier-type integrals

**Lemma 2 (Jordan).** Let $a > 0$ and let $g$ be continuous on $C_R$ with $|g| \le M_R$ there. Then $\left|\int_{C_R}g(z)e^{iaz}\,dz\right| \le \dfrac{\pi M_R}{a}$.

*Proof.* On $z = Re^{it}$, $|e^{iaz}| = e^{-aR\sin t}$ and $|dz| = R\,dt$. So the integral is at most $M_RR\int_0^\pi e^{-aR\sin t}dt = 2M_RR\int_0^{\pi/2}e^{-aR\sin t}dt$ by symmetry about $t = \pi/2$. On $[0, \pi/2]$, $\sin t \ge 2t/\pi$, so this is at most $2M_RR\int_0^{\pi/2}e^{-2aRt/\pi}dt = 2M_RR\cdot\dfrac{\pi}{2aR}\big(1 - e^{-aR}\big) < \dfrac{\pi M_R}{a}$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 3.** Let $P, Q$ be polynomials, $Q$ without real zeros, $\deg Q \ge \deg P + 1$, and $a > 0$. Then
$$\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{-R}^R\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}e^{iax}\,dx = 2\pi i\sum_{\operatorname{Im}z_0>0}\operatorname{Res}\Big(\frac{P}{Q}e^{iaz}, z_0\Big).$$
The proof is that of Theorem 1, with the semicircle integral controlled by Jordan's lemma: $M_R = \max_{C_R}|P/Q| \to 0$. Taking real and imaginary parts gives $\int\frac{P}{Q}\cos(ax)\,dx$ and $\int\frac{P}{Q}\sin(ax)\,dx$ when $P/Q$ is real on $\mathbb{R}$. For $a < 0$ close the contour in the lower half-plane instead, where $e^{iaz}$ decays.

*Real poles.* When $Q$ has simple real zeros, one indents the contour with small semicircles and obtains principal values; for instance $\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin x}{x}dx = \frac\pi2$. We do not prove the indentation lemma here.

### Series: summing over the integers

**Theorem 4.** $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6}$.

*Proof.* Let $S_N$ be the square with vertices $\pm(N + \tfrac12) \pm i(N + \tfrac12)$, $N \ge 1$, traversed counterclockwise, and $f(z) = \pi\cot(\pi z)/z^2$. *Bound on $\cot$.* For $w = s + it$ with $t > 0$, $\cot w = i\dfrac{e^{2iw} + 1}{e^{2iw} - 1}$ and $|e^{2iw}| = e^{-2t} < 1$, so $|\cot w| \le \dfrac{1 + e^{-2t}}{1 - e^{-2t}} = \coth t$; the same holds for $t < 0$ with $|t|$. On the horizontal sides $|\operatorname{Im}(\pi z)| = \pi(N + \tfrac12) \ge \pi/2$, so $|\cot(\pi z)| \le \coth(\pi/2)$. On the vertical sides $\pi z \equiv \pi/2 + i\pi y \pmod \pi$ and $\cot(\pi/2 + i\pi y) = -\tan(i\pi y) = -i\tanh(\pi y)$ has modulus at most $1$. So $|\cot(\pi z)| \le M := \coth(\pi/2)$ on $\partial S_N$. *Residues.* The poles of $f$ inside $S_N$ are the integers $-N, \dots, N$. At $n \ne 0$ the function $\pi\cot(\pi z)$ has residue $\pi\cos(\pi n)/(\pi\cos\pi n) = 1$ (rule 3 of Lesson 9), so $\operatorname{Res}(f, n) = 1/n^2$. At $0$: $\cot w = \frac1w - \frac w3 - \cdots$, hence $\pi\cot(\pi z) = \frac1z - \frac{\pi^2z}{3} - \cdots$ and $f = \frac{1}{z^3} - \frac{\pi^2}{3z} - \cdots$, so $\operatorname{Res}(f, 0) = -\pi^2/3$. The residue theorem gives $\oint_{\partial S_N}f\,dz = 2\pi i\Big(2\sum_{n=1}^N\frac{1}{n^2} - \frac{\pi^2}{3}\Big)$. *Estimate.* On $\partial S_N$, $|z| \ge N + \tfrac12$, so $|f| \le \pi M/(N + \tfrac12)^2$, and the length is $8(N + \tfrac12)$; the ML lemma bounds the integral by $8\pi M/(N + \tfrac12) \to 0$. Hence $2\sum_{n\ge1}1/n^2 = \pi^2/3$. $\blacksquare$

The same method with $f(z) = \pi\cot(\pi z)/(z^2 + a^2)$ gives $\sum_{n \ge 1}\frac{1}{n^2 + a^2} = \frac{\pi a\coth(\pi a) - 1}{2a^2}$ for $a > 0$ (stated without proof; the proof repeats the one above).

### Checking numerically

Every residue computation should be tested on the computer. Here is the midpoint rule on $[-L, L]$:

```python
import math


def midpoint(f, a, b, n):
    """Midpoint rule with n panels for the integral of f over [a, b]."""
    h = (b - a) / n
    return sum(f(a + (k + 0.5) * h) for k in range(n)) * h


value = midpoint(lambda x: 1 / (x * x + 1) ** 2, -50, 50, 20000)
print(round(value, 4))
```

This prints `1.5708`, which agrees with $\pi/2$ to four decimals (the neglected tails beyond $|x| = 50$ contribute about $10^{-5}$). Such agreement supports the residue calculation but is not a proof.

@example
title: A trigonometric integral
problem: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{5 + 3\cos\theta}$.
step: Substitute $z = e^{i\theta}$: $\cos\theta = \frac12(z + z^{-1})$ and $d\theta = dz/(iz)$. The integral becomes $\oint_{|z|=1}\dfrac{1}{5 + \frac32(z + z^{-1})}\cdot\dfrac{dz}{iz} = \oint_{|z|=1}\dfrac{2\,dz}{i(3z^2 + 10z + 3)}$.
step: Factor $3z^2 + 10z + 3 = 3(z + \tfrac13)(z + 3)$. The poles are $-\tfrac13$ (inside the unit circle) and $-3$ (outside). No pole lies on the circle.
step: Residue at the simple pole $-\tfrac13$: $\dfrac{2}{i\cdot 3\,(-\tfrac13 + 3)} = \dfrac{2}{8i} = \dfrac{1}{4i}$.
step: Apply the residue theorem: the integral equals $2\pi i\cdot\dfrac{1}{4i} = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$.
answer: $\pi/2$.

@example
title: A Fourier-type integral
problem: Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{-R}^R\frac{x\sin x}{x^2 + 4}\,dx$.
step: Consider $f(z) = \dfrac{z}{z^2 + 4}e^{iz}$. Here $P(x) = x$, $Q(x) = x^2 + 4$ has no real zeros, $\deg Q = \deg P + 1$ and $a = 1 > 0$, so Theorem 3 applies; $\operatorname{Im}\big(xe^{ix}\big) = x\sin x$.
step: The only zero of $Q$ in the upper half-plane is $2i$, a simple pole. $\operatorname{Res}(f, 2i) = \dfrac{2i\,e^{i\cdot 2i}}{2\cdot 2i} = \dfrac{e^{-2}}{2}$.
step: Theorem 3: $\lim\int_{-R}^R\dfrac{xe^{ix}}{x^2+4}dx = 2\pi i\cdot\dfrac{e^{-2}}{2} = \pi ie^{-2}$.
step: Take imaginary parts: the real part $\int\frac{x\cos x}{x^2+4}dx = 0$ (odd integrand) and the imaginary part is $\int\frac{x\sin x}{x^2+4}dx = \pi e^{-2}$.
answer: $\pi e^{-2} \approx 0.425$.

@mistakes
- Closing the contour in the wrong half-plane. For $e^{iax}$ with $a > 0$ close in the upper half-plane; for $a < 0$, in the lower; for $\cos$ or $\sin$ write them with $e^{\pm iax}$ or take real or imaginary parts of $e^{iax}$.
- Using $\int_{C_R}\to 0$ for a rational function with $\deg Q = \deg P + 1$ and no oscillating factor. For $\int x\,dx/(x^2+1)$ the semicircle integral tends to $i\pi$, not $0$.
- Including residues from the lower half-plane (or from poles on the real axis) in Theorem 1. Only the zeros of $Q$ with $\operatorname{Im} > 0$ count.
- Forgetting the factor $\dfrac{1}{iz}$ in Type 1, or the factor $2\pi i$ in front of the sum.

model:
```python
def simpson(f, a, b, n):
    """Composite Simpson's rule with n (even) panels."""
    if n < 2 or n % 2 != 0:
        raise ValueError("n must be even and at least 2")
    h = (b - a) / n
    total = f(a) + f(b)
    for k in range(1, n):
        total += (4 if k % 2 == 1 else 2) * f(a + k * h)
    return total * h / 3
```
hint: Odd-indexed interior points get weight $4$, even-indexed interior points weight $2$, the two endpoints weight $1$.
hint: Multiply the weighted sum by $h/3$, not by $h$.
solution: Sum the endpoint values, then loop over the interior nodes $x_1, \dots, x_{n-1}$ with weight $4$ when the index is odd and $2$ when it is even, and multiply by $h/3$. The two Fourier and rational tests check residue results from this lesson ($\pi/\sqrt2$ and $\pi e^{-3}$) numerically; the truncation to $[-L, L]$ is a stated source of error. Agreement is evidence about the answers, not a proof.

In the upper half-plane the simple poles are $i$ and $2i$. $\operatorname{Res}(1/Q, i) = \dfrac{1}{(2i)(-1 + 4)} = \dfrac{1}{6i}$ and $\operatorname{Res}(1/Q, 2i) = \dfrac{1}{(-4 + 1)(4i)} = -\dfrac{1}{12i}$. The sum is $\dfrac{2 - 1}{12i} = \dfrac{1}{12i}$, so the integral equals $2\pi i\cdot\dfrac{1}{12i} = \dfrac{\pi}{6}$. On $|z| = R > 2$, $|Q(z)| \ge (R^2 - 1)(R^2 - 4)$, so $\left|\int_{C_R}\dfrac{dz}{Q}\right| \le \dfrac{\pi R}{(R^2-1)(R^2-4)} \to 0$. $\blacksquare$
