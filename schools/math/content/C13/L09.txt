@article
The functions $\sin z/z$, $1/z$ and $e^{1/z}$ are all undefined at $z = 0$, but they behave very differently as $z \to 0$: the first tends to $1$, the second to $\infty$, the third has no limit at all (along $z = t > 0$ it tends to $\infty$, and along $z = -t$ it tends to $0$). The Laurent series of Lesson 8 sorts these cases, and the coefficient of $z^{-1}$ turns out to be the number that controls contour integrals.

### Classification

A point $a$ is an **isolated singularity** of $f$ if $f$ is holomorphic on a punctured disk $0 < |z - a| < \rho$ (but is not assumed to be defined or holomorphic at $a$). Let $f = \sum_{n \in \mathbb{Z}}c_n(z - a)^n$ be its Laurent series there. Then $a$ is
- **removable** if $c_n = 0$ for all $n < 0$;
- a **pole of order $m \ge 1$** if $c_{-m} \ne 0$ and $c_n = 0$ for all $n < -m$ (a simple pole if $m = 1$);
- **essential** if $c_n \ne 0$ for infinitely many $n < 0$.

Examples at $0$: $\sin z/z$ is removable; $(e^z - 1 - z)/z^3 = \frac{1}{2z} + \frac16 + \frac{z}{24} + \cdots$ is a simple pole (its order is $1$, not $3$); $e^{1/z}$ is essential.

**Theorem 1 (Riemann's removable singularity theorem).** If $a$ is an isolated singularity of $f$ and $f$ is bounded on some punctured disk around $a$, then $a$ is removable: $f$ extends to a holomorphic function on a full disk around $a$.

*Proof.* Let $|f| \le M$ on $0 < |z - a| < \rho$. For $n < 0$ and $0 < s < \rho$, the Laurent coefficient is $c_n = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|z-a|=s}f(z)(z - a)^{-n-1}dz$, and the ML lemma gives $|c_n| \le \frac{1}{2\pi}\cdot M s^{-n-1}\cdot 2\pi s = Ms^{-n} = Ms^{|n|}$. Letting $s \to 0$ gives $c_n = 0$. So $f = \sum_{n \ge 0}c_n(z - a)^n$ on the punctured disk, and the power series defines the holomorphic extension. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 2 (poles).** The point $a$ is a pole of order $m$ if and only if $f(z) = g(z)/(z - a)^m$ on a punctured disk with $g$ holomorphic near $a$ and $g(a) \ne 0$. Moreover $a$ is a pole of some order if and only if $|f(z)| \to \infty$ as $z \to a$.

*Proof.* If the Laurent series has the stated shape then $g(z) = (z - a)^mf(z) = \sum_{n \ge -m}c_n(z - a)^{n+m}$ is a power series with $g(a) = c_{-m} \ne 0$; the converse reads the Taylor series of $g$. If $|f| \to \infty$, then $f \ne 0$ near $a$ and $1/f$ is holomorphic on a punctured disk with limit $0$, so it extends by Theorem 1 with value $0$ at $a$. It is not identically zero, so $1/f = (z - a)^mh$ with $m \ge 1$, $h$ holomorphic, $h(a) \ne 0$ (Taylor series), and $f = (z - a)^{-m}/h$ is a pole of order $m$. Conversely a pole has $|f| = |g|/|z - a|^m \to \infty$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 3 (Casorati–Weierstrass).** If $a$ is an essential singularity of $f$, then for every $\rho > 0$ the image $f(\{0 < |z - a| < \rho\})$ is dense in $\mathbb{C}$.

*Proof.* Suppose not: there are $w_0 \in \mathbb{C}$, $\varepsilon > 0$ and $\rho > 0$ with $|f(z) - w_0| \ge \varepsilon$ on the punctured disk. Then $g = 1/(f - w_0)$ is holomorphic and bounded by $1/\varepsilon$, so removable by Theorem 1. If $g(a) \ne 0$, then $f = w_0 + 1/g$ extends holomorphically, so $a$ is removable; if $g$ has a zero of order $m$ at $a$, then $f - w_0$ has a pole of order $m$. Either way $a$ is not essential. $\blacksquare$

### Residues

The **residue** of $f$ at an isolated singularity $a$ is $\operatorname{Res}(f, a) = c_{-1}$, the coefficient of $(z - a)^{-1}$. Computation rules:
1. Simple pole: $\operatorname{Res}(f, a) = \lim_{z \to a}(z - a)f(z)$.
2. Pole of order $m$: $\operatorname{Res}(f, a) = \dfrac{1}{(m-1)!}\lim_{z \to a}\dfrac{d^{m-1}}{dz^{m-1}}\big[(z - a)^mf(z)\big]$.
3. If $f = g/h$ with $g, h$ holomorphic near $a$, $h(a) = 0$ and $h'(a) \ne 0$, then $\operatorname{Res}(f, a) = g(a)/h'(a)$.
4. Essential singularities: read $c_{-1}$ from the Laurent series.

Rule 2 follows because $(z - a)^mf = \sum_{n \ge -m}c_n(z - a)^{n+m}$ and the $(m-1)$-st derivative at $a$ is $(m-1)!\,c_{-1}$.

### The residue theorem

For a closed contour $\gamma$ and a point $a \notin \gamma$, the **winding number** is $n(\gamma, a) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_\gamma\frac{dz}{z - a}$. For a circle traversed once counterclockwise it is $1$ for $a$ inside and $0$ outside (Lessons 5 and 6).

**Lemma.** $n(\gamma, a)$ is an integer.

*Proof.* Let $\gamma\colon[\alpha, \beta] \to \mathbb{C}$ and $h(t) = \int_\alpha^t\frac{\gamma'(s)}{\gamma(s) - a}ds$. Then $h' = \gamma'/(\gamma - a)$ wherever $\gamma$ is smooth, so $\frac{d}{dt}\big[e^{-h(t)}(\gamma(t) - a)\big] = e^{-h}\gamma' - h'e^{-h}(\gamma - a) = 0$. The continuous function $e^{-h}(\gamma - a)$ is therefore constant, equal to $\gamma(\alpha) - a$ at $t = \alpha$. At $t = \beta$, $\gamma(\beta) = \gamma(\alpha)$ gives $e^{-h(\beta)} = 1$, so $h(\beta) \in 2\pi i\mathbb{Z}$, and $n(\gamma, a) = h(\beta)/(2\pi i) \in \mathbb{Z}$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 4 (residue theorem).** Let $D$ be an open disk, $a_1, \dots, a_m$ distinct points of $D$, $f$ holomorphic on $D\setminus\{a_1, \dots, a_m\}$, and $\gamma$ a closed contour in $D\setminus\{a_1, \dots, a_m\}$. Then
$$\oint_\gamma f\,dz = 2\pi i\sum_{k=1}^m n(\gamma, a_k)\operatorname{Res}(f, a_k).$$

*Proof.* Near $a_k$ write $f = P_k + R_k$ with principal part $P_k(z) = \sum_{n \ge 1}c^{(k)}_{-n}(z - a_k)^{-n}$ and $R_k$ holomorphic near $a_k$. $P_k$ is a power series in $w = 1/(z - a_k)$ that converges for all $w$ with $|w| > 1/\rho$, hence (Abel's lemma) for all $w$: it is holomorphic on $\mathbb{C}\setminus\{a_k\}$. The function $g = f - \sum_kP_k$ is holomorphic on $D$ minus the $a_k$ and bounded near each $a_k$, so it extends holomorphically to $D$ by Theorem 1, and $\oint_\gamma g = 0$ by Cauchy's theorem for a disk. For the principal parts, the series for $P_k$ converges uniformly on $\gamma$ (a compact set at positive distance from $a_k$), so $\oint_\gamma P_k = \sum_{n \ge 1}c^{(k)}_{-n}\oint_\gamma(z - a_k)^{-n}dz$. For $n \ge 2$ the integrand has the primitive $(z - a_k)^{1-n}/(1-n)$ and the integral is $0$; for $n = 1$ it is $2\pi i\,n(\gamma, a_k)$. Adding up, $\oint_\gamma f = 0 + 2\pi i\sum_kn(\gamma, a_k)c^{(k)}_{-1}$. $\blacksquare$

This version is for contours in a disk; the same statement holds on any simply connected region, with a proof that uses Cauchy's theorem there.

The winding number of a polygon around a point can be computed by adding the angles that its sides subtend at the point:

```python
import math


def angle_between(u, v):
    """Signed angle from u to v (nonzero complex numbers)."""
    cross = u.real * v.imag - u.imag * v.real
    dot = u.real * v.real + u.imag * v.imag
    return math.atan2(cross, dot)


print(round(angle_between(1 + 0j, 1j) / math.pi, 3))
print(round(angle_between(-1 + 0.1j, -1 - 0.1j) / math.pi, 3))
```

This prints `0.5` and `0.063`. The second angle is small and positive (the side runs downward to the left of the point, which is counterclockwise), although the arguments of the two points are about $174^\circ$ and $-174^\circ$: subtracting arguments would give about $-348^\circ$. The `atan2(cross, dot)` form has no jump at the negative real axis.

@example
title: Residues of a function with a double pole
problem: Compute $\oint_{|z|=2}\dfrac{e^z}{z^2(z - 1)}\,dz$ with the circle counterclockwise.
step: The singularities are $z = 0$ (a double pole) and $z = 1$ (a simple pole). Both lie inside $|z| = 2$, and each has winding number $1$.
step: At $z = 1$ use rule 1: $\operatorname{Res} = \lim_{z\to1}(z - 1)\dfrac{e^z}{z^2(z-1)} = \dfrac{e}{1} = e$.
step: At $z = 0$ use rule 2 with $m = 2$ and $g(z) = \dfrac{e^z}{z - 1}$: $g'(z) = \dfrac{e^z(z - 1) - e^z}{(z - 1)^2} = \dfrac{e^z(z - 2)}{(z-1)^2}$, so $\operatorname{Res} = g'(0) = \dfrac{1\cdot(-2)}{1} = -2$.
step: Apply Theorem 4: $\oint = 2\pi i\,(e - 2)$.
answer: $2\pi i(e - 2)$.

@example
title: Classifying a singularity
problem: Classify the singularity of $f(z) = \dfrac{\cos z - 1}{z^3}$ at $z = 0$ and find its residue.
step: Expand: $\cos z - 1 = -\dfrac{z^2}{2} + \dfrac{z^4}{24} - \cdots$, so $f(z) = -\dfrac{1}{2z} + \dfrac{z}{24} - \cdots$ for $z \ne 0$.
step: The Laurent series has exactly one negative power, $z^{-1}$, with $c_{-1} = -\tfrac12 \ne 0$.
step: So $z = 0$ is a simple pole (order $1$, even though the denominator has $z^3$), with $\operatorname{Res}(f, 0) = -\tfrac12$.
step: Check with rule 1: $\lim_{z\to0}zf(z) = \lim\dfrac{\cos z - 1}{z^2} = -\tfrac12$.
answer: A simple pole with residue $-1/2$.

@mistakes
- Reading the order of a pole from the power of $z$ in the denominator. The order is $m$ in $f = g/(z - a)^m$ with $g(a) \ne 0$; the numerator can cancel part of the denominator, as in $(\cos z - 1)/z^3$.
- Using rule 1 for a pole of order two or more: $\lim(z - a)f$ is then infinite. Multiply by $(z - a)^m$ and differentiate $m - 1$ times, and divide by $(m-1)!$.
- Forgetting the winding numbers and orientation: a clockwise circle gives $-1$, and a singularity outside the contour contributes $0$.
- Believing that an essential singularity has no residue: $z\cos(1/z) = z - \tfrac{1}{2z} + \cdots$ has residue $-\tfrac12$ at $0$.

model:
```python
import math


def winding_number(pts, a):
    """Winding number of a closed polygon around the point a."""
    total = 0.0
    n = len(pts)
    for k in range(n):
        u = pts[k] - a
        v = pts[(k + 1) % n] - a
        cross = u.real * v.imag - u.imag * v.real
        dot = u.real * v.real + u.imag * v.imag
        total += math.atan2(cross, dot)
    return round(total / (2 * math.pi))
```
hint: Use index `(k + 1) % n` for the next vertex, so the last vertex connects to the first.
hint: Do not subtract two `atan2(imag, real)` arguments: the jump at the negative real axis ruins the sum. The `atan2(cross, dot)` form always gives the signed angle in $(-\pi, \pi)$.
solution: Each side subtends a signed angle less than $\pi$ at `a`, and the angles add up to $2\pi$ times the winding number. The five-pointed star traced as a pentagram winds twice around its center and once around a point in an arm. Subtracting arguments of consecutive vertices instead fails whenever a side crosses the negative real axis as seen from `a`, which the tests with the square and the triangle expose. This is the numerical version of the integer-valued integral $\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint dz/(z - a)$.

solution: Since $h(a) = 0$, the Taylor series of $h$ at $a$ is $h(z) = (z - a)k(z)$ with $k(z) = \sum_{n \ge 1}\frac{h^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(z - a)^{n-1}$ holomorphic near $a$ and $k(a) = h'(a) \ne 0$. By continuity $k \ne 0$ near $a$, so $g/k$ is holomorphic near $a$. For $z \ne a$ near $a$, $\dfrac{g(z)}{h(z)} = \dfrac{1}{z - a}\cdot\dfrac{g(z)}{k(z)}$. Expanding $g/k = \sum_{n \ge 0}d_n(z - a)^n$ shows the Laurent series of $g/h$ has $c_{-1} = d_0 = g(a)/k(a) = g(a)/h'(a)$. $\blacksquare$
