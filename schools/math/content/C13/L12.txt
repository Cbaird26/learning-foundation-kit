@article
Is there a map from the infinite upper half-plane onto the bounded unit disk that preserves angles and is smooth in both directions? It sounds impossible, since one region is unbounded and the other is not, but in the complex plane such a map is a rational function. This lesson explains why holomorphic functions preserve angles and builds the maps that carry standard regions onto each other.

### Conformal maps

Let $f$ be holomorphic near $z_0$. If $\gamma(t)$ is a smooth curve with $\gamma(0) = z_0$ and $\gamma'(0) \ne 0$, then the chain rule gives $(f\circ\gamma)'(0) = f'(z_0)\gamma'(0)$. If $f'(z_0) \ne 0$, the tangent vector of the image curve is the tangent vector of $\gamma$ multiplied by the fixed complex number $f'(z_0)$: it is rotated by $\arg f'(z_0)$ and scaled by $|f'(z_0)|$, the same for every curve through $z_0$.

**Definition.** A holomorphic function $f$ is **conformal** at $z_0$ if $f'(z_0) \ne 0$, and conformal on an open set if it is conformal at every point of it.

**Theorem 1.** If $f$ is conformal at $z_0$ and $\gamma_1, \gamma_2$ are smooth curves through $z_0$ with nonzero tangent vectors $\gamma_1'(0)$, $\gamma_2'(0)$, then the angle from $f\circ\gamma_1$ to $f\circ\gamma_2$ at $f(z_0)$ equals the angle from $\gamma_1$ to $\gamma_2$ at $z_0$, in size and orientation.

*Proof.* The angle from $\gamma_1$ to $\gamma_2$ is $\arg\dfrac{\gamma_2'(0)}{\gamma_1'(0)}$ (mod $2\pi$). For the images the quotient is $\dfrac{f'(z_0)\gamma_2'(0)}{f'(z_0)\gamma_1'(0)} = \dfrac{\gamma_2'(0)}{\gamma_1'(0)}$, because $f'(z_0) \ne 0$ cancels. The tangent vectors of the image curves are nonzero, so the angle is defined. $\blacksquare$

Lengths of small curves are multiplied by $|f'(z_0)|$ and small areas by $|f'(z_0)|^2$. At a point where $f'(z_0) = 0$ angles are not preserved: $z \mapsto z^2$ doubles all angles at $0$. By the inverse function theorem (stated without proof here), a map conformal at $z_0$ is one-to-one near $z_0$ with a holomorphic inverse. The conjugation $z \mapsto \overline{z}$ preserves the size of angles but reverses their orientation and is not holomorphic.

### Möbius transformations

A **Möbius transformation** is $T(z) = \dfrac{az + b}{cz + d}$ with $ad - bc \ne 0$, regarded as a map of the Riemann sphere $\widehat{\mathbb{C}}$, with $T(-d/c) = \infty$ and $T(\infty) = a/c$ (and $T(\infty) = \infty$ if $c = 0$). Composing two Möbius maps corresponds to multiplying their matrices $\begin{pmatrix}a & b\\ c & d\end{pmatrix}$, and the inverse corresponds to the inverse matrix, so Möbius maps form a group; matrices that are nonzero multiples of each other give the same map. The condition $ad - bc \ne 0$ is exactly what makes $T$ non-constant, and $T'(z) = \dfrac{ad - bc}{(cz + d)^2} \ne 0$ shows that $T$ is conformal.

**Theorem 2.** A Möbius transformation maps every circle or line to a circle or line. (We call circles and lines *generalized circles*; a line passes through $\infty$.)

*Proof.* A generalized circle is the set $\{A|z|^2 + 2\operatorname{Re}(\overline{B}z) + C = 0\}$ with $A, C \in \mathbb{R}$, $B \in \mathbb{C}$ and $|B|^2 > AC$: for $A \ne 0$ this is a circle, for $A = 0$ a line. If $c = 0$, $T$ is $z \mapsto \alpha z + \beta$, a rotation-dilation followed by a translation, which maps generalized circles to generalized circles. If $c \ne 0$, then $T(z) = \dfrac ac - \dfrac{ad - bc}{c^2}\cdot\dfrac{1}{z + d/c}$ is a composition of a translation, the inversion $z \mapsto 1/z$, a rotation-dilation and a translation. It remains to treat $w = 1/z$. Substitute $z = 1/w$ and multiply by $|w|^2$: $A + 2\operatorname{Re}(\overline{B}\,\overline{w}) + C|w|^2 = 0$, that is, $C|w|^2 + 2\operatorname{Re}(Bw) + A = 0$ (as $\operatorname{Re}(\overline{B}\,\overline{w}) = \operatorname{Re}(Bw)$), which is again of the form above with $|B|^2 > CA$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 3 (three points).** If $z_1, z_2, z_3$ are distinct points of $\widehat{\mathbb{C}}$ and $w_1, w_2, w_3$ are distinct points of $\widehat{\mathbb{C}}$, there is exactly one Möbius transformation $T$ with $T(z_j) = w_j$ for $j = 1, 2, 3$.

*Proof.* The map $S(z) = \dfrac{(z - z_1)(z_2 - z_3)}{(z - z_3)(z_2 - z_1)}$ is Möbius (its matrix has determinant $(z_2 - z_3)(z_2 - z_1)(z_1 - z_3) \ne 0$ because the points are distinct; if one point is $\infty$, use the limiting form of $S$) and sends $z_1, z_2, z_3$ to $0, 1, \infty$. Define $R$ likewise for the $w_j$. Then $T = R^{-1}\circ S$ works. For uniqueness it is enough to see that a Möbius map $U$ fixing $0, 1, \infty$ is the identity: $U(\infty) = \infty$ forces $c = 0$; $U(0) = 0$ forces $b = 0$; then $U(z) = (a/d)z$ and $U(1) = 1$ gives $a = d$. If $T_1$ and $T_2$ both work, $U = T_1^{-1}T_2$ fixes $z_1, z_2, z_3$, so $S\circ U\circ S^{-1}$ fixes $0, 1, \infty$ and is the identity; hence $U$ is the identity and $T_1 = T_2$. $\blacksquare$

### Standard maps

**Theorem 4.** (a) $C(z) = \dfrac{z - i}{z + i}$ maps the upper half-plane $\mathbb{H}$ one-to-one onto the unit disk $\mathbb{D}$. (b) For $|a| < 1$, $\varphi_a(z) = \dfrac{z - a}{1 - \overline{a}z}$ maps $\mathbb{D}$ one-to-one onto $\mathbb{D}$, with $\varphi_a(a) = 0$.

*Proof.* (a) $|C(z)| < 1$ if and only if $|z - i| < |z + i|$, that is, if and only if $z$ is closer to $i$ than to $-i$, which holds exactly when $\operatorname{Im} z > 0$. The inverse is $w \mapsto i(1 + w)/(1 - w)$. (b) Expanding gives the identity $|1 - \overline{a}z|^2 - |z - a|^2 = (1 - |a|^2)(1 - |z|^2)$. For $|z| < 1$ the right side is positive, so $|\varphi_a(z)| < 1$ (and $1 - \overline{a}z \ne 0$). A direct computation shows $\varphi_{-a}(\varphi_a(z)) = z$, and $\varphi_{-a}$ also maps $\mathbb{D}$ into $\mathbb{D}$, so $\varphi_a$ is a bijection of $\mathbb{D}$. $\blacksquare$

Two facts we state without proof: every conformal bijection of $\mathbb{D}$ onto itself has the form $e^{i\theta}\varphi_a$, and the **Riemann mapping theorem** says that any simply connected domain $U \ne \mathbb{C}$ is mapped onto $\mathbb{D}$ by a conformal bijection. Other useful maps: $z \mapsto z^2$ doubles angles at $0$ and maps $\mathbb{H}$ onto $\mathbb{C}\setminus[0, \infty)$; $e^z$ maps the strip $0 < \operatorname{Im} z < \pi$ onto $\mathbb{H}$.

### Checking by computer

```python
def mobius(a, b, c, d):
    """The map z -> (a z + b)/(c z + d) as a Python function."""
    return lambda z: (a * z + b) / (c * z + d)


cayley = mobius(1, -1j, 1, 1j)
print([round(abs(cayley(x)), 6) for x in (-2.0, 0.5, 7.0)])
print(round(abs(cayley(1j)), 6), round(abs(cayley(2 + 3j)), 6))
```

The first line prints `[1.0, 1.0, 1.0]`: real points go to the unit circle. The second prints `0.0 0.632456`: $i \mapsto 0$ and an upper half-plane point lands inside the disk. Computed values illustrate Theorem 4 but only the proof covers all points.

@example
title: A half-plane onto the disk with prescribed values
problem: Find a Möbius transformation that maps the upper half-plane $\mathbb{H}$ onto the unit disk $\mathbb{D}$ with $T(2 + i) = 0$ and $T(\infty) = 1$.
step: A map $\mathbb{H} \to \mathbb{D}$ sending $2 + i$ to $0$ must have its zero at $2 + i$ and its pole at the reflection $2 - i$: try $T(z) = \lambda\dfrac{z - (2 + i)}{z - (2 - i)}$ with $|\lambda| = 1$.
step: Check that this maps $\mathbb{H}$ into $\mathbb{D}$: $|z - (2 + i)| < |z - (2 - i)|$ means $z$ is closer to $2 + i$ than to $2 - i$, which holds exactly for $\operatorname{Im} z > 0$. So $|T| = |\lambda|\cdot\dfrac{|z - 2 - i|}{|z - 2 + i|} < 1$ on $\mathbb{H}$.
step: Use $T(\infty) = 1$: $\lim_{z\to\infty}T(z) = \lambda$, so $\lambda = 1$ and $T(z) = \dfrac{z - 2 - i}{z - 2 + i}$.
step: Verify: $T(2 + i) = 0$, $T(\infty) = 1$, and $T$ is a Möbius map with determinant $(1)(-2 + i) - (-2 - i)(1) = 2i \ne 0$. It maps the real line $\operatorname{Im} z = 0$ to the unit circle because $|x - 2 - i| = |x - 2 + i|$ for real $x$.
answer: $T(z) = \dfrac{z - 2 - i}{z - 2 + i}$.

@example
title: The Möbius map through three points
problem: Find the Möbius transformation with $T(0) = 1$, $T(1) = i$, $T(\infty) = -1$, and find the image of the real line.
step: Write $T(z) = \dfrac{az + b}{cz + d}$. From $T(\infty) = a/c = -1$ we get $a = -c$. From $T(0) = b/d = 1$ we get $b = d$.
step: From $T(1) = \dfrac{a + b}{c + d} = \dfrac{-c + d}{c + d} = i$: $-c + d = ic + id$, so $d(1 - i) = c(1 + i)$ and $c = d\dfrac{1 - i}{1 + i} = -id$.
step: Take $d = 1$: $c = -i$, $a = i$, $b = 1$. So $T(z) = \dfrac{iz + 1}{-iz + 1} = \dfrac{1 + iz}{1 - iz}$, with determinant $a d - bc = i + i = 2i \ne 0$.
step: For real $x$, $|1 + ix| = |1 - ix|$, so $|T(x)| = 1$; the real line (together with $\infty \mapsto -1$) goes onto the unit circle, by Theorems 2 and 3 (three points of the line go to three points of the circle, and a generalized circle is determined by three points).
step: Check the upper half-plane: $T(i) = \dfrac{1 - 1}{1 + 1} = 0$, so $T$ maps $\mathbb{H}$ onto $\mathbb{D}$.
answer: $T(z) = \dfrac{1 + iz}{1 - iz}$; it maps $\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}$ onto the unit circle and $\mathbb{H}$ onto the disk.

@mistakes
- Believing a conformal map preserves distances or areas. It preserves angles only; lengths scale by $|f'|$ and areas by $|f'|^2$, a factor that varies from point to point.
- Calling $z^2$ conformal on all of $\mathbb{C}$. Since $(z^2)' = 2z$ vanishes at $0$, the map is conformal on $\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$ but is not one-to-one there; angles at $0$ are doubled.
- Forgetting the determinant condition: $(2z + 4)/(z + 2)$ has $ad - bc = 0$ and is the constant $2$, not a Möbius transformation.
- Mapping a half-plane to the disk with a zero in the wrong place: $\dfrac{z - a}{z - \overline{a}}$ with $\operatorname{Im} a > 0$ sends the half-plane to the disk, but with $\operatorname{Im} a < 0$ it sends it to the outside of the disk.

model:
```python
def mobius_through(zs, ws):
    """Möbius map with T(zs[k]) = ws[k], built from cross ratios."""
    def normalizer(p):
        p1, p2, p3 = p
        return [[p2 - p3, -p1 * (p2 - p3)], [p2 - p1, -p3 * (p2 - p1)]]

    mz = normalizer(zs)
    mw = normalizer(ws)
    inv = [[mw[1][1], -mw[0][1]], [-mw[1][0], mw[0][0]]]
    a = inv[0][0] * mz[0][0] + inv[0][1] * mz[1][0]
    b = inv[0][0] * mz[0][1] + inv[0][1] * mz[1][1]
    c = inv[1][0] * mz[0][0] + inv[1][1] * mz[1][0]
    d = inv[1][0] * mz[0][1] + inv[1][1] * mz[1][1]
    return lambda z: (a * z + b) / (c * z + d)
```
hint: Build the matrix of `S` from `zs` and of `R` from `ws`, take the adjugate of `R`'s matrix, and multiply: `inv @ mz`, entry by entry.
hint: Check your function on the points `zs` first; a transposed matrix product will fail there.
solution: The matrix of $T = R^{-1}\circ S$ is the product (matrix of $R^{-1}$)(matrix of $S$), in that order. The adjugate stands in for the inverse because multiplying a Möbius matrix by a nonzero scalar changes nothing. The last test, a pair of translations, is a case where $T(z) = z + 5$; the map is determined by three values (Theorem 3). Verifying a computed map on three points is evidence only for the points tested; Theorem 3 is what guarantees uniqueness.

prompt: Prove that $C(z) = \dfrac{z - i}{z + i}$ maps the upper half-plane $\mathbb{H} = \{\operatorname{Im} z > 0\}$ onto the open unit disk $\mathbb{D}$, and that the map is one-to-one.
hint: Show $|C(z)| < 1$ on $\mathbb{H}$ by comparing distances; for onto, solve $w = C(z)$ for $z$.
hint: Solving gives $z = i(1 + w)/(1 - w)$. Compute $\operatorname{Im} z$ by multiplying numerator and denominator by $\overline{1 - w}$.
solution: *Into.* For $z \in \mathbb{H}$, $|z - i| < |z + i|$ (the point $z$ is closer to $i$ than to $-i$, since $|z - i|^2 - |z + i|^2 = -4\operatorname{Im} z < 0$), so $|C(z)| < 1$. *One-to-one.* $C$ is a Möbius transformation, hence injective on $\widehat{\mathbb{C}}$. *Onto.* Let $w \in \mathbb{D}$; then $w \ne 1$, and $z = i\dfrac{1 + w}{1 - w}$ satisfies $C(z) = w$ (solve $w(z + i) = z - i$). Hence $\operatorname{Im} z = \operatorname{Re}\dfrac{1 + w}{1 - w} > 0$, so $z \in \mathbb{H}$. $\blacksquare$
