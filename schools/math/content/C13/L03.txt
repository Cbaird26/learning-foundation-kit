@article
The function $f(z) = \overline{z}$ is continuous and looks as innocent as $f(x) = x$. But try the difference quotient at $z_0 = 0$: $\dfrac{\overline{h}}{h}$ equals $1$ when $h$ is real and $-1$ when $h$ is purely imaginary. In the plane there are infinitely many ways for $h$ to approach $0$, and the quotient must settle on one value from all of them. That demand is what makes complex differentiability so strong.

### The derivative

**Definition.** Let $f$ be defined on a neighborhood of $z_0$. Then $f$ is **differentiable at $z_0$** if
$$f'(z_0) = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{f(z_0 + h) - f(z_0)}{h}$$
exists, where $h \in \mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$. A function is **holomorphic** (or analytic) on an open set $U$ if it is differentiable at every point of $U$; it is **entire** if it is holomorphic on $\mathbb{C}$. "Holomorphic at a point" means holomorphic on some disk around it.

The proofs of the sum, product, quotient and chain rules and of $(z^n)' = nz^{n-1}$ are the same as in real calculus. Differentiability at $z_0$ implies continuity there: $f(z_0 + h) - f(z_0) = h\cdot\dfrac{f(z_0 + h) - f(z_0)}{h} \to 0\cdot f'(z_0) = 0$. Polynomials are entire, and rational functions are holomorphic off the zeros of the denominator.

### The Cauchy–Riemann equations

**Theorem 1 (necessity).** Write $f = u + iv$ with $z = x + iy$. If $f$ is differentiable at $z_0 = x_0 + iy_0$, then the partial derivatives of $u$ and $v$ exist at $(x_0, y_0)$ and
$$u_x = v_y, \qquad u_y = -v_x, \qquad f'(z_0) = u_x + iv_x$$
there.

*Proof.* Let $h = t$ be real and let $t \to 0$. The quotient equals $\dfrac{u(x_0 + t, y_0) - u(x_0, y_0)}{t} + i\dfrac{v(x_0 + t, y_0) - v(x_0, y_0)}{t}$. Its limit is $f'(z_0)$, so by Theorem 1 of Lesson 2 (limits of real and imaginary parts) $u_x$ and $v_x$ exist and $f'(z_0) = u_x + iv_x$. Now let $h = it$ with $t$ real. The quotient is $\dfrac{1}{i}\Big[\dfrac{u(x_0, y_0 + t) - u}{t} + i\dfrac{v(x_0, y_0 + t) - v}{t}\Big]$, which tends to $f'(z_0)$. Multiplying by $i$: $u_y + iv_y = if'(z_0) = iu_x - v_x$. Comparing real and imaginary parts gives $u_y = -v_x$ and $v_y = u_x$. $\blacksquare$

The equations alone are not enough at a single point. Let $f(z) = \sqrt{|xy|}$ (so $u = \sqrt{|xy|}$, $v = 0$). All four partial derivatives at $0$ are $0$, since $u = 0$ on both axes, so the equations hold at $0$. But along $z = t(1 + i)$ with $t > 0$ the quotient $f(z)/z = t/(t(1 + i)) = 1/(1 + i)$ does not tend to $0$, which is its value along the real axis. So $f$ is not differentiable at $0$.

**Theorem 2 (sufficiency).** Let $U$ be open and $f = u + iv$ with $u, v$ having continuous first partial derivatives on $U$. If the Cauchy–Riemann equations $u_x = v_y$, $u_y = -v_x$ hold at every point of $U$, then $f$ is holomorphic on $U$, and $f' = u_x + iv_x$.

*Proof.* Fix $z_0 = x_0 + iy_0 \in U$ and write $h = \xi + i\eta$. Continuous partial derivatives make $u$ and $v$ differentiable in the real sense: $u(x_0 + \xi, y_0 + \eta) - u(x_0, y_0) = u_x\xi + u_y\eta + \varepsilon_1(h)|h|$ and likewise for $v$ with $\varepsilon_2$, where $\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2 \to 0$ as $h \to 0$ and the partials are evaluated at $(x_0, y_0)$. Therefore
$$f(z_0 + h) - f(z_0) = (u_x + iv_x)\xi + (u_y + iv_y)\eta + (\varepsilon_1 + i\varepsilon_2)|h|.$$
By the Cauchy–Riemann equations, $u_y + iv_y = -v_x + iu_x = i(u_x + iv_x)$, so the first two terms equal $(u_x + iv_x)(\xi + i\eta) = (u_x + iv_x)h$. Dividing by $h$: $\dfrac{f(z_0 + h) - f(z_0)}{h} = u_x + iv_x + (\varepsilon_1 + i\varepsilon_2)\dfrac{|h|}{h}$, and $\big|\,|h|/h\,\big| = 1$, so the last term tends to $0$. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary.** If $f$ is holomorphic on a domain and $f' = 0$ there, then $f$ is constant. Indeed $u_x = v_x = 0$ and, by Cauchy–Riemann, $u_y = v_y = 0$, so $u$ and $v$ have zero gradient, hence are constant along any polygonal path, hence constant on the connected set.

### Harmonic functions

A real function $u$ with continuous second partials is **harmonic** if $u_{xx} + u_{yy} = 0$. We will prove in Lesson 6 that a holomorphic function has derivatives of all orders, so $u$ and $v$ are $C^2$. Then, using $u_x = v_y$, $u_y = -v_x$ and equality of mixed partials, $u_{xx} = v_{yx}$ and $u_{yy} = -v_{xy}$, so $u_{xx} + u_{yy} = 0$. The same works for $v$. If $u$ is harmonic, a function $v$ with $u + iv$ holomorphic is a **harmonic conjugate** of $u$; it is found by integrating the Cauchy–Riemann equations (the example below), and is unique up to an additive constant on a domain.

### Looking at difference quotients

```python
def quotients(f, z0, h):
    """Difference quotients at z0 along four directions (step size h)."""
    z0 = complex(z0)
    return [(f(z0 + d * h) - f(z0)) / (d * h) for d in (1, -1, 1j, -1j)]


print([round(q.real, 3) for q in quotients(lambda z: z.conjugate(), 0, 0.01)])
print([round(q.real, 2) for q in quotients(lambda z: z * z, 1 + 1j, 0.001)])
```

The first line prints `[1.0, 1.0, -1.0, -1.0]`: four directions, two different values, so $\overline{z}$ has no derivative. The second prints `[2.0, 2.0, 2.0, 2.0]`, as expected for $(z^2)' = 2z = 2 + 2i$. Real parts of four sample directions are evidence, not a proof.

@example
title: Where is a function differentiable?
problem: Find all points where $f(x + iy) = x^2 + iy^2$ is differentiable, and decide whether $f$ is holomorphic anywhere.
step: Here $u = x^2$ and $v = y^2$, which have continuous partials everywhere: $u_x = 2x$, $u_y = 0$, $v_x = 0$, $v_y = 2y$.
step: Cauchy–Riemann: $u_x = v_y$ gives $2x = 2y$, and $u_y = -v_x$ gives $0 = 0$. So the equations hold exactly on the line $y = x$.
step: At a point of that line, Theorem 2 applies in the real sense at that point (continuous partials) together with the equations at the point, and the proof of Theorem 2 only used the equations at $z_0$ itself. So $f$ is differentiable at $x + ix$ with $f' = u_x + iv_x = 2x$. Off the line, Theorem 1 shows $f$ is not differentiable.
step: A holomorphic function must be differentiable on an open set. The line $y = x$ contains no disk, so $f$ is nowhere holomorphic.
answer: $f$ is differentiable exactly at the points $x + ix$ (with $f'(x + ix) = 2x$) and is holomorphic nowhere.

@example
title: Finding a harmonic conjugate
problem: Show that $u(x, y) = x^3 - 3xy^2 - x$ is harmonic and find a function $v$ such that $f = u + iv$ is entire. Write $f$ in terms of $z$.
step: Compute $u_x = 3x^2 - 3y^2 - 1$, $u_{xx} = 6x$, $u_y = -6xy$, $u_{yy} = -6x$. Then $u_{xx} + u_{yy} = 0$, so $u$ is harmonic.
step: Cauchy–Riemann: $v_y = u_x = 3x^2 - 3y^2 - 1$. Integrate in $y$: $v = 3x^2y - y^3 - y + g(x)$.
step: Use the other equation: $v_x = 6xy + g'(x)$ must equal $-u_y = 6xy$. So $g'(x) = 0$ and $g$ is a constant $c$.
step: Thus $v = 3x^2y - y^3 - y + c$. Since $z^3 = (x^3 - 3xy^2) + i(3x^2y - y^3)$, we recognize $f = z^3 - z + ic$.
step: The partials of $u$ and $v$ are polynomials, hence continuous, and the equations hold everywhere, so $f$ is entire by Theorem 2.
answer: $v = 3x^2y - y^3 - y + c$ and $f(z) = z^3 - z + ic$.

@mistakes
- Using Cauchy–Riemann at a single point to conclude differentiability. The equations are necessary; to conclude, add continuous partial derivatives (Theorem 2) or check the definition, as with $\sqrt{|xy|}$ above.
- Mixing "differentiable at a point" with "holomorphic": $f(z) = |z|^2$ is differentiable at $0$ only, and is holomorphic nowhere.
- Treating $x$ and $y$ as if $z$ and $\overline{z}$ do not matter: an expression containing $\overline{z}$, $\operatorname{Re} z$, $\operatorname{Im} z$ or $|z|$ is usually not holomorphic.
- Forgetting the constant in a harmonic conjugate: $v$ is determined only up to an additive real constant.

model:
```python
def cr_residuals(f, z0, h=1e-4):
    """Numerical residuals of the Cauchy-Riemann equations at z0."""
    dx = (f(z0 + h) - f(z0 - h)) / (2 * h)
    dy = (f(z0 + 1j * h) - f(z0 - 1j * h)) / (2 * h)
    u_x, v_x = dx.real, dx.imag
    u_y, v_y = dy.real, dy.imag
    return (u_x - v_y, u_y + v_x)
```
hint: `dx` is the central difference in the real direction; its real part is $u_x$ and its imaginary part is $v_x$.
hint: `dy` is the central difference in the imaginary direction; its real part is $u_y$ and its imaginary part is $v_y$ (no division by `i`).
solution: Compute the two central differences once each, then read off the four partial derivatives from real and imaginary parts. Dividing the imaginary-direction difference by `i` would give the derivative $f'$ instead of the partial derivatives $u_y$, $v_y$. Zero residuals at a point are consistent with differentiability but, as $\sqrt{|xy|}$ shows, do not prove it.
