@article
The real function $1/(1 + x^2)$ is smooth on all of $\mathbb{R}$, yet its Taylor series $1 - x^2 + x^4 - \cdots$ at $0$ diverges for $|x| \ge 1$. Nothing on the real line explains why. Over $\mathbb{C}$ the explanation is visible: $1/(1 + z^2)$ has poles at $z = \pm i$, at distance $1$ from the center.

### Taylor series

**Theorem 1 (Taylor).** If $f$ is holomorphic on the disk $D(a, R)$ ($0 < R \le \infty$), then
$$f(z) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(z - a)^n \quad\text{for every } z \in D(a, R).$$

*Proof.* Fix $0 < r < R$. By the proof of Theorem 3 of Lesson 6 (applied to $\overline{D}(a, r)$, which lies in the open set $D(a, R)$), $f$ equals a power series $\sum c_n(z - a)^n$ on $D(a, r)$, and $c_n = f^{(n)}(a)/n!$ because a power series is differentiated termwise (Theorem 2 of Lesson 4). The coefficients do not depend on $r$, and $r < R$ was arbitrary. $\blacksquare$

So the radius of convergence of the Taylor series at $a$ is at least the distance from $a$ to the nearest point where $f$ is not holomorphic. For $1/(1 + z^2)$ at $0$ it is at least $1$; it is exactly $1$, since the series $\sum(-1)^nz^{2n}$ diverges at $z = i$ (its terms equal $1$).

### The identity theorem

**Theorem 2 (identity theorem).** Let $f$ be holomorphic on a domain $D$. If the zero set of $f$ has an accumulation point in $D$ (a point $b \in D$ that is a limit of zeros of $f$ different from $b$), then $f \equiv 0$ on $D$.

*Proof.* Let $A = \{z \in D : f^{(n)}(z) = 0 \text{ for all } n \ge 0\}$. It is closed in $D$, because each $f^{(n)}$ is continuous. It is open: if $z \in A$, Theorem 1 shows $f = 0$ on a disk around $z$, so all derivatives vanish at every point of the disk. Let $b$ be an accumulation point of zeros. By continuity $f(b) = 0$. Suppose $b \notin A$ and let $m \ge 1$ be the least index with $f^{(m)}(b) \ne 0$. Then the Taylor series gives $f(z) = (z - b)^mg(z)$ with $g(z) = \sum_{n \ge m}\frac{f^{(n)}(b)}{n!}(z - b)^{n-m}$ holomorphic near $b$ and $g(b) \ne 0$, so $g \ne 0$ near $b$ and $f$ has no zero in a punctured disk around $b$, contradicting accumulation. So $b \in A$, $A$ is nonempty, open and closed in the connected set $D$, and $A = D$. $\blacksquare$

This finishes the proof of the maximum modulus principle in Lesson 7 (constant on a disk implies constant on $D$). It also says that identities between holomorphic functions extend from small sets: if two holomorphic functions on a domain agree on a set with an accumulation point inside the domain, they agree everywhere. For example $\sin^2z + \cos^2z - 1$ is entire and vanishes on $\mathbb{R}$, so it vanishes on $\mathbb{C}$. The hypothesis "accumulation point in $D$" is essential: $\sin(\pi z)$ is entire and vanishes at all integers, which have no accumulation point.

### Laurent series

An **annulus** is $A = \{r < |z - a| < R\}$ with $0 \le r < R \le \infty$.

**Theorem 3 (Laurent).** If $f$ is holomorphic on $A$, then
$$f(z) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}c_n(z - a)^n, \qquad c_n = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|w - a| = \rho}\frac{f(w)}{(w - a)^{n+1}}\,dw,$$
for every $z \in A$ and any $\rho \in (r, R)$. The series converges absolutely and uniformly on compact subsets of $A$, and the coefficients are unique: if $f(z) = \sum b_n(z - a)^n$ on $A$ with such convergence, then $b_n = c_n$.

*Proof of uniqueness.* Integrate $f(w)/(w - a)^{k+1} = \sum b_n(w - a)^{n-k-1}$ termwise over $|w - a| = \rho$ (uniform convergence on the circle and the ML lemma allow this). By Theorem 3 of Lesson 5 only $n = k$ survives, giving $\oint = 2\pi ib_k$. So $b_k = c_k$. *Outline of existence.* For $z \in A$, apply Cauchy's theorem in the annulus (cut it into two simply connected pieces) to $(f(w) - f(z))/(w - z)$ to get $f(z) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|w-a|=R_1}\frac{f(w)}{w - z}dw - \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|w-a|=r_1}\frac{f(w)}{w - z}dw$ for $r < r_1 < |z - a| < R_1 < R$. Expand $\frac{1}{w - z} = \sum_{n \ge 0}\frac{(z-a)^n}{(w-a)^{n+1}}$ on the outer circle and $-\frac{1}{w - z} = \sum_{m \ge 0}\frac{(w-a)^m}{(z-a)^{m+1}}$ on the inner circle; integrating termwise gives the series, with the circle radius changeable by Cauchy's theorem in the annulus. $\blacksquare$

The part $\sum_{n \ge 0}c_n(z - a)^n$ is the **regular part** and $\sum_{n < 0}c_n(z - a)^n$ the **principal part**. Because of uniqueness, **any method** that produces a series of this form on $A$ gives *the* Laurent series. The tools are the geometric series, valid in two regimes, and the known Taylor series:
$$\frac{1}{1 - w} = \sum_{n \ge 0}w^n \ (|w| < 1), \qquad \frac{1}{1 - w} = -\sum_{n \ge 1}w^{-n} \ (|w| > 1).$$
The same function has different Laurent series in different annuli, separated by its singularities.

```python
import math


def laurent_coefficient(f, a, r, k, n=128):
    """Coefficient of (z - a)^k from the n-point trapezoid rule on |z-a|=r."""
    total = 0
    for j in range(n):
        w = r * complex(math.cos(2 * math.pi * j / n),
                        math.sin(2 * math.pi * j / n))
        total += f(a + w) * w ** (-k)
    return total / n


g = lambda z: 1 / (z * (z - 2))
print(round(laurent_coefficient(g, 0, 1, -1).real, 6))
print(abs(laurent_coefficient(g, 0, 3, -1)) < 1e-9)
```

This prints `-0.5` and `True`: in $0 < |z| < 2$ the coefficient of $z^{-1}$ is $-1/2$ and in $|z| > 2$ it is $0$, so the same function has different coefficients in different annuli. The trapezoid rule on a circle is extremely accurate here because the integrand is analytic in an annulus around the circle; the numbers support the computation but are not a proof.

@example
title: Three Laurent series of one function
problem: Find the Laurent series about $0$ of $f(z) = \dfrac{1}{(z - 1)(z - 2)}$ in (a) $|z| < 1$, (b) $1 < |z| < 2$, (c) $|z| > 2$.
step: Partial fractions: $f(z) = \dfrac{1}{z - 2} - \dfrac{1}{z - 1}$. The singularities $1$ and $2$ separate the three regions.
step: (a) For $|z| < 1$: $\dfrac{1}{z - 2} = -\dfrac12\cdot\dfrac{1}{1 - z/2} = -\sum_{n \ge 0}\dfrac{z^n}{2^{n+1}}$ and $-\dfrac{1}{z - 1} = \dfrac{1}{1 - z} = \sum_{n \ge 0}z^n$. So $f(z) = \sum_{n \ge 0}\big(1 - 2^{-n-1}\big)z^n$.
step: (b) For $1 < |z| < 2$: keep $\dfrac{1}{z - 2} = -\sum_{n \ge 0}\dfrac{z^n}{2^{n+1}}$ (valid for $|z| < 2$) but expand $-\dfrac{1}{z - 1} = -\dfrac{1}{z}\cdot\dfrac{1}{1 - 1/z} = -\sum_{n \ge 0}z^{-n-1}$ (valid for $|z| > 1$). So $f(z) = -\sum_{n \ge 0}\dfrac{z^n}{2^{n+1}} - \sum_{n \ge 1}z^{-n}$.
step: (c) For $|z| > 2$: $\dfrac{1}{z - 2} = \dfrac{1}{z}\cdot\dfrac{1}{1 - 2/z} = \sum_{n \ge 0}2^nz^{-n-1}$ and $-\dfrac{1}{z - 1} = -\sum_{n \ge 0}z^{-n-1}$. So $f(z) = \sum_{n \ge 0}(2^n - 1)z^{-n-1}$.
step: By uniqueness each series is the Laurent series in its annulus. Only (b) has both positive and negative powers.
answer: (a) $\sum_{n\ge0}(1 - 2^{-n-1})z^n$; (b) $-\sum_{n\ge0}2^{-n-1}z^n - \sum_{n\ge1}z^{-n}$; (c) $\sum_{n\ge0}(2^n - 1)z^{-n-1}$.

@example
title: An essential singularity's Laurent series
problem: Find the Laurent series of $z^2e^{1/z}$ in $0 < |z| < \infty$ and the coefficient of $z^{-1}$.
step: Substitute $w = 1/z$ in the exponential series, valid for all $w$: $e^{1/z} = \sum_{n \ge 0}\dfrac{z^{-n}}{n!}$.
step: Multiply by $z^2$: $z^2e^{1/z} = \sum_{n \ge 0}\dfrac{z^{2-n}}{n!} = z^2 + z + \dfrac12 + \dfrac{1}{6z} + \dfrac{1}{24z^2} + \cdots$.
step: The exponent $2 - n$ equals $-1$ when $n = 3$, so the coefficient of $z^{-1}$ is $1/3! = 1/6$.
step: Infinitely many negative powers appear, so the principal part does not terminate.
answer: $z^2e^{1/z} = \sum_{n\ge0}z^{2-n}/n!$, and the coefficient of $z^{-1}$ is $1/6$.

@mistakes
- Using one Laurent series for the whole plane minus the singularities. The expansion depends on the annulus; the three series of $1/((z-1)(z-2))$ are all correct, each in its own region.
- Expanding $\frac{1}{1-w}$ as $\sum w^n$ when $|w| > 1$. Rewrite with $1/w$ so that the ratio has modulus less than $1$.
- Thinking the radius of convergence is the distance to the nearest *real* singularity: $1/(1 + x^2)$ has no real singularity, and the radius at $0$ is $1$, set by $\pm i$.
- Applying the identity theorem to zeros that accumulate only at the boundary or outside the domain, such as the zeros $1/n$ of $\sin(1/z)$, which accumulate at $0$, where the function is not defined.

model:
```python
import math


def reconstruction_error(f, a, r, kmin, kmax, n=128):
    """Largest error on |z - a| = r of the Laurent sum over kmin..kmax."""
    ws = [r * complex(math.cos(2 * math.pi * j / n),
                      math.sin(2 * math.pi * j / n)) for j in range(n)]
    coeffs = {}
    for k in range(kmin, kmax + 1):
        coeffs[k] = sum(f(a + w) * w ** (-k) for w in ws) / n
    worst = 0.0
    for w in ws:
        partial = sum(c * w ** k for k, c in coeffs.items())
        worst = max(worst, abs(f(a + w) - partial))
    return worst
```
hint: Compute the dictionary of coefficients first, with the formula from the lesson, and only then compare the partial sum with `f` at every sample point.
hint: Raise `w` to the power `k` for negative `k` too: Python's `**` handles negative integer exponents for complex numbers.
solution: First compute every coefficient $c_k$ in the range by the trapezoid rule on the circle, then rebuild $\sum c_kw^k$ at each sample point and take the largest deviation from $f$. Truncating the range at `kmin = 0` throws away the principal part, and the error stays near $0.5$ on the circles where $c_{-1} \ne 0$; keeping `-1` or more negative powers repairs it, and on the circle $|z| = 1/2$ about $0$ the function is holomorphic so a Taylor range suffices. Small error shows that these coefficients describe `f` on that circle but does not prove a series identity for every point of the annulus.
