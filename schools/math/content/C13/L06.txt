@article
Lesson 5 left a puzzle: $\oint_{|z|=1} z^n\,dz$ is $0$ for every integer $n \ne -1$ and $2\pi i$ for $n = -1$. The functions $z^n$ with $n \ge 0$ are holomorphic inside the circle, and $z^{-1}$ is not. Is holomorphy inside the contour what makes closed integrals vanish? Yes, and the proof is one of the great theorems of the subject.

### Cauchy's theorem for a disk

**Lemma 1 (Goursat).** If $f$ is holomorphic on an open set containing a closed triangle $\Delta$, then $\oint_{\partial\Delta} f\,dz = 0$.

*Proof.* Let $I = |\oint_{\partial\Delta} f|$, with diameter $d$ and perimeter $p$ of $\Delta$. Joining the midpoints of the sides cuts $\Delta$ into four similar triangles; the integrals over their boundaries (all oriented the same way) add up to $\oint_{\partial\Delta} f$, because each interior edge is traversed once in each direction. So one of the four, $\Delta_1$, has $|\oint_{\partial\Delta_1} f| \ge I/4$. Repeating gives nested triangles $\Delta_n$ with $|\oint_{\partial\Delta_n} f| \ge I/4^n$, diameter $d/2^n$ and perimeter $p/2^n$. By compactness there is a point $z^*$ in all of them. Since $f$ is differentiable at $z^*$, $f(z) = f(z^*) + f'(z^*)(z - z^*) + \eta(z)(z - z^*)$ with $\eta(z) \to 0$ as $z \to z^*$. The linear part has a primitive, so its closed integral is $0$ (Theorem 2 of Lesson 5), and the ML lemma gives $I/4^n \le \sup_{\Delta_n}|\eta|\cdot\frac{d}{2^n}\cdot\frac{p}{2^n}$. Hence $I \le dp\sup_{\Delta_n}|\eta| \to 0$, so $I = 0$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 1 (Cauchy's theorem for a disk).** If $f$ is holomorphic on an open disk $D$, then $\oint_\gamma f\,dz = 0$ for every closed contour $\gamma$ in $D$.

*Proof.* Let $c$ be the center of $D$ and define $F(z) = \int_{[c, z]} f(w)\,dw$ along the segment. For $z, z + h \in D$ the triangle with vertices $c, z, z + h$ lies in $D$ (a disk is convex), so by the Lemma $F(z + h) - F(z) = \int_{[z, z+h]} f(w)\,dw$. Since $\int_{[z, z+h]}dw = h$,
$$\left|\frac{F(z+h) - F(z)}{h} - f(z)\right| = \frac{1}{|h|}\left|\int_{[z, z+h]}(f(w) - f(z))\,dw\right| \le \sup_{w \in [z, z+h]}|f(w) - f(z)| \to 0$$
by the ML lemma and continuity of $f$. So $F' = f$ on $D$, and Theorem 2 of Lesson 5 gives $\oint_\gamma f = 0$. $\blacksquare$

**Lemma 2 (one exceptional point).** The same conclusion holds if $g$ is continuous on $D$ and holomorphic on $D\setminus\{p\}$. *(Outline.)* Take a triangle with $p$ as a vertex; cut off a small triangle at $p$ with perimeter $\varepsilon$. The remaining pieces are triangles on which $g$ is holomorphic, so Lemma 1 applies, while the small triangle contributes at most $\sup|g|\cdot\varepsilon \to 0$. A triangle with $p$ inside or on an edge splits into triangles having $p$ as a vertex. The rest of the proof of Theorem 1 is unchanged.

### Cauchy's integral formula

**Theorem 2 (Cauchy's integral formula).** Let $f$ be holomorphic on an open set $U$ containing the closed disk $\overline{D}(a, r)$, and let $|z_0 - a| < r$. Then
$$f(z_0) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{C(a, r)}\frac{f(z)}{z - z_0}\,dz,$$
with the circle traversed counterclockwise.

*Proof.* Since $\overline{D}(a, r)$ is compact and $U$ is open, $U$ contains a larger disk $D(a, r')$, $r' > r$. Define $g(z) = \dfrac{f(z) - f(z_0)}{z - z_0}$ for $z \ne z_0$ and $g(z_0) = f'(z_0)$. Then $g$ is continuous on $D(a, r')$ and holomorphic except at $z_0$, so $\oint_{C(a,r)}g = 0$ by Lemma 2. Therefore $\oint\frac{f(z)}{z - z_0}dz = f(z_0)\oint\frac{dz}{z - z_0}$. It remains to show $\oint_{C(a,r)}\frac{dz}{z - z_0} = 2\pi i$. For $z$ on the circle, $q = |z_0 - a|/r < 1$ and $\dfrac{1}{z - z_0} = \dfrac{1}{(z - a)\big(1 - \frac{z_0 - a}{z - a}\big)} = \sum_{n \ge 0}\dfrac{(z_0 - a)^n}{(z - a)^{n+1}}$, a series whose terms are bounded by $q^n/r$, so it converges uniformly on the circle. By the ML lemma we may integrate termwise, and Theorem 3 of Lesson 5 gives $2\pi i$ from the term $n = 0$ and $0$ from the others. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 3 (derivatives).** Under the hypotheses of Theorem 2, $f$ has complex derivatives of all orders in $D(a, r)$, and for $z_0 \in D(a, r)$ and $k \ge 0$,
$$f^{(k)}(z_0) = \frac{k!}{2\pi i}\oint_{C(a,r)}\frac{f(z)}{(z - z_0)^{k+1}}\,dz.$$

*Proof.* Choose $r < r'' < r'$ as above. For $|w - a| < r''$ the formula of Theorem 2 on $C(a, r'')$ and the expansion $\frac{1}{z - w} = \sum_n\frac{(w - a)^n}{(z - a)^{n+1}}$ (uniform for $z \in C(a, r'')$) give $f(w) = \sum_n c_n(w - a)^n$ with $c_n = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{C(a,r'')}\frac{f(z)}{(z - a)^{n+1}}dz$. So $f$ is a power series of radius at least $r''$ and, by Theorem 2 of Lesson 4, differentiable to all orders. The series converges uniformly on $C(a, r)$, so we may integrate $f(z)/(z - z_0)^{k+1}$ termwise; for each polynomial $(z - a)^n$, expand about $z_0$ and use $\oint(z - z_0)^m\,dz = 0$ for $m \ne -1$ and $2\pi i$ for $m = -1$ (shown above and in Lesson 5), which yields $\frac{2\pi i}{k!}$ times the $k$-th derivative of $(z - a)^n$ at $z_0$. Summing over $n$ gives the formula. $\blacksquare$

Here is the formula checked numerically:

```python
import math


def circle_integral(f, c, r, n):
    """Trapezoid rule with n points for the integral of f over C(c, r)."""
    total = 0
    for j in range(n):
        z = c + r * complex(math.cos(2 * math.pi * j / n),
                            math.sin(2 * math.pi * j / n))
        total += f(z) * (z - c)
    return 2j * math.pi * total / n


f = lambda z: z * z + 1
value = circle_integral(lambda z: f(z) / (z - 0.5), 0, 1, 64) / (2j * math.pi)
print(round(value.real, 6))
```

This prints `1.25`, the value $f(0.5) = 1.25$. For a periodic integrand the trapezoid rule on a circle converges very fast, but a number is not a proof.

@example
title: Cauchy's integral formula
problem: Evaluate $\oint_{|z|=2}\dfrac{e^z}{z - 1}\,dz$, with the circle counterclockwise.
step: Identify $f(z) = e^z$, entire, and $z_0 = 1$, which lies inside the circle $|z| = 2$.
step: Hypotheses: $f$ is holomorphic on $\mathbb{C} \supset \overline{D}(0, 2)$ and $|z_0| = 1 < 2$.
step: Apply Theorem 2: $\oint_{|z|=2}\dfrac{e^z}{z - 1}\,dz = 2\pi i\,f(1) = 2\pi ie$.
answer: $2\pi ie$.

@example
title: A higher-order pole by the derivative formula
problem: Evaluate $\oint_{|z|=3}\dfrac{\cos z}{(z - i)^3}\,dz$.
step: Here $f(z) = \cos z$ is entire, $z_0 = i$ with $|i| = 1 < 3$, and the exponent is $k + 1 = 3$, so $k = 2$.
step: Theorem 3 gives $\oint\dfrac{f(z)}{(z - z_0)^3}dz = \dfrac{2\pi i}{2!}f''(z_0)$.
step: Compute $f'' = -\cos z$, so $f''(i) = -\cos i = -\cosh 1$ (because $\cos(iy) = \cosh y$).
step: The integral is $\pi i\cdot(-\cosh 1) = -\pi i\cosh 1$.
answer: $-\pi i\cosh 1 \approx -4.848i$.

@mistakes
- Using the formula with $z_0$ outside the circle. If $z_0$ is outside, $f(z)/(z - z_0)$ is holomorphic on a disk containing the circle and the integral is $0$ by Cauchy's theorem.
- Forgetting the factor $\dfrac{k!}{2\pi i}$, or using the exponent $k$ instead of $k + 1$ in $(z - z_0)^{k+1}$.
- Applying the formula when $f$ itself has a singularity inside the circle. The numerator must be holomorphic on a neighborhood of the closed disk; split with partial fractions to isolate each singularity first.
- Reversing the orientation: for a clockwise circle the integral has the opposite sign.

model:
```python
import math


def cif_derivative(f, z0, c, r, k, n=64):
    """k-th derivative of f at z0 from Cauchy's integral formula."""
    total = 0
    for j in range(n):
        z = c + r * complex(math.cos(2 * math.pi * j / n),
                            math.sin(2 * math.pi * j / n))
        total += f(z) / (z - z0) ** (k + 1) * (z - c)
    return math.factorial(k) * total / n
```
hint: Since $dz = i(z - c)\,d\theta$ on the circle, $\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint g\,dz \approx \frac{1}{n}\sum_j g(z_j)(z_j - c)$.
hint: Multiply by `math.factorial(k)` at the end; the exponent in the denominator is `k + 1`.
solution: Sample the integrand at the $n$ points, multiply by $(z_j - c)$ (which accounts for $dz/(i\,d\theta)$), average, and multiply by $k!$. Forgetting the factor $(z_j - c)$, the $k!$ or the exponent $k + 1$ gives wrong values on the tests with $k = 2$ and $k = 5$. The result agrees with the true derivative to rounding because the trapezoid rule on a circle converges geometrically for functions holomorphic on an annulus around the circle.
