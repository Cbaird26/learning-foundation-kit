@article
The function $\sin x$ is differentiable everywhere on $\mathbb{R}$ and bounded by $1$. Can a function be differentiable at every point of the complex plane, bounded, and still not constant? For real functions the answer is yes. For complex functions the answer is no, and this one theorem proves the fundamental theorem of algebra: every nonconstant polynomial has a complex root.

### Cauchy's estimates and Liouville's theorem

**Theorem 1 (Cauchy's estimates).** Let $f$ be holomorphic on an open set containing $\overline{D}(a, R)$ and suppose $|f(z)| \le M$ on the circle $|z - a| = R$. Then for every $n \ge 0$,
$$|f^{(n)}(a)| \le \frac{n!\,M}{R^n}.$$

*Proof.* By Theorem 3 of Lesson 6 with $z_0 = a$, $f^{(n)}(a) = \dfrac{n!}{2\pi i}\oint_{C(a,R)}\dfrac{f(z)}{(z - a)^{n+1}}dz$. On the circle the integrand has modulus at most $M/R^{n+1}$ and the circle has length $2\pi R$. The ML lemma gives $|f^{(n)}(a)| \le \dfrac{n!}{2\pi}\cdot\dfrac{M}{R^{n+1}}\cdot 2\pi R = \dfrac{n!\,M}{R^n}$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 2 (Liouville).** If $f$ is entire and there is $M$ with $|f(z)| \le M$ for all $z \in \mathbb{C}$, then $f$ is constant.

*Proof.* Fix $a \in \mathbb{C}$. For every $R > 0$, Theorem 1 with $n = 1$ gives $|f'(a)| \le M/R$. Letting $R \to \infty$ gives $f'(a) = 0$. So $f' = 0$ on $\mathbb{C}$, and $f$ is constant (Corollary in Lesson 3). $\blacksquare$

The hypotheses matter. Both "entire" and "bounded" are needed: $\sin z$ is entire but unbounded, since $|\sin(iy)| = \sinh y \to \infty$; and $f(z) = z$ is bounded on the open unit disk and not constant, but it is not entire.

### The fundamental theorem of algebra

**Theorem 3.** Every nonconstant polynomial $p(z) = a_nz^n + \dots + a_0$ with complex coefficients ($n \ge 1$, $a_n \ne 0$) has a root in $\mathbb{C}$. Consequently $p(z) = a_n(z - z_1)\cdots(z - z_n)$ for some $z_1, \dots, z_n \in \mathbb{C}$ (not necessarily distinct).

*Proof.* Suppose $p$ has no zero. Then $1/p$ is entire. For $z \ne 0$, $p(z) = a_nz^n\big(1 + \sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{a_{n-k}}{a_n}z^{-k}\big)$, and the sum tends to $0$ as $|z| \to \infty$, so there is $R_0$ such that $|p(z)| \ge \tfrac12|a_n||z|^n \ge \tfrac12|a_n|R_0^n$ for $|z| \ge R_0$; enlarging $R_0$ we may assume this is at least $1$. Thus $|1/p| \le 1$ for $|z| \ge R_0$. On the compact disk $|z| \le R_0$ the continuous function $1/p$ is bounded. So $1/p$ is bounded and entire, hence constant by Liouville's theorem, and then $p$ is constant, a contradiction. For the factorization, if $p(z_1) = 0$ then dividing $p$ by $z - z_1$ leaves remainder $p(z_1) = 0$, so $p(z) = (z - z_1)q(z)$ with $q$ of degree $n - 1$ and leading coefficient $a_n$; induct on $n$. $\blacksquare$

### The maximum modulus principle

By the mean value property (Lesson 6), if $f$ is holomorphic on an open set containing $\overline{D}(a, \rho)$ then $f(a) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f(a + \rho e^{it})\,dt$.

**Lemma.** If $f$ is holomorphic on a disk and $|f|$ is constant there, then $f$ is constant.

*Proof.* Let $|f|^2 = u^2 + v^2 = c$. If $c = 0$, $f = 0$. Otherwise differentiate in $x$ and in $y$: $uu_x + vv_x = 0$ and $uu_y + vv_y = 0$. By Cauchy–Riemann, $u_y = -v_x$ and $v_y = u_x$, so the second equation is $-uv_x + vu_x = 0$. The linear system $uu_x + vv_x = 0$, $vu_x - uv_x = 0$ in the unknowns $(u_x, v_x)$ has determinant $-(u^2 + v^2) = -c \ne 0$, so $u_x = v_x = 0$, $f' = u_x + iv_x = 0$, and $f$ is constant. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 4 (maximum modulus principle).** Let $f$ be holomorphic on a domain $D$. If $|f|$ has a local maximum at a point $a \in D$ (that is, $|f(z)| \le |f(a)|$ for $z$ near $a$), then $f$ is constant on a neighborhood of $a$, and in fact constant on $D$.

*Proof.* Take $\rho$ small. By the mean value property and the inequality $|\int g| \le \int |g|$ (Lemma 1 of Lesson 5), $|f(a)| \le \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}|f(a + \rho e^{it})|\,dt \le |f(a)|$, so $\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\big(|f(a)| - |f(a + \rho e^{it})|\big)dt = 0$. The integrand is continuous and $\ge 0$, hence identically $0$: $|f| = |f(a)|$ on every small circle around $a$, so on a disk around $a$. By the Lemma, $f$ is constant near $a$. The extension from a disk to the whole connected domain $D$ is the identity theorem, proved in Lesson 8. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary.** Let $D$ be a bounded domain, $f$ continuous on $\overline{D}$ and holomorphic on $D$. Then $\max_{\overline{D}}|f|$ is attained at a point of the boundary $\partial D$. (The maximum exists by compactness; if it were attained only in $D$, Theorem 4 would make $f$ constant on $D$, hence on $\overline{D}$ by continuity, and then the maximum is also attained on $\partial D$.) If $f$ has no zeros on $\overline{D}$, apply this to $1/f$: the minimum of $|f|$ is also on the boundary. (If $f$ has no zeros in $D$ but vanishes somewhere on $\partial D$, the minimum $0$ is attained there, so the conclusion still holds.)

```python
def horner(coeffs, z):
    """Value and derivative of the polynomial with coefficients listed
    from the highest power down, at the point z."""
    p, dp = 0, 0
    for c in coeffs:
        dp = dp * z + p
        p = p * z + c
    return p, dp


print(horner([1, 0, 1], 2j))
```

This prints `((-3+0j), 4j)`: the value $p(2i) = (2i)^2 + 1 = -3$ and the derivative $p'(2i) = 2\cdot 2i = 4i$.

@example
title: Growth bounds force a polynomial
problem: Let $f$ be entire with $|f(z)| \le 2|z|^3 + 1$ for all $z$. Prove that $f$ is a polynomial of degree at most $3$.
step: Fix $n \ge 4$. For $R > 0$ the maximum of $|f|$ on $|z| = R$ is at most $M(R) = 2R^3 + 1$. Cauchy's estimates at $a = 0$ give $|f^{(n)}(0)| \le \dfrac{n!(2R^3 + 1)}{R^n}$.
step: For $n \ge 4$, $\dfrac{2R^3 + 1}{R^n} = 2R^{3-n} + R^{-n} \to 0$ as $R \to \infty$. Hence $f^{(n)}(0) = 0$ for every $n \ge 4$.
step: An entire function equals its Taylor series at $0$ on all of $\mathbb{C}$ (Theorem 3 of Lesson 6 gives radius at least $R$ for every $R$): $f(z) = \sum_n \dfrac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n!}z^n$.
step: Only the terms with $n \le 3$ survive, so $f(z) = c_0 + c_1z + c_2z^2 + c_3z^3$.
answer: $f$ is a polynomial of degree at most $3$.

@example
title: A non-negative real part
problem: Prove that if $f$ is entire and $\operatorname{Re} f(z) \ge 0$ for all $z$, then $f$ is constant.
step: Define $g(z) = e^{-f(z)}$. It is entire, as a composition of entire functions.
step: Bound it: $|g(z)| = e^{-\operatorname{Re} f(z)} \le e^0 = 1$ because $|e^w| = e^{\operatorname{Re} w}$ (Lesson 4) and $\operatorname{Re} f \ge 0$.
step: By Liouville's theorem $g$ is constant, so $g' = -f'e^{-f} = 0$ on $\mathbb{C}$.
step: Since $e^{-f} \ne 0$, $f' = 0$ everywhere, and $f$ is constant (Corollary in Lesson 3).
answer: $f$ is constant.

@mistakes
- Applying Liouville's theorem to a function that is bounded but not entire, such as $z$ on the unit disk or $1/(1 + z^2)$ on $\{|z| > 2\}$. Both hypotheses are needed.
- Believing that "bounded on $\mathbb{R}$" is enough: $\sin z$ is bounded on the real axis and entire but unbounded on $\mathbb{C}$.
- Applying the maximum modulus principle to a function that is not holomorphic. The real-valued function $f(z) = 2 - |z|^2$ is nonconstant, yet $|f|$ has its maximum over the unit disk at the interior point $0$.
- Forgetting that the maximum principle needs a bounded region (or at least the closure to be compact) to guarantee the maximum exists: $|e^z|$ on the half-plane $\{\operatorname{Re} z \ge 0\}$ has no maximum.

model:
```python
def newton_root(coeffs, z0, tol=1e-12, max_iter=50):
    """Newton's method for a polynomial; returns (root, updates used)."""
    z = z0
    for steps in range(max_iter + 1):
        p, dp = 0, 0
        for c in coeffs:
            dp = dp * z + p
            p = p * z + c
        if abs(p) < tol:
            return z, steps
        if steps == max_iter:
            break
        if dp == 0:
            raise ValueError("zero derivative")
        z = z - p / dp
    raise RuntimeError("no convergence")
```
hint: In Horner's rule update `dp` before `p` inside the loop, using the old value of `p`.
hint: Test `abs(p) < tol` before updating, so a starting point that is already a root uses zero steps.
solution: Evaluate $p$ and $p'$ together with Horner's rule (the derivative update must use the previous value of `p`), return as soon as $|p| <$ `tol`, and otherwise take a Newton step. Checking convergence before the step gives `steps == 0` for a start that is already a root. A start at $0$ for $z^2 + 1$ has $p'(0) = 0$, the case that must raise `ValueError`. That Newton's method finds the roots whose existence the theorem guarantees is evidence, not a proof of convergence from every start.
