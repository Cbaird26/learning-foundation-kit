@article
The determinant packs one number into a square matrix, and that number answers several questions at once: is the matrix invertible, how does the transformation change areas and volumes, and does it flip orientation?

### The 2 by 2 case

$$\det\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix} = ad - bc.$$
It is the signed area of the parallelogram whose sides are the columns $(a, c)$ and $(b, d)$. The sign is negative when the columns come in clockwise order.

### Three defining properties

For $n \times n$ matrices the determinant is the one function with these properties:

1. $\det I = 1$;
2. swapping two rows changes the sign of $\det$;
3. $\det$ is linear in each row separately (scaling one row scales $\det$ by the same factor).

From them follow the rules you actually use:

- adding a multiple of one row to another does **not** change $\det$;
- a zero row, or two equal rows, forces $\det = 0$;
- for a triangular matrix, $\det$ is the product of the diagonal entries;
- $\det(AB) = \det A \cdot \det B$ and $\det A^T = \det A$;
- $\det(cA) = c^n \det A$ for an $n \times n$ matrix.

### Computing

**Elimination.** Reduce to upper triangular form using row swaps and row additions. Then $\det A = (-1)^s \times (\text{product of pivots})$, where $s$ is the number of swaps. This is what software does.

**Cofactors.** Expanding along row $i$,
$$\det A = \sum_{j} (-1)^{i+j}\, a_{ij}\, M_{ij},$$
where $M_{ij}$ is the determinant of the matrix with row $i$ and column $j$ deleted. The signs form a checkerboard starting with $+$ in the top left corner. Expand along a row or column with many zeros.

### What the number tells you

- $\det A \neq 0$ exactly when $A$ is invertible (all $n$ pivots nonzero), and then $\det A^{-1} = 1/\det A$.
- The map $\mathbf{x} \mapsto A\mathbf{x}$ multiplies areas (volumes in 3D) by $|\det A|$. A negative determinant means orientation is reversed.

### Same transformation, two bases

If $A$ is the matrix of $T$ in the standard basis and $B = P^{-1}AP$ is its matrix in another basis, then
$$\det B = \det P^{-1} \cdot \det A \cdot \det P = \det A.$$
The determinant belongs to the transformation, not to the basis you picked. The trace (the sum of the diagonal entries) has the same property. That makes it a useful check on any change-of-basis computation.

```python
import numpy as np
A = np.array([[4., 1.], [2., 3.]])
P = np.array([[1., 1.], [0., 1.]])      # columns are the new basis vectors
B = np.linalg.inv(P) @ A @ P
print(B.round(6).tolist(), round(np.linalg.det(A), 6), round(np.linalg.det(B), 6))
# approximately [[2, 0], [2, 5]], and the determinant is 10.0 for both
```

@example
title: A determinant with a row swap
problem: Compute $\det A$ for $A = \begin{bmatrix}0&2&1\\1&1&1\\2&-1&3\end{bmatrix}$ by elimination, then check by cofactors.
step: The top-left entry is $0$, so swap rows $1$ and $2$. This costs a factor of $-1$. The matrix is now $\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\\0&2&1\\2&-1&3\end{bmatrix}$.
step: Apply $R_3 \to R_3 - 2R_1$ to get $[0\ -3\ 1]$. Then $R_3 \to R_3 + \tfrac32 R_2$ gives $[0\ 0\ \tfrac52]$. Neither step changes the determinant.
step: The pivots are $1, 2, \tfrac52$ with product $5$. With one swap, $\det A = -5$.
step: Cofactor check along row 1 of the original matrix: $\det A = 0\cdot M_{11} - 2 M_{12} + 1 \cdot M_{13}$, with $M_{12} = \det\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\2&3\end{bmatrix} = 1$ and $M_{13} = \det\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\2&-1\end{bmatrix} = -3$. So $\det A = -2 - 3 = -5$.

@example
title: One transformation in two bases
problem: A transformation has standard matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{bmatrix}$. Find its matrix $B$ in the basis $\mathbf{b}_1 = (1, 0)$, $\mathbf{b}_2 = (1, 1)$, and compare the determinants.
step: The basis vectors are the columns of $P = \begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$, and $P^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$.
step: Compute $AP$. Its columns are $A\mathbf{b}_1 = (4, 2)$ and $A\mathbf{b}_2 = (5, 5)$, so $AP = \begin{bmatrix}4&5\\2&5\end{bmatrix}$.
step: Multiply by $P^{-1}$: $B = P^{-1}AP = \begin{bmatrix}4-2&5-5\\2&5\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}2&0\\2&5\end{bmatrix}$.
step: Check column 1 directly: $T(\mathbf{b}_1) = (4, 2) = 2\mathbf{b}_1 + 2\mathbf{b}_2$, so the first column of $B$ is $(2, 2)$.
step: Compare: $\det A = 12 - 2 = 10$ and $\det B = 10 - 0 = 10$. The traces agree too: $4 + 3 = 7 = 2 + 5$.

@mistakes
- Using $\det(A + B) = \det A + \det B$. The determinant is multiplicative, not additive.
- Forgetting the sign change when you swap rows, or forgetting to include it in the final answer.
- Writing $\det(2A) = 2\det A$ for an $n \times n$ matrix. Each of the $n$ rows picks up a factor $2$, so $\det(2A) = 2^n \det A$.

@practice num
prompt: Compute $\det\begin{bmatrix}0&1&2\\1&0&3\\4&-3&8\end{bmatrix}$.
answer: -2
check: det([[0,1,2],[1,0,3],[4,-3,8]])
hint: Expand along the first row. One of its entries is $0$, so only two cofactors are needed.
solution: $\det = 0 \cdot M_{11} - 1\cdot\det\begin{bmatrix}1&3\\4&8\end{bmatrix} + 2\cdot\det\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\4&-3\end{bmatrix} = -(8 - 12) + 2(-3) = 4 - 6 = -2$.

@practice num
prompt: A $3 \times 3$ matrix $A$ has $\det A = 4$. What is $\det(2A)$?
answer: 32
check: det(2 * np.diag([1, 2, 2]))
hint: Scaling the whole matrix by $2$ scales each of the three rows by $2$.
solution: Each row contributes a factor $2$, so $\det(2A) = 2^3 \det A = 8 \cdot 4 = 32$.

@quiz num
prompt: The vectors $(1, 2, 0)$, $(0, 1, 3)$ and $(2, 0, 1)$ are the edges of a parallelepiped. What is its volume?
answer: 13
check: abs(dot(cross([1,2,0],[0,1,3]), [2,0,1]))
hint: The volume is $|\det|$ of the matrix whose rows (or columns) are the three vectors.
solution: $\det\begin{bmatrix}1&2&0\\0&1&3\\2&0&1\end{bmatrix} = 1(1 - 0) - 2(0 - 6) + 0 = 1 + 12 = 13$, so the volume is $13$.

@quiz self
prompt: Let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix and $P$ an invertible matrix. Prove that $\det(P^{-1}AP) = \det A$, and explain in one sentence what this says about a linear transformation described in two different bases.
hint: Use the product rule for determinants.
hint: Recall that $\det(P^{-1}) = 1/\det P$.
solution: By the product rule, $\det(P^{-1}AP) = \det(P^{-1})\det(A)\det(P)$. Since $\det(P^{-1})\det(P) = \det(P^{-1}P) = \det I = 1$, the product equals $\det A$. So every matrix representing the same transformation, in any basis, has the same determinant: the determinant is a property of the transformation itself and not of the coordinates.
rubric: Applies $\det(XYZ) = \det X \det Y \det Z$ to the three factors.
rubric: Shows $\det P^{-1} \det P = 1$ (or $\det P^{-1} = 1/\det P$, with $\det P \ne 0$ because $P$ is invertible).
rubric: States the interpretation: the determinant is the same in every basis, because it measures the transformation's volume scaling.
