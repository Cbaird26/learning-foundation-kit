@project
title: A coupled two-tank model: exact and numerical evolution
overview: Two connected tanks exchange a dissolved substance, and each tank also loses some of it. You will write this as $\mathbf{u}' = A\mathbf{u}$, diagonalize $A$ to solve it exactly, and compare the exact solution with a numerical simulation. You will then change the model so that $A$ is not diagonalizable and see exactly what breaks.
goal: Diagonalize a $2 \times 2$ coupled linear model and use the eigenbasis to write the exact solution.
goal: Compare the analytic solution with forward Euler for several step sizes, and explain the error and the stability limit in terms of eigenvalues.
goal: Treat a non-diagonalizable variant correctly and present the model, method and results clearly.
step: Set up the model. Let $x(t)$ and $y(t)$ be the amounts of substance in tank 1 and tank 2, with time $t$ in minutes. Use $x' = -3x + y$ and $y' = 2x - 2y$ (the coefficients are rates per minute) with $\mathbf{u}(0) = (1, 0)$. Write the matrix $A$ and explain in words what each entry means physically, including why the signs are what they are.
step: Find the eigenvalues from $\lambda^2 - (\operatorname{tr}A)\lambda + \det A = 0$ and the eigenvectors from $N(A - \lambda I)$. Check each pair with $A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$, check that the eigenvalues add to the trace and multiply to the determinant, and write $A = PDP^{-1}$.
step: Expand $\mathbf{u}(0)$ in the eigenbasis by solving $P\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{u}(0)$. Write the exact solution $\mathbf{u}(t) = c_1e^{\lambda_1t}\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2e^{\lambda_2t}\mathbf{v}_2$ and verify that it satisfies the initial condition and both differential equations. Describe the fast mode, the slow mode and the long-time direction.
step: In Python, implement forward Euler, $\mathbf{u}_{k+1} = (I + hA)\mathbf{u}_k$, for $h = 0.2, 0.1, 0.05, 0.01$ up to $t = 2$. Evaluate the exact solution with NumPy as `P @ np.diag(np.exp(lam * t)) @ np.linalg.inv(P) @ u0` and tabulate the error at $t = 2$ for each $h$. Describe how the error changes when $h$ is halved.
step: Explain stability. Forward Euler multiplies the $j$-th eigencomponent by $1 + h\lambda_j$ at every step, so it decays only if $|1 + h\lambda_j| < 1$. Compute the largest stable $h$ for your eigenvalues, then run $h = 0.6$ and compare the behavior with your prediction.
step: Change basis. Define $\mathbf{z} = P^{-1}\mathbf{u}$ and show that the model decouples into $z_1' = \lambda_1z_1$ and $z_2' = \lambda_2z_2$. Sketch the $(x, y)$ plane with the eigen-directions and the trajectory, and explain how the same motion looks in standard coordinates and in eigen-coordinates.
step: Break the model. Replace $A$ by $A' = \begin{bmatrix}-1&1\\0&-1\end{bmatrix}$, which has a repeated eigenvalue and only one eigenvector. Show that no $P$ exists, solve the second equation first to find $\mathbf{u}(t) = e^{-t}(x_0 + ty_0,\ y_0)$, and compare with Euler.
step: Prepare a 10-minute presentation of the model, the method and the results. Practice explaining one major result aloud without notes.

deliverable: A Python script that reproduces your error table and at least one plot comparing the exact and numerical solutions.
deliverable: A 10-minute presentation (slides or notes) that you can give aloud without reading from a script.

@review
### Week by week

**Weeks 1 and 2: systems and matrices.** Elimination uses row swaps, scaling and adding multiples of rows. A row $[0\ \cdots\ 0 \mid c]$ with $c \ne 0$ means no solution; a pivot-free column is a free variable and gives infinitely many solutions; $\operatorname{rank}A = \operatorname{rank}[A \mid \mathbf{b}] = n$ means a unique solution. Products are row times column, $AB \ne BA$, $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$, $(AB)^T = B^TA^T$, and $A^{-1}$ comes from row reducing $[A \mid I]$.

**Weeks 3 and 4: spaces.** A subspace contains $\mathbf{0}$ and is closed under sums and multiples. $C(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$ and $N(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$. A basis is independent and spanning, and the dimension is its size. Use the pivot columns of the **original** $A$ for $C(A)$, special solutions for $N(A)$, and $\dim C(A) + \dim N(A) = n$.

**Weeks 5 and 6: maps, bases, determinants.** The columns of a matrix are the images of the basis vectors, and "first $T$, then $S$" is $BA$. In a basis $\mathcal{B}$ the matrix is $P^{-1}AP$. Determinants: row-operation rules, $\det(AB) = \det A\det B$, $\det A = 0$ exactly when $A$ is singular, $|\det A|$ is the volume factor, and $\det$ is the same in every basis.

**Weeks 7 and 8: eigenvalues.** Solve $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$, then $N(A - \lambda I)$; check with trace and determinant. If there are $n$ independent eigenvectors, $A = PDP^{-1}$ and $A^k = PD^kP^{-1}$. A matrix is defective when an eigenspace is smaller than the multiplicity of its eigenvalue.

**Weeks 9 and 10: orthogonality.** $\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|}$, Cauchy-Schwarz, and $N(A) = C(A^T)^\perp$. The projection onto $C(A)$ is $A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T$. The normal equations $A^TA\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T\mathbf{b}$ make the residual orthogonal to $C(A)$. Gram-Schmidt gives $A = QR$.

**Weeks 11 and 12: spectral theorem and SVD.** Symmetric and Hermitian matrices have real eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenvectors, $A = Q\Lambda Q^T$. Positive definite means all eigenvalues, or all leading minors, are positive. The SVD $A = U\Sigma V^T$ exists for every matrix, with $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i(A^TA)}$.

### Self-check

- I can reduce a system to echelon form and say whether it has one, no or infinitely many solutions.
- I can test a subset for being a subspace and find bases for $C(A)$ and $N(A)$.
- I can compute the matrix of a transformation in a new basis and verify it with the determinant and trace.
- I can find eigenpairs, decide diagonalizability and compute $A^k$.
- I can set up and solve the normal equations and interpret the residual.
- I can orthogonally diagonalize a symmetric matrix and compute a $2 \times 2$ SVD.
- I can explain aloud why the coordinates of a vector depend on the basis.

### Most common errors

- Taking pivot columns of the reduced matrix as a basis for $C(A)$.
- Reversing the order in compositions, inverses and transposes.
- Subtracting $\lambda$ from the determinant instead of from the diagonal.
- Assuming a repeated eigenvalue forces a defective matrix, or that any matrix with real entries has real eigenvalues.
- Confusing singular values with eigenvalues.
- Writing the normal equations without the factor $A^T$ on both sides.

@exam tuple
prompt: Two coupled quantities satisfy $x' = -2x + y$ and $y' = x - 2y$ with $x(0) = 1$ and $y(0) = 0$. Diagonalize the coefficient matrix, solve the system, and enter $(x(1), y(1))$ rounded to three decimal places.
answer: (0.209, 0.159)
check: sum(np.linalg.matrix_power(np.array([[-2.,1.],[1.,-2.]]), k) / factorial(k) for k in range(40)) @ np.array([1.,0.])
tol: 0.001
hint: The coefficient matrix is $\begin{bmatrix}-2&1\\1&-2\end{bmatrix}$. It is symmetric, so its eigenvalues are real and its eigenvectors are orthogonal.
hint: The eigenpairs are $\lambda = -1$ with $(1, 1)$ and $\lambda = -3$ with $(1, -1)$. Write the initial vector $(1, 0)$ as a combination of the two eigenvectors.
solution: The eigenpairs are $\lambda_1 = -1$, $\mathbf{v}_1 = (1, 1)$ and $\lambda_2 = -3$, $\mathbf{v}_2 = (1, -1)$. Since $(1, 0) = \tfrac12\mathbf{v}_1 + \tfrac12\mathbf{v}_2$, the solution is $\mathbf{u}(t) = \tfrac12e^{-t}(1, 1) + \tfrac12e^{-3t}(1, -1)$. At $t = 1$, $x = \tfrac12(e^{-1} + e^{-3}) \approx 0.209$ and $y = \tfrac12(e^{-1} - e^{-3}) \approx 0.159$.

@exam self
prompt: Let $A = \begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{b} = (2, 1, 4)$. (a) Show that $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has no solution. (b) Write and solve the normal equations for the least squares solution $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$. (c) Compute the residual $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ and verify that it is orthogonal to $C(A)$. (d) Find a basis for $N(A^T)$ and explain why $\mathbf{r}$ lies in it. (e) Find $\|\mathbf{r}\|^2$ and say what it measures.
hint: For (a), eliminate on $[A \mid \mathbf{b}]$ and look for a row $[0\ 0 \mid c]$ with $c \ne 0$.
hint: For (d), $N(A^T)$ is the set of vectors orthogonal to both columns of $A$. Its dimension is $3 - \operatorname{rank}A$.
solution: (a) Eliminating on $[A \mid \mathbf{b}]$: $R_2 - R_1 = [0\ 1 \mid -1]$ and $R_3 - R_1 = [0\ 2 \mid 2]$, then $R_3 - 2R_2 = [0\ 0 \mid 4]$, a contradiction. (b) $A^TA = \begin{bmatrix}3&3\\3&5\end{bmatrix}$ and $A^T\mathbf{b} = (7, 9)$. Solving gives $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = (\tfrac43, 1)$, the line $y = \tfrac43 + t$. (c) $A\hat{\mathbf{x}} = (\tfrac43, \tfrac73, \tfrac{10}{3})$, so $\mathbf{r} = (\tfrac23, -\tfrac43, \tfrac23)$. Then $A^T\mathbf{r} = (\tfrac23 - \tfrac43 + \tfrac23,\ -\tfrac43 + \tfrac43) = (0, 0)$, so $\mathbf{r}$ is orthogonal to both columns and hence to $C(A)$. (d) $A^T\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{0}$ gives $y_1 + y_2 + y_3 = 0$ and $y_2 + 2y_3 = 0$, so $N(A^T)$ has basis $(1, -2, 1)$. Since $\mathbf{r}$ is orthogonal to $C(A)$ we have $A^T\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{0}$, and indeed $\mathbf{r} = \tfrac23(1, -2, 1)$. (e) $\|\mathbf{r}\|^2 = \tfrac49 + \tfrac{16}{9} + \tfrac49 = \tfrac83$, the smallest possible sum of squared vertical gaps between the data and any line.
rubric: Shows inconsistency by elimination (or by showing $\mathbf{b}$ is not in $C(A)$).
rubric: Writes $A^TA\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T\mathbf{b}$ correctly and obtains $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = (\tfrac43, 1)$.
rubric: Computes $\mathbf{r} = (\tfrac23, -\tfrac43, \tfrac23)$ and verifies $A^T\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{0}$.
rubric: Identifies $N(A^T) = \operatorname{span}\{(1, -2, 1)\} = C(A)^\perp$, explains why $\mathbf{r}$ lies in it, and interprets $\|\mathbf{r}\|^2 = \tfrac83$ as the minimum sum of squared residuals.

@exam num
prompt: Let $M = \begin{bmatrix}2&1&1\\1&2&1\\1&1&2\end{bmatrix}$. Find the sum of all nine entries of $M^6$. You do not need to multiply six times: use an eigenvector of $M$.
answer: 12288
check: np.ones(3) @ np.linalg.matrix_power(np.array([[2,1,1],[1,2,1],[1,1,2]]), 6) @ np.ones(3)
hint: Try the vector $\mathbf{1} = (1, 1, 1)$. What is $M\mathbf{1}$?
hint: $M\mathbf{1} = 4\cdot\mathbf{1}$, so $M^6\mathbf{1} = 4^6\cdot\mathbf{1}$. The sum of all entries of a matrix $B$ equals $\mathbf{1}^TB\mathbf{1}$.
solution: Each row of $M$ sums to $4$, so $M\mathbf{1} = 4\cdot\mathbf{1}$ and $M^6\mathbf{1} = 4^6\cdot\mathbf{1} = 4096\cdot\mathbf{1}$. The sum of all entries of $M^6$ is $\mathbf{1}^TM^6\mathbf{1} = 4096\,\mathbf{1}^T\mathbf{1} = 4096 \cdot 3 = 12288$.
