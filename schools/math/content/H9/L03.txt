@article
**Dynamics** explains why motion changes. The answer is force: a push or pull measured in **newtons** ($1\ \text{N} = 1\ \text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2$).

### The three laws

**First law (inertia).** An object keeps its velocity (including rest) unless a nonzero net force acts on it. Constant velocity means the net force is zero.

**Second law.** The acceleration of an object is proportional to the net force and inversely proportional to its mass:

$$\sum \vec F = m\vec a .$$

The acceleration points in the direction of the net force. Because it is a vector equation, write it separately for each axis: $\sum F_x = ma_x$ and $\sum F_y = ma_y$.

**Third law.** If object A exerts a force on object B, then B exerts an equal and opposite force on A. The two forces of a pair act on **different objects**, so they never cancel in one object's free-body diagram.

### Common forces

- **Weight** $W = mg$, directed down. Mass is the amount of matter (kg); weight is a force (N).
- **Normal force** $N$, perpendicular to a surface, pushing the object away from it. It is not always equal to $mg$.
- **Tension** $T$, along a rope or string, pulling on whatever it is attached to. An ideal light rope has the same tension throughout.

### Free-body diagrams

A **free-body diagram** (FBD) shows one object as a dot or box with an arrow for every force acting on it, and nothing else. Recipe:

1. Choose the object (or system) to analyze.
2. Draw only forces **on** that object: weight, normal, tension, applied pushes.
3. Choose axes, usually along the direction of acceleration.
4. Write $\sum F = ma$ for each axis and solve.

### Multiple objects

For blocks connected by a rope, draw a separate FBD for each, write the second law for each, and link them with the facts that they share the same acceleration magnitude and the rope has one tension. For an **Atwood machine** (two masses over a pulley), with $m_1 > m_2$:

$$a = \frac{(m_1 - m_2)g}{m_1 + m_2}, \qquad T = \frac{2m_1m_2\,g}{m_1 + m_2}.$$

### Apparent weight

A scale reads the normal force. In an elevator accelerating upward at $a$, $N - mg = ma$, so $N = m(g + a)$ and you feel heavier; accelerating downward, $N = m(g - a)$ and you feel lighter. In free fall the reading is zero.

@example
title: Mass hanging from a rope
problem: A $6\ \text{kg}$ mass hangs from a light rope and is accelerated upward at $1.2\ \text{m/s}^2$. Find the tension. Use $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.
step: Draw the FBD of the mass: tension $T$ upward and weight $mg$ downward. Choose up as positive.
step: Write the second law: $T - mg = ma$.
step: Solve: $T = m(g + a) = 6(9.8 + 1.2) = 6(11.0)$.
step: $T = 66\ \text{N}$. Check: it is larger than the weight $58.8\ \text{N}$, as it must be for upward acceleration.

@mistakes
- Putting a force the object exerts on something else into its own free-body diagram, or drawing "the force of motion" as if motion itself were a force.
- Treating the two forces of a third-law pair as canceling; they act on different objects.
- Assuming the normal force always equals $mg$ (it does not on an incline, or when other vertical forces act or the object accelerates vertically).
- Mixing up mass (kg) and weight (N).

@practice num
prompt: A net force of $20\ \text{N}$ acts on a $4\ \text{kg}$ cart on a frictionless track. What is its acceleration in $\text{m/s}^2$?
answer: 5
check: 20 / 4
hint: Use $\sum F = ma$.
hint: Divide the net force by the mass.
solution: $a = F/m = 20/4 = 5\ \text{m/s}^2$.
unit: m/s^2

@practice mc
prompt: A book rests on a table. The Earth pulls down on the book with its weight. According to Newton's third law, what is the reaction force to that weight?
choice: The normal force of the table pushing up on the book
choice*: The book pulling up on the Earth with an equal force
choice: The table pushing down on the floor
choice: There is no reaction force because the book is at rest
hint: A third-law pair acts on two different objects and is the same kind of force.
solution: Gravity is an interaction between book and Earth, so the reaction is the book's gravitational pull on the Earth. The normal force balances the weight, but both act on the book, so they are not a third-law pair.

@practice num
prompt: A $70\ \text{kg}$ person stands on a bathroom scale in an elevator that accelerates upward at $2\ \text{m/s}^2$. What force does the scale read, in newtons? Use $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.
answer: 826
check: 70 * (9.8 + 2)
tol: 0.5
hint: The scale reads the normal force on the person. Choose up as positive.

solution: $N = m(g + a) = 70(9.8 + 2) = 826\ \text{N}$.

@practice num
prompt: A $2\ \text{kg}$ block and a $3\ \text{kg}$ block sit in contact on a frictionless table. A horizontal $10\ \text{N}$ force pushes on the $2\ \text{kg}$ block, which pushes the $3\ \text{kg}$ block. What force does the $2\ \text{kg}$ block exert on the $3\ \text{kg}$ block, in newtons?
answer: 6
check: 3 * (10 / (2 + 3))
hint: Find the acceleration of both blocks together first.
hint: $a = 10/(2+3) = 2\ \text{m/s}^2$. The $3\ \text{kg}$ block is accelerated by only the contact force.
solution: Both blocks accelerate at $a = 10/5 = 2\ \text{m/s}^2$. The contact force on the $3\ \text{kg}$ block is $F = (3)(2) = 6\ \text{N}$.
unit: N

@quiz num
prompt: In an Atwood machine, a $5\ \text{kg}$ mass and a $3\ \text{kg}$ mass hang from a light rope over a frictionless pulley. Using $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$, what is the magnitude of the acceleration in $\text{m/s}^2$?
answer: 2.45
check: (5 - 3) * 9.8 / (5 + 3)
tol: 0.01
hint: Write the second law for each mass with the same acceleration magnitude and the same tension.
hint: $m_1 g - T = m_1 a$ and $T - m_2 g = m_2 a$. Add them.
solution: Adding the equations gives $(m_1 - m_2)g = (m_1 + m_2)a$, so $a = (2)(9.8)/8 = 2.45\ \text{m/s}^2$.
unit: m/s^2

@quiz mc
prompt: A hockey puck slides across ice at a constant $4\ \text{m/s}$ in a straight line. Ignoring friction, what is true of the net force on the puck?
choice: It points in the direction of motion and has magnitude $m \times 4$.
choice*: It is zero.
choice: It equals the puck's weight.
choice: It is small but nonzero, enough to keep the puck moving.
hint: Constant velocity means zero acceleration.
solution: By the first and second laws, constant velocity means $a = 0$, so the net force is zero. No force is needed to keep an object moving.
