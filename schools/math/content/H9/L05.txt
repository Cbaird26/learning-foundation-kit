@article
**Energy** is a bookkeeping quantity that makes many motion problems easy: instead of tracking forces moment by moment, you compare the situation at two instants.

### Work

When a constant force $F$ acts on an object that moves a displacement $d$, the **work** done by the force is

$$W = F\,d\cos\theta,$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between the force and the displacement. Work is a scalar measured in **joules** ($1\ \text{J} = 1\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$). It is positive when the force has a component along the motion, negative when it opposes the motion (friction), and zero when the force is perpendicular to the motion (a centripetal force, or the normal force on a level path).

### Kinetic energy and the work-energy theorem

**Kinetic energy** is the energy of motion:

$$KE = \tfrac12 mv^2.$$

The **work-energy theorem** says the net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy:

$$W_{net} = \Delta KE = \tfrac12 mv_f^2 - \tfrac12 mv_i^2.$$

### Potential energy

Near Earth's surface, **gravitational potential energy** relative to a chosen reference height is $PE = mgh$. Only differences matter, so choose the zero level wherever it is convenient. A spring stretched or compressed by $x$ stores $PE = \tfrac12 kx^2$, where $k$ is the spring constant.

### Conservation of energy

If only **conservative** forces (gravity, ideal springs) do work, mechanical energy is conserved:

$$KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f.$$

For a dropped object this gives $mgh = \tfrac12 mv^2$, so $v = \sqrt{2gh}$, independent of mass. If friction or air resistance acts, mechanical energy is converted to thermal energy, and

$$KE_i + PE_i + W_{nc} = KE_f + PE_f,$$

where $W_{nc}$ is the (negative) work done by the nonconservative force. The **total** energy, counting heat, is always conserved.

### Power

**Power** is the rate of doing work or transferring energy:

$$P = \frac{W}{t} = Fv\cos\theta.$$

Its SI unit is the **watt** ($1\ \text{W} = 1\ \text{J/s}$). A common non-SI unit is the horsepower, about $746\ \text{W}$. Energy bills use the kilowatt-hour: $1\ \text{kWh} = 3.6\times 10^6\ \text{J}$.

### Strategy

Pick an initial and final instant, write the energy at each, include any work by friction, and solve. Energy methods give speeds directly but not times.

@example
title: Speed of a dropped ball
problem: A $2\ \text{kg}$ ball is dropped from rest from a height of $10\ \text{m}$ above the ground. Ignoring air resistance and using $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$, find its kinetic energy and speed just before it lands.
step: Choose the ground as $PE = 0$. Initially $KE_i = 0$ and $PE_i = mgh = 2(9.8)(10) = 196\ \text{J}$.
step: Only gravity does work, so mechanical energy is conserved. Just before landing $PE_f = 0$ and so $KE_f = 196\ \text{J}$.
step: Solve $\tfrac12 mv^2 = 196$: $v^2 = 196$, so $v = 14\ \text{m/s}$.
step: Check with kinematics: $v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{196} = 14\ \text{m/s}$. It agrees, and the mass canceled.

@mistakes
- Forgetting the $\cos\theta$ factor, or using the angle with the surface instead of the angle between force and displacement.
- Using $v$ instead of $v^2$ in $KE = \tfrac12 mv^2$, or forgetting that doubling speed quadruples kinetic energy.
- Ignoring friction's negative work when the problem says the object ends slower than energy conservation predicts.
- Confusing work (energy, J) with power (rate, W) and with force.

@practice num
prompt: A person pulls a sled with a rope that makes an angle of $60^\circ$ above the horizontal. The rope force is $50\ \text{N}$ and the sled moves $6\ \text{m}$ horizontally. How much work does the rope do, in joules?
answer: 150
check: 50 * 6 * cos(pi / 3)
tol: 0.01
hint: Use $W = Fd\cos\theta$ with $\theta$ between the force and the displacement.
hint: $\cos 60^\circ = 0.5$.
solution: $W = (50)(6)\cos 60^\circ = 300(0.5) = 150\ \text{J}$.
unit: J

@practice num
prompt: A $1200\ \text{kg}$ car travels at $20\ \text{m/s}$. What is its kinetic energy in joules?
answer: 240000
check: 0.5 * 1200 * 20**2
hint: $KE = \tfrac12 mv^2$.
hint: Square the speed first: $20^2 = 400$.
solution: $KE = \tfrac12(1200)(400) = 240\,000\ \text{J}$ (that is $2.4\times 10^5\ \text{J}$).
unit: J

@practice num
prompt: A roller-coaster car starts from rest at the top of a frictionless hill $5\ \text{m}$ above the bottom. What is its speed at the bottom, in m/s, to the nearest $0.1\ \text{m/s}$? Use $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.
answer: 9.9
check: sqrt(2 * 9.8 * 5)
tol: 1%
hint: Mechanical energy is conserved: the potential energy at the top becomes kinetic energy at the bottom.
hint: $mgh = \tfrac12 mv^2$, so $v = \sqrt{2gh}$.
solution: $v = \sqrt{2(9.8)(5)} = \sqrt{98} \approx 9.9\ \text{m/s}$.
unit: m/s

@practice mc
prompt: A satellite moves at constant speed in a circular orbit around Earth. How much work does Earth's gravitational force do on the satellite during one quarter of an orbit?
choice: Positive work, because gravity pulls on the satellite
choice: Negative work, because the satellite is held back
choice*: Zero, because the force is perpendicular to the displacement at every instant
choice: It depends on the satellite's mass but not its speed
hint: Compare the direction of the force (toward the center) with the direction of motion (tangent to the circle).
solution: In a circular orbit the gravitational force points toward the center while the velocity is tangent to the path, so $\theta = 90^\circ$ and $W = Fd\cos 90^\circ = 0$. The kinetic energy stays constant.

@practice num
prompt: A motor lifts a $60\ \text{kg}$ load at constant speed through a height of $10\ \text{m}$ in $20\ \text{s}$. What average power does the motor deliver to the load, in watts? Use $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.
answer: 294
check: 60 * 9.8 * 10 / 20
tol: 0.5
hint: At constant speed the lifting force equals the weight, so the work equals the potential-energy gain.
hint: $P = W/t = mgh/t$.
solution: $W = mgh = 60(9.8)(10) = 5880\ \text{J}$, so $P = 5880/20 = 294\ \text{W}$.
unit: W

@quiz num
prompt: A $1000\ \text{kg}$ car speeds up from $10\ \text{m/s}$ to $20\ \text{m/s}$ on a level road. How much net work is done on the car, in joules?
answer: 150000
check: 0.5 * 1000 * (20**2 - 10**2)
hint: Net work equals the change in kinetic energy.
hint: Compute $\tfrac12 m(v_f^2 - v_i^2)$.
solution: $W = \tfrac12(1000)(400 - 100) = 150\,000\ \text{J}$.
unit: J

@quiz num
prompt: A $0.5\ \text{kg}$ ball is dropped from rest from a height of $2.0\ \text{m}$ onto a hard floor and rebounds to a height of $1.2\ \text{m}$. How much mechanical energy is lost in the bounce, in joules? Use $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.
answer: 3.92
check: 0.5 * 9.8 * (2.0 - 1.2)
tol: 0.01
hint: At the top of the drop and the top of the rebound the ball is momentarily at rest, so all the mechanical energy is potential.
hint: Energy lost $= mg(h_1 - h_2)$.
solution: $\Delta E = mg(h_1 - h_2) = 0.5(9.8)(0.8) = 3.92\ \text{J}$, converted to thermal energy and sound.
unit: J
