@article
The law of a sum of independent random variables is a convolution, which is unpleasant to compute. The Fourier transform turns convolution into multiplication. For a random variable $X$ the **characteristic function** is
$$\varphi_X(t) = \mathbb{E}\,e^{itX} = \mathbb{E}\cos(tX) + i\,\mathbb{E}\sin(tX), \qquad t \in \mathbb{R}.$$
It always exists, because $|e^{itX}| = 1$, and it depends only on the law of $X$.

### Basic properties

**Theorem 1.** (a) $\varphi(0) = 1$, $|\varphi(t)| \le 1$ and $\varphi(-t) = \overline{\varphi(t)}$. (b) $\varphi$ is uniformly continuous on $\mathbb{R}$. (c) $\varphi_{aX+b}(t) = e^{itb}\varphi_X(at)$. (d) If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, $\varphi_{X+Y} = \varphi_X \varphi_Y$.

*Proof.* (a) and (c) are immediate. (b) $|\varphi(t+h) - \varphi(t)| = |\mathbb{E}\,e^{itX}(e^{ihX} - 1)| \le \mathbb{E}|e^{ihX} - 1|$, which does not depend on $t$ and tends to $0$ as $h \to 0$ by dominated convergence (the integrand is bounded by $2$). (d) $\mathbb{E}\,e^{it(X+Y)} = \mathbb{E}\,e^{itX}\,\mathbb{E}\,e^{itY}$ by independence, applied to real and imaginary parts. $\blacksquare$

| law | $\varphi(t)$ |
|---|---|
| Bernoulli($p$) | $1 - p + p e^{it}$ |
| Poisson($\lambda$) | $\exp\bigl(\lambda(e^{it} - 1)\bigr)$ |
| Exponential, rate $1$ | $\dfrac{1}{1 - it}$ |
| Uniform on $(-1, 1)$ | $\dfrac{\sin t}{t}$ |
| Standard normal | $e^{-t^2/2}$ |
| Standard Cauchy | $e^{-|t|}$ |

For the standard normal, differentiate under the integral (justified by dominated convergence, since $|x|e^{-x^2/2}$ is integrable) and integrate by parts: $\varphi'(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int ix\,e^{itx}e^{-x^2/2}\,dx = -t\varphi(t)$, with $\varphi(0) = 1$, so $\varphi(t) = e^{-t^2/2}$.

**Caution.** $\varphi_{X+Y} = \varphi_X\varphi_Y$ does not imply that $X, Y$ are independent. If $X = Y$ is standard Cauchy, then $X + Y = 2X$ has $\varphi(t) = e^{-2|t|} = \varphi_X(t)\varphi_Y(t)$, yet $X$ and $Y$ are as dependent as possible. Independence is equivalent to factorization of the *joint* characteristic function.

### Moments and Taylor expansion

**Theorem 2.** For real $x$ and integer $n \ge 0$,
$$\left| e^{ix} - \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{(ix)^k}{k!} \right| \le \min\left( \frac{|x|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!},\ \frac{2|x|^n}{n!} \right).$$

*Proof.* Let $R_n(x)$ be the left side's expression without absolute value. Then $R_0(x) = e^{ix} - 1 = i\int_0^x e^{iy}\,dy$, and in general $R_n(x) = i\int_0^x R_{n-1}(y)\,dy$ because $\frac{d}{dx}R_n = i R_{n-1}$ and $R_n(0) = 0$. Also $|R_0(x)| \le |x|$, since $|e^{iy}| = 1$. If $|R_{n-1}(y)| \le |y|^n/n!$ then $|R_n(x)| \le \left|\int_0^{x} |y|^n/n!\,dy\right| = |x|^{n+1}/(n+1)!$; induction gives the first bound. For the second, $R_n(x) = R_{n-1}(x) - (ix)^n/n!$, so $|R_n(x)| \le |x|^n/n! + |x|^n/n!$ by the first bound applied to $R_{n-1}$. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary 3.** If $\mathbb{E}|X|^n < \infty$, then $\varphi(t) = \sum_{k=0}^n \frac{(it)^k}{k!}\mathbb{E}X^k + o(|t|^n)$ as $t \to 0$. In particular, if $\mathbb{E}X = 0$ and $\mathbb{E}X^2 = \sigma^2 < \infty$, then $\varphi(t) = 1 - \frac{\sigma^2t^2}{2} + o(t^2)$.

*Proof.* The error is $\mathbb{E}R_n(tX)$. By Theorem 2, $|t|^{-n}|R_n(tX)| \le 2|X|^n/n!$, which is integrable, and $|t|^{-n}|R_n(tX)| \le |t||X|^{n+1}/(n+1)! \to 0$ pointwise as $t \to 0$. Dominated convergence finishes. $\blacksquare$

### Inversion and continuity (stated without proof)

**Theorem 4 (inversion).** Let $\mu$ be a probability measure on $\mathbb{R}$ with characteristic function $\varphi$. For $a < b$,
$$\mu(a, b) + \tfrac12\mu\{a\} + \tfrac12\mu\{b\} = \lim_{T \to \infty}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{e^{-ita} - e^{-itb}}{it}\,\varphi(t)\,dt.$$
Hence $\varphi$ determines $\mu$. If $\int|\varphi| < \infty$, then $\mu$ has a bounded continuous density $f(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int e^{-itx}\varphi(t)\,dt$.

**Theorem 5 (Lévy's continuity theorem).** Let $\mu_n$ be probability measures with characteristic functions $\varphi_n$. (a) If $\mu_n \Rightarrow \mu$, then $\varphi_n(t) \to \varphi(t)$ for every $t$. (b) If $\varphi_n(t) \to g(t)$ for every $t$ and $g$ is continuous at $0$, then $g$ is the characteristic function of a probability measure $\mu$ and $\mu_n \Rightarrow \mu$.

Part (a) is easy since $x \mapsto e^{itx}$ is bounded and continuous. Part (b) uses that continuity of $g$ at $0$ forces tightness of $(\mu_n)$; the hypothesis cannot be dropped, as one checks by letting $\mu_n$ put mass escaping to infinity.

### Computing with characteristic functions

For a law on $\{0, 1, \dots, N-1\}$, the probabilities are recovered from $\varphi$ by a finite Fourier sum, the discrete form of inversion. A sample estimate of $\varphi$ is also a one-line sum:

```python
import math
import random


def empirical_cf(sample, t):
    """Average of exp(i t x) over the sample, as a complex number."""
    total = complex(0, 0)
    for x in sample:
        total += complex(math.cos(t * x), math.sin(t * x))
    return total / len(sample)


rng = random.Random(3)
sample = [rng.gauss(0, 1) for _ in range(2000)]
gap = max(abs(empirical_cf(sample, t) - math.exp(-t * t / 2))
          for t in (0.5, 1.0, 2.0))
print(gap < 0.1)
```

It prints `True`: the empirical characteristic function is within sampling error (about $1/\sqrt{2000}$) of $e^{-t^2/2}$. Exams usually ask for a computation with Theorem 1, a proof using Theorem 2, or a use of Theorem 5 to identify a limit law.

@example
title: Sums of independent Poisson variables
problem: Let $X \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda)$ and $Y \sim \text{Poisson}(\nu)$ be independent. Identify the law of $X + Y$.
step: Compute $\varphi_X(t) = \sum_{k \ge 0} e^{itk}e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k! = e^{-\lambda}\exp(\lambda e^{it}) = \exp\bigl(\lambda(e^{it} - 1)\bigr)$.
step: By independence, $\varphi_{X+Y}(t) = \exp\bigl(\lambda(e^{it}-1)\bigr)\exp\bigl(\nu(e^{it}-1)\bigr) = \exp\bigl((\lambda+\nu)(e^{it}-1)\bigr)$.
step: This is the characteristic function of Poisson($\lambda + \nu$).
step: By the uniqueness part of the inversion theorem, $X + Y \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda + \nu)$.
answer: $X + Y \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda + \nu)$.

@example
title: Moments from the characteristic function
problem: Use $\varphi(t) = (1 - it)^{-1}$ for the exponential law of rate $1$ to find $\mathbb{E}X$ and $\operatorname{Var}X$.
step: Differentiate: $\varphi'(t) = i(1 - it)^{-2}$ and $\varphi''(t) = 2i^2(1 - it)^{-3}$. Since $\mathbb{E}|X|^2 < \infty$, Corollary 3 shows these derivatives at $0$ exist and equal $i^k\mathbb{E}X^k$.
step: $\varphi'(0) = i$, so $i\,\mathbb{E}X = i$ and $\mathbb{E}X = 1$.
step: $\varphi''(0) = 2i^2 = -2$, and $\varphi''(0) = i^2\mathbb{E}X^2 = -\mathbb{E}X^2$, so $\mathbb{E}X^2 = 2$.
step: Therefore $\operatorname{Var}X = 2 - 1 = 1$.
answer: $\mathbb{E}X = 1$ and $\operatorname{Var}X = 1$.

@mistakes
- Concluding independence from $\varphi_{X+Y} = \varphi_X\varphi_Y$; the Cauchy example $Y = X$ shows it fails.
- Using Lévy's theorem (b) without checking that the pointwise limit is continuous at $0$. For $N(0, n)$ the limit is the indicator of $\{0\}$ and no weak limit exists.
- Differentiating $\varphi$ at $0$ to find moments without checking that the moment is finite: a $\varphi$ with a derivative at $0$ does not by itself give $\mathbb{E}|X| < \infty$.
- Believing every continuous function with $\varphi(0) = 1$ and $|\varphi| \le 1$ is a characteristic function; positive definiteness (Bochner's theorem) is needed.

model:
```python
import math


def pmf_from_cf(phi, N):
    """Recover a pmf on {0, ..., N-1} from its characteristic function."""
    probs = []
    for j in range(N):
        total = complex(0, 0)
        for s in range(N):
            angle = 2 * math.pi * s * j / N
            total += phi(2 * math.pi * s / N) * complex(
                math.cos(angle), -math.sin(angle))
        probs.append(total.real / N)
    return probs
```
hint: Evaluate `phi` at the $N$ points $2\pi s/N$ and multiply by the conjugate phase $e^{-2\pi i sj/N}$.
hint: The minus sign in the exponent matters: with the wrong sign the pmf comes out reversed.
solution: If $p_k$ is the pmf then $\varphi(2\pi s/N) = \sum_k p_k e^{2\pi i sk/N}$, and $\sum_{s=0}^{N-1} e^{2\pi i s(k-j)/N}$ equals $N$ if $k = j$ and $0$ for other $k$ in $\{0, \dots, N-1\}$, so the sum picks out $N p_j$. This is exact only when the support fits in $\{0, \dots, N-1\}$; for $N = 4$ the binomial law with $n = 3$ just fits. A wrong sign returns $p_{-j \bmod N}$, which the asymmetric binomial test detects.

prompt: Let $X$ be a real random variable with characteristic function $\varphi$, and suppose $|\varphi(t_0)| = 1$ for some $t_0 \ne 0$. Prove that there are $a \in \mathbb{R}$ and $h = 2\pi/|t_0|$ such that $X \in a + h\mathbb{Z}$ almost surely.
hint: Write $\varphi(t_0) = e^{i\theta}$ and consider $\mathbb{E}\,e^{i(t_0X - \theta)}$.
hint: Take real parts and use that $1 - \cos y \ge 0$ with equality exactly when $y \in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}$.
solution: Write $\varphi(t_0) = e^{i\theta}$ with $\theta$ real. Then $\mathbb{E}\,e^{i(t_0X - \theta)} = e^{-i\theta}\varphi(t_0) = 1$. Taking real parts, $\mathbb{E}\cos(t_0X - \theta) = 1$, that is $\mathbb{E}[1 - \cos(t_0X - \theta)] = 0$. The integrand is nonnegative, so $1 - \cos(t_0X - \theta) = 0$ almost surely, and therefore $t_0X - \theta \in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}$ almost surely. Hence $X \in \theta/t_0 + (2\pi/|t_0|)\mathbb{Z}$ almost surely, so take $a = \theta/t_0$. $\blacksquare$

@quiz mc
prompt: Let $X_n \sim N(0, n)$, so $\varphi_n(t) = e^{-nt^2/2}$. Which statement is correct?
choice*: $\varphi_n(t)$ converges for every $t$ to the indicator of $\{0\}$, which is discontinuous at $0$, and $X_n$ has no weak limit.
choice: $\varphi_n(t) \to 0$ for every $t$, so $X_n \Rightarrow \delta_0$.
choice: $\varphi_n(t) \to 1$ for every $t$, so $X_n \Rightarrow \delta_0$.
choice: $X_n \Rightarrow N(0, 1)$ because each $X_n$ is normal.
hint: Evaluate the limit at $t = 0$ separately from $t \ne 0$.
solution: $\varphi_n(0) = 1$ for every $n$ while $\varphi_n(t) \to 0$ for $t \ne 0$, so the pointwise limit is the indicator of $\{0\}$, which is not continuous at $0$. Lévy's theorem therefore gives no weak limit, and indeed $P(|X_n| \le K) \to 0$ for every $K$, so the mass escapes to infinity. A limit of normal laws is normal only when the means and variances converge.
