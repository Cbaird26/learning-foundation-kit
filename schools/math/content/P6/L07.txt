@article
Roll a fair die and learn only whether the result is even. What is your best guess of the value? If it is even, the guess is the average of $2, 4, 6$, which is $4$; if odd, the average of $1, 3, 5$, which is $3$. The guess is a function of the information, so it is a random variable. That is conditional expectation: the best prediction of $X$ given a $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{G}$ of available information.

### Definition

Let $X$ be integrable on $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ and $\mathcal{G} \subseteq \mathcal{F}$ a $\sigma$-algebra. A random variable $Y$ is a *version* of $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ if (i) $Y$ is $\mathcal{G}$-measurable and integrable, and (ii) $\int_A Y\,dP = \int_A X\,dP$ for every $A \in \mathcal{G}$.

**Theorem 1.** A version exists and any two versions agree almost surely.

*Proof.* Uniqueness: if $Y, Y'$ are versions, then $A = \{Y > Y'\} \in \mathcal{G}$ and $\int_A (Y - Y')\,dP = 0$ with an integrand that is strictly positive on $A$, so $P(A) = 0$; symmetrically for $\{Y < Y'\}$. Existence (using the Radon–Nikodym theorem from P1): for $X \ge 0$, $\nu(A) = \int_A X\,dP$ is a finite measure on $\mathcal{G}$ that is absolutely continuous with respect to $P|_{\mathcal{G}}$, and its Radon–Nikodym derivative is a $\mathcal{G}$-measurable $Y$ satisfying (ii). For general $X$ use $X^+ - X^-$. $\blacksquare$

If $\mathcal{G} = \sigma(Z)$ we write $\mathbb{E}[X \mid Z]$; it equals $h(Z)$ for a Borel $h$. If $\mathcal{G}$ is generated by a finite partition $A_1, \dots, A_m$ with $P(A_i) > 0$, then
$$\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] = \sum_{i=1}^m \frac{\mathbb{E}[X;\,A_i]}{P(A_i)}\,\mathbf{1}_{A_i},$$
the block averages. If $(X, Y)$ has a joint density $f$, then $\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y] = h(Y)$ with $h(y) = \int x f(x,y)\,dx \big/ \int f(x,y)\,dx$.

### Properties

**Theorem 2.** Let $X, Y$ be integrable and $\mathcal{H} \subseteq \mathcal{G} \subseteq \mathcal{F}$ be $\sigma$-algebras. Almost surely:
(a) linearity; (b) if $X \le Y$ then $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] \le \mathbb{E}[Y \mid \mathcal{G}]$; (c) $\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\bigr] = \mathbb{E}X$;
(d) **tower property:** $\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] \mid \mathcal{H}\bigr] = \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{H}]$;
(e) **taking out what is known:** if $Z$ is $\mathcal{G}$-measurable and $XZ$ is integrable, then $\mathbb{E}[ZX \mid \mathcal{G}] = Z\,\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$;
(f) if $X$ is independent of $\mathcal{G}$, then $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] = \mathbb{E}X$;
(g) **conditional Jensen:** if $\varphi$ is convex and $X$, $\varphi(X)$ are integrable, then $\varphi(\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]) \le \mathbb{E}[\varphi(X) \mid \mathcal{G}]$.

*Proof of (d) and (e).* (d) The variable $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{H}]$ is $\mathcal{H}$-measurable. For $A \in \mathcal{H} \subseteq \mathcal{G}$: $\int_A \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] = \int_A X = \int_A \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{H}]$, where the first equality is (ii) for $\mathcal{G}$ (valid because $A \in \mathcal{G}$). Hence $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{H}]$ satisfies the defining properties of $\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] \mid \mathcal{H}\bigr]$, and equals it almost surely by uniqueness. (e) $Z\,\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ is $\mathcal{G}$-measurable. For $Z = \mathbf{1}_B$ with $B \in \mathcal{G}$ and $A \in \mathcal{G}$: $\int_A \mathbf{1}_B\,\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] = \int_{A \cap B}\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] = \int_{A \cap B}X = \int_A \mathbf{1}_BX$. Linearity gives simple $Z$; for $X, Z \ge 0$ approximate $Z$ by simple $Z_n \uparrow Z$ and use monotone convergence on both sides; then split into positive and negative parts, which is where integrability of $XZ$ is used. $\blacksquare$

**Counterexample without measurability.** Let $\mathcal{G}$ be trivial and $X = Y$ a fair sign. Then $\mathbb{E}[XY \mid \mathcal{G}] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] = 1$ but $Y\,\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] = Y \cdot 0 = 0$: $Y$ is not $\mathcal{G}$-measurable.

### Projection

**Theorem 3.** If $\mathbb{E}X^2 < \infty$, then $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ is the orthogonal projection of $X$ onto the closed subspace $L^2(\mathcal{G})$ of $\mathcal{G}$-measurable square-integrable variables. Equivalently, it minimizes $\mathbb{E}(X - Z)^2$ over $\mathcal{G}$-measurable $Z$, and $\mathbb{E}(X - Z)^2 = \mathbb{E}(X - Y)^2 + \mathbb{E}(Y - Z)^2$ with $Y = \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$.

*Proof.* By (g) with $\varphi(x) = x^2$, $Y^2 \le \mathbb{E}[X^2 \mid \mathcal{G}]$, so $\mathbb{E}Y^2 \le \mathbb{E}X^2$ and $Y \in L^2(\mathcal{G})$. For bounded $\mathcal{G}$-measurable $W$, (e) gives $\mathbb{E}[WX] = \mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{E}[WX \mid \mathcal{G}]\bigr] = \mathbb{E}[WY]$, so $X - Y \perp W$; bounded variables are dense in $L^2(\mathcal{G})$, so $X - Y \perp L^2(\mathcal{G})$. For $Z \in L^2(\mathcal{G})$, expand $X - Z = (X - Y) + (Y - Z)$ with orthogonal summands. $\blacksquare$

Corollary: $\operatorname{Var}X = \mathbb{E}\operatorname{Var}(X \mid \mathcal{G}) + \operatorname{Var}\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$. On exams, the standard moves are: guess the answer from symmetry or a block average, then verify the defining property (ii) on a generating $\pi$-system of $\mathcal{G}$; and remember that the result is a random variable, not a number.

@example
title: Block averages and the law of total variance
problem: Let $\Omega = \{1, \dots, 8\}$ with the uniform measure, $X(\omega) = \omega$, and $\mathcal{G} = \sigma(\{\text{even}\}, \{\text{odd}\})$. Find $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ and verify $\operatorname{Var}X = \mathbb{E}\operatorname{Var}(X \mid \mathcal{G}) + \operatorname{Var}\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$.
step: On the even block $\{2,4,6,8\}$ the average is $5$; on the odd block $\{1,3,5,7\}$ it is $4$. So $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] = 5$ on evens and $4$ on odds.
step: Check (ii) with $A$ the even block: $\int_A X\,dP = (2+4+6+8)/8 = \frac{20}{8}$ and $\int_A \mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]\,dP = 5 \cdot \frac48 = \frac{20}{8}$. Also $\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]] = \frac{5+4}{2} = 4.5 = \mathbb{E}X$.
step: Conditional variances: on evens $\mathbb{E}[X^2 \mid \text{even}] = \frac{4+16+36+64}{4} = 30$ and $30 - 25 = 5$; on odds $\frac{1+9+25+49}{4} = 21$ and $21 - 16 = 5$. So $\mathbb{E}\operatorname{Var}(X \mid \mathcal{G}) = 5$.
step: $\operatorname{Var}\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ is the variance of a variable equal to $5$ or $4$ with probability $\frac12$ each: $\frac14$. The sum is $5.25$, which equals $\operatorname{Var}X = \frac{8^2 - 1}{12} = 5.25$.
answer: $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] = 5$ on evens, $4$ on odds; the variance decomposition checks: $5 + \frac14 = 5.25$.

@example
title: Conditioning with a joint density
problem: $(X, Y)$ has joint density $f(x,y) = x + y$ on $[0,1]^2$. Find $\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]$ and verify the tower property $\mathbb{E}\,\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y] = \mathbb{E}X$.
step: The marginal of $Y$ is $f_Y(y) = \int_0^1 (x + y)\,dx = \frac12 + y$.
step: $\int_0^1 x(x + y)\,dx = \frac13 + \frac y2$, so $h(y) = \dfrac{1/3 + y/2}{1/2 + y} = \dfrac{2 + 3y}{3 + 6y}$ and $\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y] = h(Y)$.
step: Tower: $\mathbb{E}\,h(Y) = \int_0^1 \left(\frac13 + \frac y2\right)dy = \frac13 + \frac14 = \frac7{12}$, because the denominator of $h$ cancels $f_Y$.
step: Directly, $\mathbb{E}X = \int_0^1\int_0^1 x(x+y)\,dy\,dx = \frac13 + \frac14 = \frac7{12}$. They agree.
answer: $\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y] = \dfrac{2 + 3Y}{3 + 6Y}$, and $\mathbb{E}X = \frac{7}{12}$.

@mistakes
- Treating $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ as a number: it is a $\mathcal{G}$-measurable random variable defined up to null sets.
- Pulling out a factor that is not $\mathcal{G}$-measurable: $\mathbb{E}[XY \mid \mathcal{G}] = Y\,\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ needs $Y$ to be $\mathcal{G}$-measurable (and $XY$ integrable).
- Misplacing the $\sigma$-algebra in the tower property: for $\mathcal{H} \subseteq \mathcal{G}$ both $\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{H}] \mid \mathcal{G}]$ and $\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] \mid \mathcal{H}]$ equal $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{H}]$, the answer for the smaller $\sigma$-algebra; neither equals $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ in general.
- Verifying (ii) only for single points or for $A = \Omega$ instead of every $A \in \mathcal{G}$ (or a generating $\pi$-system, which suffices by the $\pi$–$\lambda$ theorem).

answer: 77/3

tol: 0.0001
hint: Point $5$ lies in the block $\{4, 5, 6\}$.
hint: Average $X$ over that block.
solution: On the block $\{4,5,6\}$ the three equally likely values of $X$ are $16, 25, 36$, so $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}](5) = \frac{16 + 25 + 36}{3} = \frac{77}{3}$.

@practice num
prompt: $(X, Y)$ is uniformly distributed on the triangle $\{(x, y) : 0 < y < x < 1\}$, with density $2$. Compute $\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y = \frac13]$ exactly.
answer: 2/3
check: float((1 + Fraction(1, 3)) / 2)
tol: 0.0001
hint: For fixed $y$, which $x$ satisfy $y < x < 1$?
hint: Given $Y = y$, the density of $X$ is proportional to $f(x, y)$ on that interval.

@practice mc
prompt: Let $\mathcal{H} \subseteq \mathcal{G}$ be sub-$\sigma$-algebras and $X$ integrable. Which identity holds almost surely in general?
choice*: $\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] \mid \mathcal{H}\bigr] = \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{H}]$
choice: $\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{H}] \mid \mathcal{G}\bigr] = \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$
choice: $\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] \mid \mathcal{H}\bigr] = \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$
choice: $\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] \mid \mathcal{H}\bigr] = \mathbb{E}X$
hint: Conditioning on the smaller $\sigma$-algebra last leaves the coarsest information.
solution: This is the tower property. The identity $\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{H}] \mid \mathcal{G}] = \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ is false: the left side is $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{H}]$, since that variable is already $\mathcal{G}$-measurable. Claiming that $\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] \mid \mathcal{H}]$ equals $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ would make the finer variable $\mathcal{H}$-measurable, and equating it with $\mathbb{E}X$ holds only when $\mathcal{H}$ is trivial.

@practice ms
prompt: Let $X$ be integrable and $\mathcal{G}$ a sub-$\sigma$-algebra. Which statements hold almost surely?
choice*: If $X \ge 0$ then $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] \ge 0$.
choice*: If $X$ is independent of $\mathcal{G}$ then $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] = \mathbb{E}X$.
choice: $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ is always $\sigma(X)$-measurable.
choice*: $\bigl|\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\bigr| \le \mathbb{E}\bigl[|X| \mid \mathcal{G}\bigr]$.
choice: $\mathbb{E}[X^2 \mid \mathcal{G}] \le \bigl(\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\bigr)^2$ when $\mathbb{E}X^2 < \infty$.
hint: Conditional Jensen gives the direction of the last inequality; for measurability, recall the definition.
solution: Positivity is monotonicity (b). Independence gives the constant $\mathbb{E}X$ by (f). The third fails: $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ is $\mathcal{G}$-measurable, not $\sigma(X)$-measurable. For $X = \varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2$ with independent fair signs and $\mathcal{G} = \sigma(\varepsilon_1)$ it equals $\varepsilon_1$, which is not a function of $X$ (when $X = 0$ the sign $\varepsilon_1$ is undetermined). The fourth is conditional Jensen with $\varphi = |\cdot|$. The fifth reverses conditional Jensen for $x^2$: the correct inequality is $(\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}])^2 \le \mathbb{E}[X^2 \mid \mathcal{G}]$.

@practice self
prompt: Let $X$ and $Y$ be integrable random variables on one probability space with $\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y] = Y$ and $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X] = X$ almost surely (conditioning on $\sigma(Y)$ and $\sigma(X)$ respectively). Prove that $X = Y$ almost surely. Do not assume square integrability.
hint: For a real number $c$, compare $\mathbb{E}[X - Y;\ Y > c]$ and $\mathbb{E}[Y - X;\ X > c]$ with $\mathbb{E}[X - Y;\ X > c,\ Y > c]$.
hint: Show $P(Y > c \ge X) = 0$ and $P(X > c \ge Y) = 0$ for every $c$, then use rational $c$.
solution: Fix $c \in \mathbb{R}$ and let $I = \mathbb{E}[X - Y;\ X > c,\ Y > c]$. Since $\{Y > c\} \in \sigma(Y)$ and $\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y] = Y$, $\mathbb{E}[X - Y;\ Y > c] = 0$. Subtracting the part on $\{X > c\}$, $\mathbb{E}[X - Y;\ Y > c,\ X \le c] = 0 - I = -I$. Symmetrically, $\{X > c\} \in \sigma(X)$ and $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X] = X$ give $\mathbb{E}[Y - X;\ X > c] = 0$, so $\mathbb{E}[Y - X;\ X > c,\ Y \le c] = 0 - \mathbb{E}[Y - X;\ X > c,\ Y > c] = I$. On $\{Y > c \ge X\}$ we have $X - Y < 0$, so $-I \le 0$ and $I \ge 0$. On $\{X > c \ge Y\}$ we have $Y - X < 0$, so $I \le 0$. Hence $I = 0$ and both expectations vanish. Each is the integral of a strictly negative function over its event, so $P(Y > c \ge X) = 0$ and $P(X > c \ge Y) = 0$. If $X < Y$ at some point of $\Omega$, pick a rational $c$ with $X \le c < Y$; so $P(X < Y) \le \sum_{c \in \mathbb{Q}}P(Y > c \ge X) = 0$. Likewise $P(Y < X) = 0$. Hence $X = Y$ almost surely. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Uses the defining property of conditional expectation on the sets $\{Y > c\}$ and $\{X > c\}$, which belong to the correct $\sigma$-algebras.
rubric: Combines the two identities to show a single quantity $I$ is both nonnegative and nonpositive, with the sign of the integrands justified.
rubric: Concludes $P(Y > c \ge X) = 0$ and $P(X > c \ge Y) = 0$ and passes to $X = Y$ a.s. through a countable union over rational $c$.
