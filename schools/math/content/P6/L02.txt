@article
Flip a fair coin forever. Is it certain that heads appears infinitely often? What if the $n$th flip is heads with probability $1/n$, or $1/n^2$? The Borel–Cantelli lemmas answer all of these by comparing a series with a probability.

### Independence

Events $A_1, \dots, A_n$ are **independent** if $P\left(\bigcap_{i \in I} A_i\right) = \prod_{i \in I} P(A_i)$ for every subset $I \subseteq \{1, \dots, n\}$. $\sigma$-algebras $\mathcal{F}_1, \dots, \mathcal{F}_n \subseteq \mathcal{F}$ are independent if this holds for all choices $A_i \in \mathcal{F}_i$, and random variables are independent if the $\sigma$-algebras $\sigma(X_i)$ are. An infinite family is independent if every finite subfamily is.

Pairwise independence is strictly weaker. Let $X, Y$ be independent fair bits and $Z = X + Y \bmod 2$. Each pair among $X, Y, Z$ is independent (each of the four value pairs has probability $\frac14$), but $P(X = Y = Z = 1) = 0 \ne \frac18$.

**Theorem 1.** Let $\mathcal{P}_1, \dots, \mathcal{P}_n$ be $\pi$-systems, each containing $\Omega$, with $P(A_1 \cap \dots \cap A_n) = \prod_i P(A_i)$ for all $A_i \in \mathcal{P}_i$. Then $\sigma(\mathcal{P}_1), \dots, \sigma(\mathcal{P}_n)$ are independent.

*Proof.* Fix $A_2 \in \mathcal{P}_2, \dots, A_n \in \mathcal{P}_n$ and let $\mathcal{L}$ be the family of $A \in \mathcal{F}$ with $P(A \cap A_2 \cap \dots \cap A_n) = P(A) \prod_{i \ge 2} P(A_i)$. It contains $\Omega$; it is closed under proper differences because both sides are additive; and it is closed under increasing unions by continuity from below. So $\mathcal{L}$ is a $\lambda$-system containing $\mathcal{P}_1$, hence containing $\sigma(\mathcal{P}_1)$. Repeat with the second slot, and so on. $\blacksquare$

Consequences: random variables are independent if and only if $P(X_1 \le x_1, \dots, X_n \le x_n) = \prod_i F_{X_i}(x_i)$ for all $x_i$ (the half-lines form $\pi$-systems), and functions of disjoint blocks of independent variables are independent.

### The Borel–Cantelli lemmas

Write $\{A_n \text{ i.o.}\} = \limsup A_n = \bigcap_{n} \bigcup_{k \ge n} A_k$, the event that infinitely many $A_n$ occur.

**Lemma 2 (first Borel–Cantelli).** If $\sum_n P(A_n) < \infty$, then $P(A_n \text{ i.o.}) = 0$. No independence is needed.

*Proof.* For each $n$, $P(A_n \text{ i.o.}) \le P\left(\bigcup_{k \ge n} A_k\right) \le \sum_{k \ge n} P(A_k)$, the tail of a convergent series, which tends to $0$. $\blacksquare$

**Lemma 3 (second Borel–Cantelli).** If $A_1, A_2, \dots$ are independent and $\sum_n P(A_n) = \infty$, then $P(A_n \text{ i.o.}) = 1$.

*Proof.* It suffices to show $P\left(\bigcap_{k \ge n} A_k^c\right) = 0$ for each $n$, since $(\limsup A_k)^c = \bigcup_n \bigcap_{k \ge n} A_k^c$. Complements of independent events are independent, and $1 - x \le e^{-x}$, so for $m > n$
$$P\left(\bigcap_{k=n}^m A_k^c\right) = \prod_{k=n}^m \bigl(1 - P(A_k)\bigr) \le \exp\left(-\sum_{k=n}^m P(A_k)\right) \to 0 \quad (m \to \infty).$$
Continuity from above gives $P\left(\bigcap_{k \ge n} A_k^c\right) = 0$. $\blacksquare$

**Independence is needed in Lemma 3.** Take $A_n = A$ for all $n$ with $P(A) = \frac12$: the series diverges but $P(A_n \text{ i.o.}) = P(A) = \frac12$. Take $A_n = (0, 1/n)$ on $(0,1)$: the series diverges, yet $\limsup A_n = \emptyset$.

**Example (a word in coin flips).** Fix a finite word $w$ of $m$ letters from $\{H, T\}$ and let $A_k$ be the event that flips $(k-1)m + 1, \dots, km$ spell $w$. These events depend on disjoint blocks, so they are independent, with $P(A_k) = 2^{-m}$ and divergent sum. Hence $w$ appears infinitely often, almost surely.

### Kolmogorov's zero–one law

Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be independent. The **tail $\sigma$-algebra** is $\mathcal{T} = \bigcap_n \sigma(X_n, X_{n+1}, \dots)$. Examples of tail events: $\{\sum X_n \text{ converges}\}$, $\{\limsup X_n/n > 1\}$, $\{S_n/n \text{ converges}\}$ where $S_n = X_1 + \dots + X_n$.

**Theorem 4.** If $A \in \mathcal{T}$ then $P(A) \in \{0, 1\}$.

*Proof.* For each $n$, $\sigma(X_1, \dots, X_n)$ is independent of $\sigma(X_{n+1}, \dots)$, hence of $\mathcal{T}$. The union $\bigcup_n \sigma(X_1, \dots, X_n)$ is a $\pi$-system (the $\sigma$-algebras increase) generating $\sigma(X_1, X_2, \dots)$, and it is independent of $\mathcal{T}$; by Theorem 1, $\sigma(X_1, X_2, \dots)$ is independent of $\mathcal{T}$. But $\mathcal{T} \subseteq \sigma(X_1, X_2, \dots)$, so $A \in \mathcal{T}$ is independent of itself: $P(A) = P(A \cap A) = P(A)^2$. $\blacksquare$

The theorem says nothing about which value; computing it needs another argument, often Borel–Cantelli.

### Exact computation

For independent events with known probabilities, exact arithmetic shows the dichotomy:

```python
from fractions import Fraction


def prob_none(ps):
    """P(no event occurs) for independent events with probabilities ps."""
    out = Fraction(1)
    for p in ps:
        out *= 1 - p
    return out


harmonic = [Fraction(1, k) for k in range(2, 101)]
square = [Fraction(1, k * k) for k in range(2, 101)]
print(prob_none(harmonic), prob_none(square))
```

This prints `1/100 101/200`. With $p_k = 1/k$ the product telescopes to $1/M$ and tends to $0$; with $p_k = 1/k^2$ it stays near $\frac12$. On exams, the standard attack is: compute $P(A_n)$, decide whether the series converges, and check independence before invoking the second lemma.

@example
title: Heads with probability one over n
problem: Flip independent coins, the $n$th landing heads with probability $1/n$ for $n \ge 2$. Does heads occur infinitely often? What if the probability is $1/n^2$?
step: Let $A_n$ be the event that flip $n$ is heads. The $A_n$ are independent because the flips are.
step: For $P(A_n) = 1/n$: $\sum_{n \ge 2} 1/n = \infty$, so by the second Borel–Cantelli lemma (independence holds), $P(A_n \text{ i.o.}) = 1$.
step: For $P(A_n) = 1/n^2$: $\sum_{n \ge 2} 1/n^2 < \infty$, so by the first Borel–Cantelli lemma, $P(A_n \text{ i.o.}) = 0$: almost surely only finitely many heads.
step: Note the asymmetry of tools: the second case needed no independence, the first case did.
answer: Infinitely many heads almost surely for $1/n$; only finitely many almost surely for $1/n^2$.

@example
title: A tail event with probability zero or one
problem: Let $X_n$ be independent with $P(X_n = 1) = P(X_n = -1) = \tfrac12$. Show that $P(\sum_n X_n/n \text{ converges}) \in \{0, 1\}$, and that the event $\{X_1 = 1\}$ does not have this property in general.
step: Let $C$ be the event that $\sum_n X_n/n$ converges. Changing finitely many of the $X_n$ changes the series by a finite amount, so convergence of the series depends only on $(X_m, X_{m+1}, \dots)$ for every $m$.
step: Hence $C \in \sigma(X_m, X_{m+1}, \dots)$ for each $m$, that is $C \in \mathcal{T}$, and Kolmogorov's zero–one law gives $P(C) \in \{0, 1\}$.
step: By contrast $\{X_1 = 1\}$ has probability $\frac12$, so it is not a tail event; it depends on the first variable.
answer: $P(C) \in \{0,1\}$ by the zero–one law; $\{X_1 = 1\}$ is not a tail event.

@mistakes
- Using the second Borel–Cantelli lemma without independence: $A_n = A$ with $P(A) = \frac12$ gives $P(A_n \text{ i.o.}) = \frac12$, not $1$.
- Concluding "infinitely often" from a divergent series alone. Divergence only matters together with independence; convergence alone already gives "finitely often".
- Treating pairwise independence as independence: three events can be pairwise independent with $P(A \cap B \cap C) \ne P(A)P(B)P(C)$.
- Reading the zero–one law as "the probability is $\frac12$ or undetermined": it gives $0$ or $1$ and says nothing about which.

prompt: Independent events $A_2, A_3, \dots$ have $P(A_n) = \frac{2}{n(n+1)}$. Find the probability, exactly, that none of $A_2, A_3, \dots, A_9$ occurs.

hint: By independence the probability is the product of $1 - P(A_n)$.
hint: Write $1 - \frac{2}{n(n+1)} = \frac{(n+2)(n-1)}{n(n+1)}$ and look for telescoping.
solution: $1 - \frac{2}{n(n+1)} = \frac{n^2 + n - 2}{n(n+1)} = \frac{(n-1)(n+2)}{n(n+1)}$. The product over $n = 2, \dots, 9$ splits into $\prod \frac{n-1}{n} = \frac{1}{9}$ and $\prod \frac{n+2}{n+1} = \frac{11}{3}$, giving $\frac{11}{27}$.

model:
```python
from fractions import Fraction


def count_dist(ps):
    """Exact distribution of the number of occurring independent events."""
    dist = [Fraction(1)]
    for p in ps:
        new = [Fraction(0)] * (len(dist) + 1)
        for k, mass in enumerate(dist):
            new[k] += mass * (1 - p)
            new[k + 1] += mass * p
        dist = new
    return dist
```
hint: Add the events one at a time and keep the distribution of the count so far.
hint: Conditioning on the new event: with probability $p$ the count goes from $k$ to $k+1$, otherwise it stays $k$.
solution: Let `dist[k]` be the probability of exactly $k$ occurrences among the first $j$ events. By independence the new event occurs with probability $p$ regardless of the past, so the new distribution is `new[k] = dist[k](1-p) + dist[k-1]p`. Building a new list at each step avoids overwriting entries still needed. Exact fractions make the equality tests exact. (The distribution is not binomial unless all $p$ are equal.)

@practice self
prompt: Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be independent exponential random variables with rate $1$, so $P(X_n > x) = e^{-x}$ for $x \ge 0$. Prove that $\limsup_{n \to \infty} \dfrac{X_n}{\log n} = 1$ almost surely.
hint: Compute $P(X_n > c \log n) = n^{-c}$ and apply a Borel–Cantelli lemma for $c > 1$ and for $c = 1$.
hint: To show the limsup is at most $1$, take $c = 1 + 1/m$ for $m = 1, 2, \dots$ and intersect countably many probability-one events.
solution: For $c > 0$ and $n \ge 2$, $P(X_n > c \log n) = e^{-c \log n} = n^{-c}$. Upper bound: if $c > 1$ then $\sum_n n^{-c} < \infty$, so by the first Borel–Cantelli lemma, almost surely $X_n \le c \log n$ for all large $n$, hence $\limsup X_n/\log n \le c$. Intersecting over $c = 1 + 1/m$, $m \in \mathbb{N}$ (a countable intersection of probability-one events) gives $\limsup X_n/\log n \le 1$ almost surely. Lower bound: for $c = 1$, the events $\{X_n > \log n\}$ are independent (they depend on different variables) and $\sum_n n^{-1} = \infty$, so by the second Borel–Cantelli lemma $X_n > \log n$ infinitely often almost surely, hence $\limsup X_n/\log n \ge 1$. Combining the two bounds, the limsup equals $1$ almost surely. $\blacksquare$

rubric: Proves the upper bound with the first lemma for each $c > 1$ and handles the passage to $c \downarrow 1$ through a countable intersection.
rubric: Proves the lower bound with the second lemma and explicitly checks independence and divergence of $\sum 1/n$.

@quiz num

answer: 3/8
check: float(Fraction(3, 8))

hint: What does it mean for infinitely many of the sets $A_n$ to occur when they are all the same set?
solution: $\limsup A_n = A$ because $\bigcup_{k \ge n} A_k = A$ for each $n$. So the probability is $P(A) = \frac38$, even though $\sum P(A_n) = \infty$: the events are not independent, and the second lemma does not apply.
