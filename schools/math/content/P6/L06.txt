@article
Roll many dice and add them: the histogram of the total is bell-shaped, whatever the faces of the dice. The central limit theorem explains the shape, and its proof is the payoff of last week's characteristic functions. Let $S_n = X_1 + \dots + X_n$ and let $\Phi$ be the standard normal distribution function.

### Two lemmas

**Lemma 1.** If $a_1, \dots, a_n, b_1, \dots, b_n$ are complex numbers of modulus at most $M$, then $\left|\prod a_i - \prod b_i\right| \le M^{n-1}\sum|a_i - b_i|$.

*Proof.* Induction on $n$: $|a_1 \cdots a_n - b_1 \cdots b_n| \le |a_1|\,|a_2 \cdots a_n - b_2 \cdots b_n| + |a_1 - b_1|\,|b_2 \cdots b_n|$, and apply the hypothesis to the first term. $\blacksquare$

**Lemma 2.** If complex numbers $c_n \to c$, then $(1 + c_n/n)^n \to e^c$.

*Proof.* For $|z| \le 1$, $|e^z - 1 - z| \le \sum_{k \ge 2}|z|^k/k! \le |z|^2$. Put $z = c_n/n$ (eventually $|z| \le 1$). Both $1 + z$ and $e^z$ have modulus at most $e^{|z|}$, so $M = e^{|c_n|/n}$ and $M^{n-1} \le e^{|c_n|}$ is bounded. Lemma 1 gives $|(1+z)^n - e^{nz}| \le e^{|c_n|}\cdot n\,|c_n/n|^2 = e^{|c_n|}|c_n|^2/n \to 0$, and $e^{nz} = e^{c_n} \to e^c$. $\blacksquare$

### The theorem

**Theorem 3 (Lindeberg–Lévy central limit theorem).** Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be independent and identically distributed with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2 \in (0, \infty)$. Then
$$\frac{S_n - n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{n}} \Rightarrow N(0, 1).$$

*Proof.* Replace $X_i$ by $(X_i - \mu)/\sigma$: the standardized variables have mean $0$ and variance $1$, and $(S_n - n\mu)/(\sigma\sqrt n)$ is unchanged. By Corollary 3 of last week, $\varphi(t) = 1 - \frac{t^2}{2} + r(t)$ with $r(t) = o(t^2)$. By independence and identical distribution, the characteristic function of $S_n/\sqrt{n}$ is
$$\varphi\!\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{n}}\right)^{n} = \left(1 + \frac{c_n}{n}\right)^n, \qquad c_n = -\frac{t^2}{2} + n\,r\!\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt n}\right) \to -\frac{t^2}{2},$$
because $n\,r(t/\sqrt{n}) = t^2\cdot\frac{r(t/\sqrt n)}{(t/\sqrt n)^2} \to 0$ for fixed $t \ne 0$. By Lemma 2 the characteristic functions converge to $e^{-t^2/2}$, which is continuous at $0$ and is the characteristic function of $N(0,1)$. Lévy's continuity theorem finishes. $\blacksquare$

**Hypotheses.** Finite variance cannot be dropped: for standard Cauchy variables $S_n/\sqrt n$ is not even tight, since $S_n/n$ is Cauchy for every $n$. The independent but not identically distributed case needs a condition.

**Theorem 4 (Lindeberg–Feller; stated without proof).** Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be independent with $\mathbb{E}X_k = 0$, $\sigma_k^2 = \mathbb{E}X_k^2 < \infty$ and $s_n^2 = \sum_{k \le n}\sigma_k^2$. If for every $\varepsilon > 0$ the **Lindeberg condition**
$$\frac{1}{s_n^2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\bigl[X_k^2;\ |X_k| > \varepsilon s_n\bigr] \to 0$$
holds, then $S_n/s_n \Rightarrow N(0, 1)$. Conversely, if $\max_{k \le n}\sigma_k^2/s_n^2 \to 0$ and $S_n/s_n \Rightarrow N(0,1)$, then the Lindeberg condition holds.

For example, if $|X_k| \le K$ and $s_n \to \infty$, the Lindeberg sums are eventually $0$, so the condition holds.

**Theorem 5 (Berry–Esseen; stated without proof).** If $X_i$ are i.i.d. with mean $0$, variance $\sigma^2 > 0$ and $\rho = \mathbb{E}|X_1|^3 < \infty$, there is an absolute constant $C$ such that
$$\sup_x\left|P\!\left(\frac{S_n}{\sigma\sqrt n} \le x\right) - \Phi(x)\right| \le \frac{C\rho}{\sigma^3\sqrt{n}}.$$
The rate $n^{-1/2}$ cannot be improved in general: a lattice variable has jumps of size of order $n^{-1/2}$.

### Checking the rate

For a fair coin the distribution function of $S_n$ is exact, so the Kolmogorov distance to $\Phi$ can be computed. The distance is the largest gap at an atom, measured on both sides of the jump:

```python
import math
from math import comb


def normal_cdf(x):
    return 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2)))


def binomial_distance(n, p):
    """sup_x |P(Bin(n,p) standardized <= x) - Phi(x)|."""
    sd = math.sqrt(n * p * (1 - p))
    below, worst = 0.0, 0.0
    for k in range(n + 1):
        z = (k - n * p) / sd
        above = below + comb(n, k) * p ** k * (1 - p) ** (n - k)
        worst = max(worst, abs(below - normal_cdf(z)),
                    abs(above - normal_cdf(z)))
        below = above
    return worst


print([round(math.sqrt(n) * binomial_distance(n, 0.5), 2)
       for n in (16, 64, 256)])
```

The output is `[0.39, 0.4, 0.4]`: the distance is about $0.4/\sqrt n$, consistent with Berry–Esseen. This is a numerical check of one family, not a proof. For qualifying exams, know the proof of Theorem 3 line by line, the statement of Theorem 4 with the Lindeberg condition, and the use of the normal approximation with a continuity correction for integer-valued sums.

@example
title: Normal approximation with a continuity correction
problem: A fair die is rolled $n = 100$ times. Approximate $P(S_{100} \le 330)$, where $S_{100}$ is the total of the faces.
step: One roll has mean $3.5$ and variance $\frac{35}{12}$, so $S_{100}$ has mean $350$ and variance $\frac{3500}{12} \approx 291.67$, standard deviation about $17.08$.
step: The total is integer-valued, so $\{S \le 330\} = \{S < 330.5\}$; the continuity correction uses the midpoint $330.5$.
step: $z = \dfrac{330.5 - 350}{17.08} \approx -1.14$.
step: By the central limit theorem $P(S_{100} \le 330) \approx \Phi(-1.14) \approx 0.127$.
answer: About $0.127$.

@example
title: The central limit theorem fails for scaled signs
problem: Let $\varepsilon_k$ be independent fair signs and $X_k = 2^k\varepsilon_k$. Show that $S_n/s_n \not\Rightarrow N(0,1)$, where $s_n^2 = \operatorname{Var}S_n$.
step: $s_n^2 = \sum_{k=1}^n 4^k = \frac{4^{n+1} - 4}{3}$, so $s_n \sim 2^{n+1}/\sqrt{3}$.
step: Deterministically $|S_n| \le \sum_{k \le n}2^k = 2^{n+1} - 2$, hence $|S_n|/s_n \le \dfrac{2^{n+1} - 2}{s_n} \to \sqrt{3} < 1.75$.
step: Therefore $P(|S_n/s_n| > 1.75) = 0$ for all large $n$, whereas $P(|Z| > 1.75) > 0$ for $Z \sim N(0,1)$.
step: Convergence in distribution would force $\liminf_n P(|S_n/s_n| > 1.75) \ge P(|Z| > 1.75) > 0$ (the set $\{|x| > 1.75\}$ is open, so the portmanteau theorem applies), a contradiction. The Lindeberg condition fails: the last term carries a fixed fraction of the variance.
answer: $S_n/s_n$ is bounded by about $\sqrt3$ and cannot converge to $N(0,1)$.

@mistakes
- Dividing by $n$ instead of $\sqrt{n}$: $S_n/n \to \mu$ (law of large numbers) while $(S_n - n\mu)/\sqrt n$ has a nondegenerate limit.
- Omitting the continuity correction for integer-valued sums, which costs an error of order $1/\sigma$ at a single threshold.
- Applying the central limit theorem without finite variance, or without independence; and confusing convergence in distribution with convergence of densities.
- Invoking the Lindeberg condition for bounded variables when $s_n$ stays bounded: the condition then fails, and the sum need not be asymptotically normal.

answer: 0.841

@practice self
prompt: Use the central limit theorem to prove that
$$\lim_{n \to \infty} e^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{n^k}{k!} = \frac12.$$
hint: Interpret $e^{-n}n^k/k!$ as a Poisson probability, and a Poisson$(n)$ variable as a sum of $n$ independent Poisson$(1)$ variables.
hint: Rewrite the sum as $P(S_n \le n)$ and standardize.
solution: Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be independent Poisson$(1)$ variables. Each has mean $1$ and variance $1$, and $S_n = X_1 + \dots + X_n$ is Poisson$(n)$ (characteristic functions multiply: $\exp(n(e^{it}-1))$). Thus $P(S_n \le n) = e^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}n^k/k!$. By the central limit theorem, $(S_n - n)/\sqrt{n} \Rightarrow Z \sim N(0,1)$, and since $\Phi$ is continuous, the distribution functions converge at every point, in particular at $0$: $P(S_n \le n) = P\bigl((S_n - n)/\sqrt n \le 0\bigr) \to \Phi(0) = \frac12$. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Identifies the sum with $P(S_n \le n)$ for a Poisson$(n)$ variable and justifies that it is a sum of $n$ independent Poisson$(1)$ variables.
rubric: States the hypotheses of the central limit theorem (mean and variance $1$, independent, identically distributed).
rubric: Notes that $0$ is a continuity point of $\Phi$ so the distribution functions converge there, and evaluates $\Phi(0) = \frac12$.

@quiz self
prompt: (Slutsky) Suppose $X_n \Rightarrow X$ and $Y_n \to c$ in probability, where $c$ is a constant. Prove that $X_n + Y_n \Rightarrow X + c$. You may use Lévy's continuity theorem.
hint: Compare the characteristic functions of $X_n + Y_n$ and $X_n + c$.
hint: Bound $|e^{itY_n} - e^{itc}|$ by $\min(|t||Y_n - c|, 2)$ and split on the event $|Y_n - c| \le \varepsilon$.
solution: Fix $t \in \mathbb{R}$. Then $\bigl|\mathbb{E}\,e^{it(X_n+Y_n)} - \mathbb{E}\,e^{it(X_n+c)}\bigr| \le \mathbb{E}\,|e^{itY_n} - e^{itc}| = \mathbb{E}\,|e^{it(Y_n - c)} - 1|$, since $|e^{itX_n}| = 1$. Because $|e^{iu} - 1| \le \min(|u|, 2)$, for any $\varepsilon > 0$ this is at most $|t|\varepsilon + 2P(|Y_n - c| > \varepsilon)$, whose limsup is at most $|t|\varepsilon$. Letting $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$ shows the difference tends to $0$. Next, $\mathbb{E}\,e^{it(X_n + c)} = e^{itc}\varphi_{X_n}(t) \to e^{itc}\varphi_X(t) = \varphi_{X+c}(t)$ by Lévy's theorem (a). So $\varphi_{X_n+Y_n}(t) \to \varphi_{X+c}(t)$ for every $t$, and the limit is a characteristic function, in particular continuous at $0$. By Lévy's theorem (b), $X_n + Y_n \Rightarrow X + c$. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Reduces to comparing characteristic functions and bounds the difference by $\mathbb{E}|e^{it(Y_n-c)} - 1|$.
rubric: Splits the expectation on $\{|Y_n - c| \le \varepsilon\}$ and its complement to show the bound tends to $0$.
rubric: Uses Lévy's theorem in both directions correctly to pass from $X_n \Rightarrow X$ to the characteristic function of $X_n + c$ and back to weak convergence.
