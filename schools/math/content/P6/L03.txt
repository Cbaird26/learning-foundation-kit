@article
Let $X_n = 1$ with probability $\frac1n$ and $0$ otherwise. Does $X_n \to 0$? In probability, yes: $P(X_n \ne 0) = \frac1n \to 0$. Pathwise, the answer depends on how the $X_n$ are coupled. Probability theory has several notions of convergence, and an exam answer must say which one is meant.

### Expectation and the basic inequalities

For a random variable $X \ge 0$, $\mathbb{E}X = \int X\,dP \in [0, \infty]$; in general $\mathbb{E}X = \mathbb{E}X^+ - \mathbb{E}X^-$ when at least one is finite, and $X$ is *integrable* when $\mathbb{E}|X| < \infty$. If $X$ has law $\mu$ and $g$ is Borel, then $\mathbb{E}\,g(X) = \int g\,d\mu$ whenever either side is defined (change of variables, from P1).

**Theorem 1 (Markov).** If $X$ is a random variable, $a > 0$ and $p > 0$, then $P(|X| \ge a) \le \mathbb{E}|X|^p / a^p$.

*Proof.* Pointwise, $a^p \mathbf{1}_{\{|X| \ge a\}} \le |X|^p$. Take expectations. $\blacksquare$

With $p = 2$ applied to $X - \mathbb{E}X$ this is **Chebyshev's inequality**: if $\operatorname{Var} X < \infty$ then $P(|X - \mathbb{E}X| \ge a) \le \operatorname{Var}(X)/a^2$.

**Theorem 2 (Jensen).** Let $\varphi\colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be convex, and let $X$ and $\varphi(X)$ be integrable. Then $\varphi(\mathbb{E}X) \le \mathbb{E}\,\varphi(X)$.

*Proof.* Let $m = \mathbb{E}X$. A convex function has a supporting line at $m$: there is $c$ with $\varphi(x) \ge \varphi(m) + c(x - m)$ for all $x$. Substitute $X$ and take expectations: $\mathbb{E}\varphi(X) \ge \varphi(m) + c(\mathbb{E}X - m) = \varphi(m)$. $\blacksquare$

We also use **Hölder's inequality**, $\mathbb{E}|XY| \le (\mathbb{E}|X|^p)^{1/p}(\mathbb{E}|Y|^q)^{1/q}$ for $p, q > 1$ with $\frac1p + \frac1q = 1$, and its case $p = q = 2$, the **Cauchy–Schwarz inequality**. On a probability space Hölder gives $\|X\|_r \le \|X\|_s$ for $r \le s$, so $L^s \subseteq L^r$.

### Four modes of convergence

Let $X_n, X$ be random variables on one probability space. Then $X_n \to X$

- **almost surely** if $P(\lim X_n = X) = 1$;
- **in probability** if $P(|X_n - X| > \varepsilon) \to 0$ for every $\varepsilon > 0$;
- **in $L^p$** ($p \ge 1$) if $\mathbb{E}|X_n - X|^p \to 0$;
- **in distribution** ($X_n \Rightarrow X$) if $F_n(x) \to F(x)$ at every continuity point $x$ of $F$.

**Theorem 3.** (a) Almost sure convergence implies convergence in probability. (b) Convergence in $L^p$ implies convergence in probability. (c) Convergence in probability implies convergence in distribution.

*Proof.* (a) Let $B_n = \{|X_k - X| > \varepsilon \text{ for some } k \ge n\}$. Then $B_n \downarrow B \subseteq \{X_n \not\to X\}$, so $P(B_n) \to P(B) = 0$ and $P(|X_n - X| > \varepsilon) \le P(B_n) \to 0$. (b) By Markov, $P(|X_n - X| > \varepsilon) \le \mathbb{E}|X_n - X|^p/\varepsilon^p \to 0$. (c) For $\varepsilon > 0$, $F_n(x) \le F(x + \varepsilon) + P(|X_n - X| > \varepsilon)$ and $F(x - \varepsilon) \le F_n(x) + P(|X_n - X| > \varepsilon)$. Let $n \to \infty$, then $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$ at a continuity point $x$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 4 (subsequence criterion).** $X_n \to X$ in probability if and only if every subsequence of $(X_n)$ has a further subsequence converging to $X$ almost surely.

*Proof.* ($\Rightarrow$) Given a subsequence, choose indices $n_1 < n_2 < \cdots$ in it with $P(|X_{n_k} - X| > 2^{-k}) < 2^{-k}$. The series of these probabilities converges, so by the first Borel–Cantelli lemma almost surely $|X_{n_k} - X| \le 2^{-k}$ for all large $k$, hence $X_{n_k} \to X$ a.s. ($\Leftarrow$) If $X_n \not\to X$ in probability, there are $\varepsilon, \delta > 0$ and a subsequence with $P(|X_{n_k} - X| > \varepsilon) \ge \delta$ for all $k$. A further subsequence converges a.s., hence in probability by (a), contradicting the bound. $\blacksquare$

### What fails

No other implication holds in general.

| sequence on $((0,1), \text{Lebesgue})$ | converges | does not converge |
|---|---|---|
| $X_n = \sqrt{n}\,\mathbf{1}_{(0, 1/n)}$ | a.s., in probability, in $L^1$ | in $L^2$ ($\mathbb{E}X_n^2 = 1$) |
| $X_n = n\,\mathbf{1}_{(0,1/n)}$ | a.s., in probability | in $L^1$ ($\mathbb{E}X_n = 1$) |
| typewriter sequence (below) | in probability, in $L^p$ for all $p$ | almost surely |
| $X_n = (-1)^n X$, $X$ symmetric, nonconstant | in distribution | in probability |

Convergence in distribution to a *constant* $c$ does imply convergence in probability, because $P(|X_n - c| > \varepsilon) \le F_n(c - \varepsilon) + 1 - F_n(c + \varepsilon) \to 0$. Convergence in probability plus uniform integrability gives $L^1$ convergence (Vitali's theorem, proved in P1 for general measure spaces; stated here without proof).

@example
title: The typewriter sequence
problem: For $n = 2^k + j$ with $k \ge 0$ and $0 \le j < 2^k$, let $I_n = [j 2^{-k}, (j+1)2^{-k})$ and $X_n = \mathbf{1}_{I_n}$ on $((0,1), \text{Lebesgue})$. Show that $X_n \to 0$ in $L^p$ for every $p \ge 1$ but not almost surely.
step: $\mathbb{E}|X_n|^p = |I_n| = 2^{-k}$, and $k \to \infty$ as $n \to \infty$. So $X_n \to 0$ in $L^p$ for every $p$, and hence in probability.
step: Fix $\omega \in [0,1)$. For each level $k$ exactly one interval $I_{2^k + j}$ contains $\omega$, so $X_n(\omega) = 1$ for at least one $n$ at every level, hence for infinitely many $n$.
step: Also $X_n(\omega) = 0$ for infinitely many $n$ (all other indices at each level). So $X_n(\omega)$ has no limit, for every $\omega$.
step: By Theorem 4, some subsequence converges a.s. to $0$: for example $n_k = 2^k$, where $I_{2^k} = [0, 2^{-k})$ and $X_{2^k}(\omega) = 0$ as soon as $2^{-k} \le \omega$ (with $\omega > 0$).
answer: $X_n \to 0$ in every $L^p$ and in probability, yet $X_n(\omega)$ converges for no $\omega \in [0,1)$.

@example
title: Which exponents give L^p convergence
problem: On $((0,1), \text{Lebesgue})$ let $X_n = n^{2} \mathbf{1}_{(0, n^{-3})}$. For which $p \ge 1$ does $X_n \to 0$ in $L^p$? Does $X_n \to 0$ almost surely?
step: For each $\omega \in (0,1)$, $X_n(\omega) = 0$ as soon as $n^{-3} \le \omega$. So $X_n \to 0$ pointwise, hence a.s. and in probability.
step: $\mathbb{E}|X_n|^p = n^{2p} \cdot n^{-3} = n^{2p - 3}$.
step: This tends to $0$ if and only if $2p - 3 < 0$, that is $p < \frac32$. At $p = \frac32$ it equals $1$, and for $p > \frac32$ it tends to $\infty$.
step: Hence $X_n \to 0$ in $L^1$ but not in $L^2$; the almost sure limit does not control the moments.
answer: $X_n \to 0$ a.s.; in $L^p$ exactly for $1 \le p < \frac32$.

@mistakes
- Believing convergence in probability implies almost sure convergence. It implies it only along a subsequence; the typewriter sequence converges in probability to $0$ but nowhere pointwise.
- Using Jensen with the wrong direction for a concave function: $\mathbb{E}\log X \le \log \mathbb{E}X$ for $X > 0$.
- Forgetting that convergence in distribution is a statement about laws only: $X_n$ and $X$ need not live on one probability space, and $(-1)^n X$ shows it does not imply convergence in probability.
- Applying Chebyshev's inequality without finite variance, or Markov's inequality to a random variable that may be negative without taking $|X|$.

answer: 0.25

@practice ms
prompt: Let $X_n$ and $X$ be random variables on a single probability space. Which implications hold in general?
choice*: Convergence in $L^2$ implies convergence in $L^1$.
choice*: Almost sure convergence implies convergence in probability.
choice: Convergence in probability implies almost sure convergence.
choice: Convergence in distribution implies convergence in probability.
choice: Convergence in $L^1$ implies almost sure convergence.
hint: Use $\|\cdot\|_1 \le \|\cdot\|_2$ on a probability space for the first, and recall the typewriter sequence for the failures.
solution: On a probability space, $\|Y\|_1 \le \|Y\|_2$ (Cauchy–Schwarz), so $L^2$ convergence implies $L^1$ convergence. Almost sure convergence implies convergence in probability by Theorem 3(a). The typewriter sequence converges in $L^1$ and in probability but not a.s.; and $(-1)^n X$ with $X$ symmetric and nonconstant converges in distribution but not in probability.

@practice num
prompt: On $((0,1), \text{Lebesgue})$ let $X_n = n^{3} \mathbf{1}_{(0, n^{-4})}$. Let $s$ be the supremum of those $p > 0$ for which $\mathbb{E}|X_n|^p \to 0$. Find $s$ exactly.
answer: 4/3
check: float(Fraction(4, 3))
tol: 0.001
hint: Compute $\mathbb{E}|X_n|^p$ as a power of $n$.
solution: $\mathbb{E}|X_n|^p = n^{3p} \cdot n^{-4} = n^{3p - 4}$, which tends to $0$ if and only if $3p < 4$. So the set of good $p$ is $(0, \frac43)$ and the supremum is $\frac43$ (at $p = \frac43$ the moment equals $1$).

@practice mc
prompt: Let $X$ be a random variable. Which inequality is true whenever the expectations involved are finite?
choice*: $\mathbb{E}\,e^{X} \ge e^{\mathbb{E}X}$.
choice: $\mathbb{E}\sqrt{|X|} \ge \sqrt{\mathbb{E}|X|}$.
choice: $\mathbb{E}|X|^3 \le |\mathbb{E}X|^3$.
choice: $\mathbb{E}\,e^{X} \le e^{\mathbb{E}X}$.
hint: Check the convexity or concavity of each function before applying Jensen.
solution: $x \mapsto e^x$ is convex, so Jensen gives $\mathbb{E}e^X \ge e^{\mathbb{E}X}$. The square root is concave, which reverses the inequality for $\mathbb{E}\sqrt{|X|}$. The cubic inequality fails for $X = \pm 1$ with equal probability ($\mathbb{E}|X|^3 = 1 > 0$), and $\mathbb{E}e^X \le e^{\mathbb{E}X}$ reverses Jensen.

@practice self
prompt: Let $X_n \to X$ in probability and let $f\colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be continuous. Prove that $f(X_n) \to f(X)$ in probability. (Do not assume $f$ is uniformly continuous or $X$ bounded.)
hint: Use the subsequence criterion in both directions.
hint: Given any subsequence, extract a further subsequence along which $X_n \to X$ almost surely, and then use continuity pointwise.
solution: $f$ is Borel, so $f(X_n)$ and $f(X)$ are random variables. Let $(n_k)$ be any subsequence. Since $X_n \to X$ in probability, Theorem 4 ($\Rightarrow$) gives a further subsequence $(n_{k_j})$ with $X_{n_{k_j}} \to X$ almost surely. On the probability-one event where this holds, continuity of $f$ gives $f(X_{n_{k_j}}) \to f(X)$ pointwise. Thus every subsequence of $f(X_n)$ has a further subsequence converging to $f(X)$ almost surely. By Theorem 4 ($\Leftarrow$), $f(X_n) \to f(X)$ in probability. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Takes an arbitrary subsequence and extracts an almost surely convergent further subsequence of $X_n$.
rubric: Applies continuity of $f$ pointwise on the almost sure event.
rubric: Invokes the reverse direction of the subsequence criterion to conclude convergence in probability of the whole sequence.

@quiz num

answer: 0.1875

@quiz mc
prompt: The random variables $X_n$ are independent with $P(X_n = 1) = \frac1n$ and $P(X_n = 0) = 1 - \frac1n$. Which statement is true?
choice*: $X_n \to 0$ in probability and in $L^1$, but not almost surely.
choice: $X_n \to 0$ almost surely, because $P(X_n = 1) \to 0$.
choice: $X_n$ does not converge to $0$ in probability.
choice: $X_n \to 0$ almost surely but not in $L^1$.
hint: Compare $P(X_n \ne 0)$ and $\mathbb{E}|X_n|$ with the Borel–Cantelli series.
solution: $P(|X_n| > \varepsilon) \le \frac1n \to 0$ and $\mathbb{E}|X_n| = \frac1n \to 0$, so $X_n \to 0$ in probability and in $L^1$. The events $\{X_n = 1\}$ are independent with $\sum 1/n = \infty$, so by the second Borel–Cantelli lemma $X_n = 1$ infinitely often almost surely; hence $X_n$ does not converge almost surely.
