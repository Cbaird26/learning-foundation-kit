@article
A fair coin is flipped $n$ times. The fraction of heads should be near $\frac12$, but "near" can mean two different things: for a given large $n$ the fraction is probably close to $\frac12$ (a *weak* law), or along a single infinite sequence of flips the fractions eventually stay close (a *strong* law). The two statements are the two modes of convergence from last week.

Throughout, $S_n = X_1 + \dots + X_n$.

### The weak law

**Theorem 1 (weak law, $L^2$ version).** Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be pairwise uncorrelated with $\mathbb{E}X_i = \mu$ and $\operatorname{Var}X_i \le C < \infty$. Then $S_n/n \to \mu$ in $L^2$, hence in probability, and
$$P\left(\left|\frac{S_n}{n} - \mu\right| \ge \varepsilon\right) \le \frac{C}{n\varepsilon^2}.$$

*Proof.* Uncorrelatedness means the covariances vanish, so $\operatorname{Var}(S_n) = \sum_i \operatorname{Var}X_i \le nC$ and $\mathbb{E}|S_n/n - \mu|^2 = \operatorname{Var}(S_n)/n^2 \le C/n \to 0$. Chebyshev's inequality for $S_n/n$ gives the displayed bound. $\blacksquare$

To guarantee $P(|S_n/n - \mu| \ge \varepsilon) \le \delta$ it suffices that $n \ge C/(\varepsilon^2 \delta)$. For a poll of Bernoulli variables, $C = \frac14$, so $\varepsilon = 0.04$ and $\delta = 0.05$ need $n \ge 3125$.

### The strong law, first version

**Theorem 2.** Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be independent and identically distributed with $\mathbb{E}X_1^4 < \infty$ and $\mathbb{E}X_1 = \mu$. Then $S_n/n \to \mu$ almost surely.

*Proof.* Replacing $X_i$ by $X_i - \mu$ (which still has a finite fourth moment, by Minkowski's inequality) we may assume $\mu = 0$. Expand $S_n^4 = \sum_{i,j,k,l \le n} X_i X_j X_k X_l$. If some index occurs exactly once in $(i,j,k,l)$, independence and $\mathbb{E}X = 0$ make the term's expectation $0$. The surviving terms have all four indices equal ($n$ terms, each with expectation $\mathbb{E}X^4$) or two distinct indices each occurring twice ($3n(n-1)$ ordered terms, each with expectation $(\mathbb{E}X^2)^2$). So
$$\mathbb{E}S_n^4 = n\,\mathbb{E}X^4 + 3n(n-1)(\mathbb{E}X^2)^2 \le 3n^2\,\mathbb{E}X^4,$$
using $(\mathbb{E}X^2)^2 \le \mathbb{E}X^4$ (Cauchy–Schwarz). Markov's inequality with $p = 4$ gives $P(|S_n| \ge \varepsilon n) \le \dfrac{3\,\mathbb{E}X^4}{\varepsilon^4 n^2}$, which is summable in $n$. By the first Borel–Cantelli lemma, almost surely $|S_n|/n < \varepsilon$ for all large $n$. Intersecting over $\varepsilon = 1/m$, $m \in \mathbb{N}$, gives $S_n/n \to 0$ almost surely. $\blacksquare$

### Kolmogorov's strong law

**Theorem 3 (Kolmogorov).** If $X_1, X_2, \dots$ are independent and identically distributed with $\mathbb{E}|X_1| < \infty$, then $S_n/n \to \mathbb{E}X_1$ almost surely. Conversely, if $\mathbb{E}|X_1| = \infty$ then $\limsup |S_n|/n = \infty$ almost surely. If $\mathbb{E}X_1^- < \infty$ and $\mathbb{E}X_1^+ = \infty$, then $S_n/n \to +\infty$ almost surely.

These statements are quoted here without proof. Etemadi's proof of the first part needs only pairwise independence; it truncates at level $i$, applies Chebyshev along the subsequence $\lfloor \alpha^k \rfloor$ and the first Borel–Cantelli lemma, and interpolates using monotonicity for $X_i \ge 0$. Without finite mean, no strong law holds: if $X_i$ are standard Cauchy, then $S_n/n$ has the same Cauchy law as $X_1$ for every $n$ (shown with characteristic functions next week), so it cannot converge to a constant even in probability.

### Simulation and the rate

Chebyshev's bound is usually far from the truth. The code estimates $P(|S_n/n - p| > \varepsilon)$ for Bernoulli variables and compares it with the bound $p(1-p)/(n\varepsilon^2)$.

```python
import random


def freq_exceeding(rng, n, p, eps, trials):
    """Fraction of trials with |S_n/n - p| > eps."""
    count = 0
    for _ in range(trials):
        s = sum(1 for _ in range(n) if rng.random() < p)
        if abs(s / n - p) > eps:
            count += 1
    return count / trials


rng = random.Random(11)
n, p, eps = 100, 0.5, 0.1
estimate = freq_exceeding(rng, n, p, eps, 300)
bound = p * (1 - p) / (n * eps ** 2)
print(estimate <= bound, bound)
```

The bound is $0.25$ while the true probability is about $0.035$, and the estimate is a random number with its own error of order $\sqrt{0.035 \cdot 0.965/300} \approx 0.01$. A simulation checks a rate; it does not prove one. On exams, Chebyshev with a variance computation is the standard weak-law proof, and the fourth-moment expansion above is the standard first strong-law proof.

@example
title: Sample size from Chebyshev
problem: Independent Bernoulli variables have unknown success probability $p$. How many are enough so that the sample mean is within $0.04$ of $p$ with probability at least $0.95$, using only Chebyshev's inequality?
step: $\operatorname{Var}X_i = p(1-p) \le \frac14$ for every $p \in [0,1]$, because $p(1-p)$ has maximum $\frac14$ at $p = \frac12$.
step: Chebyshev gives $P(|S_n/n - p| \ge 0.04) \le \dfrac{1/4}{n\,(0.04)^2} = \dfrac{156.25}{n}$.
step: Require $\dfrac{156.25}{n} \le 0.05$, that is $n \ge 3125$.
step: This bound is sufficient and conservative: the central limit theorem (week 6) suggests about $600$ suffice for the same guarantee.
answer: $n = 3125$ suffices by Chebyshev.

@example
title: The fourth moment of a sum of signs
problem: Let $X_i$ be independent with $P(X_i = 1) = P(X_i = -1) = \frac12$. Compute $\mathbb{E}S_n^4$ and verify it for $n = 2$.
step: $\mathbb{E}X^2 = 1$ and $\mathbb{E}X^4 = 1$. The formula from the proof gives $\mathbb{E}S_n^4 = n \cdot 1 + 3n(n-1)\cdot 1 = 3n^2 - 2n$.
step: For $n = 2$, $S_2 \in \{-2, 0, 2\}$ with probabilities $\frac14, \frac12, \frac14$, so $\mathbb{E}S_2^4 = 16 \cdot \frac14 + 16 \cdot \frac14 = 8$.
step: The formula gives $3 \cdot 4 - 4 = 8$. The two agree.
step: Markov then gives $P(|S_n| \ge \varepsilon n) \le \dfrac{3n^2 - 2n}{\varepsilon^4 n^4} < \dfrac{3}{\varepsilon^4 n^2}$, summable, so the strong law holds for fair signs.
answer: $\mathbb{E}S_n^4 = 3n^2 - 2n$.

@mistakes
- Using Chebyshev without checking that the variance is finite, then "proving" a law of large numbers for Cauchy variables.
- Confusing the two laws: the weak law bounds a probability for each $n$; the strong law makes a statement about almost every infinite sequence.
- Forgetting that $S_n/n \to \mu$ needs $\mu$ to exist: for $\mathbb{E}|X| = \infty$ the averages need not converge, and the Cauchy averages do not.
- Writing $\mathbb{E}S_n^4 = n\,\mathbb{E}X^4$; the cross terms $3n(n-1)(\mathbb{E}X^2)^2$ are of order $n^2$ and are exactly what makes the Borel–Cantelli series converge.

model:
```python
from fractions import Fraction


def last_exit(draws, p, eps):
    """Last index k with |S_k/k - p| > eps (0 if none)."""
    last = 0
    total = 0
    for k, x in enumerate(draws, start=1):
        total += x
        if abs(Fraction(total, k) - p) > eps:
            last = k
    return last
```
hint: Keep a running sum and compare each running mean with `p`; remember the latest index that violates the tolerance.
hint: The inequality is strict: a deviation exactly equal to `eps` is not a violation.
solution: Scan once, updating the running sum, and record every $k$ at which the deviation strictly exceeds `eps`; the last recorded $k$ is the answer. A strong law says this index is finite almost surely, a statement about whole sequences that no finite list can verify. Returning the first violation instead would answer a different question.

@practice self
prompt: Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be independent (not necessarily identically distributed) random variables with $\mathbb{E}X_i = 0$ and $\mathbb{E}X_i^4 \le K < \infty$ for all $i$. Prove that $S_n/n \to 0$ almost surely.
hint: Expand $\mathbb{E}S_n^4$ and keep only the terms in which every index appears at least twice.
hint: Bound $\mathbb{E}X_i^2 \le (\mathbb{E}X_i^4)^{1/2}$ and then apply Markov's inequality with $p = 4$ and the first Borel–Cantelli lemma.
solution: Expand $\mathbb{E}S_n^4 = \sum_{i,j,k,l} \mathbb{E}[X_iX_jX_kX_l]$. If an index occurs exactly once among $i,j,k,l$, independence and mean zero (all moments are finite since $\mathbb{E}X_i^4 \le K$) make the term vanish. What remains is $\sum_i \mathbb{E}X_i^4$ plus $3\sum_{i \ne j}\mathbb{E}X_i^2\,\mathbb{E}X_j^2$ (each unordered pair $\{i, j\}$ arises from $6$ tuples). By Cauchy–Schwarz $\mathbb{E}X_i^2 \le \sqrt{K}$, so $\mathbb{E}S_n^4 \le nK + 3n(n-1)K \le 3n^2K$. For $\varepsilon > 0$, Markov's inequality with $p = 4$ gives $P(|S_n| \ge \varepsilon n) \le 3K/(\varepsilon^4 n^2)$, which is summable. By the first Borel–Cantelli lemma, almost surely $|S_n|/n < \varepsilon$ for all large $n$. Taking $\varepsilon = 1/m$ over all $m \in \mathbb{N}$ gives $S_n/n \to 0$ almost surely. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Correctly identifies which terms of the fourth-power expansion survive and justifies the others vanish by independence and mean zero.
rubric: Bounds $\mathbb{E}S_n^4$ by a constant times $n^2$ using Cauchy–Schwarz without assuming identical distribution.
rubric: Applies Markov with $p = 4$, notes the series is summable, and uses Borel–Cantelli plus a countable intersection over $\varepsilon = 1/m$.

@quiz mc
prompt: Let $X_i$ be independent and identically distributed with $\mathbb{E}X_1^- < \infty$ and $\mathbb{E}X_1^+ = \infty$. What does Kolmogorov's strong law (in its extended form) say about $S_n/n$?
