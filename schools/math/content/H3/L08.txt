@article
Rectangular coordinates locate a point by two steps along perpendicular axes: $x$ across and $y$ up. **Polar coordinates** locate it the way a radar screen does: choose a direction, then say how far to go.

### The polar system

Fix a center $O$ (the **pole**) and a ray from it (the **polar axis**, the positive $x$-axis). The point $P = (r, \theta)$ is found by turning counterclockwise through $\theta$ from the polar axis and then moving out a distance $r$ in that direction. If $r$ is negative, you move $|r|$ in the **opposite** direction.

Unlike rectangular coordinates, polar names are not unique:

$$(r, \theta) = (r, \theta + 2\pi k) = (-r, \theta + \pi).$$

The pole itself is $(0, \theta)$ for any $\theta$. For a unique name we usually ask for $r \ge 0$ and $0 \le \theta < 2\pi$.

### Converting

The unit-circle point at angle $\theta$ is $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$. Scaling by $r$ gives

$$x = r\cos\theta, \qquad y = r\sin\theta, \qquad r^2 = x^2 + y^2, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{y}{x}.$$

**Be careful with the angle.** The equation $\tan\theta = y/x$ has two solutions per turn, and $\arctan$ returns only angles in $(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2})$. For the point $(-1, 1)$ it gives $\arctan(-1) = -\frac{\pi}{4}$, which points into Quadrant IV, but the point is in Quadrant II, so $\theta = \frac{3\pi}{4}$. Decide the quadrant from the signs of $x$ and $y$, or use `atan2(y, x)`.

```python
import math
r, theta = 2.0, 5*math.pi/6
x, y = r*math.cos(theta), r*math.sin(theta)
print(round(x, 3), round(y, 3))                                # -1.732 1.0
print(round(math.hypot(x, y), 3), round(math.atan2(y, x), 3))  # 2.0 2.618
```

### Polar equations and curves

A polar equation relates $r$ and $\theta$. The simplest ones are:

- $r = a$: a circle of radius $|a|$ centered at the pole.
- $\theta = \alpha$: a line through the pole.
- $r = a\cos\theta$ or $r = a\sin\theta$: a circle through the pole with diameter $|a|$.
- $r = a(1 + \cos\theta)$: a heart-shaped **cardioid**.
- $r = \theta$: a spiral that moves outward as the angle grows.

To identify an unfamiliar curve, convert it to rectangular form using the four relations above. For $r = a\cos\theta$, first multiply both sides by $r$, so that $r^2$ and $r\cos\theta$ appear and can be replaced by $x^2 + y^2$ and $x$.

To sketch a curve, tabulate $r$ at multiples of $\frac{\pi}{6}$ or $\frac{\pi}{4}$, plot the points, and watch where $r$ changes sign: there the curve passes through the pole.

@example
title: From rectangular to polar
problem: Write the point $(-3, 3)$ in polar form with $r > 0$ and $0 \le \theta < 2\pi$, and give a second polar name with $r < 0$.
step: Find the distance from the origin: $r = \sqrt{(-3)^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{18} = 3\sqrt{2}$.
step: Find the angle. Here $\tan\theta = \frac{3}{-3} = -1$, so the reference angle is $\frac{\pi}{4}$. The point has $x < 0$ and $y > 0$, so it lies in Quadrant II and $\theta = \pi - \frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{3\pi}{4}$.
step: So the point is $\left(3\sqrt{2}, \frac{3\pi}{4}\right)$.
step: For a negative $r$, flip the sign of $r$ and add $\pi$ to the angle: $\left(-3\sqrt{2}, \frac{7\pi}{4}\right)$. Check by converting back: $-3\sqrt{2}\cos\frac{7\pi}{4} = -3$ and $-3\sqrt{2}\sin\frac{7\pi}{4} = 3$.

@example
title: Converting a polar equation
problem: Convert $r = 4\sin\theta$ to rectangular form and identify the curve.
step: Multiply both sides by $r$ so that the equation is expressed in terms that have rectangular equivalents: $r^2 = 4r\sin\theta$.
step: Substitute $r^2 = x^2 + y^2$ and $r\sin\theta = y$: $x^2 + y^2 = 4y$.
step: Complete the square in $y$: $x^2 + y^2 - 4y + 4 = 4$, so $x^2 + (y - 2)^2 = 4$.
step: This is a circle with center $(0, 2)$ and radius $2$. It passes through the pole, as expected for $r = a\sin\theta$ with diameter $a = 4$.

@mistakes
- Using $\theta = \arctan(y/x)$ without checking the quadrant. Every point with $x < 0$ comes out half a turn off.
- Ignoring the sign of $r$. The point $\left(-2, \frac{\pi}{3}\right)$ is on the opposite side of the pole from $\left(2, \frac{\pi}{3}\right)$.
- Believing a point has only one polar name. $\left(2, \frac{\pi}{3}\right)$, $\left(2, \frac{7\pi}{3}\right)$ and $\left(-2, \frac{4\pi}{3}\right)$ are the same point.
- Typing degrees into a calculator that is in radian mode.

@practice num
prompt: The polar curve $r = 6\cos\theta$ is a circle. Find the area it encloses. Give an exact answer.
answer: 9pi
check: quad(lambda t: 0.5*(6*cos(t))**2, -pi/2, pi/2)
hint: Multiply both sides by $r$ and convert to rectangular coordinates.
hint: You should get $x^2 + y^2 = 6x$. Complete the square in $x$ to read off the radius, then use $\pi r^2$.
solution: $r^2 = 6r\cos\theta$ becomes $x^2 + y^2 = 6x$, so $(x - 3)^2 + y^2 = 9$: a circle of radius $3$. Its area is $\pi\cdot 3^2 = 9\pi$.

@practice num
prompt: Two points are given in polar form: $P = \left(3, \frac{\pi}{6}\right)$ and $Q = \left(5, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$. Find the distance between them. Give an exact answer.
answer: sqrt(19)
check: norm([3*cos(pi/6) - 5*cos(pi/2), 3*sin(pi/6) - 5*sin(pi/2)])
hint: The pole, $P$ and $Q$ form a triangle with two known sides. What is the angle between them at the pole?
hint: The angle at the pole is $\frac{\pi}{2} - \frac{\pi}{6}$. Apply the law of cosines (or convert both points to rectangular coordinates).
solution: The angle at the pole is $\frac{\pi}{3}$, so $PQ^2 = 3^2 + 5^2 - 2(3)(5)\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = 34 - 15 = 19$ and $PQ = \sqrt{19}$. Check with rectangular coordinates: $P = \left(\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}, \frac{3}{2}\right)$ and $Q = (0, 5)$, giving $PQ^2 = \frac{27}{4} + \frac{49}{4} = 19$.
