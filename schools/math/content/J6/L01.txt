@article
**Matter** is anything that has mass and takes up space. A rock, the water in a glass and the air in a balloon are all matter. Light and heat are not matter, because they have no mass.

### Particles in solids, liquids and gases

All matter is made of tiny particles. In a **solid** the particles are packed tightly and only vibrate in place, so a solid keeps its shape and volume. In a **liquid** the particles touch but slide past each other, so a liquid keeps its volume but takes the shape of its container. In a **gas** the particles are far apart and move freely, so a gas fills whatever container it is in.

### Atoms

The smallest unit of an element that still has the properties of that element is the **atom**. An atom has three kinds of particles:

| Particle | Charge | Where it is |
|---|---|---|
| Proton | $+1$ | in the nucleus |
| Neutron | $0$ | in the nucleus |
| Electron | $-1$ | in a cloud around the nucleus |

The **atomic number** is the number of protons. It decides which element the atom is: every atom with 6 protons is carbon, and no other element has 6 protons. A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons, so the charges cancel. The **mass number** is the number of protons plus the number of neutrons. Electrons are so light that they add almost nothing to the mass.

$$\text{neutrons} = \text{mass number} - \text{atomic number}$$

### Elements, compounds and mixtures

An **element** contains only one kind of atom (oxygen, iron, gold). A **compound** is a substance in which two or more elements are chemically joined in a fixed ratio, such as water, $\mathrm{H_2O}$. A **mixture** is two or more substances that are only mixed together, not chemically joined, such as salt water or air. A mixture can be separated by physical means, such as evaporation or filtering. A compound cannot.

### Density

**Density** tells you how much mass is packed into a given volume:

$$\text{density} = \frac{\text{mass}}{\text{volume}}$$

In the lab, mass is measured in grams (g) and volume in $\mathrm{cm^3}$ or mL, so density is in g/$\mathrm{cm^3}$. Water has a density of about $1.0\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}$. Objects with a lower density float in it.

@example
title: Counting the particles in an atom
problem: A neutral sodium atom has atomic number $11$ and mass number $23$. How many protons, neutrons and electrons does it have?
step: The atomic number is the number of protons, so there are $11$ protons.
step: A neutral atom has as many electrons as protons, so there are $11$ electrons.
step: Neutrons equal mass number minus atomic number: $23 - 11 = 12$ neutrons.
step: Check the mass number: $11$ protons $+\ 12$ neutrons $= 23$.

@example
title: Mixture or compound?
problem: A student stirs sand into water, then pours the mixture through filter paper. The sand stays on the paper. Is the sand-water combination a compound or a mixture?
step: Ask whether the parts can be separated by a physical method. Filtering is a physical method.
step: Since the sand was separated by filtering and nothing new was formed, the sand and water were never chemically joined.
step: So it is a mixture.

@mistakes
- Thinking the mass number is the number of protons. The atomic number is the proton count, and the mass number counts protons plus neutrons.
- Calling any substance with more than one ingredient a compound. A mixture has ingredients that are not chemically joined and can have any amounts.
- Saying that atoms in a gas are smaller or lighter than atoms in a solid. The atoms are the same; only the spacing and motion change.
- Forgetting that a neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons.

@practice self
prompt: Describe how the particles are arranged and how they move in a solid, a liquid and a gas. Then use your descriptions to explain why a gas can be squeezed into a smaller container but a solid cannot.
hint: Compare how close the particles are and whether they can move past each other.
solution: In a solid the particles are tightly packed and vibrate in fixed positions. In a liquid they touch but slide past one another. In a gas they are far apart and move freely. A gas can be compressed because there is a lot of empty space between its particles, so they can be pushed closer together. In a solid the particles are already touching, so there is almost no space to squeeze into.
rubric: Says solid particles are tightly packed and vibrate in place.
rubric: Says liquid particles touch but can slide past each other.
rubric: Says gas particles are far apart and move freely.
rubric: Explains compression using the empty space between gas particles.
