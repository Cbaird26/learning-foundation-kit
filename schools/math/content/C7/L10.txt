@article
A point estimate such as $\bar x = 52.3$ says nothing about its precision. A **confidence interval** (CI) adds a range:

$$\text{estimate} \pm \text{margin of error}, \qquad \text{margin} = (\text{critical value}) \times \text{SE}.$$

### What "95% confident" means

The procedure, not the particular interval, carries the probability. Before sampling, the endpoints are random, and a 95% procedure produces an interval that covers the true parameter in 95% of repeated samples. After sampling, your one interval either contains the parameter or it does not. So "there is a 95% probability that $\mu$ lies in $(48.9, 55.7)$" is the wrong reading: $\mu$ is a fixed number. A CI is also not a range for individual observations.

### Standard intervals

| Situation | Interval | Critical value |
|---|---|---|
| Mean, $\sigma$ known (or $n$ large) | $\bar x \pm z^*\,\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}$ | $z^* = 1.645,\ 1.960,\ 2.576$ for $90\%,\ 95\%,\ 99\%$ |
| Mean, $\sigma$ unknown, roughly normal data | $\bar x \pm t^*_{n-1}\,\dfrac{s}{\sqrt n}$ | $t^*_{n-1} > z^*$; for $n = 10$ and $95\%$, $t^* = 2.262$ |
| Proportion | $\hat p \pm z^*\sqrt{\dfrac{\hat p(1 - \hat p)}{n}}$ | $z^*$, when $n\hat p$ and $n(1 - \hat p)$ are at least about $10$ |

Higher confidence needs a larger critical value, so the interval widens. More data shrinks the margin like $1/\sqrt n$, so quadrupling $n$ halves the width.

### Planning the sample size

To get a margin of at most $m$ for a mean, solve $z^*\sigma/\sqrt n \le m$:

$$n \ge \left(\frac{z^*\sigma}{m}\right)^2.$$

For a proportion with no prior estimate, use $p(1 - p) \le \frac{1}{4}$, which gives $n \ge \left(\frac{z^*}{2m}\right)^2$. Always round $n$ **up**.

### Checking coverage by simulation

Coverage is a long-run property, so you can test it: simulate many samples from a known population, build the interval from each, and count how often it covers the truth.

```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
mu, sigma, n, reps = 5.0, 2.0, 10, 20000
x = rng.normal(mu, sigma, size=(reps, n))
xbar, s = x.mean(axis=1), x.std(axis=1, ddof=1)
se = s / np.sqrt(n)
for name, crit in (("z = 1.96", 1.96), ("t = 2.262", 2.262)):
    covered = (xbar - crit * se <= mu) & (mu <= xbar + crit * se)
    print(name, covered.mean())     # about 0.92 and 0.95
```

Using $z^* = 1.96$ with an estimated $s$ and only $n = 10$ observations covers about $92\%$ of the time, not $95\%$. The $t$ critical value repairs this by accounting for the randomness of $s$. Every nominal level also assumes the model is right (independent observations, roughly normal data for small $n$), and a simulation lets you see what happens when it is not.

@example
title: A t interval for a mean
problem: A sample of $n = 16$ measurements has mean $52.3$ and standard deviation $s = 6.4$. Find a 95% confidence interval for the population mean, assuming roughly normal data.
step: Collect the summaries: $\bar x = 52.3$, $s = 6.4$, $n = 16$, so there are $n - 1 = 15$ degrees of freedom.
step: Standard error: $\text{SE} = \dfrac{s}{\sqrt{n}} = \dfrac{6.4}{4} = 1.6$.
step: Critical value: for 95% confidence, $t^*_{15} = 2.131$ (the $0.975$ quantile of the $t$ distribution with $15$ degrees of freedom, from a table or software). This is larger than $z^* = 1.96$.
step: Margin of error: $2.131 \times 1.6 \approx 3.41$.
step: Interval: $52.3 \pm 3.41$, that is $(48.89,\ 55.71)$.
step: Interpretation: the method that produced this interval covers the true mean in about 95% of repeated samples. It does not say that $\mu$ has a 95% chance of being in this particular interval.

@example
title: A poll interval and the sample size to improve it
problem: In a poll, $520$ of $1000$ respondents favor a measure. Find a 95% confidence interval for the population proportion. Then find the sample size needed for a margin of error of at most $0.02$ when nothing is known in advance about $p$.
step: The estimate is $\hat p = 0.52$. Both $n\hat p = 520$ and $n(1 - \hat p) = 480$ exceed $10$, so the normal approximation is reasonable.
step: Standard error: $\sqrt{\dfrac{0.52 \cdot 0.48}{1000}} = \sqrt{0.0002496} \approx 0.0158$.
step: Margin: $1.96 \times 0.0158 \approx 0.031$, so the interval is $0.52 \pm 0.031 = (0.489,\ 0.551)$. It contains $0.5$, so the poll does not establish a majority.
step: Planning: with the worst case $p(1 - p) \le \frac{1}{4}$ we need $n \ge \left(\dfrac{1.96}{2 \cdot 0.02}\right)^2 = 49^2 = 2401$.

@mistakes
- Reading a computed 95% interval as "$\mu$ has a 95% probability of being inside". The 95% describes the procedure over repeated samples.
- Using $z^*$ instead of $t^*$ with a small sample and an estimated standard deviation, which makes the interval too narrow and the coverage too low.
- Treating the interval as the range where 95% of the data fall. It is a statement about the mean (or proportion), not about individual values.
- Rounding a required sample size to the nearest integer instead of up, or using the proportion interval when $n\hat p$ or $n(1 - \hat p)$ is tiny.

@practice num
prompt: A sample of $n = 36$ observations has mean $20$ and the population standard deviation is known to be $\sigma = 6$. Find the lower endpoint of the 95% confidence interval for the mean (use $z^* = 1.96$).
answer: 18.04
check: 20 - 1.96 * 6 / sqrt(36)
tol: 0.005
hint: The standard error is $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$.
solution: $\text{SE} = \frac{6}{6} = 1$, so the margin is $1.96$ and the interval is $20 \pm 1.96$. The lower endpoint is $18.04$.

answer: 217

solution: $\frac{1.96 \cdot 15}{2} = 14.7$ and $14.7^2 = 216.09$, so $n \ge 216.09$. Rounding up gives $n = 217$.

solution: One way to write it:
```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
mu, sigma, n, reps = 5.0, 2.0, 10, 20000
x = rng.normal(mu, sigma, size=(reps, n))
```

Typical output is about $0.92$ for the $z$ interval and about $0.95$ for the $t$ interval. The $z$ interval ignores that $s$ is itself random and often too small when $n = 10$, so its intervals are too narrow and miss $\mu$ more often than $5\%$ of the time. @quiz num
prompt: A researcher builds a "95%" interval for a normal mean by using $z^* = 1.96$ with the estimated standard deviation $s$ and only $n = 10$ observations. The exact coverage is $P(|T| \le 1.96)$, where $T$ has a $t$ distribution with $9$ degrees of freedom. Find this coverage, rounded to three decimal places.
answer: 0.918
check: 2 * special.stdtr(9, 1.96) - 1
tol: 0.003
hint: With $\sigma$ replaced by $s$, the standardized mean $T = (\bar X - \mu)/(s/\sqrt{n})$ has a $t_{9}$ distribution, and the interval covers $\mu$ exactly when $|T| \le 1.96$.
hint: Use the $t_9$ cdf $F$: the coverage is $F(1.96) - F(-1.96) = 2F(1.96) - 1$. In Python, `scipy.stats.t.cdf(1.96, 9)` gives $F(1.96)$.
solution: The $t_9$ cdf at $1.96$ is about $0.959$, so the coverage is $2(0.959) - 1 \approx 0.918$, noticeably below the nominal $0.95$. Using the critical value $t^*_9 = 2.262$ instead gives exactly $0.95$.

@quiz num
prompt: A sample of $n = 25$ observations has mean $100$ and standard deviation $s = 15$. Find the upper endpoint of the 95% $t$ interval for the mean, using $t^*_{24} = 2.064$. Round to two decimal places.
answer: 106.19
check: 100 + special.stdtrit(24, 0.975) * 15 / sqrt(25)
tol: 0.02
hint: The standard error is $s/\sqrt{n}$.
hint: The upper endpoint is $\bar x + t^* \cdot \text{SE}$ with $t^* = 2.064$.
solution: $\text{SE} = \frac{15}{5} = 3$, so the margin is $2.064 \times 3 = 6.192$ and the upper endpoint is $100 + 6.19 = 106.19$.
