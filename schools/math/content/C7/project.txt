@project
title: A preregistered simulated experiment
overview: You will design, preregister and analyze a simulated two-group experiment in which you control the true effect, so you can check your methods against the truth. You will plan the sample size with a power calculation, estimate the effect with a confidence interval, check assumptions with residuals, and use repeated simulation to measure Type I error, power and interval coverage. You will also write up a null result honestly.
goal: Plan a study before seeing any data: state the hypotheses, a data model, a sample size justified by power, and a single analysis plan.
goal: Estimate an effect with uncertainty, and verify by repeated simulation that your error rates and interval coverage behave as claimed.
goal: Report assumptions, limitations and a null result truthfully, with code that anyone can rerun.
step: Choose a question and a data model, for example "does a new study technique raise quiz scores?", with control scores $\text{Normal}(70, 10^2)$ and a treatment effect that you set. Write down the model, the smallest effect of interest (for example $3$ points), the primary outcome and the sample size per group before generating any data.
step: Preregister the analysis in a short dated document: the hypotheses, a two-sided test at $\alpha = 0.05$ (name the test), the estimate and 95% interval you will report, exclusion rules, a fixed sample size with no peeking, and what you will do if the result is null.
step: Do the power calculation by formula and check it by simulation. Find the sample size per group that gives $80\%$ power at the smallest effect of interest, then confirm with at least $5000$ simulated experiments that the rejection rate matches.
step: Generate one data set with a fixed seed and run the preregistered analysis exactly once. Report the estimated difference, its 95% confidence interval and a standardized effect size, and make residual or spread diagnostics for both groups.
step: Repeat the whole experiment at least $5000$ times in three worlds: no true effect, an effect equal to the smallest effect of interest, and an effect half that size. Record the rejection rate, the coverage of the 95% interval and the average estimate among the significant results only. Report the Monte Carlo error of each rate.
step: Write the null-result section for a world where the true effect is zero or small. Report the estimate and interval, compare the interval with the smallest effect of interest, and do not say "no effect" unless the interval rules out the effects you care about. Mark any deviation from your preregistration as a deviation.
step: Assemble a one- to two-page report and a single script that reproduces every number and figure from a seed, and list the software versions you used.
deliverable: A dated preregistration document, written before any data are generated.
deliverable: One script or notebook that reruns from scratch with a fixed seed and reproduces all results and figures.
deliverable: A one- to two-page report with the estimate and interval, diagnostics, simulation evidence on error rates and coverage, a null-result paragraph and a limitations section.
rubric: The preregistration is complete and unambiguous: hypotheses, data model, smallest effect of interest, sample size with a power justification, test, $\alpha$, stopping rule and exclusions.
rubric: The power calculation is correct and agrees with the simulation within Monte Carlo error.
rubric: The effect estimate and 95% interval are correct and interpreted as properties of the procedure; an effect size is reported, not only a p-value; residual diagnostics are shown and discussed.
rubric: The simulations show a Type I error rate near $\alpha$, power near the target and coverage near 95%, with Monte Carlo error stated, and the inflation of estimates among significant results is noticed and explained.
rubric: The null result is reported honestly, without accepting $H_0$ or changing the analysis after the fact, and deviations are labeled.
rubric: The code is reproducible (seeded and runs top to bottom), and the assumptions and limitations are stated.
safety: All data are simulated, so no participants are involved. Do not present simulated results as evidence about real people or real treatments.

@review
This review covers the whole course. Use it to decide what to revisit before the cumulative final.

### Week by week

1. **Counting and axioms.** Multiplication rule, $\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}$, $\binom{n}{k}$ and multinomials; $P(E^c) = 1 - P(E)$; inclusion-exclusion; $P = |E|/|S|$ only when outcomes are equally likely.
2. **Conditional probability.** $P(A \mid B) = P(A \cap B)/P(B)$; the multiplication rule; total probability; Bayes' rule; independence means $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$.
3. **Discrete variables.** The pmf, $E[g(X)] = \sum g(x)p(x)$, linearity, $\operatorname{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - \mu^2$; binomial, geometric and Poisson models.
4. **Continuous variables.** A density is not a probability; normalize with $c = 1/\int h$; the cdf; uniform, exponential (memoryless) and normal (standardize).
5. **Joint distributions.** Marginals by summing or integrating out, conditionals as joint over marginal, independence as a product everywhere, and integration regions.
6. **Covariance.** $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = E[XY] - E[X]E[Y]$, the variance of a sum, $\rho$; independent implies uncorrelated, not the converse.
7. **Limit theorems.** Markov and Chebyshev bounds, the law of large numbers, the central limit theorem, the continuity correction, and the conditions behind them.
8. **Sampling distributions.** $\text{SE}(\bar X) = \sigma/\sqrt{n}$, $\text{SE}(\hat p)$, $S^2$ with divisor $n - 1$, the $t$ statistic; simulation is evidence, not proof.
9. **Estimation.** Method of moments, likelihood and log-likelihood, bias, variance, $\text{MSE} = \text{Var} + \text{Bias}^2$, consistency.
10. **Confidence intervals.** Estimate $\pm$ critical value $\times$ SE; coverage is a property of the procedure; $n \ge (z^*\sigma/m)^2$.
11. **Hypothesis tests.** The statistic, the p-value, Type I and II errors, power $= \Phi\!\left(\frac{\delta\sqrt{n}}{\sigma} - z_\alpha\right)$, effect size, multiple testing.
12. **Regression and model checking.** Least squares, $R^2$, $\text{SE}(b_1)$ on $n - 2$ degrees of freedom, and residual plots for curvature, funnels and outliers.

### Self-check

You are ready for the final if you can, without notes:

- Solve a Bayes' rule problem from a table or a tree.
- Normalize a density, find a marginal from a joint density, and compute $E$, $\operatorname{Var}$ and $\operatorname{Cov}$ by integration.
- Give an example of uncorrelated but dependent variables, and say what it shows.
- State what the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem promise, and when they fail.
- Compute a standard error, a $t$ interval and a p-value, and explain a confidence level in one sentence.
- Compute power or a required sample size, and explain what a non-significant result does and does not mean.
- Read a residual plot and name the assumption it questions.
- Describe how to check a coverage claim or a power claim by simulation.

### Common errors

- Counting with the wrong sample space, or confusing $P(A \mid B)$ with $P(B \mid A)$ and ignoring base rates.
- Reading a density as a probability, or forgetting to normalize.
- Concluding independence from zero covariance or from similar-looking marginals.
- Using $\sigma$ where $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ belongs, or dividing by $n$ instead of $n - 1$.
- Reading a p-value as $P(H_0 \text{ true})$, or a 95% interval as a 95% chance for this one interval.
- Treating "fail to reject" as "no effect" and significance as importance.
- Trusting $R^2$ without plotting the residuals.

@exam num
prompt: A screening test is applied to a population in which $2\%$ of people have a condition. The test is positive for $95\%$ of people who have the condition and for $10\%$ of people who do not. Four hundred people are screened independently. Using the normal approximation to the binomial with a continuity correction, estimate the probability that at least $60$ of the $400$ test positive. Round to four decimal places.
answer: 0.0241
check: (lambda p: quad(lambda s: exp(-(s - 400 * p)**2 / (2 * 400 * p * (1 - p))) / sqrt(2 * pi * 400 * p * (1 - p)), 59.5, 400))(0.02 * 0.95 + 0.98 * 0.10)
tol: 0.001
hint: First find the probability that a randomly chosen person tests positive, using the law of total probability.
hint: The number of positives is Binomial$(400, p)$. Find its mean $np$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{np(1-p)}$.
hint: "At least $60$" starts at $59.5$ after the continuity correction. Standardize and use the upper tail.
solution: $p = 0.02(0.95) + 0.98(0.10) = 0.117$. The count has mean $400(0.117) = 46.8$ and variance $400(0.117)(0.883) \approx 41.32$, so $\sigma \approx 6.43$. Then $z = \frac{59.5 - 46.8}{6.43} \approx 1.975$ and $P \approx 1 - \Phi(1.976) \approx 0.0241$.

@exam num
prompt: The point $(X, Y)$ is uniformly distributed on the triangle $0 \le y \le x \le 1$, so its joint density is $2$ there and $0$ elsewhere. Find $\operatorname{Var}(X - Y)$ as an exact fraction.
answer: 1/18
check: quad2(lambda x, y: 2 * (x - y)**2, 0, 1, 0, lambda x: x) - quad2(lambda x, y: 2 * (x - y), 0, 1, 0, lambda x: x)**2
hint: Find the marginal densities $f_X(x) = 2x$ and $f_Y(y) = 2(1 - y)$, then the means and variances of $X$ and $Y$.
hint: Compute $E[XY] = \int_0^1\!\int_0^x 2xy\,dy\,dx$ to get $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$, then use $\operatorname{Var}(X - Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(Y) - 2\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$.
solution: $E[X] = \frac{2}{3}$ and $E[X^2] = \frac{1}{2}$, so $\operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{1}{18}$. Likewise $E[Y] = \frac{1}{3}$, $E[Y^2] = \frac{1}{6}$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \frac{1}{18}$. Also $E[XY] = \int_0^1 x \cdot x^2\,dx = \frac{1}{4}$, so $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \frac{1}{4} - \frac{2}{9} = \frac{1}{36}$. Hence $\operatorname{Var}(X - Y) = \frac{1}{18} + \frac{1}{18} - \frac{2}{36} = \frac{1}{18}$. The variables are dependent (the support is a triangle), with correlation $\rho = \frac{1}{2}$.

@exam self
prompt: A simulated trial compares two study techniques with $n = 40$ students per group, and the score standard deviation is $10$ in each group. The estimated difference in mean quiz score (new minus old) is $4.0$ points, with a 95% confidence interval from $-0.4$ to $8.4$ and a two-sided p-value of $0.074$. The authors write: "The p-value is above $0.05$, so we accept the null hypothesis: the new technique has no effect." (a) Give two reasons why this conclusion is not justified. (b) Write a more defensible one-sentence conclusion. (c) The smallest improvement that matters to the team is $3$ points. Find the sample size per group needed for $80\%$ power at a two-sided $\alpha = 0.05$ (use $z = 1.96$ and $z = 0.842$), and the approximate power the original study had against a $3$-point effect.
hint: Compare the interval with both $0$ and the smallest effect of interest, $3$ points.
hint: For two groups of size $n$ the standard error of the difference is $\sigma\sqrt{2/n}$, and the sample size for power $1 - \beta$ is $n = \frac{2\sigma^2 (z_{\alpha/2} + z_\beta)^2}{\Delta^2}$.
solution: (a) Failing to reject is not accepting $H_0$: the data are simply not strong enough to rule it out. Also the interval $(-0.4, 8.4)$ is compatible with no effect, with the $3$-point effect the team cares about, and with large effects up to about $8$ points, so the study is inconclusive rather than evidence of no effect. (b) "The estimated improvement was 4.0 points (95% CI $-0.4$ to $8.4$); the study was too small to distinguish no effect from a meaningful improvement." (c) $n = \frac{2(100)(1.96 + 0.842)^2}{3^2} = \frac{2(100)(7.85)}{9} \approx 174.4$, so about $175$ students per group. In the original study $\text{SE} = 10\sqrt{2/40} \approx 2.24$, so the standardized shift is $\frac{3}{2.24} \approx 1.34$ and the power is about $\Phi(1.34 - 1.96) = \Phi(-0.62) \approx 0.27$: a real $3$-point effect would be missed about three times out of four.
rubric: States that failing to reject $H_0$ is not evidence that $H_0$ is true, and uses the confidence interval to show that the data are compatible with both no effect and meaningful effects.
rubric: Gives a conclusion that reports the estimate and interval and says the study was inconclusive, without claiming either "no effect" or "proven effect".
rubric: Computes about $175$ students per group using $n = 2\sigma^2(z_{\alpha/2} + z_\beta)^2/\Delta^2$ with the stated values.
rubric: Finds that the original power against a $3$-point effect is only about $0.27$, and draws the conclusion that a non-significant result was likely even if the effect were real.
