@article
A **parameter** is a fixed, usually unknown, number describing a population: its mean $\mu$, its standard deviation $\sigma$, a proportion $p$. A **statistic** is a number computed from a sample, such as $\bar X$, $\hat p$ or $S^2$. Because the sample is random, the statistic is a random variable. Its distribution over repeated samples of the same size is the **sampling distribution**, and its spread is the **standard error** (SE).

### The sample mean

For iid data with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$:

$$E[\bar X] = \mu, \qquad \text{SE}(\bar X) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}.$$

If the population is normal, $\bar X$ is exactly normal; otherwise it is approximately normal for large $n$ by the central limit theorem.

### The sample proportion

If $X \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p)$ counts successes, then $\hat p = X/n$ has

$$E[\hat p] = p, \qquad \text{SE}(\hat p) = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}},$$

and is approximately normal when both $np$ and $n(1-p)$ are at least about $10$.

### The sample variance, chi-square and $t$

The sample variance divides by $n - 1$, not $n$:

$$S^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar X)^2, \qquad E[S^2] = \sigma^2.$$

Dividing by $n$ would underestimate $\sigma^2$ on average, because deviations from $\bar X$ are smaller than deviations from the unknown $\mu$. For a **normal** population, $(n-1)S^2/\sigma^2$ follows a chi-square distribution with $n - 1$ degrees of freedom (mean $n - 1$, variance $2(n-1)$), and $\bar X$ and $S^2$ are independent.

In practice $\sigma$ is unknown, so we estimate it with $S$. The standardized mean then follows a **$t$ distribution** with $n - 1$ degrees of freedom:

$$T = \frac{\bar X - \mu}{S/\sqrt{n}} \sim t_{n-1}.$$

It has heavier tails than the normal because $S$ is itself random: an unluckily small $S$ inflates $T$. As $n$ grows, $t_{n-1}$ approaches the standard normal.

### Simulating a sampling distribution

You can see all of this by repeating the sampling many times:

```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
for n in (5, 30, 200):
    means = rng.exponential(2.0, size=(10000, n)).mean(axis=1)
    skew = ((means - means.mean())**3).mean() / means.std()**3
    print(n, round(means.std(), 3), round(2.0 / np.sqrt(n), 3), round(skew, 2))
```

Each row compares the simulated SD of the means with $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ and reports the skewness, which should shrink toward $0$ as the shape becomes normal. A simulation is evidence, not proof: it covers only the population, sample sizes and number of repetitions you chose, and it carries its own Monte Carlo error of order $1/\sqrt{\text{repetitions}}$.

@example
title: Standard error and a normal approximation for the mean
problem: A population has mean $100$ and standard deviation $15$. A random sample of $n = 36$ is taken. Find the standard error of $\bar X$ and approximate $P(\bar X > 105)$.
step: The standard error is $\text{SE} = \frac{15}{\sqrt{36}} = \frac{15}{6} = 2.5$.
step: With $n = 36$ the sample mean is approximately Normal$(100,\ 2.5^2)$ by the central limit theorem.
step: Standardize: $z = \frac{105 - 100}{2.5} = 2$.
step: $P(\bar X > 105) \approx 1 - \Phi(2) \approx 0.0228$. A sample mean this far above $100$ would be unusual, but not shocking.

@example
title: Simulating convergence and its limits
problem: Use a simulation to study the sampling distribution of $\bar X$ for an exponential population with mean $2$ (so $\sigma = 2$) at sample sizes $n = 5$, $30$ and $200$.
step: Theory predicts $\text{SE} = 2/\sqrt{n}$, which is about $0.894$, $0.365$ and $0.141$, and a distribution of $\bar X$ that becomes more normal as $n$ grows.
step: For each $n$, draw $10{,}000$ samples of size $n$ and record the mean of each sample (the code above does this with `.mean(axis=1)`). Each row of the output compares the simulated SD with $2/\sqrt{n}$.
step: Convergence in spread: the simulated SDs track $2/\sqrt{n}$ closely, so the means concentrate around $2$ as the law of large numbers predicts.
step: Convergence in shape: the skewness of $\bar X$ is about $2/\sqrt{n}$, roughly $0.89$, $0.37$ and $0.14$. At $n = 5$ the histogram is clearly right-skewed, so a normal approximation would be poor.
step: Limitations: the run covers one population, three sample sizes and one seed; its numbers wobble by about $1/\sqrt{10000} = 1\%$; and repeating it for a population with no mean, such as density $\frac{1}{\pi(1 + x^2)}$, would show sample means that never settle.

@mistakes
- Confusing the standard deviation of the data, $\sigma$, with the standard error of the mean, $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$.
- Dividing by $n$ instead of $n - 1$ when computing the sample variance, which underestimates $\sigma^2$ on average.
- Treating one simulation run as proof. It shows behavior for the chosen population, sizes and seed only.
- Using the normal approximation for $\hat p$ when $np$ or $n(1-p)$ is small.
