@article
A **random variable** is a numerical quantity determined by the outcome of an experiment: the number of heads in ten flips, the sum of two dice, the number of calls arriving this minute. Formally it is a function from the sample space to the real numbers. It is **discrete** when it takes countably many values, and then its distribution is described by the **probability mass function** (pmf)

$$p(x) = P(X = x).$$

A pmf is valid exactly when $p(x) \ge 0$ for every $x$ and $\sum_x p(x) = 1$.

### Expectation and variance

The **expectation** (mean) is the probability-weighted average of the values, and the same weights give the expectation of any function of $X$:

$$E[X] = \sum_x x\,p(x), \qquad E[g(X)] = \sum_x g(x)\,p(x).$$

Expectation is **linear**: $E[aX + bY + c] = aE[X] + bE[Y] + c$ for any random variables $X$ and $Y$, with no independence needed. This is the most useful fact in the course. If $X$ counts the heads in $n$ flips, write $X = I_1 + \dots + I_n$, where $I_j$ is $1$ when flip $j$ is heads and $0$ otherwise. Then $E[X] = \sum_j E[I_j] = np$ without any summation over the pmf.

The **variance** measures spread around the mean $\mu = E[X]$:

$$\operatorname{Var}(X) = E[(X - \mu)^2] = E[X^2] - \mu^2, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(aX + b) = a^2 \operatorname{Var}(X).$$

The **standard deviation** $\sigma = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}$ has the same units as $X$. Shifting by $b$ does not change the spread; scaling by $a$ scales the standard deviation by $|a|$.

### Three families to know

| Name | Models | $P(X = k)$ | Mean | Variance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Binomial$(n, p)$ | successes in $n$ independent trials | $\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$, $k = 0, \dots, n$ | $np$ | $np(1-p)$ |
| Geometric$(p)$ | trials up to and including the first success | $(1-p)^{k-1}p$, $k = 1, 2, \dots$ | $1/p$ | $(1-p)/p^2$ |
| Poisson$(\lambda)$ | counts of rare independent events at average rate $\lambda$ | $e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!$, $k = 0, 1, \dots$ | $\lambda$ | $\lambda$ |

The Poisson pmf approximates Binomial$(n, p)$ when $n$ is large, $p$ is small and $\lambda = np$ is moderate.

A short computation confirms the binomial mean and variance straight from the pmf:

```python
from math import comb
n, p = 6, 1/3
pmf = [comb(n, k) * p**k * (1 - p)**(n - k) for k in range(n + 1)]
mean = sum(k * q for k, q in enumerate(pmf))
var = sum((k - mean)**2 * q for k, q in enumerate(pmf))
print(round(mean, 4), round(var, 4))   # 2.0 1.3333
```

@example
title: Mean and variance from a pmf
problem: A random variable $X$ has $p(0) = 0.1$, $p(1) = 0.3$, $p(2) = 0.4$ and $p(3) = 0.2$. Find $E[X]$, $\operatorname{Var}(X)$ and the standard deviation $\sigma$.
step: Check that the pmf is valid: every entry is nonnegative and $0.1 + 0.3 + 0.4 + 0.2 = 1$.
step: Mean: $E[X] = 0(0.1) + 1(0.3) + 2(0.4) + 3(0.2) = 0.3 + 0.8 + 0.6 = 1.7$.
step: Second moment: $E[X^2] = 0(0.1) + 1(0.3) + 4(0.4) + 9(0.2) = 0.3 + 1.6 + 1.8 = 3.7$.
step: Variance: $\operatorname{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2 = 3.7 - 1.7^2 = 3.7 - 2.89 = 0.81$.
step: Standard deviation: $\sigma = \sqrt{0.81} = 0.9$.

@example
title: Guessing on a quiz
problem: A quiz has $10$ multiple-choice questions with $4$ options each. A student guesses every answer at random. What is the probability of getting at least $3$ right?
step: Each guess is an independent trial with success probability $p = \frac{1}{4}$, so the number correct is $X \sim \text{Binomial}(10, \frac{1}{4})$.
step: "At least 3" has many terms, but its complement "at most 2" has only three: $P(X \ge 3) = 1 - [P(0) + P(1) + P(2)]$.
step: Compute them: $P(0) = 0.75^{10} \approx 0.0563$, $P(1) = 10(0.25)(0.75)^9 \approx 0.1877$ and $P(2) = 45(0.25)^2(0.75)^8 \approx 0.2816$.
step: So $P(X \ge 3) \approx 1 - 0.5256 = 0.4744$. Pure guessing gets three or more right almost half the time, so $3$ out of $10$ is weak evidence of knowing the material.

@mistakes
- Writing $E[g(X)] = g(E[X])$. That holds only for linear $g$; in general compute $\sum_x g(x)p(x)$. In particular $E[X^2] \ne (E[X])^2$, and the gap is the variance.
- Using $\operatorname{Var}(aX + b) = a\operatorname{Var}(X) + b$. The shift drops out and the factor is squared: $a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)$.
- Using a binomial model when trials are not independent or the success probability changes, for example when drawing cards without replacement.
- Mixing up the two geometric conventions: counting trials including the success ($k = 1, 2, \dots$) or counting failures before it ($k = 0, 1, \dots$). State which one you use.

@practice num
prompt: A game awards $0$ points with probability $0.5$, $2$ points with probability $0.3$ and $10$ points with probability $0.2$. What is the expected number of points?
answer: 2.6
check: 0 * 0.5 + 2 * 0.3 + 10 * 0.2
hint: Multiply each value by its probability and add.
solution: $E = 0(0.5) + 2(0.3) + 10(0.2) = 0.6 + 2 = 2.6$ points.

@practice num
prompt: Let $X$ be the number of successes in $6$ independent trials, each with success probability $\frac{1}{3}$. Find $\operatorname{Var}(X)$. Give an exact fraction.
answer: 4/3
check: sum(comb(6, k) * (1/3)**k * (2/3)**(6 - k) * k**2 for k in range(7)) - sum(comb(6, k) * (1/3)**k * (2/3)**(6 - k) * k for k in range(7))**2
hint: $X$ is Binomial$(6, \frac{1}{3})$. Recall the variance formula for a binomial.
solution: $\operatorname{Var}(X) = np(1-p) = 6 \cdot \frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{2}{3} = \frac{4}{3}$.

prompt: Which situation is best modeled by a geometric random variable?
choice: The number of heads in $10$ flips of a fair coin.
choice*: The number of flips of a fair coin needed to see the first head.
choice: The number of emails arriving in an hour at an average rate of $4$ per hour.
choice: The number of aces in a $5$-card hand dealt from a standard deck.
hint: A geometric variable counts independent trials up to and including the first success.
solution: The waiting time for the first success in repeated independent trials is geometric. The number of heads in $10$ flips is binomial and the number of emails per hour is Poisson. The number of aces in a $5$-card hand has dependent draws (no replacement), so it is neither binomial nor geometric.

@practice num
prompt: The number of calls arriving at a switchboard in one minute is Poisson with mean $\lambda = 3$. Find the probability of at most $2$ calls in a given minute. Round to four decimal places.
answer: 0.4232
check: sum(exp(-3) * 3**k / factorial(k) for k in range(3))
tol: 0.0002
hint: "At most 2" means $k = 0, 1, 2$; add the three Poisson probabilities.
hint: $P(X \le 2) = e^{-3}\left(1 + 3 + \frac{3^2}{2!}\right)$.
solution: $e^{-3}(1 + 3 + 4.5) = 8.5\,e^{-3} \approx 8.5 \times 0.049787 \approx 0.4232$.

@quiz num
prompt: A random variable $X$ takes the values $-1$, $0$ and $3$ with probabilities $0.2$, $0.5$ and $0.3$. Find $E[(2X - 1)^2]$.
answer: 9.8
check: 0.2 * (2 * (-1) - 1)**2 + 0.5 * (2 * 0 - 1)**2 + 0.3 * (2 * 3 - 1)**2
hint: Apply $E[g(X)] = \sum g(x)p(x)$ with $g(x) = (2x - 1)^2$. Do not square the mean.
hint: The values of $g$ at $-1$, $0$ and $3$ are $9$, $1$ and $25$.
solution: $E[(2X-1)^2] = 9(0.2) + 1(0.5) + 25(0.3) = 1.8 + 0.5 + 7.5 = 9.8$.

@quiz num
prompt: A fair die is rolled repeatedly until a six appears. What is the probability that more than $6$ rolls are needed? Round to four decimal places.
answer: 0.3349
check: sum((5/6)**(k - 1) * (1/6) for k in range(7, 500))
tol: 0.0002
hint: More than $6$ rolls are needed exactly when the first $6$ rolls are all non-sixes.
hint: Each roll is a non-six with probability $\frac{5}{6}$, independently of the others.
solution: $P = \left(\frac{5}{6}\right)^6 = \frac{15625}{46656} \approx 0.3349$. Summing the geometric pmf over $k \ge 7$ gives the same value.
