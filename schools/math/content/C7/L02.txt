@article
Probabilities change when you learn something. The chance that a random card is a heart is $\frac{1}{4}$; the chance that it is a heart **given** that it is red is $\frac{1}{2}$. Conditioning means shrinking the sample space to the outcomes consistent with what you know, then rescaling so that they have total probability $1$:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \qquad P(B) > 0.$$

Multiplying through gives the **multiplication rule**, the basic tool for sequences of events:

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)\,P(B) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A).$$

### Joint models, marginals and conditionals

A **joint model** assigns a probability to every combination of outcomes. Adding the joint probabilities over the other variable gives a **marginal** probability. Dividing a joint probability by a marginal gives a **conditional** probability. Here is a screening test for a rare condition:

| | test positive | test negative | marginal |
|---|---|---|---|
| has condition | 0.009 | 0.001 | 0.010 |
| no condition | 0.099 | 0.891 | 0.990 |
| marginal | 0.108 | 0.892 | 1 |

The marginal probability of a positive test is $0.009 + 0.099 = 0.108$. The test finds $0.009/0.010 = 90\%$ of true cases, yet the probability of having the condition given a positive test is only $0.009/0.108 = \frac{1}{12} \approx 0.083$, because the condition is so rare.

### Total probability and Bayes' rule

Suppose $B_1, \dots, B_n$ **partition** the sample space: they are disjoint and together cover $S$. Split any event $A$ along the partition and use the multiplication rule on each piece:

$$P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A \mid B_i)\,P(B_i). \qquad \text{(law of total probability)}$$

Reversing the conditioning gives **Bayes' rule**:

$$P(B_j \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B_j)\,P(B_j)}{\sum_{i} P(A \mid B_i)\,P(B_i)}.$$

The denominator is just $P(A)$. In words: the updated probability of a cause is its prior probability times how well it explains the evidence, normalized so that all the causes add to $1$.

### Independence

Events $A$ and $B$ are **independent** when learning one tells you nothing about the other:

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B), \quad\text{equivalently}\quad P(A \mid B) = P(A) \ \text{ when } P(B) > 0.$$

Independence is not the same as being disjoint. If $A$ and $B$ are disjoint and both have positive probability, then $P(A \cap B) = 0 \ne P(A)P(B)$: knowing that $B$ happened tells you $A$ did not, which is as dependent as two events can be.

@example
title: Marginals and conditionals from a joint table
problem: A joint model for the weather and a commuter's train is $P(\text{rain, late}) = 0.12$, $P(\text{rain, on time}) = 0.18$, $P(\text{dry, late}) = 0.08$ and $P(\text{dry, on time}) = 0.62$. Find $P(\text{rain})$, $P(\text{late})$, $P(\text{late} \mid \text{rain})$ and $P(\text{rain} \mid \text{late})$, and decide whether rain and lateness are independent.
step: First check that the model is valid: $0.12 + 0.18 + 0.08 + 0.62 = 1$.
step: Marginals come from summing the joint probabilities: $P(\text{rain}) = 0.12 + 0.18 = 0.30$ and $P(\text{late}) = 0.12 + 0.08 = 0.20$.
step: Conditioning on rain divides by the marginal of the condition: $P(\text{late} \mid \text{rain}) = \dfrac{0.12}{0.30} = 0.4$.
step: Conditioning the other way divides by a different marginal: $P(\text{rain} \mid \text{late}) = \dfrac{0.12}{0.20} = 0.6$. The two conditionals differ, so the order of conditioning matters.
step: Independence would need $P(\text{rain})P(\text{late}) = 0.30 \cdot 0.20 = 0.06$ to equal $P(\text{rain, late}) = 0.12$. It does not, so the events are dependent: rain doubles the chance of a late train from $0.2$ to $0.4$.

@example
title: Which urn did the ball come from?
problem: Urn I holds $3$ red and $1$ blue ball; urn II holds $1$ red and $3$ blue. An urn is chosen by a fair coin flip and one ball is drawn from it. The ball is red. What is the probability that it came from urn I?
step: Name the partition: $U_1$ (urn I) and $U_2$ (urn II), with $P(U_1) = P(U_2) = \frac{1}{2}$. Let $R$ be "red". Then $P(R \mid U_1) = \frac{3}{4}$ and $P(R \mid U_2) = \frac{1}{4}$.
step: Law of total probability: $P(R) = \frac{3}{4} \cdot \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{2}$.
step: Bayes' rule: $P(U_1 \mid R) = \dfrac{P(R \mid U_1)P(U_1)}{P(R)} = \dfrac{3/8}{1/2} = \dfrac{3}{4}$.
step: Check: $P(U_2 \mid R) = \dfrac{1/8}{1/2} = \dfrac{1}{4}$, and $\frac{3}{4} + \frac{1}{4} = 1$.

@mistakes
- Confusing $P(A \mid B)$ with $P(B \mid A)$; they have different denominators and are often very different numbers.
- Ignoring the prior (base rate) in Bayes' rule, so that a good test for a rare condition looks far more conclusive than it is.
- Believing that disjoint events are independent; when both have positive probability they are the opposite.
- Replacing $P(A \cap B)$ by $P(A)P(B)$ without first knowing that the events are independent.

@practice num
prompt: A fair die is rolled. Given that the result is even, what is the probability that it is greater than $3$?
answer: 2/3
check: sum(1 for k in range(1, 7) if k % 2 == 0 and k > 3) / sum(1 for k in range(1, 7) if k % 2 == 0)
hint: Conditioning on "even" shrinks the sample space to $\{2, 4, 6\}$.
hint: How many of those three equally likely outcomes are greater than $3$?
solution: Given "even", the outcomes $2, 4, 6$ are equally likely, and two of them ($4$ and $6$) exceed $3$. So the probability is $\frac{2}{3}$. By the formula, $\frac{2/6}{3/6} = \frac{2}{3}$.

@practice num
prompt: Two cards are dealt one after the other, without replacement, from a standard 52-card deck. What is the probability that both are hearts?
answer: 1/17
check: comb(13, 2) / comb(52, 2)
hint: Use the multiplication rule: $P(H_1 \cap H_2) = P(H_1)\,P(H_2 \mid H_1)$.
hint: After one heart is gone, $12$ hearts remain among $51$ cards.
solution: $\frac{13}{52} \cdot \frac{12}{51} = \frac{1}{4} \cdot \frac{4}{17} = \frac{1}{17}$.

@practice num
prompt: A joint model for tomorrow's weather and a flight has $P(\text{snow, delayed}) = 0.06$, $P(\text{snow, on time}) = 0.04$, $P(\text{no snow, delayed}) = 0.09$ and $P(\text{no snow, on time}) = 0.81$. Find $P(\text{snow} \mid \text{delayed})$.
answer: 0.4
check: 0.06 / (0.06 + 0.09)
hint: First find the marginal probability that the flight is delayed by adding the two "delayed" entries.
hint: The conditional probability is the matching joint entry divided by that marginal.
solution: $P(\text{delayed}) = 0.06 + 0.09 = 0.15$, so $P(\text{snow} \mid \text{delayed}) = \frac{0.06}{0.15} = 0.4$.

@practice mc
prompt: Events $A$ and $B$ are disjoint, with $P(A) = 0.3$ and $P(B) = 0.5$. Which statement is true?
choice*: $A$ and $B$ are not independent.
choice: $A$ and $B$ are independent, because they share no outcomes.
choice: $P(A \cup B) = 0.15$.
choice: $P(A \mid B) = 0.3$.
hint: Compare $P(A \cap B)$ with $P(A)P(B)$.
solution: Disjoint means $P(A \cap B) = 0$, but $P(A)P(B) = 0.15 \ne 0$, so the events are not independent. Also $P(A \cup B) = 0.8$ and $P(A \mid B) = 0$.

@practice num
prompt: A plant has three machines. Machine 1 makes $50\%$ of the items and $1\%$ of them are defective; machine 2 makes $30\%$ with $2\%$ defective; machine 3 makes $20\%$ with $5\%$ defective. An item is chosen at random and is defective. What is the probability that it came from machine 3? Give an exact fraction or a decimal rounded to three places.
answer: 10/21
check: (0.2 * 0.05) / (0.5 * 0.01 + 0.3 * 0.02 + 0.2 * 0.05)
tol: 0.001
hint: The three machines partition the items. Use the law of total probability to find $P(\text{defective})$.
hint: $P(\text{defective}) = 0.5(0.01) + 0.3(0.02) + 0.2(0.05)$. Then apply Bayes' rule for machine 3.
solution: $P(D) = 0.005 + 0.006 + 0.010 = 0.021$. Then $P(M_3 \mid D) = \frac{0.010}{0.021} = \frac{10}{21} \approx 0.476$.

@quiz num
prompt: A bag holds a fair coin and a biased coin that lands heads with probability $0.8$. One coin is picked at random and flipped twice, and both flips are heads. Given this, what is the probability that the biased coin was picked? Round to three decimal places.
answer: 0.719
check: 0.5 * 0.8**2 / (0.5 * 0.8**2 + 0.5 * 0.5**2)
tol: 0.001
hint: Given which coin was picked, the two flips are independent, so $P(HH \mid \text{coin})$ is the square of that coin's heads probability.
hint: Apply Bayes' rule with $P(\text{biased}) = P(\text{fair}) = 0.5$ and likelihoods $0.8^2$ and $0.5^2$.
solution: $P(HH \mid \text{biased}) = 0.64$ and $P(HH \mid \text{fair}) = 0.25$. So $P(\text{biased} \mid HH) = \frac{0.5 \cdot 0.64}{0.5 \cdot 0.64 + 0.5 \cdot 0.25} = \frac{0.64}{0.89} \approx 0.719$.

@quiz mc
prompt: For events $A$ and $B$ you are told $P(A) = 0.6$, $P(B) = 0.5$ and $P(A \cup B) = 0.8$. Which conclusion is correct?
choice*: They are independent, because $P(A \cap B) = 0.3 = P(A)P(B)$.
choice: They are dependent, because $P(A \cup B) \ne P(A) + P(B)$.
choice: They are dependent, because $P(A \mid B) = 0.6$ differs from $P(B) = 0.5$.
choice: They can be independent only if they are also disjoint.
hint: Use inclusion-exclusion to find $P(A \cap B)$ first.
solution: $P(A \cap B) = 0.6 + 0.5 - 0.8 = 0.3 = P(A)P(B)$, so the events are independent. Equivalently $P(A \mid B) = 0.3/0.5 = 0.6 = P(A)$. That the union is smaller than the sum only says the events overlap, and the right comparison is $P(A \mid B)$ with $P(A)$, not with $P(B)$.
