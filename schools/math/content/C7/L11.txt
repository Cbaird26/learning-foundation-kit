@article
A **hypothesis test** asks: if a specific claim (the **null hypothesis** $H_0$) were true, would the data I saw be surprising? The competing claim is the **alternative** $H_1$. For a mean, a typical pair is $H_0\colon \mu = \mu_0$ against $H_1\colon \mu \ne \mu_0$ (two-sided) or $H_1\colon \mu > \mu_0$ (one-sided).

### Test statistic and p-value

Standardize the evidence against $H_0$. With known $\sigma$ use $z$; with unknown $\sigma$ use $t$ on $n - 1$ degrees of freedom:

$$z = \frac{\bar x - \mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}, \qquad t = \frac{\bar x - \mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}}.$$

The **p-value** is the probability, *computed assuming $H_0$ is true*, of a test statistic at least as extreme as the one observed. For a two-sided $z$ test, $p = 2\bigl(1 - \Phi(|z|)\bigr)$. Decide on a **significance level** $\alpha$ (commonly $0.05$) in advance, and reject $H_0$ when $p \le \alpha$.

A p-value is **not** the probability that $H_0$ is true; it is a statement about the data given $H_0$. And "fail to reject" is not "accept $H_0$": the study may simply have been too small to see a real effect.

### Errors and power

| | $H_0$ true | $H_0$ false |
|---|---|---|
| Reject $H_0$ | Type I error (probability $\alpha$) | correct: **power** $= 1 - \beta$ |
| Do not reject | correct | Type II error (probability $\beta$) |

**Power** is the probability of rejecting $H_0$ when a particular alternative is true: the test's sensitivity to that effect. For a one-sided $z$ test of $H_0\colon \mu = \mu_0$ against $\mu > \mu_0$, if the true mean is $\mu_1$,

$$\text{power} = \Phi\!\left(\frac{(\mu_1 - \mu_0)\sqrt{n}}{\sigma} - z_\alpha\right).$$

Power rises with a larger effect $\mu_1 - \mu_0$, a larger $n$, a smaller $\sigma$ and a larger $\alpha$. Solving for the sample size that achieves power $1 - \beta$ gives $n = \left(\dfrac{(z_\alpha + z_\beta)\,\sigma}{\mu_1 - \mu_0}\right)^2$.

### Significance is not importance

With enough data, a negligible difference becomes "significant": $n = 10^6$, $\sigma = 1$ and $\bar x - \mu_0 = 0.01$ give $z = 10$, yet the difference is one hundredth of a standard deviation. Always report an **effect size** (the estimated difference, perhaps in units of $\sigma$) with a confidence interval, not only a p-value.

### Many tests, many chances

If you run $m$ independent tests of true nulls at level $\alpha$, the chance of at least one false rejection is $1 - (1 - \alpha)^m$. With $m = 20$ and $\alpha = 0.05$ that is about $64\%$. Decide which tests you will run before looking at the data, and lower the per-test level (for example, by dividing $\alpha$ by the number of tests) when you do look at many.

```python
from math import erf, sqrt
def Phi(z): return 0.5 * (1 + erf(z / sqrt(2)))
mu0, mu1, sigma, n, z_alpha = 100, 105, 15, 36, 1.645
print(round(Phi((mu1 - mu0) * sqrt(n) / sigma - z_alpha), 3))   # power: 0.639
```

@example
title: A t test with its interval and effect size
problem: A process is supposed to have mean $50$. A sample of $n = 25$ items has mean $52.1$ and standard deviation $s = 5$. Test $H_0\colon \mu = 50$ against $H_1\colon \mu \ne 50$ at $\alpha = 0.05$, and report an interval and an effect size.
step: The test statistic is $t = \dfrac{52.1 - 50}{5/\sqrt{25}} = \dfrac{2.1}{1} = 2.1$, with $n - 1 = 24$ degrees of freedom.
step: The two-sided critical value is $t^*_{24} = 2.064$. Since $|t| = 2.1 > 2.064$, reject $H_0$. Equivalently the p-value is about $0.046 < 0.05$.
step: The matching 95% confidence interval is $52.1 \pm 2.064(1) = (50.04,\ 54.16)$. It excludes $50$, consistent with the rejection.
step: The estimated effect is $52.1 - 50 = 2.1$ units, or $\frac{2.1}{5} = 0.42$ standard deviations.
step: Interpretation: the result is statistically significant, but only just. The interval shows that plausible effects range from almost nothing ($0.04$) to fairly large ($4.16$), so the data do not pin the effect down.

@example
title: Power and sample size for a one-sided z test
problem: Test $H_0\colon \mu = 100$ against $H_1\colon \mu > 100$ with $\sigma = 15$, $n = 36$ and $\alpha = 0.05$. Find the power when the true mean is $105$, and the sample size needed for power $0.80$.
step: The standard error is $\frac{15}{\sqrt{36}} = 2.5$. The test rejects when $z \ge 1.645$, that is when $\bar x \ge 100 + 1.645(2.5) \approx 104.11$.
step: If the true mean is $105$, then $\bar X$ is Normal$(105,\ 2.5^2)$. Power $= P(\bar X \ge 104.11) = P\!\left(Z \ge \frac{104.11 - 105}{2.5}\right) = P(Z \ge -0.356) = \Phi(0.356) \approx 0.639$.
step: So this design misses a real $5$-point increase about $36\%$ of the time ($\beta \approx 0.36$).
step: For power $0.80$ we need $\frac{(\mu_1 - \mu_0)\sqrt{n}}{\sigma} = z_{0.05} + z_{0.20} = 1.645 + 0.842 = 2.487$.
step: Solve: $\sqrt{n} = \frac{2.487 \cdot 15}{5} = 7.461$, so $n \approx 55.7$. Round up to $n = 56$.

@mistakes
- Reading the p-value as the probability that $H_0$ is true. It is computed assuming $H_0$ is true.
- Treating "fail to reject" as proof that $H_0$ is true. A low-power study can miss real effects.
- Equating statistical significance with practical importance. Large samples make tiny effects significant, so report the effect size and an interval.
- Running many tests, or peeking and stopping when $p < 0.05$, without accounting for it. The false-positive rate is then much larger than $\alpha$.

@practice num
prompt: A researcher runs $20$ independent tests, each at level $\alpha = 0.05$, and every null hypothesis is actually true. What is the probability of at least one false rejection? Round to four decimal places.
answer: 0.6415
check: sum(comb(20, k) * 0.05**k * 0.95**(20 - k) for k in range(1, 21))
tol: 0.0003
hint: The number of false rejections is Binomial$(20, 0.05)$. "At least one" is easiest through the complement.
solution: $P(\text{none}) = 0.95^{20} \approx 0.3585$, so $P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - 0.3585 = 0.6415$.

@practice num
prompt: A one-sided $z$ test of $H_0\colon \mu = 50$ against $H_1\colon \mu > 50$ uses $\sigma = 20$, $n = 100$ and $\alpha = 0.05$. If the true mean is $53$, find the power. Round to three decimal places.
answer: 0.442
check: special.ndtr((53 - 50) * sqrt(100) / 20 - special.ndtri(0.95))
tol: 0.003
hint: The test rejects when $z \ge z_{0.05} = 1.645$. Under the true mean, the standardized shift is $\frac{(\mu_1 - \mu_0)\sqrt{n}}{\sigma}$.
hint: Power $= \Phi\!\left(\frac{(\mu_1 - \mu_0)\sqrt{n}}{\sigma} - 1.645\right)$.
solution: The shift is $\frac{3 \cdot 10}{20} = 1.5$, so power $= \Phi(1.5 - 1.645) = \Phi(-0.145) \approx 0.442$. The test misses a real $3$-unit increase more than half the time.
