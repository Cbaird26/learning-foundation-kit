@article
A **probability model** has two ingredients: a **sample space** $S$, the set of all possible outcomes of an experiment, and a rule $P$ that gives each **event** (a subset of $S$) a number between $0$ and $1$. Roll one die: $S = \{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$, and the event "even" is the subset $\{2,4,6\}$.

### The three axioms

Every legitimate $P$ obeys three rules:

1. $P(E) \ge 0$ for every event $E$.
2. $P(S) = 1$.
3. If $E$ and $F$ are **disjoint** (they cannot both happen), then $P(E \cup F) = P(E) + P(F)$. For countably many pairwise disjoint events, the probability of the union is the sum of the probabilities.

Everything else is a consequence. Because $E$ and its complement $E^c$ are disjoint and together make up $S$,

$$P(E^c) = 1 - P(E).$$

If $E$ and $F$ may overlap, adding $P(E)$ and $P(F)$ counts the overlap twice, so subtract it once:

$$P(E \cup F) = P(E) + P(F) - P(E \cap F).$$

### Equally likely outcomes

When $S$ is finite and every outcome is equally likely,

$$P(E) = \frac{|E|}{|S|},$$

so probability becomes **counting**. Choose the sample space carefully: for two dice, use the $36$ ordered pairs, which are equally likely. The $21$ unordered pairs are not (the pair $\{3,5\}$ can happen two ways, the pair $\{4,4\}$ only one).

### Counting tools

- **Multiplication rule.** If a procedure has stages with $n_1, n_2, \dots, n_k$ choices, and the number of choices at each stage does not depend on earlier choices, there are $n_1 n_2 \cdots n_k$ outcomes in total.
- **Permutations** (order matters, no repeats). Arranging $k$ of $n$ distinct objects in a row can be done in $\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}$ ways, and all $n$ objects in $n!$ ways.
- **Combinations** (order ignored). Choosing a $k$-element subset of $n$ objects can be done in $\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}$ ways. Each subset can be ordered in $k!$ ways, which is why this is the permutation count divided by $k!$.
- **Rearrangements with repeats.** The distinct arrangements of $n$ items in which $n_1$ are alike, $n_2$ are alike, and so on, number $\frac{n!}{n_1!\,n_2!\cdots n_r!}$.

Python computes these directly:

```python
from math import comb, perm, factorial
print(comb(5, 2), perm(5, 2), factorial(5))   # 10 20 120
```

### Work with the complement

Events described as "at least one" are usually easier through the complement: count the outcomes where *none* happens, then subtract the result from $1$.

@example
title: Exactly two red marbles
problem: A bag holds $5$ red and $3$ blue marbles. Three marbles are drawn at once, without replacement, and every set of three is equally likely. What is the probability that exactly two are red?
step: The sample space is all unordered sets of $3$ marbles from $8$, so $|S| = \binom{8}{3} = 56$.
step: Count the favorable sets. Choose the $2$ red marbles from $5$ in $\binom{5}{2} = 10$ ways, and the $1$ blue marble from $3$ in $\binom{3}{1} = 3$ ways. By the multiplication rule there are $10 \cdot 3 = 30$ favorable sets.
step: Divide: $P(\text{exactly two red}) = \dfrac{30}{56} = \dfrac{15}{28} \approx 0.536$.
step: Sanity check: the counts for $0, 1, 2, 3$ red marbles are $1, 15, 30, 10$. They add to $56$, so the four probabilities add to $1$, as the axioms require.

@example
title: Shared birthdays by complement
problem: Assume $365$ equally likely birthdays and independent people. What is the probability that at least two of $23$ people share a birthday?
step: Let $A$ be "at least two share a birthday". Counting $A$ directly is messy, so count the complement $A^c$: all $23$ birthdays are different.
step: The $23$ birthdays form an ordered list, so there are $365^{23}$ equally likely lists. For $A^c$ the first person has $365$ choices, the second $364$, and so on down to $343$ for the 23rd person.
step: Therefore $P(A^c) = \dfrac{365}{365} \cdot \dfrac{364}{365} \cdots \dfrac{343}{365}$. In Python, `math.prod((365 - k) / 365 for k in range(23))` gives about $0.4927$.
step: $P(A) = 1 - P(A^c) \approx 0.507$. Twenty-three people already make a shared birthday slightly more likely than not.

@mistakes
- Treating the $21$ unordered outcomes of two dice as equally likely; use the $36$ ordered pairs.
- Using permutations when order does not matter (or combinations when it does), which is off by a factor of $k!$.
- Adding $P(E)$ and $P(F)$ for overlapping events without subtracting $P(E \cap F)$.
- Counting the favorable cases of "at least one" directly instead of computing $1 - P(\text{none})$.

answer: 1757600
check: 26**3 * 10**2
hint: Use the multiplication rule: count the choices at each of the five positions.

solution: $26 \cdot 26 \cdot 26 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 = 17576 \cdot 100 = 1757600$.

@practice num
prompt: A club with $10$ members chooses a committee of $4$ people. The committee has no officers, so only who is on it matters. How many different committees are possible?
answer: 210
check: sum(1 for a in range(10) for b in range(a + 1, 10) for c in range(b + 1, 10) for d in range(c + 1, 10))
hint: Order does not matter, so this is a combination, not a permutation.
hint: Compute $\frac{10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8 \cdot 7}{4!}$, which is the same as $\frac{10!}{4!\,6!}$.
solution: $\binom{10}{4} = \frac{10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8 \cdot 7}{24} = \frac{5040}{24} = 210$.

prompt: Which of these is a valid probability assignment for a spinner with outcomes $a$, $b$ and $c$?
choice: $P(a) = 0.5,\ P(b) = 0.3,\ P(c) = 0.3$
choice: $P(a) = 0.6,\ P(b) = 0.5,\ P(c) = -0.1$
choice: $P(a) = 0.2,\ P(b) = 0.3,\ P(c) = 0.4$
choice*: $P(a) = 0.25,\ P(b) = 0.25,\ P(c) = 0.5$
hint: Axiom 1 forbids negative values. Axioms 2 and 3 together force the three probabilities to add to $1$.
solution: The outcomes are disjoint and fill $S$, so the probabilities must be nonnegative and add to $1$. The set $0.5, 0.3, 0.3$ adds to $1.1$, the set $0.6, 0.5, -0.1$ contains a negative value, and the set $0.2, 0.3, 0.4$ adds to $0.9$. Only the set $0.25, 0.25, 0.5$ works.

@practice num
prompt: A number is chosen at random from $1, 2, \dots, 100$, all equally likely. What is the probability that it is divisible by $2$ or by $3$ (or both)?
answer: 0.67
check: sum(1 for n in range(1, 101) if n % 2 == 0 or n % 3 == 0) / 100
hint: Let $A$ be "divisible by 2" and $B$ be "divisible by 3". Use $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$.
hint: Count the multiples of $2$, of $3$ and of $6$ among $1, \dots, 100$.
solution: There are $50$ multiples of $2$, $33$ multiples of $3$ and $16$ multiples of $6$ (the overlap). So $P = \frac{50 + 33 - 16}{100} = 0.67$.

@practice num
prompt: Five cards are dealt from a well-shuffled 52-card deck. Find the probability that the hand has exactly two aces. Round to four decimal places.
answer: 0.0399
check: comb(4, 2) * comb(48, 3) / comb(52, 5)
tol: 0.0002
hint: All $\binom{52}{5}$ hands are equally likely, so count the favorable hands.
hint: Choose which $2$ of the $4$ aces are in the hand, then fill the other $3$ places from the $48$ non-aces.
solution: $P = \frac{\binom{4}{2}\binom{48}{3}}{\binom{52}{5}} = \frac{6 \cdot 17296}{2598960} \approx 0.0399$.

@quiz num
prompt: How many distinct arrangements are there of the letters of the word MISSISSIPPI?
answer: 34650
check: comb(11, 4) * comb(7, 4) * comb(3, 2)
hint: There are $11$ letters, but some repeat. Count how many times each letter appears.
hint: Divide $11!$ by $k!$ for each letter that appears $k$ times.
solution: The letters are M (1), I (4), S (4), P (2). The count is $\frac{11!}{1!\,4!\,4!\,2!} = \frac{39916800}{1152} = 34650$.

prompt: A 4-digit PIN is chosen so that each of the $10^4$ strings of digits from $0$ to $9$ is equally likely. What is the probability that all four digits are different?
answer: 0.504
check: sum(1 for a in range(10) for b in range(10) for c in range(10) for d in range(10) if a != b and a != c and a != d and b != c and b != d and c != d) / 10**4
hint: Count the PINs with four different digits using the multiplication rule: how many choices for the first digit, then the second, and so on?
hint: The favorable count is $10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8 \cdot 7$; divide it by $10^4$.
solution: $\frac{10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8 \cdot 7}{10^4} = \frac{5040}{10000} = 0.504$.
