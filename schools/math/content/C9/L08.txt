@article
The three points $(1, 2)$, $(2, 3)$, $(3, 7)$ do not lie on a line, so the system $a + b x_i = y_i$ has no solution. What is the best line? "Best" needs a notion of distance, and an inner product supplies one: we look for the point of a subspace closest to a given vector. The tool is orthogonal projection, and the tool for building it is Gram–Schmidt.

### Gram–Schmidt

**Theorem 1 (Gram–Schmidt procedure).** Let $v_1, \dots, v_m$ be linearly independent in an inner product space. Put $e_1 = v_1/\|v_1\|$ and, for $k \ge 2$,
$$w_k = v_k - \sum_{j < k} \langle v_k, e_j \rangle e_j, \qquad e_k = w_k / \|w_k\|.$$
Then $e_1, \dots, e_m$ is orthonormal and $\operatorname{span}(e_1, \dots, e_k) = \operatorname{span}(v_1, \dots, v_k)$ for every $k$.

*Proof.* Induct on $k$. For $k = 1$, $v_1 \ne 0$ by independence. Assume $e_1, \dots, e_{k-1}$ are orthonormal with the same span as $v_1, \dots, v_{k-1}$. Then $v_k$ is not in that span, so $w_k \ne 0$ (otherwise $v_k$ would be a combination of $e_1, \dots, e_{k-1}$), and the division is legitimate. For $i < k$, $\langle w_k, e_i \rangle = \langle v_k, e_i \rangle - \langle v_k, e_i \rangle = 0$, so $e_k$ is orthogonal to $e_1, \dots, e_{k-1}$, and it has norm $1$. Also $e_k \in \operatorname{span}(v_1, \dots, v_k)$ and $v_k \in \operatorname{span}(e_1, \dots, e_k)$, so the two spans agree: each is contained in the other, and $e_1, \dots, e_k$ are independent (Lesson 7, Theorem 3). $\blacksquare$

**Corollary.** Every finite-dimensional inner product space has an orthonormal basis, and every orthonormal list extends to an orthonormal basis. (Extend the list to a basis and apply Gram–Schmidt; it leaves an orthonormal start unchanged.)

```python
import math


def gram_schmidt(vectors):
    """Orthonormalize independent vectors (lists of numbers), as in Theorem 1."""
    basis = []
    for v in vectors:
        w = list(v)
        for e in basis:
            c = sum(a * b for a, b in zip(v, e))
            w = [a - c * b for a, b in zip(w, e)]
        norm = math.sqrt(sum(a * a for a in w))
        basis.append([a / norm for a in w])
    return basis


for e in gram_schmidt([[1, 1, 0], [1, 0, 1], [0, 1, 1]]):
    print([round(a, 4) for a in e])
```

Floating-point rounding makes the output only approximately orthonormal, and it divides by zero for dependent input; the proof assumes independence.

### Orthogonal complements and projection

For a subset $U \subseteq V$, the **orthogonal complement** is $U^\perp = \{v \in V : \langle v, u \rangle = 0 \text{ for all } u \in U\}$, a subspace.

**Theorem 2.** If $U$ is a finite-dimensional subspace of $V$, then $V = U \oplus U^\perp$.

*Proof.* Let $e_1, \dots, e_m$ be an orthonormal basis of $U$ (Corollary). For $v \in V$ put $u = \sum \langle v, e_j \rangle e_j \in U$ and $w = v - u$. Then $\langle w, e_k \rangle = \langle v, e_k \rangle - \langle v, e_k \rangle = 0$ for each $k$, so $w \in U^\perp$. Hence $V = U + U^\perp$. If $x \in U \cap U^\perp$ then $\langle x, x \rangle = 0$, so $x = 0$; the sum is direct by Lesson 1, Theorem 3. $\blacksquare$

The **orthogonal projection** $P_U\colon V \to U$ takes $v = u + w$ (with $u \in U$, $w \in U^\perp$) to $u$; in the proof, $P_U v = \sum_j \langle v, e_j \rangle e_j$. For a single nonzero vector $a$, the projection onto $\operatorname{span}\{a\}$ is $v \mapsto (\langle v, a \rangle / \|a\|^2)\,a$.

**Theorem 3 (best approximation).** Let $U$ be a finite-dimensional subspace and $v \in V$. For every $u \in U$, $\|v - P_U v\| \le \|v - u\|$, with equality if and only if $u = P_U v$.

*Proof.* Write $v - u = (v - P_U v) + (P_U v - u)$. The first term lies in $U^\perp$ and the second in $U$, so they are orthogonal, and the Pythagorean theorem gives $\|v - u\|^2 = \|v - P_U v\|^2 + \|P_U v - u\|^2$. The last term is $\ge 0$ and is $0$ iff $u = P_U v$. $\blacksquare$

### Least squares

Let $A$ be a real $m \times n$ matrix and $b \in \mathbb{R}^m$. Minimizing $\|Ax - b\|$ over $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ means finding the point of $U = \operatorname{range} A$ closest to $b$.

**Theorem 4 (normal equations).** $x$ minimizes $\|Ax - b\|$ if and only if $A^\top A x = A^\top b$. If the columns of $A$ are independent, $A^\top A$ is invertible and the minimizer is unique.

*Proof.* By Theorem 3, $x$ is a minimizer iff $Ax = P_U b$, iff $b - Ax \in U^\perp$ (Theorem 2 makes the decomposition unique), iff $\langle b - Ax, Ay \rangle = y^\top A^\top (b - Ax) = 0$ for all $y$, iff $A^\top(b - Ax) = 0$. If $A^\top A y = 0$ then $\|Ay\|^2 = y^\top A^\top A y = 0$, so $Ay = 0$ and $y = 0$ by independence of the columns: $A^\top A$ is injective, hence invertible. $\blacksquare$

@example
title: Gram–Schmidt on two vectors
problem: Apply Gram–Schmidt to $v_1 = (1, 1, 0)$ and $v_2 = (1, 2, 1)$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$.
step: $\|v_1\| = \sqrt2$, so $e_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1, 0)$.
step: $\langle v_2, e_1 \rangle = 3/\sqrt2$, so $w_2 = v_2 - \frac{3}{\sqrt2} e_1 = (1, 2, 1) - \frac32 (1, 1, 0) = (-\tfrac12, \tfrac12, 1)$.
step: $\|w_2\|^2 = \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 + 1 = \tfrac32$, so $\|w_2\| = \sqrt6/2$ and $e_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt6}(-1, 1, 2)$.
step: Check: $\langle e_1, e_2 \rangle = (-1 + 1 + 0)/\sqrt{12} = 0$ and $\|e_2\|^2 = 6/6 = 1$. Also $v_2 = \frac{3}{\sqrt2}e_1 + \frac{\sqrt6}{2}e_2$, so the spans agree.
answer: $e_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1, 0)$ and $e_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt6}(-1, 1, 2)$.

problem: Find the line $y = a + bx$ that minimizes the sum of squared vertical errors for the points $(1, 2)$, $(2, 3)$, $(3, 7)$.
step: Set up $Ac = y$ with $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$, $c = (a, b)$, $y = (2, 3, 7)$. The columns are independent, so the minimizer is unique.
step: Compute $A^\top A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 6 & 14 \end{pmatrix}$ and $A^\top y = (12, 29)$.
step: Solve $3a + 6b = 12$, $6a + 14b = 29$. From the first, $a = 4 - 2b$; substituting gives $24 - 12b + 14b = 29$, so $b = \tfrac52$ and $a = -1$.
step: Check orthogonality of the residual $r = y - Ac = (\tfrac12, -1, \tfrac12)$ to both columns: $\tfrac12 - 1 + \tfrac12 = 0$ and $\tfrac12 - 2 + \tfrac32 = 0$.
answer: $y = -1 + \tfrac52 x$.

@mistakes
- Subtracting projections onto the $v_j$ instead of the orthonormal $e_j$: the formula $\langle v_k, e_j \rangle e_j$ is the projection only when $e_j$ has norm $1$ and the $e_j$ are mutually orthogonal.
- Running Gram–Schmidt on dependent vectors: some $w_k$ is $0$ and the normalization divides by zero.
- Forgetting that $U^\perp$ and the decomposition $V = U \oplus U^\perp$ need $U$ finite-dimensional (or at least complete); in infinite dimensions $(U^\perp)^\perp$ can be larger than $U$.
- Solving $A^\top A x = A^\top b$ when the columns of $A$ are dependent and then claiming the answer is unique.

model:
```python
from fractions import Fraction


def fit_line(xs, ys):
    """Exact least-squares line y = a + b x via the normal equations."""
    n = len(xs)
    sx = sum(xs)
    sxx = sum(x * x for x in xs)
    sy = sum(ys)
    sxy = sum(x * y for x, y in zip(xs, ys))
    det = n * sxx - sx * sx
    if det == 0:
        return None
    a = Fraction(sy * sxx - sx * sxy, det)
    b = Fraction(n * sxy - sx * sy, det)
    return (a, b)
```
hint: Compute the four sums, then solve the $2 \times 2$ system by Cramer's rule. Check the determinant first.
solution: Form $n$, $\sum x_i$, $\sum x_i^2$, $\sum y_i$ and $\sum x_i y_i$. Cramer's rule on the $2 \times 2$ normal system gives $a = (\sum y \sum x^2 - \sum x \sum xy)/D$ and $b = (n \sum xy - \sum x \sum y)/D$ with $D = n \sum x^2 - (\sum x)^2$. The determinant $D$ is $0$ exactly when all $x_i$ are equal (Cauchy–Schwarz with equality), where the line is not unique. Using `Fraction` with integer numerators and denominators keeps the result exact.

prompt: Let $U$ be a finite-dimensional subspace of an inner product space $V$ and $v \in V$. Prove that $\|P_U v\| \le \|v\|$, with equality if and only if $v \in U$.

model:
```python
from fractions import Fraction


def orthogonalize(vectors):
    """Exact Gram-Schmidt without normalizing; dependent vectors are skipped."""
    kept = []
    for v in vectors:
        w = [Fraction(x) for x in v]
        for b in kept:
            c = sum(p * q for p, q in zip(w, b)) / sum(q * q for q in b)
            w = [p - c * q for p, q in zip(w, b)]
        if any(w):
            kept.append(w)
    return kept
```
hint: The projection of $w$ onto a kept vector $b$ is $(\langle w, b \rangle / \langle b, b \rangle)\,b$; subtract it for each kept $b$ in turn.
hint: After subtracting, test `any(w)`: it is `False` exactly when the remainder is the zero vector.
solution: Without normalization the projection coefficient is $\langle w, b \rangle / \langle b, b \rangle$. Subtracting the projections one at a time (subtracting from the running remainder $w$) keeps the kept vectors mutually orthogonal, as in Theorem 1. A remainder of $0$ means the input vector lies in the span of the earlier ones, which is exactly the case where the lesson's floating-point version would divide by zero; here it is skipped. Exact fractions avoid square roots and rounding, so equality tests are meaningful.

@quiz self
prompt: Let $U$ be a finite-dimensional subspace of an inner product space $V$. Prove that $P_U$ is linear, that $\operatorname{range} P_U = U$ and $\operatorname{null} P_U = U^\perp$.
hint: Use the unique decomposition $v = u + w$ with $u \in U$, $w \in U^\perp$ (Theorem 2). Linearity follows from uniqueness.
solution: By Theorem 2 every $v$ has a unique decomposition $v = u + w$ with $u \in U$ and $w \in U^\perp$, and $P_U v = u$. If $v = u + w$ and $v' = u' + w'$, then $av + v' = (au + u') + (aw + w')$ with $au + u' \in U$ and $aw + w' \in U^\perp$, so by uniqueness $P_U(av + v') = aP_U v + P_U v'$: $P_U$ is linear. Its range is contained in $U$ by definition, and each $u \in U$ is $u + 0$ with $0 \in U^\perp$, so $P_U u = u$ and $\operatorname{range} P_U = U$. Finally $P_U v = 0$ iff $v = 0 + w$ with $w \in U^\perp$ iff $v \in U^\perp$, so $\operatorname{null} P_U = U^\perp$. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Proves linearity from uniqueness of the decomposition $V = U \oplus U^\perp$.
rubric: Shows $P_U u = u$ for $u \in U$, so the range is exactly $U$.
rubric: Shows $P_U v = 0$ if and only if $v \in U^\perp$.
