@article
The symmetric matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $3$ and $1$ with eigenvectors $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$, which are perpendicular. The rotation $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has no real eigenvalue, and $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has only one eigendirection. Is perpendicularity a coincidence for symmetric matrices? It is a theorem, and its general form is the most useful result in this course.

### The adjoint

Let $V, W$ be finite-dimensional inner product spaces. Every linear functional $\varphi$ on $V$ has the form $\varphi(v) = \langle v, u \rangle$ for a unique $u \in V$ (the **Riesz representation**): if $e_1, \dots, e_n$ is an orthonormal basis, take $u = \sum \overline{\varphi(e_j)}\,e_j$; then $\langle v, u \rangle = \sum \langle v, e_j \rangle \varphi(e_j) = \varphi(v)$ by Lesson 7, Theorem 4, and if $\langle v, u \rangle = \langle v, u' \rangle$ for all $v$, then $v = u - u'$ gives $u = u'$.

For $T \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$ and $w \in W$, the map $v \mapsto \langle Tv, w \rangle$ is a linear functional, so there is a unique vector $T^* w \in V$ with $\langle Tv, w \rangle = \langle v, T^* w \rangle$ for all $v$. This defines the **adjoint** $T^* \in \mathcal{L}(W, V)$ (linearity follows from uniqueness). If $e_k$ and $f_j$ are orthonormal bases of $V$ and $W$, then $\mathcal{M}(T)_{jk} = \langle Te_k, f_j \rangle$ and $\mathcal{M}(T^*)_{kj} = \langle T^* f_j, e_k \rangle = \overline{\langle e_k, T^* f_j \rangle} = \overline{\langle Te_k, f_j \rangle}$: the matrix of $T^*$ is the **conjugate transpose** of the matrix of $T$, which is the transpose for real spaces.

An operator is **self-adjoint** if $T = T^*$ and **normal** if $TT^* = T^* T$. Self-adjoint operators are normal. For normal $T$, $\|Tv\|^2 = \langle T^* T v, v \rangle = \langle TT^* v, v \rangle = \|T^* v\|^2$.

**Theorem 1.** Let $T$ be self-adjoint. (a) Every eigenvalue of $T$ is real. (b) Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.

*Proof.* (a) If $Tv = \lambda v$ with $v \ne 0$, then $\lambda\|v\|^2 = \langle Tv, v \rangle = \langle v, Tv \rangle = \overline{\lambda}\|v\|^2$, so $\lambda = \overline{\lambda}$. (b) Let $Tu = \alpha u$, $Tv = \beta v$ with $\alpha \ne \beta$, both real by (a). Then $\alpha\langle u, v \rangle = \langle Tu, v \rangle = \langle u, Tv \rangle = \beta\langle u, v \rangle$, so $(\alpha - \beta)\langle u, v \rangle = 0$ and $\langle u, v \rangle = 0$. $\blacksquare$

### The spectral theorem for a real symmetric $2 \times 2$ matrix

**Theorem 2.** Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ b & c \end{pmatrix}$ with $a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}$. Then $\mathbb{R}^2$ has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of $A$.

*Proof.* The characteristic polynomial is $\lambda^2 - (a + c)\lambda + (ac - b^2)$ with discriminant $(a + c)^2 - 4(ac - b^2) = (a - c)^2 + 4b^2 \ge 0$, so both roots are real. If the discriminant is $0$ then $b = 0$ and $a = c$, so $A = aI$ and any orthonormal basis works. Otherwise there are two distinct real eigenvalues; $A$ is self-adjoint for the dot product (its matrix is symmetric), so by Theorem 1(b) the eigenvectors are orthogonal. Dividing each by its norm gives an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. $\blacksquare$

### The spectral theorem in general

**Theorem 3 (real spectral theorem).** Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional real inner product space and $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$. Then $T$ is self-adjoint if and only if $V$ has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of $T$.

*Proof.* ($\Leftarrow$) In such a basis $\mathcal{M}(T)$ is real diagonal, so $\mathcal{M}(T^*) = \mathcal{M}(T)^\top = \mathcal{M}(T)$ and $T^* = T$. ($\Rightarrow$) Induct on $n = \dim V$; the case $n = 0$ is empty. Let $n \ge 1$. In an orthonormal basis, $\mathcal{M}(T) = A$ is a real symmetric matrix. Regard $A$ as acting on $\mathbb{C}^n$. Its characteristic polynomial has a complex root $\lambda$ (fundamental theorem of algebra), so $Az = \lambda z$ for some $z \in \mathbb{C}^n$, $z \ne 0$. Writing $z^*$ for the conjugate transpose, $\lambda z^* z = z^* A z = (A^* z)^* z = (Az)^* z = \overline{\lambda} z^* z$, so $\lambda$ is real. Then $A - \lambda I$ is a real matrix with a nonzero complex null vector, so $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$ and it has a nonzero real null vector. Thus $T$ has a real eigenvector $v$, and we may take $\|v\| = 1$. The subspace $U = \operatorname{span}(v)^\perp$ is invariant: if $u \perp v$ then $\langle Tu, v \rangle = \langle u, Tv \rangle = \lambda\langle u, v \rangle = 0$. The restriction $T|_U$ is self-adjoint on the $(n-1)$-dimensional space $U$, so $U$ has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors; adjoining $v$ gives one for $V$ (Lesson 8, Theorem 2). $\blacksquare$

**Complex version (outline).** If $V$ is a complex inner product space, $T$ is normal if and only if $V$ has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. The easy direction is as above (diagonal matrices commute with their conjugate transposes). For the hard direction, Theorem 1 of Lesson 6 plus Gram–Schmidt gives an orthonormal basis in which $\mathcal{M}(T)$ is upper triangular (Gram–Schmidt preserves the spans of initial segments). If that triangular matrix $(a_{jk})$ comes from a normal $T$, then $\|Te_1\|^2 = |a_{11}|^2$ (the first column has only the entry $a_{11}$) equals $\|T^* e_1\|^2 = \sum_k |a_{1k}|^2$ (the first column of the conjugate transpose is the conjugated first row), forcing $a_{1k} = 0$ for $k > 1$; repeating with the second row and so on shows the matrix is diagonal.

Equivalently, with orthonormal eigenvectors $e_j$ and eigenvalues $\lambda_j$, $T = \sum_j \lambda_j\,e_j e_j^*$: a sum of eigenvalues times orthogonal projections onto the eigenlines.

Two examples show how far Theorem 3 reaches. Over $\mathbb{R}$, self-adjointness cannot be weakened to normality: the rotation $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ is normal but not self-adjoint and has no real eigenvector (over $\mathbb{C}$ it does have the orthonormal eigenbasis $(1, -i)/\sqrt2$, $(1, i)/\sqrt2$, as the complex spectral theorem below predicts). And without normality there may be no eigenbasis at all, even over $\mathbb{C}$: the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ is not normal, and its only eigenvectors are the multiples of $(1, 0)$.

@example
title: Spectral decomposition of a symmetric matrix
problem: Find an orthonormal eigenbasis of $A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and write $A = \lambda_1 e_1 e_1^\top + \lambda_2 e_2 e_2^\top$.
step: $\det(A - \lambda I) = (3 - \lambda)(-\lambda) - 4 = \lambda^2 - 3\lambda - 4 = (\lambda - 4)(\lambda + 1)$, so the eigenvalues are $4$ and $-1$ (real, as Theorem 1(a) requires).
step: For $\lambda = 4$: $A - 4I = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 2 & -4 \end{pmatrix}$ gives $x = 2y$, eigenvector $(2, 1)$. For $\lambda = -1$: $A + I = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ gives $y = -2x$, eigenvector $(1, -2)$.
step: These are orthogonal: $2 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot (-2) = 0$, as Theorem 1(b) predicts. Normalize: $e_1 = (2, 1)/\sqrt5$, $e_2 = (1, -2)/\sqrt5$.
step: Assemble: $4 e_1 e_1^\top = \frac45\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $-1\,e_2 e_2^\top = -\frac15\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ -2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$; their sum is $\frac15\begin{pmatrix} 15 & 10 \\ 10 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = A$.
answer: $A = 4 e_1 e_1^\top - e_2 e_2^\top$ with $e_1 = (2,1)/\sqrt5$ and $e_2 = (1,-2)/\sqrt5$.

@example
title: A normal operator that is not self-adjoint
problem: Show that $R = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ is normal, not self-adjoint, and find an orthonormal eigenbasis of $\mathbb{C}^2$.
step: $R^* = R^\top = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = -R \ne R$, so $R$ is not self-adjoint.
step: $RR^* = -R^2 = I$ and $R^*R = -R^2 = I$, so $R$ is normal.
step: $R(1, -i) = (i, 1) = i\,(1, -i)$ and $R(1, i) = (-i, 1) = -i\,(1, i)$, so the eigenvalues are $i$ and $-i$.
step: In $\mathbb{C}^2$, $\langle (1, -i), (1, i) \rangle = 1\cdot\overline{1} + (-i)\overline{i} = 1 + (-i)(-i) = 1 - 1 = 0$, and each vector has norm $\sqrt2$. So $(1, -i)/\sqrt2$, $(1, i)/\sqrt2$ is an orthonormal eigenbasis. The eigenvalues are not real, consistent with Theorem 1(a) failing for a non-self-adjoint operator.
answer: Eigenvalues $\pm i$ with orthonormal eigenvectors $(1, \mp i)/\sqrt2$.

@mistakes
- Forgetting the conjugate in the adjoint of a complex matrix: $A^*$ is the conjugate transpose, and $A^*$ is the transpose only for real matrices.
- Using the spectral theorem for a matrix that is symmetric but complex: complex symmetric matrices (with $A = A^\top$) need not be diagonalizable; the right condition over $\mathbb{C}$ is $A = A^*$, or more generally normality.
- Believing that eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix are automatically orthogonal when they share an eigenvalue: within one eigenspace, an orthonormal basis must be chosen (for example, by Gram–Schmidt).
- Treating "self-adjoint" as meaning "orthogonal eigenvectors": normal operators have the orthogonality but may have complex eigenvalues.

prompt: Find the smaller eigenvalue of the real symmetric matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$.
answer: -3
check: min(np.linalg.eigvalsh(mat([[1, 2], [2, -2]])))
hint: The trace is the sum of the eigenvalues and the determinant is their product.

@practice self
prompt: Let $A$ be a real symmetric $2 \times 2$ matrix with distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$ and orthonormal eigenvectors $e_1, e_2$ (as in the spectral theorem). Prove that $A = \lambda_1 e_1 e_1^\top + \lambda_2 e_2 e_2^\top$.
hint: Two linear maps on $\mathbb{R}^2$ that agree on a basis are equal. Apply the right-hand side to $e_1$ and $e_2$.
solution: Let $B = \lambda_1 e_1 e_1^\top + \lambda_2 e_2 e_2^\top$. For a vector $x$, $e_j e_j^\top x = (e_j^\top x)\,e_j = \langle x, e_j \rangle e_j$. Apply $B$ to $e_1$: $Be_1 = \lambda_1 \langle e_1, e_1 \rangle e_1 + \lambda_2 \langle e_1, e_2 \rangle e_2 = \lambda_1 e_1$, since $e_1, e_2$ are orthonormal. Similarly $Be_2 = \lambda_2 e_2$. Also $Ae_1 = \lambda_1 e_1$ and $Ae_2 = \lambda_2 e_2$. So $A$ and $B$ agree on the basis $e_1, e_2$ of $\mathbb{R}^2$, and linear maps that agree on a basis are equal (Lesson 3, Theorem 1). Hence $A = B$. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Identifies $e_j e_j^\top x$ with $\langle x, e_j \rangle e_j$.
rubric: Evaluates both sides on $e_1$ and $e_2$ using orthonormality.
rubric: Concludes equality because linear maps agreeing on a basis are equal.

@quiz self
prompt: Let $T$ be a normal operator on a finite-dimensional complex inner product space. Prove that if $Tu = \alpha u$ and $Tv = \beta v$ with $\alpha \ne \beta$, then $\langle u, v \rangle = 0$.
hint: First show $T - \alpha I$ is normal, so $\|(T - \alpha I)u\| = \|(T - \alpha I)^* u\|$, and deduce $T^* u = \overline{\alpha} u$.
solution: The operator $S = T - \alpha I$ is normal: $S^* = T^* - \overline{\alpha}I$ commutes with $S$ because $T$ and $T^*$ commute and $I$ commutes with everything. For a normal operator $\|Sx\| = \|S^* x\|$ for all $x$ (as in the lesson). With $x = u$, $\|S^* u\| = \|Su\| = 0$, so $T^* u = \overline{\alpha} u$. Now $\alpha\langle u, v \rangle = \langle Tu, v \rangle = \langle u, T^* v \rangle$. Applying the same argument to $v$ gives $T^* v = \overline{\beta} v$, so $\langle u, T^* v \rangle = \langle u, \overline{\beta} v \rangle = \beta\langle u, v \rangle$. Hence $(\alpha - \beta)\langle u, v \rangle = 0$ and $\langle u, v \rangle = 0$. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Shows $T - \alpha I$ is normal with adjoint $T^* - \overline{\alpha}I$.
rubric: Uses $\|Sx\| = \|S^* x\|$ to get $T^* u = \overline{\alpha} u$ and $T^* v = \overline{\beta} v$.
rubric: Computes $\langle Tu, v \rangle$ in two ways to conclude $(\alpha - \beta)\langle u, v \rangle = 0$.
