@article
The Fibonacci numbers satisfy $F_{n+1} = F_n + F_{n-1}$, so $(F_{n+1}, F_n)^\top = A^n (1, 0)^\top$ with $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. The ratios $F_{n+1}/F_n$ approach $\varphi = (1 + \sqrt5)/2 \approx 1.618$. Why? Because $A(\varphi, 1)^\top = (\varphi + 1, \varphi)^\top = \varphi\,(\varphi, 1)^\top$, using $\varphi^2 = \varphi + 1$. On the line through $(\varphi, 1)$ the matrix acts as multiplication by $\varphi$, and repeated application pulls every vector toward that line.

### Invariant subspaces and eigenvectors

An **operator** is a linear map $T \in \mathcal{L}(V) = \mathcal{L}(V, V)$. A subspace $U$ is **invariant** under $T$ if $Tu \in U$ for all $u \in U$. The smallest nonzero invariant subspaces are lines: a scalar $\lambda \in F$ is an **eigenvalue** of $T$ if there is a vector $v \ne 0$ with $Tv = \lambda v$, and such a $v$ is an **eigenvector**. The **eigenspace** is $E(\lambda, T) = \operatorname{null}(T - \lambda I)$. So $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue exactly when $T - \lambda I$ is not injective; for $\dim V < \infty$ this means not invertible.

Examples. For $Tp = xp'$ on $\mathcal{P}_3(\mathbb{R})$ we have $T x^k = k x^k$, so $0, 1, 2, 3$ are eigenvalues. The subspace $\mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R})$ is invariant under differentiation. Rotation of $\mathbb{R}^2$ by $90^\circ$, $R(x, y) = (-y, x)$, has no eigenvalue: $Rv = \lambda v$ would give $R^2 v = -v = \lambda^2 v$, impossible for real $\lambda$ and $v \ne 0$. Over $\mathbb{C}$, the same matrix has eigenvalues $\pm i$.

**Theorem 1.** Eigenvectors belonging to distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent.

*Proof.* Suppose $v_1, \dots, v_k$ are eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k$ and the list is dependent. Let $m$ be the least index such that $v_1, \dots, v_m$ is dependent; $m \ge 2$ because $v_1 \ne 0$. Take $a_1 v_1 + \dots + a_m v_m = 0$ with not all $a_j = 0$; minimality forces $a_m \ne 0$. Apply $T - \lambda_m I$: $\sum_{j < m} a_j (\lambda_j - \lambda_m) v_j = 0$. By minimality $v_1, \dots, v_{m-1}$ are independent, so $a_j(\lambda_j - \lambda_m) = 0$ and hence $a_j = 0$ for $j < m$. Then $a_m v_m = 0$ with $a_m \ne 0$ gives $v_m = 0$, a contradiction. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary.** An operator on an $n$-dimensional space has at most $n$ distinct eigenvalues (Lesson 2: an independent list has length at most $n$).

### Existence over the complex numbers

For a polynomial $p(z) = a_0 + a_1 z + \dots + a_m z^m$ put $p(T) = a_0 I + a_1 T + \dots + a_m T^m$. Then $(pq)(T) = p(T)q(T)$, because powers of $T$ commute.

**Theorem 2.** If $V \ne \{0\}$ is a finite-dimensional complex vector space, every $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ has an eigenvalue.

*Proof.* Let $n = \dim V$ and $v \ne 0$. The $n + 1$ vectors $v, Tv, \dots, T^n v$ are dependent: $a_0 v + a_1 Tv + \dots + a_n T^n v = 0$ with not all $a_j = 0$. Let $m$ be the largest index with $a_m \ne 0$; then $m \ge 1$, since $m = 0$ would give $a_0 v = 0$ with $a_0 \ne 0$. The polynomial $p(z) = a_0 + \dots + a_m z^m$ is nonconstant, so by the fundamental theorem of algebra $p(z) = a_m (z - \lambda_1) \cdots (z - \lambda_m)$. Hence $0 = p(T)v = a_m (T - \lambda_1 I) \cdots (T - \lambda_m I) v$. The product kills $v \ne 0$, so it is not injective; a composition of injective maps is injective, so some $T - \lambda_j I$ is not injective, and $\lambda_j$ is an eigenvalue. $\blacksquare$

The hypotheses matter: the rotation above is a real operator on a nonzero finite-dimensional space with no eigenvalue, because $\mathbb{R}$ is not algebraically closed.

### The power method

Eigenvalues are rarely computed from roots of a polynomial. Suppose $A$ has independent eigenvectors $v_1, \dots, v_n$ with $|\lambda_1| > |\lambda_2| \ge \dots \ge |\lambda_n|$ and $x_0 = \sum c_i v_i$ with $c_1 \ne 0$. Then
$$A^k x_0 = \lambda_1^k \Big( c_1 v_1 + \sum_{i \ge 2} c_i (\lambda_i / \lambda_1)^k v_i \Big),$$
and the bracket tends to $c_1 v_1$ at the rate $|\lambda_2 / \lambda_1|^k$. Normalizing at each step avoids overflow.

```python
import math


def power_method(A, x, steps):
    """Estimate the dominant eigenvalue of a square matrix (list of rows)."""
    n = len(A)
    for _ in range(steps):
        y = [sum(A[i][j] * x[j] for j in range(n)) for i in range(n)]
        norm = math.sqrt(sum(t * t for t in y))
        x = [t / norm for t in y]
    Ax = [sum(A[i][j] * x[j] for j in range(n)) for i in range(n)]
    return sum(a * b for a, b in zip(Ax, x))  # x is a unit vector


print(round(power_method([[1, 1], [1, 0]], [1, 0], 30), 6))
```

It prints `1.618034`. The method fails if $c_1 = 0$ or if $|\lambda_1| = |\lambda_2|$ (for example, the rotation by $90^\circ$ keeps turning the vector forever). Passing a test on one matrix is evidence of the idea, not a proof of convergence in general; the displayed formula is the proof for the case above.

@example
title: Eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a $2 \times 2$ matrix
problem: Find the eigenvalues and eigenspaces of $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 4 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ and check Theorem 1.
step: $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue iff $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$. Here $\det(A - \lambda I) = (1 - \lambda)(3 - \lambda) - 8 = \lambda^2 - 4\lambda - 5 = (\lambda - 5)(\lambda + 1)$, so $\lambda = 5$ or $\lambda = -1$.
step: For $\lambda = 5$: $A - 5I = \begin{pmatrix} -4 & 2 \\ 4 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$, and $-4x + 2y = 0$ gives $y = 2x$. So $E(5, A) = \operatorname{span}\{(1, 2)\}$.
step: For $\lambda = -1$: $A + I = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 4 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, and $2x + 2y = 0$ gives $y = -x$. So $E(-1, A) = \operatorname{span}\{(1, -1)\}$.
step: The eigenvectors $(1,2)$ and $(1,-1)$ are independent because neither is a multiple of the other, as Theorem 1 predicts for distinct eigenvalues, and they form a basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$.
answer: Eigenvalues $5$ and $-1$ with eigenspaces $\operatorname{span}\{(1,2)\}$ and $\operatorname{span}\{(1,-1)\}$.

@example
title: All eigenvalues of $T p = x p'$
problem: Let $T \in \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}_3(\mathbb{R}))$ be $Tp = xp'$. Show that the eigenvalues of $T$ are exactly $0, 1, 2, 3$ and find each eigenspace.
step: $T x^k = x \cdot k x^{k-1} = k x^k$ for $k = 0, \dots, 3$, so each $k \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$ is an eigenvalue with eigenvector $x^k$.
step: Let $Tp = \lambda p$ with $p = a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + a_3 x^3$ and $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$. Comparing coefficients of $x^k$ in $\sum k a_k x^k = \lambda \sum a_k x^k$ gives $(k - \lambda) a_k = 0$ for each $k$.
step: If $\lambda \notin \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$, every $k - \lambda \ne 0$, so all $a_k = 0$ and $p = 0$: $\lambda$ is not an eigenvalue.
step: If $\lambda = k_0 \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$, then $a_k = 0$ for $k \ne k_0$, so $p = a_{k_0} x^{k_0}$ and $E(k_0, T) = \operatorname{span}\{x^{k_0}\}$.
answer: The eigenvalues are $0, 1, 2, 3$, with $E(k, T) = \operatorname{span}\{x^k\}$.

@mistakes
- Accepting $v = 0$ as an eigenvector: $T0 = \lambda 0$ holds for every $\lambda$, which is why eigenvectors must be nonzero.
- Assuming every operator has an eigenvalue: this needs a complex space (or a special argument); the rotation by $90^\circ$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$ has none.
- Reading Theorem 1 as "independent eigenvectors have distinct eigenvalues": two independent vectors of one eigenspace share an eigenvalue.
- Running the power method when the two largest eigenvalues have equal absolute value, or from a start vector with no component along the dominant eigenvector.

model:
```python
import math


def power_until(A, x, tol, max_steps):
    """Power iteration that stops when the Rayleigh quotient settles."""
    n = len(A)
    prev = None
    for k in range(1, max_steps + 1):
        y = [sum(A[i][j] * x[j] for j in range(n)) for i in range(n)]
        norm = math.sqrt(sum(t * t for t in y))
        x = [t / norm for t in y]
        Ax = [sum(A[i][j] * x[j] for j in range(n)) for i in range(n)]
        r = sum(a * b for a, b in zip(Ax, x))
        if prev is not None and abs(r - prev) < tol:
            return (r, k)
        prev = r
    return (prev, max_steps)
```
hint: Keep the previous quotient in a variable that starts as `None`, and compare only once it has a value.
hint: Compute the Rayleigh quotient after normalizing, using the new unit vector, and count the steps from $1$.
solution: Each pass multiplies, normalizes and records the Rayleigh quotient of the new unit vector. The test is skipped on the first pass because there is no earlier quotient. With `tol = 0.0` the strict inequality never holds, so the loop ends after `max_steps` passes, which is the cap the prompt asks for. The number of passes needed depends on the ratio $|\lambda_2 / \lambda_1|$ of the two largest eigenvalues in absolute value: here it is $1/2$ and the Rayleigh quotient of a symmetric matrix converges at the square of that ratio per step.

answer: 3.414

hint: Expand $\det(A - \lambda I)$ along the first row, writing $a = 2 - \lambda$.

@quiz self
prompt: Let $V$ be a complex vector space and $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$. Prove that $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$ is an eigenvalue of $T^2$ if and only if $\lambda = \mu^2$ for some eigenvalue $\mu$ of $T$.
hint: Choose $\mu$ with $\mu^2 = \lambda$ and factor $T^2 - \lambda I = (T - \mu I)(T + \mu I)$.
solution: ($\Leftarrow$) If $Tv = \mu v$ with $v \ne 0$ then $T^2 v = \mu^2 v$. ($\Rightarrow$) Let $\lambda$ be an eigenvalue of $T^2$. Choose $\mu \in \mathbb{C}$ with $\mu^2 = \lambda$. Then $T^2 - \lambda I = (T - \mu I)(T + \mu I)$, since $T$ commutes with $I$. This operator is not injective. A composition of injective maps is injective, so $T - \mu I$ or $T + \mu I$ is not injective. In the first case $\mu$ is an eigenvalue of $T$ with $\mu^2 = \lambda$; in the second $-\mu$ is an eigenvalue of $T$ with $(-\mu)^2 = \lambda$. $\blacksquare$
