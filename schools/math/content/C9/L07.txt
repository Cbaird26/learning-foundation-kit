@article
The functions $\sin x$ and $\cos x$ on $[-\pi, \pi]$ are "perpendicular" in a sense that has nothing to do with angles in the plane: $\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \sin x \cos x\,dx = 0$, while $\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \sin^2 x\,dx = \pi$ plays the role of a squared length. The dot product of $\mathbb{R}^n$ has an abstract version that measures lengths and angles in any vector space where it exists.

### Inner products

Let $F$ be $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$. An **inner product** on $V$ is a function $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle\colon V \times V \to F$ with, for all $u, v, w \in V$ and $a \in F$:
1. $\langle v, v \rangle \ge 0$, and $\langle v, v \rangle = 0$ only if $v = 0$ (positive definiteness);
2. $\langle u + v, w \rangle = \langle u, w \rangle + \langle v, w \rangle$ and $\langle au, w \rangle = a\langle u, w \rangle$ (linear in the first slot);
3. $\langle u, v \rangle = \overline{\langle v, u \rangle}$ (conjugate symmetry).

It follows that $\langle u, av \rangle = \overline{a}\langle u, v \rangle$ and $\langle u, v + w \rangle = \langle u, v \rangle + \langle u, w \rangle$. Examples: $\langle x, y \rangle = \sum x_j \overline{y_j}$ on $F^n$; $\langle f, g \rangle = \int_a^b f\,\overline{g}$ on the continuous functions on $[a, b]$; $\langle x, y \rangle = x_1 y_1 + 2x_2 y_2$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$. The **norm** is $\|v\| = \sqrt{\langle v, v \rangle}$, and $u, v$ are **orthogonal** if $\langle u, v \rangle = 0$.

**Theorem 1 (Pythagorean theorem).** If $\langle u, v \rangle = 0$, then $\|u + v\|^2 = \|u\|^2 + \|v\|^2$.

*Proof.* $\|u + v\|^2 = \|u\|^2 + \langle u, v \rangle + \langle v, u \rangle + \|v\|^2$, and both cross terms vanish. $\blacksquare$

The converse holds over $\mathbb{R}$, where the cross terms add to $2\langle u, v \rangle$, but fails over $\mathbb{C}$: in $\mathbb{C}$ with $\langle a, b \rangle = a\overline{b}$, take $u = 1$ and $v = i$. Then $\|u + v\|^2 = |1 + i|^2 = 2 = \|u\|^2 + \|v\|^2$, yet $\langle 1, i \rangle = -i \ne 0$.

**Theorem 2 (Cauchy–Schwarz inequality).** For all $u, v \in V$, $|\langle u, v \rangle| \le \|u\|\,\|v\|$, with equality if and only if one of $u, v$ is a scalar multiple of the other.

*Proof.* If $v = 0$ both sides are $0$. Let $v \ne 0$ and $c = \langle u, v \rangle / \|v\|^2$, $w = u - cv$. Then $\langle w, v \rangle = \langle u, v \rangle - c\|v\|^2 = 0$, so $u = cv + w$ with $cv \perp w$. By Theorem 1, $\|u\|^2 = |c|^2\|v\|^2 + \|w\|^2 \ge |\langle u, v \rangle|^2 / \|v\|^2$. Multiplying by $\|v\|^2$ gives the inequality. Equality holds iff $\|w\|^2 = 0$, that is $u = cv$. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary (triangle inequality).** $\|u + v\| \le \|u\| + \|v\|$.

*Proof.* $\|u + v\|^2 = \|u\|^2 + 2\operatorname{Re}\langle u, v \rangle + \|v\|^2 \le \|u\|^2 + 2\|u\|\|v\| + \|v\|^2 = (\|u\| + \|v\|)^2$, using $\operatorname{Re} z \le |z|$ and Theorem 2; take square roots of nonnegative numbers. $\blacksquare$

Every norm from an inner product satisfies the **parallelogram law** $\|u + v\|^2 + \|u - v\|^2 = 2\|u\|^2 + 2\|v\|^2$ (expand both squares; the cross terms cancel). The norm $\|(x, y)\|_1 = |x| + |y|$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$ violates it: for $u = (1, 0)$, $v = (0, 1)$ the left side is $4 + 4 = 8$ and the right side is $2 + 2 = 4$. So not every norm comes from an inner product.

### Orthonormal bases

A list $e_1, \dots, e_m$ is **orthonormal** if $\langle e_j, e_k \rangle$ is $1$ for $j = k$ and $0$ otherwise.

**Theorem 3.** For an orthonormal list, $\|a_1 e_1 + \dots + a_m e_m\|^2 = |a_1|^2 + \dots + |a_m|^2$. In particular an orthonormal list is linearly independent.

*Proof.* Expanding $\langle \sum a_j e_j, \sum a_k e_k \rangle = \sum_{j,k} a_j \overline{a_k}\langle e_j, e_k \rangle = \sum |a_j|^2$. If the combination is $0$, every $|a_j|^2 = 0$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 4.** If $e_1, \dots, e_n$ is an orthonormal basis of $V$, then every $v \in V$ satisfies $v = \sum_j \langle v, e_j \rangle e_j$ and $\|v\|^2 = \sum_j |\langle v, e_j \rangle|^2$ (Parseval's identity).

*Proof.* Write $v = \sum a_j e_j$. Taking the inner product with $e_k$ gives $\langle v, e_k \rangle = a_k$. The norm identity is Theorem 3. $\blacksquare$

Orthonormal bases turn coordinates into inner products: no linear system has to be solved. Lesson 8 shows how to build one from any basis.

@example
title: Checking an inner product and expanding in a basis
problem: Show that $\langle x, y \rangle = x_1 y_1 + 2x_2 y_2$ is an inner product on $\mathbb{R}^2$. Then find $\|(3, 1)\|$ and a vector of norm $1$ orthogonal to $(2, 1)$ in this inner product.
step: Positivity: $\langle x, x \rangle = x_1^2 + 2x_2^2 \ge 0$, and it is $0$ only if $x_1 = x_2 = 0$. Linearity in the first slot and symmetry are clear from the formula.
step: $\|(3, 1)\|^2 = 9 + 2 = 11$, so $\|(3, 1)\| = \sqrt{11}$.
step: A vector $(a, b)$ is orthogonal to $(2, 1)$ iff $2a + 2b = 0$, so $(a, b) = (1, -1)$ up to scaling.
step: $\|(1, -1)\|^2 = 1 + 2 = 3$, so $(1, -1)/\sqrt3$ has norm $1$. Note that $(1, -1)$ is not orthogonal to $(2, 1)$ for the ordinary dot product, which gives $1$.
answer: $\|(3, 1)\| = \sqrt{11}$, and $(1, -1)/\sqrt3$ is a unit vector orthogonal to $(2, 1)$.

@example
title: Parseval's identity in an orthonormal basis
problem: The vectors $e_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt5}(1, 2)$ and $e_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt5}(2, -1)$ form an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$ with the dot product. Expand $v = (7, 1)$ and check Parseval's identity.
step: Check orthonormality: $\langle e_1, e_2 \rangle = (2 - 2)/5 = 0$ and $\|e_1\|^2 = \|e_2\|^2 = 5/5 = 1$.
step: Coefficients: $\langle v, e_1 \rangle = (7 + 2)/\sqrt5 = 9/\sqrt5$ and $\langle v, e_2 \rangle = (14 - 1)/\sqrt5 = 13/\sqrt5$.
step: Parseval: $\|v\|^2 = 49 + 1 = 50$, and $|9/\sqrt5|^2 + |13/\sqrt5|^2 = (81 + 169)/5 = 50$.
answer: $v = \frac{9}{\sqrt5}e_1 + \frac{13}{\sqrt5}e_2$, and both sides of Parseval equal $50$.

@mistakes
- Forgetting the conjugate over $\mathbb{C}$: $\langle u, av \rangle = \overline{a}\langle u, v \rangle$, so $\langle v, v \rangle$ is $\sum |v_j|^2$, not $\sum v_j^2$.
- Treating $\|u + v\|^2 = \|u\|^2 + \|v\|^2$ as equivalent to orthogonality over $\mathbb{C}$: only the real parts of the cross terms cancel.
- Believing every norm comes from an inner product: the parallelogram law fails for $\|\cdot\|_1$ and $\|\cdot\|_\infty$.
- Writing $\langle v, e_j \rangle e_j$ expansions for a basis that is not orthonormal; the formula needs $\langle e_j, e_k \rangle = \delta_{jk}$.

@practice mc
prompt: Which formula defines an inner product on $\mathbb{R}^2$?
choice*: $\langle x, y \rangle = 2x_1 y_1 + x_1 y_2 + x_2 y_1 + 2x_2 y_2$
choice: $\langle x, y \rangle = x_1 y_1 - x_2 y_2$
choice: $\langle x, y \rangle = x_1 y_2 + x_2 y_1$
choice: $\langle x, y \rangle = |x_1 y_1| + |x_2 y_2|$
hint: Test $\langle x, x \rangle$ on a few vectors, and test linearity in the first slot with a negative scalar.
solution: For the first formula, $\langle x, x \rangle = 2x_1^2 + 2x_1 x_2 + 2x_2^2 = x_1^2 + x_2^2 + (x_1 + x_2)^2 \ge 0$ with equality only at $0$; it is bilinear and symmetric. The second fails positivity: $\langle (0,1), (0,1) \rangle = -1$. The third gives $\langle x, x \rangle = 2x_1 x_2$, negative at $(1, -1)$. The fourth is not linear: $\langle -x, y \rangle \ne -\langle x, y \rangle$ in general.

@practice ms
prompt: Which statements hold for every inner product space $V$ (over $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$) and all $u, v \in V$?
choice*: $|\langle u, v \rangle| \le \|u\|\,\|v\|$
choice*: $\|u + v\|^2 + \|u - v\|^2 = 2\|u\|^2 + 2\|v\|^2$
choice: $\|u + v\| = \|u\| + \|v\|$
choice*: If $\langle u, v \rangle = 0$, then $\|u + v\|^2 = \|u\|^2 + \|v\|^2$.
choice: If $\|u + v\|^2 = \|u\|^2 + \|v\|^2$, then $\langle u, v \rangle = 0$.
hint: The last statement is true over $\mathbb{R}$. What happens over $\mathbb{C}$ for $u = 1$, $v = i$?
solution: The first three true statements are Cauchy–Schwarz, the parallelogram law and the Pythagorean theorem. The statement $\|u + v\| = \|u\| + \|v\|$ is false for $u = (1, 0)$ and $v = (0, 1)$ (it holds only when one is a nonnegative multiple of the other). The converse of the Pythagorean theorem fails over $\mathbb{C}$: for $u = 1$, $v = i$ in $\mathbb{C}$ the norm identity holds but $\langle u, v \rangle = -i \ne 0$.

@practice self
prompt: Let $V$ be a real inner product space and $u, v \in V$. Prove that $\|u\| = \|v\|$ if and only if $u + v$ and $u - v$ are orthogonal.
hint: Expand $\langle u + v, u - v \rangle$ using bilinearity and symmetry.
solution: By bilinearity, $\langle u + v, u - v \rangle = \langle u, u \rangle - \langle u, v \rangle + \langle v, u \rangle - \langle v, v \rangle$. In a real inner product space $\langle v, u \rangle = \langle u, v \rangle$, so the middle terms cancel and $\langle u + v, u - v \rangle = \|u\|^2 - \|v\|^2$. This is $0$ if and only if $\|u\|^2 = \|v\|^2$, that is (norms are nonnegative) iff $\|u\| = \|v\|$. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Expands $\langle u + v, u - v \rangle$ into four terms by bilinearity.
rubric: Uses real symmetry to cancel the cross terms, getting $\|u\|^2 - \|v\|^2$.
rubric: Passes from $\|u\|^2 = \|v\|^2$ to $\|u\| = \|v\|$ because norms are nonnegative.

@quiz mc
prompt: Which of these norms on $\mathbb{R}^2$ does not come from any inner product?
choice*: $\|(x, y)\| = \max\{|x|, |y|\}$
choice: $\|(x, y)\| = \sqrt{x^2 + 4y^2}$
choice: $\|(x, y)\| = \sqrt{x^2 + xy + y^2}$
choice: $\|(x, y)\| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$
hint: Test the parallelogram law with $u = (1, 0)$ and $v = (0, 1)$.
solution: For the max norm, $\|u + v\| = \|u - v\| = 1$, so the left side of the parallelogram law is $2$, while the right side is $2 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 1 = 4$: it fails, so the max norm is not induced by an inner product. The other three are induced by $\langle x, y \rangle = x_1 y_1 + 4x_2 y_2$, by $x_1 y_1 + \tfrac12(x_1 y_2 + x_2 y_1) + x_2 y_2$ (positive definite since $x^2 + xy + y^2 = (x + y/2)^2 + \tfrac34 y^2$), and by the dot product.

@quiz self
prompt: Let $e_1, \dots, e_m$ be an orthonormal list in an inner product space $V$ and let $v \in V$. Prove Bessel's inequality: $\sum_{j=1}^m |\langle v, e_j \rangle|^2 \le \|v\|^2$.
hint: Write $v = \sum \langle v, e_j \rangle e_j + w$ and show $w$ is orthogonal to each $e_j$, then use the Pythagorean theorem.
solution: Let $a_j = \langle v, e_j \rangle$ and $w = v - \sum_j a_j e_j$. For each $k$, $\langle w, e_k \rangle = \langle v, e_k \rangle - \sum_j a_j \langle e_j, e_k \rangle = a_k - a_k = 0$. So $w$ is orthogonal to every $e_k$, hence to $\sum a_j e_j$ (linearity in the second slot, with conjugates). By the Pythagorean theorem and Theorem 3, $\|v\|^2 = \|\sum a_j e_j\|^2 + \|w\|^2 = \sum |a_j|^2 + \|w\|^2 \ge \sum |a_j|^2$. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Defines the remainder $w = v - \sum \langle v, e_j \rangle e_j$ and proves $w \perp e_k$ for each $k$.
rubric: Applies the Pythagorean theorem to $v = \sum a_j e_j + w$.
rubric: Uses Theorem 3 to evaluate $\|\sum a_j e_j\|^2 = \sum |a_j|^2$ and drops $\|w\|^2 \ge 0$.
