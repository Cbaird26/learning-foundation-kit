### Week-by-week

**Weeks 1–2: vector spaces.** A vector space over a field $F$ satisfies eight axioms; consequences like $0v = 0$ and $(-1)v = -v$ follow. The subspace test needs $0$, closure under sums and under scalars. $U + W$ is direct iff $U \cap W = \{0\}$. Span, independence, basis; any independent list is no longer than any spanning list, so dimension is well defined; $\dim(U + W) = \dim U + \dim W - \dim(U \cap W)$.

**Weeks 3–4: linear maps.** $T$ is injective iff $\operatorname{null} T = \{0\}$. Rank–nullity: $\dim V = \dim \operatorname{null} T + \dim \operatorname{range} T$. Matrices, $\mathcal{M}(ST) = \mathcal{M}(S)\mathcal{M}(T)$, change of basis $P^{-1}AP$; quotient spaces and the first isomorphism theorem; dual bases and $\mathcal{M}(T') = \mathcal{M}(T)^\top$.

**Weeks 5–6: eigenvalues.** Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are independent; complex operators have eigenvalues; complex operators have upper-triangular matrices, whose eigenvalues are the diagonal entries. $T$ is diagonalizable iff $\dim V = \sum \dim E(\lambda_j, T)$.

**Weeks 7–8: inner products.** Cauchy–Schwarz, triangle inequality, Parseval. Gram–Schmidt; $V = U \oplus U^\perp$; the projection $P_U v$ is the closest point of $U$ to $v$; normal equations $A^\top A x = A^\top b$.

**Weeks 9–10: the spectral theorem and SVD.** Self-adjoint operators have real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors; real self-adjoint iff orthonormal eigenbasis (complex: normal). Positive operators, $T = R^*R$, isometries ($S^*S = I$), $A = U\Sigma V^\top$ with singular values the square roots of the eigenvalues of $A^\top A$.

**Weeks 11–12: determinants, traces, Jordan form.** $\det$ and $\operatorname{tr}$ are basis independent; product and sum of eigenvalues; Cayley–Hamilton. $V = \bigoplus G(\lambda_j, T)$; nilpotent operators have chain bases; Jordan block counts from $d_k = \dim \operatorname{null}(T - \lambda I)^k$.

### Key formulas

- $\dim V = \dim \operatorname{null} T + \dim \operatorname{range} T$.
- $|\langle u, v \rangle| \le \|u\|\|v\|$ and $\|v\|^2 = \sum |\langle v, e_j \rangle|^2$.
- $P_U v = \sum \langle v, e_j \rangle e_j$ and $A^\top A x = A^\top b$.
- $T = \sum \lambda_j e_j e_j^*$ and $A = \sum \sigma_j u_j v_j^\top$.
- Blocks of size exactly $k$: $2d_k - d_{k-1} - d_{k+1}$.

### Common errors

- Confusing a spanning list, an independent list and a basis.
- Treating a computed example as a proof.
- Using the spectral theorem for non-self-adjoint or complex symmetric matrices.
- Mixing up the eigenvalues of $A$ with the singular values of $A$.
- Ignoring the field: statements true over $\mathbb{C}$ can fail over $\mathbb{R}$.

@exam self
prompt: Let $A$ be a real symmetric $n \times n$ matrix with eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$, listed with multiplicity. Prove that (a) $\operatorname{rank} A$ is the number of nonzero $\lambda_j$; (b) $|\det A| = |\lambda_1|\cdots|\lambda_n|$; (c) the singular values of $A$ are the numbers $|\lambda_j|$ that are nonzero.
hint: Use the spectral theorem to write $A = Q\Lambda Q^\top$ with $Q$ orthogonal and $\Lambda$ diagonal. Rank is unchanged by multiplying by invertible matrices, and $A^\top A = A^2$.
solution: By the spectral theorem, $A = Q\Lambda Q^\top$ with $Q^\top Q = I$ (orthonormal eigenvectors as columns) and $\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$. (a) $Q$ is invertible with $Q^{-1} = Q^\top$, so $A$ is similar to $\Lambda$ and has the same rank (null spaces correspond under the invertible map $Q^\top$, so nullities agree and rank–nullity gives equal ranks). The rank of a diagonal matrix is the number of nonzero diagonal entries. (b) $\det A = \det Q \det \Lambda \det Q^\top = \det \Lambda \cdot (\det Q)^2 = \lambda_1 \cdots \lambda_n$, since $(\det Q)^2 = \det(Q^\top Q) = 1$; taking absolute values gives the claim (in fact $\det A$ itself is the product). (c) $A^\top A = A^2 = Q\Lambda^2 Q^\top$, so the eigenvalues of $A^\top A$ are $\lambda_j^2$, and the singular values, which are the square roots of the positive eigenvalues of $A^\top A$, are the numbers $\sqrt{\lambda_j^2} = |\lambda_j|$ with $\lambda_j \ne 0$. $\blacksquare$

rubric: Derives the determinant formula from multiplicativity and $(\det Q)^2 = 1$.

@exam num
prompt: Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$. Find $\operatorname{tr}(A^5)$.
answer: 1023
check: int(round(np.trace(np.linalg.matrix_power(mat([[0, 2], [2, 3]]), 5))))
hint: $A$ is symmetric with real eigenvalues. The trace of $A^5$ is the sum of the fifth powers of the eigenvalues (triangularize $A$).
solution: The characteristic polynomial is $\lambda^2 - 3\lambda - 4 = (\lambda - 4)(\lambda + 1)$, so the eigenvalues are $4$ and $-1$. In a basis in which $A$ is diagonal, $A^5$ has diagonal entries $4^5$ and $(-1)^5$, and the trace is basis independent: $\operatorname{tr}(A^5) = 1024 - 1 = 1023$.

@exam ms
prompt: Which of these statements are true for operators on finite-dimensional inner product spaces over $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$ as indicated?
choice*: A normal operator on a complex inner product space is diagonalizable.
choice: Every diagonalizable operator on a complex inner product space is normal.
choice: A real symmetric matrix can fail to be diagonalizable over $\mathbb{R}$.
choice*: Every square complex matrix is similar to an upper-triangular matrix.
choice*: The rank of a real matrix equals the number of its nonzero singular values.
hint: For the statement about diagonalizable operators, find a counterexample among $2 \times 2$ matrices, such as $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$; for the statement about real symmetric matrices, recall the real spectral theorem.
solution: Normal operators on complex spaces have orthonormal eigenbases (complex spectral theorem), so they are diagonalizable. The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ has distinct eigenvalues $1, 2$, so it is diagonalizable, but it is not normal: $AA^\top = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ while $A^\top A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$. Real symmetric matrices always have orthonormal eigenbases (real spectral theorem). Every complex matrix has an upper-triangular form (Lesson 6). The number of nonzero singular values is the rank $r$ in the SVD.
