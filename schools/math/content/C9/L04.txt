title: Matrices of linear maps, change of basis, quotients and duality
summary: Represent a linear map by a matrix, change bases, and use quotient spaces and dual spaces to restate rank–nullity.
objective: Compute the matrix of a linear map with respect to chosen bases and prove $\mathcal{M}(ST) = \mathcal{M}(S)\mathcal{M}(T)$.
objective: Prove the change-of-basis formula $\mathcal{M}(T, B') = P^{-1}\mathcal{M}(T, B)P$ and use it.

@article
The reflection of $\mathbb{R}^2$ across the line $y = x$ has the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ in the standard basis. In the basis $(1, 1), (1, -1)$ it is $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$: the first basis vector lies on the mirror and is fixed, the second is flipped. The map is the same; the numbers changed. Which numbers describe the map, and which describe the basis?

### Matrices of linear maps

Let $T \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$ with bases $v_1, \dots, v_n$ of $V$ and $w_1, \dots, w_m$ of $W$. The **matrix** $\mathcal{M}(T)$ is the $m \times n$ matrix $A$ with $Tv_k = \sum_j A_{jk} w_j$: column $k$ holds the coordinates of $Tv_k$. Write $[v]$ for the coordinate column of $v$.

**Theorem 1.** $[Tv] = \mathcal{M}(T)\,[v]$, and for $S \in \mathcal{L}(W, X)$ with a basis of $X$ fixed, $\mathcal{M}(ST) = \mathcal{M}(S)\mathcal{M}(T)$.

*Proof.* If $v = \sum x_k v_k$, then $Tv = \sum_k x_k \sum_j A_{jk} w_j = \sum_j (\sum_k A_{jk} x_k) w_j$, so $[Tv] = A[v]$. A matrix $C$ with $[u] \mapsto C[u]$ equal to a given linear map is determined by its action on the standard columns, since $C e_k$ is column $k$. Now $[STv] = \mathcal{M}(S)[Tv] = \mathcal{M}(S)\mathcal{M}(T)[v]$ for all $v$, so $\mathcal{M}(ST) = \mathcal{M}(S)\mathcal{M}(T)$. $\blacksquare$

### Change of basis

Let $B = (v_k)$ and $B' = (v'_k)$ be bases of $V$. The **change-of-basis matrix** $P$ has column $k$ equal to the $B$-coordinates of $v'_k$. It is invertible (it is the matrix of the identity map from basis $B'$ to basis $B$, and the identity map has an inverse).

**Theorem 2.** For $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$, $\mathcal{M}(T, B') = P^{-1}\,\mathcal{M}(T, B)\,P$.

*Proof.* If $v = \sum x'_k v'_k$ then $v = \sum_k x'_k \sum_j P_{jk} v_j$, so $[v]_B = P[v]_{B'}$. Hence $[Tv]_{B'} = P^{-1}[Tv]_B = P^{-1}\mathcal{M}(T, B)[v]_B = P^{-1}\mathcal{M}(T, B)P\,[v]_{B'}$ for every $v$, and the matrix with this property is unique. $\blacksquare$

Matrices $A$ and $P^{-1}AP$ are called **similar**. In the puzzle, $P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $P^{-1}AP = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1)$.

### Quotient spaces

For a subspace $U \subseteq V$ and $v \in V$, the **coset** is $v + U = \{v + u : u \in U\}$. Then $v + U = w + U$ if and only if $v - w \in U$. The set $V/U$ of cosets is a vector space under $(v + U) + (w + U) = (v + w) + U$ and $a(v + U) = av + U$; these are well defined because replacing $v$ by $v + u$ and $w$ by $w + u'$ changes $v + w$ by $u + u' \in U$. The map $\pi\colon V \to V/U$, $v \mapsto v + U$, is linear, surjective, with $\operatorname{null} \pi = U$. Rank–nullity applied to $\pi$ gives $\dim V/U = \dim V - \dim U$.

**Theorem 3 (first isomorphism theorem).** For $T \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$ the map $\tilde{T}\colon V/\operatorname{null} T \to \operatorname{range} T$, $v + \operatorname{null} T \mapsto Tv$, is a well-defined linear bijection.

*Proof.* Write $N = \operatorname{null} T$. If $v + N = v' + N$ then $v - v' \in N$, so $Tv = Tv'$: well defined. Linearity is inherited from $T$. If $\tilde{T}(v + N) = 0$ then $v \in N$ and $v + N = N$ is the zero coset: injective. Surjectivity onto $\operatorname{range} T$ holds by definition. $\blacksquare$

### The dual space

The **dual space** is $V' = \mathcal{L}(V, F)$. If $v_1, \dots, v_n$ is a basis of $V$, the **dual basis** $\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_n$ is defined by $\varphi_j(v_k) = 1$ if $j = k$ and $0$ otherwise (it exists by Lesson 3, Theorem 1).

**Theorem 4.** The dual basis is a basis of $V'$, so $\dim V' = \dim V$.

*Proof.* If $\sum c_j \varphi_j = 0$, evaluating at $v_k$ gives $c_k = 0$. For any $\varphi \in V'$, the maps $\varphi$ and $\sum_j \varphi(v_j)\varphi_j$ agree on every $v_k$, hence are equal. $\blacksquare$

For $T \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$ the **transpose** $T' \in \mathcal{L}(W', V')$ is $T'(\psi) = \psi \circ T$.

**Theorem 5.** With respect to the dual bases, $\mathcal{M}(T') = \mathcal{M}(T)^\top$.

*Proof.* Let $A = \mathcal{M}(T)$, $\psi_1, \dots, \psi_m$ the dual basis of $W'$ and $\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_n$ that of $V'$. Then $T'(\psi_j)(v_k) = \psi_j(Tv_k) = \sum_i A_{ik}\psi_j(w_i) = A_{jk}$, so $T'(\psi_j) = \sum_k A_{jk}\varphi_k$. Column $j$ of $\mathcal{M}(T')$ is $(A_{j1}, \dots, A_{jn})^\top$, which says $\mathcal{M}(T')_{kj} = A_{jk}$. $\blacksquare$

@example
title: Matrices of differentiation in two bases
problem: Let $D \in \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R}))$ be $Dp = p'$. Find $\mathcal{M}(D, B)$ for $B = (1, x, x^2)$ and $\mathcal{M}(D, B')$ for $B' = (1,\ 1 + x,\ 1 + x + x^2)$, and verify Theorem 2.
step: In $B$: $D1 = 0$, $Dx = 1$, $Dx^2 = 2x$, so $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
step: In $B'$: $D(1) = 0$; $D(1 + x) = 1 = 1\cdot v'_1$; $D(1 + x + x^2) = 1 + 2x = -v'_1 + 2v'_2$. So $\mathcal{M}(D, B') = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
step: The change-of-basis matrix has columns $(1,0,0)$, $(1,1,0)$, $(1,1,1)$: $P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, with $P^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
step: Compute $AP = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and then $P^{-1}(AP) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, which equals $\mathcal{M}(D, B')$.
answer: $\mathcal{M}(D, B') = P^{-1}AP = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.

@example
title: A quotient by the first isomorphism theorem
problem: Let $U = \{p \in \mathcal{P}_4(\mathbb{R}) : p(0) = 0\}$. Show that $\mathcal{P}_4(\mathbb{R})/U \cong \mathbb{R}$ and that $p + U = q + U$ if and only if $p(0) = q(0)$.
step: Let $T\colon \mathcal{P}_4(\mathbb{R}) \to \mathbb{R}$, $Tp = p(0)$. It is linear, and $\operatorname{null} T = U$.
step: $T$ is surjective: the constant polynomial $c$ satisfies $Tc = c$. So $\operatorname{range} T = \mathbb{R}$.
step: By Theorem 3, $\tilde{T}\colon \mathcal{P}_4(\mathbb{R})/U \to \mathbb{R}$, $p + U \mapsto p(0)$ is a linear bijection, so the quotient is isomorphic to $\mathbb{R}$.
step: Since $\tilde{T}$ is injective and well defined, $p + U = q + U$ if and only if $p(0) = q(0)$. The dimensions agree: $\dim \mathcal{P}_4 - \dim U = 5 - 4 = 1$.
answer: $\mathcal{P}_4(\mathbb{R})/U \cong \mathbb{R}$ via $p + U \mapsto p(0)$.

@mistakes
- Writing the coordinates of $Tv_k$ as a row instead of a column: the convention $Tv_k = \sum_j A_{jk} w_j$ puts them in column $k$, which is what makes $\mathcal{M}(ST) = \mathcal{M}(S)\mathcal{M}(T)$ work.
- Mixing up $P^{-1}AP$ and $PAP^{-1}$: the matrix $P$ whose columns are the new basis in old coordinates satisfies $\mathcal{M}(T, B') = P^{-1}\mathcal{M}(T, B)P$.
- Thinking $V/U$ is a subspace of $V$: its elements are cosets, not vectors of $V$.
- Defining a map on $V/U$ by a formula in a representative without checking that it does not depend on the representative.

@practice tuple
prompt: In $\mathbb{R}^2$ let $v_1 = (1, 1)$ and $v_2 = (1, 2)$ be a basis, with dual basis $\varphi_1, \varphi_2$. Write $\varphi_1(x, y) = ax + by$ and give the ordered pair $(a, b)$.
answer: (2, -1)
check: tuple(inv([[1, 1], [1, 2]])[0])
hint: The conditions are $\varphi_1(v_1) = 1$ and $\varphi_1(v_2) = 0$.
solution: $\varphi_1(1, 1) = a + b = 1$ and $\varphi_1(1, 2) = a + 2b = 0$. Subtracting gives $b = -1$, then $a = 2$. So $\varphi_1(x, y) = 2x - y$, and $(a, b) = (2, -1)$.

@practice self
prompt: Let $T \in \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}_4(\mathbb{R}))$ be $Tp = p'' + p$, where $p''$ is the second derivative. Prove that for every $q \in \mathcal{P}_4(\mathbb{R})$ there is a $p \in \mathcal{P}_4(\mathbb{R})$ with $p'' + p = q$. Use rank–nullity.
hint: Show $\operatorname{null} T = \{0\}$ by comparing the degrees of $p''$ and $p$ for a nonzero $p$.
solution: $T$ is linear and maps the space $\mathcal{P}_4(\mathbb{R})$ to itself (differentiation does not raise the degree). We show $\operatorname{null} T = \{0\}$. Let $p \ne 0$ have degree $d$. If $d \le 1$ then $p'' = 0$ and $Tp = p \ne 0$. If $d \ge 2$, then $p''$ has degree $d - 2 < d$, so the coefficient of $x^d$ in $p'' + p$ is the leading coefficient of $p$, which is nonzero, and $Tp \ne 0$. So $T$ is injective. By rank–nullity, $\dim \operatorname{range} T = \dim \mathcal{P}_4(\mathbb{R}) - 0 = 5 = \dim \mathcal{P}_4(\mathbb{R})$, so $\operatorname{range} T$ is a subspace of dimension $5$ in a space of dimension $5$, hence equals $\mathcal{P}_4(\mathbb{R})$. Thus every $q$ equals $Tp$ for some $p$. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Shows $T$ maps $\mathcal{P}_4(\mathbb{R})$ to itself and is linear.
rubric: Proves $\operatorname{null} T = \{0\}$ by a degree argument, treating degrees $0$, $1$ and at least $2$ correctly.
rubric: Applies rank–nullity to get $\dim \operatorname{range} T = 5$ and concludes surjectivity from equal dimensions.

@quiz num
prompt: Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$ and let $T\colon \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 2} \to \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 2}$ be $T(X) = AX - XA$. Find $\dim \operatorname{range} T$.
answer: 2
check: rank([[0, 0, 1, 0], [-1, -3, 0, 1], [0, 0, 3, 0], [0, 0, -1, 0]])
hint: Write $X = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$, compute $AX - XA$, and find which $X$ are killed.
solution: $AX - XA = \begin{pmatrix} c & -a - 3b + d \\ 3c & -c \end{pmatrix}$. This vanishes exactly when $c = 0$ and $d = a + 3b$, a $2$-parameter family ($a$ and $b$ free), so $\dim \operatorname{null} T = 2$. By rank–nullity, $\dim \operatorname{range} T = 4 - 2 = 2$.

@quiz self
prompt: Let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix and $P$ an invertible $n \times n$ matrix, over $F$. Prove that $A$ and $P^{-1}AP$ have the same rank.
hint: Compare the null spaces of $A$ and $P^{-1}AP$ as maps $F^n \to F^n$, and use rank–nullity.
solution: Let $B = P^{-1}AP$. For $x \in F^n$, $Bx = 0$ iff $P^{-1}APx = 0$ iff $APx = 0$ (multiply by $P$ or $P^{-1}$) iff $Px \in \operatorname{null} A$. So $\operatorname{null} B = P^{-1}(\operatorname{null} A)$, the image of $\operatorname{null} A$ under the invertible linear map $P^{-1}$. An invertible linear map sends a basis to a basis (it preserves independence and spanning), so $\dim \operatorname{null} B = \dim \operatorname{null} A$. By rank–nullity, $\operatorname{rank} B = n - \dim \operatorname{null} B = n - \dim \operatorname{null} A = \operatorname{rank} A$. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Shows $Bx = 0$ if and only if $Px \in \operatorname{null} A$, so $\operatorname{null} B = P^{-1}(\operatorname{null} A)$.
rubric: Argues that an invertible map preserves the dimension of a subspace.
rubric: Converts equal nullities into equal ranks with rank–nullity.
