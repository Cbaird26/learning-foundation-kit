@article
A $2 \times 2$ real matrix sends the unit circle to an ellipse (possibly flattened to a segment). How long are the axes of that ellipse, and which input directions produce them? The answer is the singular value decomposition: every matrix is a rotation, then a stretch along perpendicular axes, then another rotation. It rests on the spectral theorem for the self-adjoint, positive operator $T^* T$.

### Positive operators

A self-adjoint operator $T$ is **positive** if $\langle Tv, v \rangle \ge 0$ for all $v$. In a real space, self-adjointness is part of the definition; it is not implied by $\langle Tv, v \rangle \ge 0$ (the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has $\langle Tv, v \rangle = v_1^2 + v_2^2 \ge 0$ but is not symmetric).

**Theorem 1.** For a self-adjoint $T$ on a finite-dimensional inner product space, the following are equivalent: (a) $T$ is positive; (b) all eigenvalues of $T$ are $\ge 0$; (c) $T = R^* R$ for some operator $R$.

*Proof.* (a) $\Rightarrow$ (b): if $Tv = \lambda v$ with $v \ne 0$, then $0 \le \langle Tv, v \rangle = \lambda\|v\|^2$. (b) $\Rightarrow$ (c): by the spectral theorem take an orthonormal eigenbasis $e_j$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_j \ge 0$ and define $Re_j = \sqrt{\lambda_j}\,e_j$. Then $R$ is self-adjoint (its matrix is real diagonal) and $R^* R = R^2$ sends $e_j$ to $\lambda_j e_j$, so $R^* R = T$. (c) $\Rightarrow$ (a): $(R^* R)^* = R^* R$, and $\langle R^* R v, v \rangle = \|Rv\|^2 \ge 0$. $\blacksquare$

The operator $R$ in (b) $\Rightarrow$ (c) is a positive **square root** of $T$ (it is in fact the unique one, a fact we do not prove here).

### Isometries

An operator or linear map $S$ is an **isometry** if $\|Sv\| = \|v\|$ for all $v$. Examples: rotations and reflections of $\mathbb{R}^2$.

**Theorem 2.** For $S \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$ with $V$ finite-dimensional, the following are equivalent: (a) $S$ is an isometry; (b) $\langle Su, Sv \rangle = \langle u, v \rangle$ for all $u, v$; (c) $S^* S = I$.

*Proof.* (c) $\Rightarrow$ (b): $\langle Su, Sv \rangle = \langle S^* S u, v \rangle = \langle u, v \rangle$. (b) $\Rightarrow$ (a): take $u = v$. (a) $\Rightarrow$ (c): let $T = S^* S - I$, a self-adjoint operator with $\langle Tv, v \rangle = \|Sv\|^2 - \|v\|^2 = 0$ for all $v$. By the spectral theorem (or its complex version for self-adjoint operators) $V$ has an orthonormal eigenbasis $e_j$ of $T$ with real eigenvalues $\lambda_j$, and $0 = \langle Te_j, e_j \rangle = \lambda_j$. So $T$ is zero on a basis, and $S^* S = I$. $\blacksquare$

For matrices: $S$ is an isometry of $\mathbb{R}^n$ iff its columns are orthonormal ($S^\top S = I$).

### The singular value decomposition

For $T \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$, the operator $T^* T$ on $V$ is positive by Theorem 1(c), and $\operatorname{null}(T^* T) = \operatorname{null} T$: if $T^* T v = 0$ then $\|Tv\|^2 = \langle T^* T v, v \rangle = 0$. The **singular values** of $T$ are the square roots of the positive eigenvalues of $T^* T$, listed with multiplicity as $\sigma_1 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r > 0$.

**Theorem 3 (singular value decomposition).** Let $V, W$ be finite-dimensional inner product spaces with $\dim V = n$ and $\dim W = m$, and let $T \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$ have rank $r$. Then $r$ is the number of singular values, and there are orthonormal bases $v_1, \dots, v_n$ of $V$ and $u_1, \dots, u_m$ of $W$ with $Tv_j = \sigma_j u_j$ for $j \le r$ and $Tv_j = 0$ for $j > r$.

*Proof.* By the spectral theorem choose an orthonormal basis $v_1, \dots, v_n$ of eigenvectors of $T^* T$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_n \ge 0$. The number of positive $\lambda_j$ equals $n - \dim \operatorname{null}(T^* T) = n - \dim \operatorname{null} T = r$ (rank–nullity), and the $v_j$ with $\lambda_j = 0$ lie in $\operatorname{null} T$, so $Tv_j = 0$ for $j > r$. For $j \le r$ put $\sigma_j = \sqrt{\lambda_j}$ and $u_j = Tv_j / \sigma_j$. Then for $j, k \le r$, $\langle u_j, u_k \rangle = \langle T^* T v_j, v_k \rangle / (\sigma_j \sigma_k) = \lambda_j \langle v_j, v_k \rangle / (\sigma_j\sigma_k)$, which is $1$ if $j = k$ and $0$ otherwise. So $u_1, \dots, u_r$ is orthonormal; extend it to an orthonormal basis of $W$ (Lesson 8, Corollary). $\blacksquare$

In matrix form, a real $m \times n$ matrix factors as $A = U\Sigma V^\top$ with $U$ ($m \times m$) and $V$ ($n \times n$) having orthonormal columns and $\Sigma$ ($m \times n$) having $\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_r$ on its diagonal; equivalently $A = \sum_{j \le r} \sigma_j u_j v_j^\top$. Also $\|Av\|^2 = \sum_j \sigma_j^2 |\langle v, v_j \rangle|^2 \le \sigma_1^2\|v\|^2$, with equality at $v = v_1$: the largest stretch of a unit vector is $\sigma_1$. The Eckart–Young theorem (stated here without proof) says the best rank-$k$ approximation of $A$ in the least-squares sense is the truncated sum $\sum_{j \le k} \sigma_j u_j v_j^\top$; this is the idea behind image compression.

### Computing the top singular value

The power method (Lesson 5) applied to $A^\top A$ finds $\sigma_1^2$ and $v_1$:

```python
import math


def top_singular(A, steps=200):
    """Largest singular value of a real matrix, and a right singular vector."""
    m, n = len(A), len(A[0])
    AtA = [[sum(A[k][i] * A[k][j] for k in range(m)) for j in range(n)]
           for i in range(n)]
    x = [1.0 + j for j in range(n)]
    for _ in range(steps):
        y = [sum(AtA[i][j] * x[j] for j in range(n)) for i in range(n)]
        norm = math.sqrt(sum(t * t for t in y))
        x = [t / norm for t in y]
    Ax = [sum(A[i][j] * x[j] for j in range(n)) for i in range(m)]
    return math.sqrt(sum(t * t for t in Ax)), x


sigma, v = top_singular([[3, 0], [4, 5]])
print(round(sigma, 6))
```

It prints `6.708204`, which is $\sqrt{45}$. It needs $\sigma_1 > \sigma_2$ and a start vector with a component along $v_1$.

@example
title: A full singular value decomposition
problem: Find the SVD of $A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$.
step: Compute $A^\top A = \begin{pmatrix} 25 & 20 \\ 20 & 25 \end{pmatrix}$. Its characteristic polynomial is $(25 - \lambda)^2 - 400 = (\lambda - 45)(\lambda - 5)$, so the eigenvalues are $45$ and $5$ and the singular values are $\sigma_1 = \sqrt{45} = 3\sqrt5$ and $\sigma_2 = \sqrt5$.
step: Eigenvectors of $A^\top A$: for $45$, $(1, 1)$; for $5$, $(1, -1)$. Normalized, $v_1 = (1, 1)/\sqrt2$ and $v_2 = (1, -1)/\sqrt2$.
step: Left singular vectors: $Av_1 = (3, 9)/\sqrt2$, so $u_1 = Av_1/\sigma_1 = (1, 3)/\sqrt{10}$; $Av_2 = (3, -1)/\sqrt2$, so $u_2 = Av_2/\sigma_2 = (3, -1)/\sqrt{10}$. They are orthonormal: $\langle u_1, u_2 \rangle = (3 - 3)/10 = 0$.
step: Verify $A = \sigma_1 u_1 v_1^\top + \sigma_2 u_2 v_2^\top = \frac32\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 3 & 3 \end{pmatrix} + \frac12\begin{pmatrix} 3 & -3 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$.
answer: $\sigma_1 = 3\sqrt5$, $\sigma_2 = \sqrt5$, with $v_1 = (1,1)/\sqrt2$, $v_2 = (1,-1)/\sqrt2$, $u_1 = (1,3)/\sqrt{10}$, $u_2 = (3,-1)/\sqrt{10}$.

@example
title: A positive square root
problem: Show that $T = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$ is positive and find a positive square root.
step: $T$ is symmetric, so self-adjoint. Its characteristic polynomial is $(5 - \lambda)^2 - 16 = (\lambda - 9)(\lambda - 1)$, so the eigenvalues $9$ and $1$ are positive; $T$ is positive by Theorem 1.
step: Orthonormal eigenvectors: $e_1 = (1, 1)/\sqrt2$ for $9$ and $e_2 = (1, -1)/\sqrt2$ for $1$.
step: Take square roots of the eigenvalues: $R = 3 e_1 e_1^\top + 1\,e_2 e_2^\top = \frac32\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} + \frac12\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
step: Check: $R^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 4 + 1 & 2 + 2 \\ 2 + 2 & 1 + 4 \end{pmatrix} = T$, and $R$ is symmetric with eigenvalues $3, 1 \ge 0$.
answer: $\sqrt{T} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.

@mistakes
- Using the eigenvalues of $A$ instead of those of $A^\top A$: for $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ both eigenvalues are $0$, but the singular values are $1$ and $0$.
- Assuming $\langle Tv, v \rangle \ge 0$ alone makes a real operator positive: positivity requires self-adjointness too.
- Dropping the condition $\sigma_j > 0$ when forming $u_j = Tv_j / \sigma_j$; for $\sigma_j = 0$ the vector $u_j$ must be chosen by extending the orthonormal list.
- Expecting the SVD factors to be unique: the singular values are, but singular vectors are determined only up to sign (or, for repeated singular values, up to rotation within the eigenspace).

model:
```python
import math


def singular_values_2x2(A):
    """Singular values (s1 >= s2 >= 0) of a real 2x2 matrix."""
    (a, b), (c, d) = A
    frob = a * a + b * b + c * c + d * d
    det = a * d - b * c
    disc = max(0.0, frob * frob - 4 * det * det)
    big = (frob + math.sqrt(disc)) / 2
    small = max(0.0, (frob - math.sqrt(disc)) / 2)
    return (math.sqrt(big), math.sqrt(small))
```
hint: The sum of the roots $\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2$ is the trace of $A^\top A$ and their product is $\det(A^\top A) = (\det A)^2$.
hint: Solve the quadratic $t^2 - Ft + D^2 = 0$ with the quadratic formula, then take square roots.
solution: The eigenvalues of $A^\top A$ are $\sigma_1^2$ and $\sigma_2^2$. Their sum is $\operatorname{trace}(A^\top A) = a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + d^2$ and their product is $\det(A^\top A) = (\det A)^2$, so they are the roots of $t^2 - Ft + D^2$, namely $(F \pm \sqrt{F^2 - 4D^2})/2$. The discriminant is never negative in exact arithmetic (the eigenvalues of the symmetric matrix $A^\top A$ are real), but rounding can make it slightly negative, so it is clamped at $0$.

prompt: Let $T \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$ with $V$ finite-dimensional. Prove that $\operatorname{null}(T^* T) = \operatorname{null} T$ and conclude that $T^* T$ and $T$ have the same rank.
hint: Compute $\langle T^* T v, v \rangle$ and relate it to $\|Tv\|^2$. Then use rank–nullity for both maps on $V$.
solution: If $Tv = 0$ then $T^* T v = T^* 0 = 0$, so $\operatorname{null} T \subseteq \operatorname{null}(T^* T)$. Conversely, if $T^* T v = 0$, then $0 = \langle T^* T v, v \rangle = \langle Tv, Tv \rangle = \|Tv\|^2$, so $Tv = 0$. Hence the null spaces are equal. By rank–nullity, $\operatorname{rank}(T^* T) = \dim V - \dim \operatorname{null}(T^* T) = \dim V - \dim \operatorname{null} T = \operatorname{rank} T$. $\blacksquare$
