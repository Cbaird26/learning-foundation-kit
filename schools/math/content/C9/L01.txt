@article
Add two solutions of the differential equation $y'' + y = 0$, for example $\sin x$ and $\cos x$: the sum is again a solution. Multiply a solution by $7$: still a solution. These solutions are functions, not arrows, yet they combine exactly as arrows in the plane do. Polynomials, matrices and sequences also add and scale. What do all of these share? Eight rules, and everything that follows from them applies to all of these objects at once.

### Fields and vector spaces

A **field** $F$ is a set with addition and multiplication satisfying the usual rules, in which every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse. The fields we use are $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$ and $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ for a prime $p$ (the field $\mathbb{F}_2 = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ has $1 + 1 = 0$). Unless said otherwise, $F$ is $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$.

**Definition.** A **vector space over $F$** is a set $V$ with an addition $V \times V \to V$ and a scalar multiplication $F \times V \to V$ such that for all $u, v, w \in V$ and $a, b \in F$:

1. $u + v = v + u$ and $(u + v) + w = u + (v + w)$;
2. there is $0 \in V$ with $v + 0 = v$ for all $v$;
3. every $v$ has some $w$ with $v + w = 0$;
4. $(ab)v = a(bv)$ and $1v = v$;
5. $a(u + v) = au + av$ and $(a + b)v = av + bv$.

Examples: $F^n$; the matrices $F^{m \times n}$; the polynomials $\mathcal{P}_n(F)$ of degree at most $n$ (with the zero polynomial); the set $F^X$ of all functions $X \to F$ with pointwise operations; the continuous functions $[0,1] \to \mathbb{R}$. A non-example: $\mathbb{R}^2$ with the usual addition and $a \cdot (x, y) = (ax, 0)$ satisfies every rule except $1v = v$.

### First consequences

**Theorem 1.** Let $V$ be a vector space over $F$, $v \in V$ and $a \in F$. Then (a) the zero vector is unique; (b) each $v$ has a unique additive inverse, written $-v$; (c) $0v = 0$; (d) $a0 = 0$; (e) $(-1)v = -v$.

*Proof.* (a) If $0$ and $0'$ both satisfy rule 2, then $0 = 0 + 0' = 0'$. (b) If $w$ and $w'$ are both inverses of $v$, then $w = w + 0 = w + (v + w') = (w + v) + w' = 0 + w' = w'$. (c) $0v = (0 + 0)v = 0v + 0v$; adding the inverse of $0v$ to both sides gives $0 = 0v$. (d) $a0 = a(0 + 0) = a0 + a0$, and the same cancellation gives $a0 = 0$. (e) $v + (-1)v = 1v + (-1)v = (1 + (-1))v = 0v = 0$, so by (b) $(-1)v$ is the inverse of $v$. $\blacksquare$

### Subspaces

A subset $U \subseteq V$ is a **subspace** if it is a vector space under the operations of $V$.

**Theorem 2 (subspace test).** $U \subseteq V$ is a subspace if and only if (i) $0 \in U$, (ii) $u, w \in U$ implies $u + w \in U$, and (iii) $a \in F$, $u \in U$ imply $au \in U$.

*Proof.* If $U$ is a subspace, (ii) and (iii) hold because the operations of $U$ are those of $V$ and map into $U$. $U$ is nonempty, so it contains some $u$, and $0 = 0u \in U$ by Theorem 1(c) and (iii). Conversely, assume (i)–(iii). The operations restrict to $U$ by (ii) and (iii). The rules that are equations (commutativity, associativity, distributivity, $1v = v$) hold in $U$ because they hold in $V$. The vector $0$ lies in $U$ by (i) and works as the zero vector. The inverse of $u \in U$ is $(-1)u$ by Theorem 1(e), and it lies in $U$ by (iii). $\blacksquare$

The set $\{p \in \mathcal{P}_3(\mathbb{R}) : p(2) = 0\}$ is a subspace: the zero polynomial vanishes at $2$, and $(p + q)(2) = 0$ and $(ap)(2) = 0$ when $p(2) = q(2) = 0$. The set $\{p : p(2) = 1\}$ fails (i). The set $\{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : xy = 0\}$ contains $0$ and is closed under scalars but not sums: $(1, 0) + (0, 1)$ has $xy = 1$.

### Sums and direct sums

For subspaces $U, W \subseteq V$ put $U + W = \{u + w : u \in U, w \in W\}$, a subspace. The sum is **direct**, written $U \oplus W$, if every vector of $U + W$ can be written in only one way as $u + w$.

**Theorem 3.** $U + W$ is direct if and only if $U \cap W = \{0\}$.

*Proof.* Suppose the sum is direct and $x \in U \cap W$. Then $x = x + 0 = 0 + x$ are two decompositions, one with $x \in U$, $0 \in W$ and one with $0 \in U$, $x \in W$; uniqueness gives $x = 0$. Conversely, let $U \cap W = \{0\}$ and $u + w = u' + w'$ with $u, u' \in U$ and $w, w' \in W$. Then $u - u' = w' - w$ lies in both $U$ and $W$, so it is $0$, and $u = u'$, $w = w'$. $\blacksquare$

This criterion is for two subspaces only. Three lines through the origin in $\mathbb{R}^2$ meet pairwise in $\{0\}$, yet $(1,0) + (0,1) + (-1,-1) = 0$ is a second way to write $0$, so their sum is not direct.

@example
title: Symmetric plus skew-symmetric matrices
problem: Let $F$ be a field in which $1 + 1 \ne 0$ (such as $\mathbb{R}$). Let $S$ be the symmetric and $K$ the skew-symmetric matrices in $F^{n \times n}$ (that is, $A^\top = A$ and $A^\top = -A$). Prove that $F^{n \times n} = S \oplus K$.
step: Both are subspaces by the subspace test: $0$ is in each, and $(A + B)^\top = A^\top + B^\top$ and $(aA)^\top = aA^\top$ preserve each defining equation.
step: Every $A$ is a sum: $A = \tfrac12 (A + A^\top) + \tfrac12 (A - A^\top)$, where $\tfrac12$ exists because $2 \ne 0$ in $F$. The first matrix is symmetric because $(A + A^\top)^\top = A^\top + A$; the second is skew-symmetric because $(A - A^\top)^\top = A^\top - A = -(A - A^\top)$.
step: The intersection is zero: if $A \in S \cap K$ then $A = A^\top = -A$, so $2A = 0$ and $A = \tfrac12 (2A) = 0$.
step: By Theorem 3 the sum is direct, so $F^{n \times n} = S \oplus K$. The hypothesis $1 + 1 \ne 0$ was used twice. Over $\mathbb{F}_2$, every symmetric matrix is skew-symmetric, so $S \cap K = S \ne \{0\}$ and the sum is not direct.
answer: $F^{n \times n} = S \oplus K$ when $1 + 1 \ne 0$ in $F$.

@example
title: A subset that is not a subspace
problem: Decide whether $W = \{A \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 2} : \det A = 0\}$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^{2 \times 2}$.
step: Test (i): the zero matrix has determinant $0$, so $0 \in W$.
step: Test (iii): $\det(aA) = a^2 \det A = 0$ when $\det A = 0$, so $W$ is closed under scalar multiples.
step: Test (ii): take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. Both have determinant $0$, but $A + B = I$ has determinant $1$.
step: One failed test settles the question: $W$ is not closed under addition, so it is not a subspace.
answer: $W$ is not a subspace; it is closed under scalars but not under sums.

@mistakes
- Passing a subset that merely looks "flat": a plane not through the origin, such as $\{x + y + z = 1\}$, is not a subspace because it lacks $0$.
- Believing a union of subspaces is a subspace: the two coordinate axes in $\mathbb{R}^2$ have a union that contains $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ but not their sum.
- Using the direct-sum test $U \cap W = \{0\}$ for three or more subspaces: pairwise trivial intersections do not give uniqueness.
- Assuming every field behaves like $\mathbb{R}$: in $\mathbb{F}_2$ we have $1 + 1 = 0$, so division by $2$ is impossible.

@practice num
prompt: Let $\mathbb{F}_3 = \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ and let $W = \{(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{F}_3^3 : x + y + z = 0\}$, a subspace of $\mathbb{F}_3^3$. How many vectors does $W$ contain?
answer: 9
check: sum(1 for x in range(3) for y in range(3) for z in range(3) if (x + y + z) % 3 == 0)
hint: Choose $x$ and $y$ freely; the equation then fixes $z$.
solution: Each of $x$ and $y$ can be any of the $3$ elements of $\mathbb{F}_3$, and then $z = -(x + y)$ is determined. So $|W| = 3 \cdot 3 = 9$.

@practice self
prompt: Let $U$ and $W$ be subspaces of a vector space $V$. Prove that $U \cup W$ is a subspace of $V$ if and only if $U \subseteq W$ or $W \subseteq U$.
hint: One direction is immediate. For the other, assume $U \not\subseteq W$ and pick $u \in U$ with $u \notin W$; then study $u + w$ for an arbitrary $w \in W$.
solution: ($\Leftarrow$) If $U \subseteq W$ then $U \cup W = W$, and if $W \subseteq U$ then $U \cup W = U$; either way it is a subspace. ($\Rightarrow$) Suppose $U \cup W$ is a subspace and $U \not\subseteq W$. Choose $u \in U$ with $u \notin W$. Let $w \in W$ be arbitrary. Then $u + w \in U \cup W$ since $U \cup W$ is closed under addition. If $u + w \in W$, then $u = (u + w) - w \in W$, a contradiction. So $u + w \in U$, and $w = (u + w) - u \in U$. Hence $W \subseteq U$. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Proves the easy direction by noting that the union equals the larger subspace.
rubric: Assumes $U \not\subseteq W$, picks $u \in U \setminus W$ and an arbitrary $w \in W$, and uses closure of the union under addition.
rubric: Rules out $u + w \in W$ by subtracting $w$, deduces $u + w \in U$, and concludes $w \in U$.

@quiz mc
prompt: Let $V = \mathbb{R}^2$ with the usual addition and the scalar multiplication $a \cdot (x, y) = (ax, 0)$. Which vector space axiom fails?
choice*: $1 \cdot v = v$ for all $v$
choice: $a \cdot (u + v) = a \cdot u + a \cdot v$
choice: $(ab) \cdot v = a \cdot (b \cdot v)$
choice: the existence of additive inverses
hint: Compute $1 \cdot (3, 4)$ and test the other axioms on general vectors.
solution: $1 \cdot (3, 4) = (3, 0) \ne (3, 4)$, so the identity rule fails. The other rules hold: $a \cdot (u + v) = (a(x + x'), 0) = a \cdot u + a \cdot v$, $(ab) \cdot v = (abx, 0) = a \cdot (b \cdot v)$, and additive inverses come from the usual addition.

@quiz num
prompt: How many subspaces does the vector space $\mathbb{F}_2^2$ have, where $\mathbb{F}_2 = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$? Count $\{0\}$ and the whole space.
answer: 5
check: sum(1 for m in range(16) if m & 1 and all((m >> (a ^ b)) & 1 for a in range(4) if (m >> a) & 1 for b in range(4) if (m >> b) & 1))
hint: $\mathbb{F}_2^2$ has four vectors. A nonzero subspace is closed under addition, and the only scalars are $0$ and $1$.
solution: The vectors are $0$, $(1,0)$, $(0,1)$ and $(1,1)$, and the sum of any two distinct nonzero ones is the third. The subspaces are $\{0\}$, the three subsets $\{0, v\}$ with $v \ne 0$, and the whole space: $5$ in all. No subset with exactly two nonzero vectors is closed under addition.

@quiz self
prompt: Let $V$ be a vector space over $F$ and $v \in V$. Prove directly from the axioms that if $w$ and $w'$ both satisfy $v + w = 0$ and $v + w' = 0$, then $w = w'$, and that $(-1)v$ is this vector.
hint: Start from $w = w + 0$ and substitute $0 = v + w'$; for the second part compute $v + (-1)v$ using $1v = v$ and distributivity.
solution: $w = w + 0 = w + (v + w') = (w + v) + w' = 0 + w' = w'$, using the identity rule, the hypothesis on $w'$, associativity, commutativity with the hypothesis on $w$, and the identity rule. For the second part, $v + (-1)v = 1v + (-1)v = (1 + (-1))v = 0v$, and $0v = 0$ because $0v = (0 + 0)v = 0v + 0v$ and adding the inverse of $0v$ to both sides gives $0 = 0v$. So $(-1)v$ is an additive inverse of $v$, hence equal to the unique one. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Proves uniqueness of the additive inverse with the chain $w = w + (v + w') = (w + v) + w' = w'$, naming the rules used.
rubric: Shows $0v = 0$ from $0v = 0v + 0v$.
rubric: Computes $v + (-1)v = (1 + (-1))v = 0$ and concludes by uniqueness.
