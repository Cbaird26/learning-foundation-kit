### Week by week

**Weeks 1 and 2: normed spaces and operators.** A Banach space is a complete normed space; completeness depends on the norm ($C[0,1]$ is complete in $\|\cdot\|_\infty$ and not in $\|\cdot\|_1$). The series criterion proves completeness of quotients and sums. A linear map is continuous exactly when bounded; $B(X,Y)$ is Banach when $Y$ is; $\|T\|$ for matrices on $\ell^1$ and $\ell^\infty$ is a column sum and a row sum, and for continuous kernels on $C[0,1]$ it is $\max_x\int|K(x,y)|\,dy$.

**Weeks 3 to 5: Hilbert spaces.** Cauchy–Schwarz, the parallelogram law, the nearest-point theorem for closed convex sets, $H = M \oplus M^\perp$ and $M^{\perp\perp} = \overline{M}$. Bessel, Parseval, and the four equivalent conditions for an orthonormal basis; $\{e^{inx}\}$ is a basis of $L^2(\mathbb{T})$. Riesz representation, adjoints, $\|T^*T\| = \|T\|^2$, $\ker T^* = (\operatorname{ran}T)^\perp$, and Lax–Milgram.

**Week 6: Hahn–Banach.** Sublinear form via Zorn's lemma, normed form, norming functionals, separation of a point from a closed subspace, $\operatorname{dist}(x,\ker\varphi) = |\varphi(x)|/\|\varphi\|$.

**Weeks 7 and 8: Baire consequences.** Baire's theorem, Banach–Steinhaus, the open mapping theorem, the bounded inverse theorem, the closed graph theorem; the failures on $c_{00}$ and for differentiation.

**Week 9: weak convergence.** Weakly convergent sequences are bounded, $\|x\| \le \liminf\|x_n\|$, weak plus norm convergence of norms gives norm convergence in a Hilbert space, bounded sequences in a Hilbert space have weakly convergent subsequences; reflexivity ($c_0$, $\ell^1$ and $\ell^\infty$ are not reflexive).

**Weeks 10 to 12: compact operators and applications.** Compact means ball relatively compact; norm limits of finite-rank operators; Hilbert–Schmidt and continuous kernels are compact; the spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators; Neumann series and Fredholm alternative; the Green's operator of $-u''$ with eigenpairs $(n\pi)^{-2}$, $\sqrt2\sin n\pi x$.

### Key formulas

$\|T\| = \sup_{\|x\|\le1}\|Tx\|$; $\|T\|_{1\to1}$ = largest column sum; $\|x\|^2 = \sum|\langle x,e_n\rangle|^2$ for a basis; $\|\varphi\| = \|y\|$ if $\varphi = \langle\cdot,y\rangle$; $\|T\| = \sup|\langle Tx,x\rangle|$ for self-adjoint $T$; $Tx = \sum\lambda_n\langle x,e_n\rangle e_n$; $(I - A)^{-1} = \sum A^n$ when $\|A\| < 1$.

### Common errors

Dropping completeness or using the wrong space's completeness; writing $(\ell^\infty)^* = \ell^1$; forgetting the conjugate in Riesz; using the spectral theorem for operators that are not compact or not self-adjoint; confusing weak with norm convergence; checking the closed graph condition without both $x_n \to x$ and $Tx_n \to y$; treating a numerical check as a proof.

prompt: Let $T \ne 0$ be a compact operator on a Hilbert space $H$. Prove that there is a unit vector $x \in H$ with $\|Tx\| = \|T\|$ (the norm is attained), and that $\|T\|^2$ is the largest eigenvalue of $T^*T$.
hint: Consider $S = T^*T$: it is compact (Lesson 10), self-adjoint and positive, with $\|S\| = \|T\|^2$ (Lesson 5).
hint: Apply the lemma that $\|S\|$ or $-\|S\|$ is an eigenvalue of a compact self-adjoint operator, and use positivity to decide which.
solution: Let $S = T^*T$. It is compact because $T^*$ is bounded and $T$ is compact (compact operators form an ideal), self-adjoint because $(T^*T)^* = T^*T$, and $\|S\| = \|T\|^2 > 0$ by the $C^*$-identity (Lesson 5, Theorem 2), where $T \ne 0$. For every $x$, $\langle Sx,x\rangle = \|Tx\|^2 \ge 0$. By Lemma 2 of Lesson 11, $\|S\|$ or $-\|S\|$ is an eigenvalue of $S$. If $Sx = \lambda x$ with $\|x\| = 1$ then $\lambda = \langle Sx,x\rangle = \|Tx\|^2 \ge 0$, so $-\|S\|$ is not an eigenvalue and $\lambda = \|S\| = \|T\|^2$ is. For a corresponding unit eigenvector $x$, $\|Tx\|^2 = \langle Sx,x\rangle = \|T\|^2$, so $\|Tx\| = \|T\|$ and the norm is attained. Finally every eigenvalue $\mu$ of $S$ satisfies $\mu = \|Tx\|^2 \le \|T\|^2$ for a unit eigenvector, so $\|T\|^2$ is the largest eigenvalue.

prompt: Let $T$ be the integral operator on $L^2[-1,1]$ with kernel $K(x,y) = 3xy + x^2y^2$. It is compact and self-adjoint with exactly two nonzero eigenvalues. Find their sum.
answer: 2.4
tol: 0.0001
check: quad(lambda y: 3 * y * y, -1, 1) + quad(lambda y: y ** 4, -1, 1)
hint: The range of $T$ is spanned by $x$ and $x^2$; compute $Tx$ and $Tx^2$.
hint: $Tf = 3x\langle f, y\rangle + x^2\langle f, y^2\rangle$, and $x \perp x^2$ in $L^2[-1,1]$.
solution: $Tx = 3x\int_{-1}^1y^2\,dy + x^2\int_{-1}^1y^3\,dy = 2x$ and $Tx^2 = 3x\int_{-1}^1y^3\,dy + x^2\int_{-1}^1y^4\,dy = \tfrac25x^2$. So $x$ and $x^2$ are eigenfunctions with eigenvalues $2$ and $\tfrac25$, and $Tf = 0$ for $f \perp \{x, x^2\}$. The sum is $2 + \tfrac25 = 2.4$, which equals $\int_{-1}^1K(x,x)\,dx = \int(3x^2 + x^4)\,dx$.

@exam ms
prompt: Which of the following statements are true?
choice*: The uniform boundedness principle can fail if the domain is $c_{00}$ with the sup norm.
choice*: A weakly convergent sequence in a normed space is bounded.
choice: Every bounded linear functional on the inner product space $c_{00}$ (with the $\ell^2$ inner product) is of the form $\langle\cdot,y\rangle$ with $y \in c_{00}$.
choice: A compact self-adjoint operator on an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space always has $0$ as an eigenvalue.
choice: The closed graph theorem holds for linear maps $T\colon X \to Y$ with closed graph when $X$ is any normed space and $Y$ is Banach.
hint: Decide for each statement which of Banach, Hilbert, compact or self-adjoint is used, and find the standard counterexample.
solution: The first statement is true: on $c_{00}$ with $T_nx = nx_n$ the family is pointwise bounded and $\|T_n\| = n$. The second is true by the uniform boundedness principle on $X^*$. The third is false (note the wording): the functional $x \mapsto \sum x_n/n$ is bounded on $c_{00}$ but its representing sequence $(1/n)$ is not in $c_{00}$; Riesz needs completeness. The fourth is false: $\operatorname{diag}(1/n)$ is injective. The fifth is false: differentiation on $(C^1[0,1], \|\cdot\|_\infty)$ has closed graph but is unbounded. So only the first two statements are true.
