@article
Expanding a vector in a basis is routine in $\mathbb{R}^n$. For a function on a circle the analogous expansion is the Fourier series, and the questions are exactly the ones this course keeps asking: in which sense does it converge, and is the "basis" really complete? In a Hilbert space the answers are clean, and they are the reason Hilbert space methods dominate partial differential equations and signal processing.

### Bessel and Parseval

Let $H$ be a Hilbert space and $(e_k)_{k\ge1}$ an **orthonormal sequence**: $\langle e_j, e_k\rangle = \delta_{jk}$. Put $c_k = \langle x, e_k\rangle$.

**Theorem 1 (Bessel's inequality).** $\sum_{k}|c_k|^2 \le \|x\|^2$ for every $x \in H$.

*Proof.* For each $N$, $x - \sum_{k\le N}c_ke_k$ is orthogonal to every $e_j$ with $j \le N$, so by Pythagoras $\|x\|^2 = \|x - \sum_{k\le N}c_ke_k\|^2 + \sum_{k \le N}|c_k|^2 \ge \sum_{k\le N}|c_k|^2$. Let $N \to \infty$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 2 (Riesz–Fischer).** If $\sum|a_k|^2 < \infty$ then $\sum a_ke_k$ converges in $H$, and its norm squared is $\sum|a_k|^2$.

*Proof.* For $M < N$, $\|\sum_{M<k\le N}a_ke_k\|^2 = \sum_{M<k\le N}|a_k|^2$ by Pythagoras, so the partial sums are Cauchy and converge since $H$ is complete. Continuity of the norm gives the identity. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 3.** For an orthonormal sequence $(e_k)$ in a Hilbert space $H$ the following are equivalent. (a) The closed linear span of $\{e_k\}$ is $H$. (b) $x = \sum_k\langle x, e_k\rangle e_k$ for every $x$ (convergence in norm). (c) **Parseval's identity** $\|x\|^2 = \sum_k|\langle x, e_k\rangle|^2$ for every $x$. (d) $\langle x, e_k\rangle = 0$ for all $k$ implies $x = 0$. Such a sequence is an **orthonormal basis**.

*Proof.* (a)$\Rightarrow$(d): $x \perp e_k$ for all $k$ gives $x \perp$ the span, hence $x \perp$ its closure $= H$, so $x \perp x$. (d)$\Rightarrow$(b): by Theorems 1 and 2, $y = \sum c_ke_k$ converges; $\langle x - y, e_j\rangle = c_j - c_j = 0$ for all $j$ by continuity of the inner product, so $x = y$. (b)$\Rightarrow$(c): take norms and use Theorem 2. (c)$\Rightarrow$(d): if all $c_k = 0$ then $\|x\|^2 = 0$. (b)$\Rightarrow$(a): the partial sums lie in the span. $\blacksquare$

Orthonormality alone is not enough: in $\ell^2$ the sequence $(e_k)_{k\ge2}$ is orthonormal, Bessel holds, but Parseval fails for $x = e_1$ (the left side is $1$ and the right side is $0$).

### The trigonometric basis

Let $L^2(\mathbb{T})$ be the space of (classes of) measurable $2\pi$-periodic functions with $\langle f, g\rangle = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f\bar g\,dx$, and $e_n(x) = e^{inx}$, $n \in \mathbb{Z}$. Orthonormality is the computation $\frac1{2\pi}\int e^{i(m-n)x}\,dx = \delta_{mn}$.

**Theorem 4.** $\{e_n\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}$ is an orthonormal basis of $L^2(\mathbb{T})$.

*Proof (with one quoted ingredient).* By Theorem 3 it suffices to show that the span of the $e_n$, the trigonometric polynomials, is dense. Quoted from approximation theory: trigonometric polynomials are uniformly dense in the continuous periodic functions (the Weierstrass trigonometric approximation theorem, proved for instance with Fejér's kernel). Quoted from P1: continuous functions are dense in $L^2$. Given $f \in L^2(\mathbb{T})$ and $\varepsilon > 0$ pick a continuous $g$ with $\|f - g\|_2 < \varepsilon$ and a trigonometric polynomial $p$ with $\|g - p\|_\infty < \varepsilon$. Since the measure $dx/2\pi$ has total mass $1$, $\|g - p\|_2 \le \|g - p\|_\infty$, so $\|f - p\|_2 < 2\varepsilon$. $\blacksquare$

So every $f \in L^2(\mathbb{T})$ equals $\sum_n\hat f(n)e^{inx}$ **in $L^2$**, where $\hat f(n) = \frac1{2\pi}\int f e^{-inx}\,dx$, and $\|f\|_2^2 = \sum|\hat f(n)|^2$. This says nothing about pointwise convergence, which is much subtler (almost-everywhere convergence for $L^2$ is Carleson's theorem, 1966). The partial sum $S_Nf = \sum_{|n|\le N}\hat f(n)e_n$ is the orthogonal projection onto the trigonometric polynomials of degree $\le N$, hence the best approximation in $L^2$ from that subspace (Lesson 3), with error $\|f - S_Nf\|^2 = \|f\|^2 - \sum_{|n|\le N}|\hat f(n)|^2$.

For $f(x) = x$ on $(-\pi,\pi)$: $\hat f(0) = 0$ and $\hat f(n) = i(-1)^n/n$ for $n \ne 0$, and $\|f\|^2 = \pi^2/3$. Parseval gives $\pi^2/3 = 2\sum_{n\ge1}1/n^2$, i.e. $\sum 1/n^2 = \pi^2/6$.

### Coefficients on a computer

The trapezoid rule on $N$ equally spaced points gives $\hat f(n) \approx \frac1N\sum_j f(x_j)e^{-inx_j}$, which is exact for trigonometric polynomials of low degree and very accurate for smooth periodic functions. For a function with a jump the error is only $O(1/N)$.

```python
import math


def fourier_coeff(f, n, N):
    """Trapezoid approximation of (1/2pi) * integral f(x) e^{-inx} dx.

    Returns the pair (real part, imaginary part) of the coefficient.
    """
    re = im = 0.0
    for j in range(N):
        x = -math.pi + 2 * math.pi * j / N
        re += f(x) * math.cos(n * x)
        im -= f(x) * math.sin(n * x)
    return re / N, im / N
```

(The browser's Python has no `cmath`, so the code keeps real and imaginary parts apart.) A numerical Parseval check is evidence, not a proof; it can only agree to the quadrature error.

@example
title: Parseval for $|x|$
problem: Compute the Fourier coefficients of $f(x) = |x|$ on $[-\pi,\pi]$ and use Parseval's identity to find $\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{1}{(2k+1)^4}$.
step: Zeroth coefficient: $\hat f(0) = \frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|x|\,dx = \frac1{\pi}\int_0^\pi x\,dx = \frac\pi2$.
step: For $n \ne 0$, since $f$ is even, $\hat f(n) = \frac1\pi\int_0^\pi x\cos(nx)\,dx$. Integrating by parts, $\int_0^\pi x\cos(nx)\,dx = \Big[\frac{x\sin nx}{n}\Big]_0^\pi - \int_0^\pi\frac{\sin nx}{n}\,dx = \frac{(-1)^n - 1}{n^2}$. So $\hat f(n) = \frac{(-1)^n - 1}{\pi n^2}$, which is $-\frac{2}{\pi n^2}$ for odd $n$ and $0$ for even $n \ne 0$.
step: The norm: $\|f\|^2 = \frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^\pi x^2\,dx = \frac{\pi^2}{3}$.
step: Parseval: $\frac{\pi^2}{3} = \frac{\pi^2}{4} + 2\sum_{n\text{ odd}\ge1}\frac{4}{\pi^2n^4}$, where the factor $2$ counts $\pm n$. So $\sum_{n\text{ odd}}n^{-4} = \frac{\pi^2}{8}\cdot\Big(\frac{\pi^2}{3} - \frac{\pi^2}{4}\Big) = \frac{\pi^2}{8}\cdot\frac{\pi^2}{12} = \frac{\pi^4}{96}$.
answer: $\sum_{k\ge0}(2k+1)^{-4} = \pi^4/96 \approx 1.01468$.

@example
title: Best trigonometric approximation of $x$ and its error
problem: Find the squared $L^2(\mathbb{T})$ error of the best approximation of $f(x) = x$ by trigonometric polynomials of degree at most $2$.
step: By Theorem 3 and Lesson 3, the best approximation is the projection $S_2f = \sum_{|n|\le2}\hat f(n)e_n$, and the squared error is $\|f\|^2 - \sum_{|n|\le2}|\hat f(n)|^2$.
step: Use $\hat f(n) = i(-1)^n/n$: $|\hat f(\pm1)|^2 = 1$ and $|\hat f(\pm2)|^2 = \tfrac14$, and $\hat f(0) = 0$. So $\sum_{|n|\le2}|\hat f(n)|^2 = 2(1 + \tfrac14) = \tfrac52$.
step: With $\|f\|^2 = \pi^2/3 \approx 3.28987$ the squared error is $\pi^2/3 - \tfrac52 \approx 0.78987$.
answer: $\pi^2/3 - 5/2 \approx 0.78987$.

@mistakes
- Treating Bessel's inequality as an equality: it holds for every orthonormal sequence, while Parseval's identity holds for all $x$ exactly when the sequence is a basis.
- Mixing normalizations: with $\langle f,g\rangle = \frac1{2\pi}\int f\bar g$ the functions $e^{inx}$ are orthonormal; with the unnormalized integral you need $e^{inx}/\sqrt{2\pi}$.
- Counting only $n \ge 1$ in Parseval for complex coefficients: the terms $n$ and $-n$ are both there.
- Reading "$f = \sum\hat f(n)e^{inx}$ in $L^2$" as pointwise equality at every $x$.

@practice ms
prompt: Let $(e_k)_{k\ge1}$ be an orthonormal sequence in a separable Hilbert space $H$. Which of the following are equivalent to $(e_k)$ being an orthonormal basis of $H$?
choice*: The linear span of $\{e_k\}$ is dense in $H$.
choice*: $\|x\|^2 = \sum_k|\langle x, e_k\rangle|^2$ for every $x \in H$.
choice*: The only $x \in H$ with $\langle x, e_k\rangle = 0$ for all $k$ is $x = 0$.
choice: $\sum_k|\langle x, e_k\rangle|^2 \le \|x\|^2$ for every $x \in H$.
choice: $\sum_k\langle x, e_k\rangle e_k$ converges for every $x \in H$.
hint: One of the last two statements is true for every orthonormal sequence; the other does not say what the limit is.
solution: The first three are conditions (a), (c), (d) of Theorem 3. Bessel's inequality holds for every orthonormal sequence, e.g. $(e_k)_{k\ge2}$ in $\ell^2$, which is not a basis. Convergence of $\sum\langle x,e_k\rangle e_k$ also holds for every orthonormal sequence (Bessel plus Riesz–Fischer), but its limit is the projection of $x$ onto the closed span, which equals $x$ only when $x$ lies in it.

@practice self
prompt: Prove that every separable Hilbert space $H$ has a (finite or countably infinite) orthonormal basis, and that every orthonormal set in a separable Hilbert space is countable.
hint: For the second claim, distinct orthonormal vectors are $\sqrt2$ apart: use a countable dense set and disjoint balls.
hint: For the first, apply Gram–Schmidt to a dense sequence after discarding vectors that are linear combinations of earlier ones.
solution: Countability: if $e \ne e'$ are in an orthonormal set then $\|e - e'\|^2 = 2$. The open balls of radius $\tfrac12$ around the members of the set are pairwise disjoint (a point in two of them would put $e, e'$ within distance $1 < \sqrt2$). Each ball contains a point of a fixed countable dense set $D$; choosing one such point for each ball is injective, so the set is countable. Existence: let $(x_n)$ be a dense sequence. Delete every $x_n$ that lies in the span of $x_1, \dots, x_{n-1}$; the remaining vectors $y_1, y_2, \dots$ are linearly independent with the same span as the whole sequence. Apply Gram–Schmidt to $(y_j)$, giving an orthonormal sequence $(e_j)$ with $\operatorname{span}\{e_1, \dots, e_j\} = \operatorname{span}\{y_1, \dots, y_j\}$ for all $j$. Then $\operatorname{span}\{e_j\} = \operatorname{span}\{x_n\}$, which contains the dense sequence, so it is dense, and $(e_j)$ is an orthonormal basis by Theorem 3(a). If the process stops after finitely many steps the basis is finite.
rubric: Proves countability via disjoint balls (or an equivalent separation argument) and the countable dense set.
rubric: Builds an independent sequence with the same span as the dense sequence and applies Gram–Schmidt, preserving spans.
rubric: Concludes density of the span and invokes the equivalence (a)$\Leftrightarrow$basis from Theorem 3.

prompt: In $L^2[-1,1]$ (real functions, inner product $\int_{-1}^1 fg$) find $\min_{a,b,c\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{-1}^1\left(t^3 - a - bt - ct^2\right)^2dt$, with a proof that the value you give is the minimum.
hint: Orthogonalize $1, t, t^2$ (Legendre polynomials), or note the parity: $t^3$ is odd, so only the odd part of the subspace matters.
hint: The minimum is $\|t^3\|^2 - \|P t^3\|^2$ where $P$ projects onto $\operatorname{span}\{1, t, t^2\}$.
solution: The functions $1, t, t^2$ are not orthogonal, but $1$ and $t^2$ are even and $t$ is odd, so $t \perp 1$ and $t \perp t^2$. Orthonormalize: $e_1 = \sqrt{3/2}\,t$ (since $\|t\|^2 = 2/3$). The even functions $1$ and $t^2$ are orthogonal to $t^3$ (odd times even is odd, with integral $0$ over a symmetric interval), so they contribute nothing to the projection of $t^3$. Let $M = \operatorname{span}\{1,t,t^2\}$. For the even part, $t^3 \perp 1$ and $t^3 \perp t^2$; hence $t^3 \perp \operatorname{span}\{1, t^2\}$, which is the orthogonal complement of $t$ inside $M$ (as $t \perp 1, t^2$). Therefore $P t^3 = \langle t^3, e_1\rangle e_1 = \frac{\langle t^3, t\rangle}{\|t\|^2}t = \frac{2/5}{2/3}t = \frac35t$. By the projection theorem the minimum is $\|t^3 - Pt^3\|^2 = \|t^3\|^2 - \|Pt^3\|^2 = \frac27 - \frac{9}{25}\cdot\frac23 = \frac27 - \frac{6}{25} = \frac{8}{175}$, attained at $a = c = 0$, $b = \frac35$.
rubric: Recognizes the problem as a distance to a finite-dimensional subspace and invokes the projection theorem (minimum equals the squared distance).
rubric: Uses parity to obtain an orthogonal basis $\{1, t^2, t\}$ or applies Gram–Schmidt correctly.
rubric: Computes $\langle t^3, t\rangle = 2/5$, $\|t\|^2 = 2/3$ and the final value $8/175$.

model:
```python
import math


def parseval_gap(f, M, N):
    """Discrete (trapezoid) version of ||f||^2 - sum_{|n|<=M} |c_n|^2."""
    xs = [-math.pi + 2 * math.pi * j / N for j in range(N)]
    values = [f(x) for x in xs]
    norm_sq = sum(abs(v) ** 2 for v in values) / N
    partial = 0.0
    for n in range(-M, M + 1):
        re = sum(v * math.cos(n * x) for v, x in zip(values, xs)) / N
        im = sum(v * math.sin(n * x) for v, x in zip(values, xs)) / N
        partial += re * re + im * im
    return norm_sq - partial
```
hint: Sample $f$ once, then loop over $n$ from $-M$ to $M$ (negative frequencies too) and add up $|\hat f(n)|^2$.
solution: The trapezoid mean of $|f|^2$ approximates $\|f\|^2$, and each $\hat f(n)$ is the mean of $f(x_j)e^{-inx_j}$. Looping only over $n \ge 0$ is the typical error: for $g = \cos 2x + \tfrac12\sin 3x$ it would give $0.375$ instead of $0.125$ at $M = 2$. The value for $|x|$ agrees with the exact gap from the Fourier series only up to the quadrature error, which is why the test has a tolerance; and a small gap on a grid proves nothing about convergence of the series.
