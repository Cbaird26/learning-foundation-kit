@article
Is the dual space of a Banach space big enough to tell vectors apart? In a Hilbert space yes, by Riesz. In $\ell^\infty$ or $L^\infty$ nobody can write the dual down, and yet one can still prove that there are many functionals. The tool is the Hahn–Banach theorem, which extends a functional defined on a subspace to the whole space without increasing its size. It is the most used theorem in functional analysis, and one of the few that needs a choice principle (here Zorn's lemma).

### The theorem

A function $p\colon X \to \mathbb{R}$ on a real vector space is **sublinear** if $p(x + y) \le p(x) + p(y)$ and $p(\lambda x) = \lambda p(x)$ for all $x, y \in X$ and $\lambda \ge 0$. Every seminorm is sublinear.

**Theorem 1 (Hahn–Banach, real sublinear form).** Let $X$ be a real vector space, $p$ sublinear on $X$, $M \subseteq X$ a subspace and $f\colon M \to \mathbb{R}$ linear with $f \le p$ on $M$. Then there is a linear $F\colon X \to \mathbb{R}$ with $F|_M = f$ and $F \le p$ on $X$.

*Proof.* *Step 1 (one more dimension).* Let $x_0 \notin M$ and $M_1 = M + \mathbb{R}x_0$. We look for a value $\alpha = F(x_0)$ with $f(m) + t\alpha \le p(m + tx_0)$ for all $m \in M$, $t \in \mathbb{R}$. For $t > 0$, dividing by $t$ (and replacing $m/t$ by $m$) this says $\alpha \le p(m + x_0) - f(m)$; for $t = -s < 0$ it says $\alpha \ge f(m') - p(m' - x_0)$. For all $m, m' \in M$: $f(m') + f(m) = f(m + m') \le p(m + m') \le p(m' - x_0) + p(m + x_0)$, i.e. $f(m') - p(m' - x_0) \le p(m + x_0) - f(m)$. So $\sup_{m'}[f(m') - p(m' - x_0)] \le \inf_m[p(m + x_0) - f(m)]$ and any $\alpha$ between them works; the case $t = 0$ is the hypothesis.

*Step 2 (Zorn's lemma).* Order the pairs $(N, g)$, where $N$ is a subspace containing $M$ and $g$ is linear on $N$ with $g|_M = f$ and $g \le p$ on $N$, by extension. Every chain has an upper bound (the union of the domains with the common extension of the functionals), so there is a maximal pair $(N, g)$. If $N \ne X$, Step 1 extends $g$ to $N + \mathbb{R}x_0$ for some $x_0 \notin N$, contradicting maximality. So $N = X$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 2 (Hahn–Banach, normed form).** Let $X$ be a normed space over $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, $M$ a subspace and $f \in M^*$. Then there is $F \in X^*$ with $F|_M = f$ and $\|F\| = \|f\|$.

*Proof.* Real case: $p(x) = \|f\|\|x\|$ is a seminorm with $f \le p$ on $M$. Theorem 1 gives $F \le p$, and $-F(x) = F(-x) \le p(x)$, so $|F(x)| \le \|f\|\|x\|$ and $\|F\| = \|f\|$. Complex case: apply the real case to $\operatorname{Re}f$ (a real-linear functional with $\|\operatorname{Re}f\| \le \|f\|$) to get a real-linear $F_0$ with $|F_0(x)| \le \|f\|\|x\|$, and define $F(x) = F_0(x) - iF_0(ix)$. This is complex linear, agrees with $f$ on $M$ because $f(m) = \operatorname{Re}f(m) - i\operatorname{Re}f(im)$, and for $x$ with $F(x) \ne 0$ choose $\theta$ with $e^{i\theta}F(x) = |F(x)|$: then $|F(x)| = F(e^{i\theta}x) = F_0(e^{i\theta}x) \le \|f\|\|x\|$. $\blacksquare$

### Corollaries

**Corollary 3.** Let $X$ be a normed space. (a) For each $x \ne 0$ there is $F \in X^*$ with $\|F\| = 1$ and $F(x) = \|x\|$. (b) $\|x\| = \max\{|F(x)| : \|F\| \le 1\}$. (c) $X^*$ separates the points of $X$. (d) If $M$ is a closed subspace, $x_0 \notin M$ and $d = \operatorname{dist}(x_0, M) > 0$, there is $F \in X^*$ with $\|F\| = 1$, $F|_M = 0$ and $F(x_0) = d$.

*Proof.* (a) Define $f(\lambda x) = \lambda\|x\|$ on $\mathbb{K}x$, which has norm $1$, and extend. (b) and (c) follow from (a). (d) On $M + \mathbb{K}x_0$ (a direct sum since $x_0 \notin M$) put $f(m + \lambda x_0) = \lambda d$. For $\lambda \ne 0$, $\|m + \lambda x_0\| = |\lambda|\,\|x_0 - (-m/\lambda)\| \ge |\lambda|d = |f(m + \lambda x_0)|$, so $\|f\| \le 1$. Taking $m_k \in M$ with $\|x_0 - m_k\| \to d$ gives $d = f(x_0 - m_k) \le \|f\|\|x_0 - m_k\| \to \|f\|d$, so $\|f\| = 1$. Extend by Theorem 2. $\blacksquare$

Part (d) is the **separation of a point from a closed subspace**: $F$ vanishes on $M$ and is positive at $x_0$. Without closedness $d = 0$ and the conclusion fails for $x_0 \in \overline{M}\setminus M$, since every continuous functional vanishing on $M$ vanishes on $\overline{M}$. There are also geometric forms of the theorem (a closed convex set and a point outside it can be strictly separated by a hyperplane), obtained from the same proof with the Minkowski functional as $p$; they are quoted, not proved, here.

### Extensions need not be unique

On $(\mathbb{R}^2, \|\cdot\|_1)$ let $M = \mathbb{R}(1,0)$ and $f(a,0) = a$, so $\|f\| = 1$. The dual norm of $F(x,y) = ux + vy$ is $\max(|u|, |v|)$ (the dual of $\ell^1_2$ is $\ell^\infty_2$), so $F = x + vy$ extends $f$ with norm $1$ for every $|v| \le 1$: infinitely many Hahn–Banach extensions. In $(\mathbb{R}^2, \|\cdot\|_2)$ the dual norm is the Euclidean norm $\sqrt{1 + v^2}$, which is $1$ only for $v = 0$: the extension is unique.

### Consequences used later

For $\varphi \in X^*$ nonzero, $\operatorname{dist}(x, \ker\varphi) = |\varphi(x)|/\|\varphi\|$. (Lower bound: $|\varphi(x)| = |\varphi(x - m)| \le \|\varphi\|\|x - m\|$ for $m \in \ker\varphi$. Upper bound: choose $u$ with $\|u\| = 1$ and $|\varphi(u)| \ge \|\varphi\| - \varepsilon$, and subtract the multiple of $u$ that puts $x$ in the kernel.) The existence of many functionals also gives the **canonical embedding** $X \to X^{**}$ (Lesson 9).

@example
title: Non-uniqueness of the extension, computed
problem: Let $X = (\mathbb{R}^3, \|\cdot\|_1)$, $M = \operatorname{span}\{(1,1,0)\}$ and $f(a,a,0) = 2a$. Find $\|f\|$ and all functionals $F = (u,v,w)$ on $X$ that are Hahn–Banach extensions of $f$.
step: The dual of $\ell^1_3$ is $\ell^\infty_3$: a functional $F(x) = ux_1 + vx_2 + wx_3$ has $\|F\| = \max(|u|, |v|, |w|)$.
step: Norm of $f$: $\|(a,a,0)\|_1 = 2|a|$, so $|f(a,a,0)|/\|(a,a,0)\|_1 = 2|a|/2|a| = 1$. Hence $\|f\| = 1$.
step: $F$ extends $f$ iff $F(1,1,0) = u + v = 2$. It has norm $1$ iff $\max(|u|,|v|,|w|) = 1$. From $u + v = 2$ and $|u|, |v| \le 1$ we get $u = v = 1$.
step: The third coordinate is free subject to $|w| \le 1$. So the extensions are exactly $F = (1, 1, w)$ with $-1 \le w \le 1$: infinitely many.
answer: $\|f\| = 1$, and the norm-preserving extensions are $F(x) = x_1 + x_2 + wx_3$ with $|w| \le 1$.

@example
title: Distance to a hyperplane in $C[0,1]$
problem: On $(C[0,1], \|\cdot\|_\infty)$ let $\varphi(f) = \int_0^1 f(t)\,dt - f(0)$. Find $\|\varphi\|$ and the distance from $g(t) = t$ to $\ker\varphi$.
step: Upper bound: $|\varphi(f)| \le \int|f| + |f(0)| \le 2\|f\|_\infty$, so $\|\varphi\| \le 2$.
step: Lower bound: for $0 < \varepsilon < 1$ let $f_\varepsilon$ be $-1$ at $0$, increase linearly to $+1$ at $t = \varepsilon$, and equal $1$ on $[\varepsilon, 1]$. Then $\|f_\varepsilon\|_\infty = 1$ and $\int f_\varepsilon = (1 - \varepsilon) + 0$, because the linear piece has mean $0$. So $\varphi(f_\varepsilon) = (1 - \varepsilon) + 1 = 2 - \varepsilon$, and $\|\varphi\| = 2$ (not attained).
step: By the distance formula, $\operatorname{dist}(g, \ker\varphi) = |\varphi(g)|/\|\varphi\|$. Here $\varphi(g) = \int_0^1 t\,dt - 0 = \tfrac12$, so the distance is $\tfrac12/2 = \tfrac14$.
step: Sanity check of the upper bound: the nearest approach is by $m = g - \frac{\varphi(g)}{\varphi(f_\varepsilon)}f_\varepsilon \in \ker\varphi$, with $\|g - m\|_\infty \le \frac{1/2}{2 - \varepsilon} \to \frac14$.
answer: $\|\varphi\| = 2$ and $\operatorname{dist}(t, \ker\varphi) = \tfrac14$ (not attained).

@mistakes
- Forgetting that the extension need not be unique, or that the norm-preserving extension is in general not computed by a formula.
- Applying the separation corollary (d) to a subspace that is not closed: then $d = 0$ is possible and $x_0$ cannot be separated.
- Saying "Hahn–Banach needs $X$ complete": it holds in every normed space (even every vector space with a seminorm). Completeness is not used.
- Using the Hahn–Banach theorem to extend operators into an arbitrary normed space: it is a theorem about scalar-valued functionals.

@practice mc
prompt: Which of the following is a direct consequence of the Hahn–Banach theorem for every normed space $X$?
choice*: If $F(x) = 0$ for every $F \in X^*$, then $x = 0$.
choice: Every bounded sequence in $X$ has a norm-convergent subsequence.
choice: Every Cauchy sequence in $X$ converges.
choice: Every closed subspace $M$ of $X$ has a closed complement $N$ with $X = M \oplus N$.
hint: Only one of these is a statement about the existence of functionals.
solution: By Corollary 3(a), if $x \ne 0$ some functional has $F(x) = \|x\| \ne 0$, so functionals separate points. The other statements are false in general or unrelated: bounded sequences need not have convergent subsequences in infinite dimensions, completeness is not implied, and closed complements need not exist (for example, $c_0$ has no closed complement in $\ell^\infty$, a deeper theorem).

@practice self
prompt: Let $M$ be a linear subspace of a normed space $X$ and $x_0 \in X$. Prove that $x_0 \in \overline{M}$ if and only if every $F \in X^*$ with $F|_M = 0$ satisfies $F(x_0) = 0$. Deduce that $M$ is dense in $X$ if and only if $\{0\}$ is the only functional in $X^*$ vanishing on $M$.
hint: One direction is continuity. For the other, if $x_0 \notin \overline{M}$ apply Corollary 3(d) to the closed subspace $\overline{M}$.
hint: The closure of a subspace is a subspace.
solution: ($\Rightarrow$) Let $x_0 \in \overline{M}$, $m_k \to x_0$ with $m_k \in M$, and $F \in X^*$ with $F|_M = 0$. Then $F(x_0) = \lim F(m_k) = 0$ by continuity. ($\Leftarrow$) Suppose $x_0 \notin \overline{M}$. The closure $\overline{M}$ is a closed subspace (limits preserve linear combinations), so $d = \operatorname{dist}(x_0, \overline{M}) > 0$, and Corollary 3(d) gives $F \in X^*$ with $F|_{\overline{M}} = 0$ and $F(x_0) = d \ne 0$. This contradicts the hypothesis. For the second statement: if $M$ is dense and $F|_M = 0$, the first part gives $F(x) = 0$ for every $x \in X = \overline{M}$. If $M$ is not dense, pick $x_0 \notin \overline{M}$; by the first part some $F \in X^*$ vanishing on $M$ has $F(x_0) \ne 0$, so $F \ne 0$.
rubric: Proves the easy direction by continuity of $F$ along an approximating sequence in $M$.
rubric: Proves the converse by applying the separation corollary to the closed subspace $\overline M$ after noting $d > 0$.
rubric: Derives the density criterion from the first statement in both directions.

@practice self
prompt: Prove that if the dual space $X^*$ of a normed space $X$ is separable, then $X$ is separable.
hint: Take a countable dense subset $\{\varphi_n\}$ of the unit sphere of $X^*$ and pick $x_n$ with $\|x_n\| = 1$ and $|\varphi_n(x_n)| \ge \tfrac12$.
hint: Let $Y$ be the closed span of the $x_n$. If $Y \ne X$, use the previous problem to find a unit functional vanishing on $Y$ and compare it with some $\varphi_n$.
solution: If $X^* = \{0\}$ then $X = \{0\}$ by Corollary 3(c). Otherwise the unit sphere $S$ of $X^*$ is a subset of a separable metric space and so is separable; choose a countable dense subset $\{\varphi_n\}$ of $S$. Since $\|\varphi_n\| = 1$ there is $x_n \in X$ with $\|x_n\| = 1$ and $|\varphi_n(x_n)| \ge \tfrac12$. Let $Y$ be the closed linear span of $\{x_n\}$; it is separable (finite rational combinations of the $x_n$ form a countable dense subset). Suppose $Y \ne X$. Pick $x_0 \in X \setminus Y$. By Corollary 3(d) there is $\varphi \in X^*$ with $\|\varphi\| = 1$ and $\varphi|_Y = 0$. By density choose $n$ with $\|\varphi - \varphi_n\| < \tfrac14$. Then $\tfrac12 \le |\varphi_n(x_n)| = |\varphi_n(x_n) - \varphi(x_n)| \le \|\varphi_n - \varphi\|\|x_n\| < \tfrac14$, a contradiction. So $Y = X$ and $X$ is separable.
rubric: Chooses a countable dense set of unit functionals and vectors $x_n$ on which they are large.
rubric: Applies Hahn–Banach (separation from the closed span $Y$) to produce a unit functional vanishing on $Y$ if $Y \ne X$.
rubric: Derives the contradiction $\tfrac12 \le |\varphi_n(x_n)| < \tfrac14$ and concludes that $Y = X$ is separable.
