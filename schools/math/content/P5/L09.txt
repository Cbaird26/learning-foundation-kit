@article
In $\mathbb{R}^n$ bounded sequences have convergent subsequences. In infinite dimensions that fails for norm convergence: the orthonormal vectors $e_n \in \ell^2$ satisfy $\|e_n - e_m\| = \sqrt2$, so no subsequence converges. But $e_n$ does converge in a weaker sense: for every $y \in \ell^2$, $\langle e_n, y\rangle = \bar y_n \to 0$. This weaker mode of convergence is the one in which bounded sets become compact, and it is why existence proofs for minimization problems and differential equations work. This lesson is an outline-level treatment: we prove the elementary facts and quote the deeper compactness theorems.

### Weak and weak* convergence

Let $X$ be a normed space. A sequence $x_n \to x$ **weakly** (written $x_n \rightharpoonup x$) if $\varphi(x_n) \to \varphi(x)$ for every $\varphi \in X^*$. A sequence $\varphi_n \in X^*$ converges **weak\*** to $\varphi$ if $\varphi_n(x) \to \varphi(x)$ for every $x \in X$. Norm convergence implies weak convergence, since $|\varphi(x_n) - \varphi(x)| \le \|\varphi\|\|x_n - x\|$; the converse is false. By the Hahn–Banach theorem weak limits are unique (if $\varphi(x) = \varphi(x')$ for all $\varphi$ then $x = x'$).

*Examples.* (i) In a Hilbert space an orthonormal sequence $e_n \rightharpoonup 0$: by Bessel's inequality $\sum|\langle y, e_n\rangle|^2 < \infty$, so $\langle y, e_n\rangle \to 0$, while $\|e_n\| = 1$. By the Riesz representation theorem every functional is of this form, which is why this suffices. (ii) In $L^2[0,2\pi]$, $\sin(nx) \rightharpoonup 0$ (Riemann–Lebesgue, or Bessel for the orthogonal system $\sin nx$) although $\|\sin nx\|^2 = \pi$. (iii) Weak convergence does not commute with nonlinear maps: for $g \in L^2[0,2\pi]$, $\int\sin^2(nx)\,g = \tfrac12\int g - \tfrac12\int\cos(2nx)\,g \to \tfrac12\int g$, so $\sin^2(nx) \rightharpoonup \tfrac12$, not $0$.

**Theorem 1.** Let $x_n \rightharpoonup x$ in a normed space $X$. Then (a) $\sup_n\|x_n\| < \infty$; (b) $\|x\| \le \liminf\|x_n\|$; (c) if $X$ is a Hilbert space and $\|x_n\| \to \|x\|$, then $\|x_n - x\| \to 0$.

*Proof.* (a) Let $J\colon X \to X^{**}$ be the evaluation map $(Jz)(\varphi) = \varphi(z)$; by Hahn–Banach $\|Jz\| = \|z\|$ (Lesson 6, Corollary 3(b)). For each $\varphi \in X^*$ the sequence $(Jx_n)(\varphi) = \varphi(x_n)$ converges, so it is bounded. $X^*$ is a Banach space (Lesson 2), so the uniform boundedness principle applied to the functionals $Jx_n$ on $X^*$ gives $\sup_n\|x_n\| = \sup_n\|Jx_n\| < \infty$. (b) Choose $\varphi$ with $\|\varphi\| = 1$ and $\varphi(x) = \|x\|$. Then $\|x\| = \lim\operatorname{Re}\varphi(x_n) \le \liminf\|x_n\|$. (c) Expand $\|x_n - x\|^2 = \|x_n\|^2 - 2\operatorname{Re}\langle x_n, x\rangle + \|x\|^2 \to \|x\|^2 - 2\|x\|^2 + \|x\|^2 = 0$, because $\langle x_n, x\rangle \to \langle x, x\rangle$ by weak convergence. $\blacksquare$

Part (b) can be strict: $e_n \rightharpoonup 0$ with $\|e_n\| = 1 > 0$. Part (c) is the standard way to upgrade weak convergence to norm convergence.

### Compactness

**Theorem 2.** Every bounded sequence $(x_n)$ in a Hilbert space $H$ has a weakly convergent subsequence.

*Proof.* Let $\|x_n\| \le C$ and $Y$ the closed linear span of $\{x_n\}$, a separable subspace; let $D \subseteq Y$ be a countable dense set. Since $|\langle d, x_n\rangle| \le C\|d\|$ for each $d$, the diagonal argument gives a subsequence $(x_{n_j})$ with $\langle d, x_{n_j}\rangle$ convergent for every $d \in D$. For $y \in Y$ and $d \in D$ with $\|y - d\| < \varepsilon$, $|\langle y, x_{n_j}\rangle - \langle y, x_{n_k}\rangle| \le 2C\varepsilon + |\langle d, x_{n_j}\rangle - \langle d, x_{n_k}\rangle|$, so $\psi(y) = \lim_j\langle y, x_{n_j}\rangle$ exists for all $y \in Y$; $\psi$ is linear with $|\psi(y)| \le C\|y\|$. By the Riesz theorem in the Hilbert space $Y$ there is $x \in Y$ with $\psi(y) = \langle y, x\rangle$. For arbitrary $z \in H$ write $z = Pz + (z - Pz)$ with $P$ the projection onto $Y$; as $z - Pz \perp x_{n_j}$ and $x$, $\langle z, x_{n_j}\rangle = \langle Pz, x_{n_j}\rangle \to \langle Pz, x\rangle = \langle z, x\rangle$. Conjugating, $x_{n_j} \rightharpoonup x$. $\blacksquare$

*Stated without proof here* (the proofs use Tychonoff's theorem and are given in functional analysis courses): **Banach–Alaoglu.** If $X$ is a normed space, the closed unit ball of $X^*$ is compact in the weak\* topology. If $X$ is separable, every bounded sequence in $X^*$ has a weak\* convergent subsequence (a diagonal argument as in Theorem 2). **Kakutani.** A Banach space is reflexive if and only if its closed unit ball is weakly compact; and in a reflexive space every bounded sequence has a weakly convergent subsequence.

### Reflexivity

The map $J\colon X \to X^{**}$ is a linear isometry (Lesson 6). $X$ is **reflexive** if $J$ is onto. Then $X \cong X^{**}$ via $J$, so $X$ is complete. Finite-dimensional spaces are reflexive. Hilbert spaces are reflexive (apply Riesz twice and check $J$ corresponds to the identity). For $1 < p < \infty$, $\ell^p$ and $L^p$ are reflexive since $(L^p)^* = L^q$ and $(L^q)^* = L^p$ with $J$ corresponding to the identity. The spaces $c_0$, $\ell^1$, $\ell^\infty$, $C[0,1]$ and $L^1[0,1]$ are not reflexive (stated for the last three; for $c_0$ see the practice problem). A bounded sequence with no weakly convergent subsequence signals non-reflexivity: $x_n = e_1 + \dots + e_n$ in $c_0$ has $\|x_n\|_\infty = 1$, and a weak limit would have to be $(1,1,1,\dots) \notin c_0$ (the coordinate functionals are in $c_0^*$).

@example
title: Weak limit of a shifted sequence
problem: In $\ell^2$ let $x_n = e_1 + 3e_n$. Find the weak limit of $(x_n)$ and $\lim\|x_n\|$, and compare with Theorem 1(b) and (c).
step: For $y \in \ell^2$, $\langle x_n, y\rangle = \bar y_1 + 3\bar y_n \to \bar y_1$ because $y_n \to 0$ (the terms of a square-summable sequence tend to $0$). So $x_n \rightharpoonup e_1$.
step: The norms: $\|x_n\|^2 = 1 + 9 = 10$ for $n \ge 2$ (since $e_1 \perp e_n$), so $\|x_n\| = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.162$ for all $n \ge 2$.
step: Theorem 1(b): $\|e_1\| = 1 \le \sqrt{10} = \liminf\|x_n\|$, a strict inequality.
step: Theorem 1(c) does not apply because $\|x_n\| \not\to \|e_1\|$. Indeed $\|x_n - e_1\| = \|3e_n\| = 3$ for all $n$, so $x_n$ does not converge in norm.
answer: $x_n \rightharpoonup e_1$ and $\|x_n\| = \sqrt{10}$; the lower semicontinuity inequality is strict.

@example
title: A weak limit that is not the pointwise limit of squares
problem: Show that $f_n(x) = \sqrt2\cos(nx)$ converges weakly to $0$ in $L^2[0,2\pi]$ but $f_n^2$ converges weakly to $1$, not to $0^2 = 0$.
step: The functions $\cos(nx)/\sqrt\pi$, $n \ge 1$, are orthonormal in $L^2[0,2\pi]$, so Bessel's inequality gives $\int g\cos(nx)\,dx \to 0$ for every $g \in L^2$. Hence $f_n \rightharpoonup 0$.
step: Use $f_n^2 = 2\cos^2(nx) = 1 + \cos(2nx)$. For $g \in L^2[0,2\pi]$ we have $\int f_n^2g = \int g + \int\cos(2nx)\,g\,dx \to \int g$, since $\int g\cos(2nx)\to0$ by the same argument. (The $f_n^2$ are bounded in $L^2$ by $2\sqrt{2\pi}$, so they are in the space.)
step: Thus $f_n^2 \rightharpoonup 1$ although $f_n \rightharpoonup 0$ and $\|f_n\|_2^2 = 2\pi$.
answer: $f_n \rightharpoonup 0$ but $f_n^2 \rightharpoonup 1$: weak convergence is not preserved by nonlinear maps.

@mistakes
- Treating weak convergence as if it implied convergence of norms or of nonlinear functions of the sequence; it implies neither.
- Forgetting the boundedness assumption in compactness statements: unbounded sequences such as $ne_n$ in $\ell^2$ have no weakly convergent subsequence (part (a) of Theorem 1).
- Believing weak and norm convergence agree in infinite dimensions. They agree in $\ell^1$ (the Schur property, quoted) but not in $\ell^2$.
- Claiming $\ell^1$ or $\ell^\infty$ is reflexive because they are Banach spaces; reflexivity is a much stronger condition (and fails for them).

@practice self
prompt: (a) Prove that if $x_n \rightharpoonup x$ in a normed space, then $\|x\| \le \liminf\|x_n\|$. (b) Prove that in a Hilbert space, if $x_n \rightharpoonup x$ and $\limsup\|x_n\| \le \|x\|$, then $x_n \to x$ in norm.
hint: For (a) use a norming functional from the Hahn–Banach theorem.
hint: For (b) expand $\|x_n - x\|^2$ and use $\langle x_n, x\rangle \to \|x\|^2$.
solution: (a) If $x = 0$ the claim is trivial. Otherwise Corollary 3(a) of Lesson 6 gives $\varphi \in X^*$ with $\|\varphi\| = 1$ and $\varphi(x) = \|x\|$. Since $\varphi(x_n) \to \varphi(x) = \|x\|$ and $|\varphi(x_n)| \le \|x_n\|$, we get $\|x\| = \lim|\varphi(x_n)| \le \liminf\|x_n\|$. (b) $\|x_n - x\|^2 = \|x_n\|^2 - 2\operatorname{Re}\langle x_n, x\rangle + \|x\|^2$. Weak convergence gives $\langle x_n, x\rangle \to \langle x, x\rangle = \|x\|^2$ (the functional $z \mapsto \langle z, x\rangle$ is bounded). Hence $\limsup\|x_n - x\|^2 \le \limsup\|x_n\|^2 - 2\|x\|^2 + \|x\|^2 \le \|x\|^2 - \|x\|^2 = 0$. So $x_n \to x$.

rubric: Expands the squared norm in a Hilbert space and identifies $\langle x_n, x\rangle \to \|x\|^2$ as weak convergence tested against the functional given by $x$.
rubric: Combines the limsup bound with this limit to obtain $\|x_n - x\| \to 0$.

@practice self
prompt: Prove that $c_0$ is not reflexive. You may use the standard isometric identifications $(c_0)^* \cong \ell^1$ and $(\ell^1)^* \cong \ell^\infty$ given by the pairings $\langle a, x\rangle = \sum a_nx_n$.
hint: Compute $Jx$ as a functional on $(c_0)^* = \ell^1$ and identify it with an element of $(\ell^1)^* = \ell^\infty$.
solution: Let $x \in c_0$. Under $(c_0)^* \cong \ell^1$, an element of $(c_0)^*$ is $\varphi_a(z) = \sum a_nz_n$ with $a \in \ell^1$. The element $Jx \in (c_0)^{**}$ acts by $(Jx)(\varphi_a) = \varphi_a(x) = \sum a_nx_n$. Under the identification $(\ell^1)^* \cong \ell^\infty$, an element $y \in \ell^\infty$ acts on $a \in \ell^1$ by $\sum a_ny_n$. So $Jx$ corresponds to the sequence $y = x \in \ell^\infty$. Hence the image of $J$ corresponds to $c_0 \subseteq \ell^\infty$. The constant sequence $(1,1,1,\dots)$ lies in $\ell^\infty$ but not in $c_0$, so it is an element of $(c_0)^{**}$ not in the range of $J$. Therefore $J$ is not onto and $c_0$ is not reflexive.

rubric: Identifies $(c_0)^{**}$ with $\ell^\infty$ and the image of $J$ with $c_0$.

answer: 2.3333
tol: 0.0001
check: sum((2.0 ** -(k - 1) + (1 / math.sqrt(60) if 60 < k <= 120 else 0.0)) ** 2 for k in range(1, 400))
hint: Show $v_n \rightharpoonup 0$ with $\|v_n\| = 1$ and expand $\|u + v_n\|^2$.

@quiz mc
prompt: In $\ell^2$ let $x_n = n\,e_n$. Which statement is correct?
choice*: No subsequence of $(x_n)$ converges weakly, because weakly convergent sequences are bounded and $\|x_n\| = n$.
choice: $x_n \rightharpoonup 0$, because for every fixed $k$ the $k$th coordinate of $x_n$ tends to $0$.
choice: $x_n \to 0$ in norm.
choice: $x_n \rightharpoonup 0$, because $\langle x_n, y\rangle = n\,\bar y_n \to 0$ for every $y \in \ell^2$.
hint: Choose $y = (1/k)_k$ and compute $\langle x_n, y\rangle$.
solution: Weakly convergent sequences are bounded (Theorem 1(a), a consequence of the uniform boundedness principle), and $\|x_n\| = n$ is not. Coordinatewise convergence to $0$ is not weak convergence: for $y_k = 1/k$ we get $\langle x_n, y\rangle = n\cdot\frac1n = 1 \not\to 0$. The same reasoning applies to every subsequence.
