@article
Lesson 2 left an awkward fact: the dual of $\ell^1$ is $\ell^\infty$, but the dual of $\ell^\infty$ is not $\ell^1$, and for general Banach spaces the dual is hard to describe. In a Hilbert space the dual is as simple as it could be: it is the space itself. Every continuous linear functional is "take the inner product with a fixed vector".

### The theorem

**Theorem 1 (Riesz representation theorem).** Let $H$ be a Hilbert space over $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$ and $\varphi \in H^*$. There is exactly one $y \in H$ with $\varphi(x) = \langle x, y\rangle$ for all $x \in H$, and $\|\varphi\| = \|y\|$.

*Proof.* Uniqueness: if $\langle x, y\rangle = \langle x, y'\rangle$ for all $x$, take $x = y - y'$ to get $\|y - y'\|^2 = 0$. Existence: if $\varphi = 0$ take $y = 0$. Otherwise $N = \ker\varphi$ is a closed proper subspace (Lesson 2: $\varphi$ is continuous). By the projection theorem $N^\perp \ne \{0\}$; pick $z \in N^\perp$ with $z \ne 0$, so $\varphi(z) \ne 0$ (else $z \in N \cap N^\perp$). For any $x \in H$, the vector $\varphi(x)z - \varphi(z)x$ is in $N$, hence orthogonal to $z$:
$$\varphi(x)\|z\|^2 - \varphi(z)\langle x, z\rangle = 0, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \varphi(x) = \Big\langle x,\ \frac{\overline{\varphi(z)}}{\|z\|^2}z\Big\rangle.$$
Take $y = \overline{\varphi(z)}\,z/\|z\|^2$. Norms: $|\varphi(x)| \le \|x\|\|y\|$ by Cauchy–Schwarz, so $\|\varphi\| \le \|y\|$; and $\varphi(y) = \|y\|^2$ gives $\|\varphi\| \ge \|y\|$ (if $y \ne 0$). $\blacksquare$

So $y \mapsto \langle\cdot, y\rangle$ is a bijective isometry from $H$ onto $H^*$; it is **conjugate-linear** in the complex case, because $\langle x, \lambda y\rangle = \bar\lambda\langle x, y\rangle$. The hypotheses matter: completeness is used to get the projection theorem. In $c_{00} \subseteq \ell^2$ the functional $\varphi(x) = \sum x_n/n$ is bounded, but the only candidate $y = (1/n)_n$ is not in $c_{00}$.

### Adjoints

Let $T \in B(H,K)$ for Hilbert spaces $H, K$. For $y \in K$ the map $x \mapsto \langle Tx, y\rangle_K$ is a bounded functional on $H$, so Theorem 1 gives a unique $T^*y \in H$ with $\langle Tx, y\rangle = \langle x, T^*y\rangle$ for all $x$.

**Theorem 2.** $T^*\colon K \to H$ is linear and bounded, $\|T^*\| = \|T\|$, $T^{**} = T$, $\|T^*T\| = \|T\|^2$, and
$$\ker T^* = (\operatorname{ran}T)^\perp, \qquad \overline{\operatorname{ran}T} = (\ker T^*)^\perp .$$

*Proof.* Linearity follows from uniqueness in Theorem 1. Boundedness: $\|T^*y\|^2 = \langle TT^*y, y\rangle \le \|T\|\|T^*y\|\|y\|$, so $\|T^*\| \le \|T\|$; applying this to $T^*$ and using $T^{**} = T$ (from $\langle T^*y, x\rangle = \overline{\langle x, T^*y\rangle} = \overline{\langle Tx, y\rangle} = \langle y, Tx\rangle$) gives equality. For the $C^*$-identity: $\|Tx\|^2 = \langle T^*Tx, x\rangle \le \|T^*T\|\|x\|^2$ gives $\|T\|^2 \le \|T^*T\|$, and $\|T^*T\| \le \|T^*\|\|T\| = \|T\|^2$. Finally $y \in \ker T^*$ iff $\langle x, T^*y\rangle = 0$ for all $x$ iff $\langle Tx, y\rangle = 0$ for all $x$ iff $y \perp \operatorname{ran}T$. Taking orthogonal complements and using $M^{\perp\perp} = \overline{M}$ (Lesson 3) gives the second identity. $\blacksquare$

The last identity is the Hilbert-space form of the Fredholm alternative: $Tx = y$ can be solved approximately if and only if $y \perp \ker T^*$. For the right shift $S(x_1, x_2, \dots) = (0, x_1, x_2, \dots)$ on $\ell^2$ one finds $S^* = $ the left shift $L$, and $\ker L = \operatorname{span}\{e_1\} = (\operatorname{ran}S)^\perp$.

### Other Riesz theorems (quoted)

*Stated without proof here, proved in measure theory (P1):* (i) Let $(X,\mu)$ be a measure space, $1 < p < \infty$ and $\tfrac1p + \tfrac1q = 1$. Then every $\varphi \in (L^p(\mu))^*$ is $f \mapsto \int fg\,d\mu$ for a unique $g \in L^q(\mu)$, with $\|\varphi\| = \|g\|_q$. The same holds for $p = 1$ if $\mu$ is $\sigma$-finite, with $g \in L^\infty$; it can fail for $p = 1$ without $\sigma$-finiteness. (ii) (Riesz–Markov) If $X$ is a locally compact Hausdorff space, every $\varphi \in C_0(X)^*$ is integration against a unique regular complex Borel measure $\mu$, with $\|\varphi\| = |\mu|(X)$, the total variation. The case $p = 2$ of (i) is Theorem 1 for $L^2$.

### The Lax–Milgram idea

The theorem is the engine of existence results for differential equations (Lesson 12). A bounded bilinear form $a(u,v)$ on a real Hilbert space defines an operator $A$ by $a(u,v) = \langle Au, v\rangle$ (apply Theorem 1 to $v \mapsto a(u,v)$), and solving $a(u,\cdot) = \varphi$ means solving $Au = f$ where $f$ represents $\varphi$. Whether $A$ is onto depends on the form: this is a practice problem.

@example
title: Representing a point evaluation
problem: Let $P_1$ be the polynomials of degree at most $1$ with the inner product of $L^2[0,1]$, and $\varphi(p) = p(1)$. Find $y \in P_1$ with $\varphi(p) = \langle p, y\rangle$ for all $p \in P_1$, and $\|\varphi\|$.
step: $P_1$ is finite-dimensional, hence a Hilbert space, so Theorem 1 applies with $y \in P_1$. Use an orthonormal basis $u_0 = 1$, $u_1 = \sqrt3(2t - 1)$ (check: $\int_0^1(2t-1)\,dt = 0$ and $\int_0^1 3(2t-1)^2\,dt = 1$).
step: For an orthonormal basis, $y = \sum_k\overline{\varphi(u_k)}\,u_k$: indeed $p = \sum\langle p, u_k\rangle u_k$, so $\varphi(p) = \sum\langle p, u_k\rangle\varphi(u_k) = \langle p, \sum\overline{\varphi(u_k)}u_k\rangle$.
step: Here $\varphi(u_0) = 1$ and $\varphi(u_1) = \sqrt3$, so $y(t) = 1 + \sqrt3\cdot\sqrt3(2t - 1) = 6t - 2$.
step: Check with $p = 1$ and $p = t$: $\int_0^1(6t - 2)\,dt = 1 = p(1)$ and $\int_0^1 t(6t - 2)\,dt = 2 - 1 = 1 = p(1)$. Then $\|\varphi\|^2 = \|y\|^2 = \int_0^1(6t-2)^2\,dt = 4$, or $\sum_k|\varphi(u_k)|^2 = 1 + 3 = 4$, so $\|\varphi\| = 2$.
answer: $y(t) = 6t - 2$ and $\|\varphi\| = 2$.

@example
title: The adjoint of the right shift
problem: On $\ell^2$ let $S(x_1, x_2, \dots) = (0, x_1, x_2, \dots)$. Find $S^*$ and verify $\ker S^* = (\operatorname{ran}S)^\perp$.
step: Compute $\langle Sx, y\rangle = \sum_{n\ge1}x_n\overline{y_{n+1}}$, since the $(n+1)$st coordinate of $Sx$ is $x_n$.
step: This equals $\langle x, Ly\rangle$ with $Ly = (y_2, y_3, \dots)$. By uniqueness in Theorem 1, $S^* = L$, the left shift.
step: $\ker L = \{y : y_2 = y_3 = \cdots = 0\} = \operatorname{span}\{e_1\}$, and $\operatorname{ran}S = \{x : x_1 = 0\}$ has orthogonal complement $\operatorname{span}\{e_1\}$. The two agree.
step: Note $LS = I$ but $SL \ne I$ (the vector $e_1$ is sent to $0$), so adjoints of one-sided inverses are one-sided inverses of the adjoint, and $\|S\| = \|L\| = 1$.
answer: $S^* = L$, the left shift, and $\ker L = (\operatorname{ran}S)^\perp = \operatorname{span}\{e_1\}$.

@mistakes
- Forgetting the conjugate: in a complex Hilbert space the functional $\lambda\varphi$ is represented by $\bar\lambda y$, not $\lambda y$.
- Applying the theorem to an incomplete inner product space such as $c_{00}$ or $C[0,1]$ with the $L^2$ inner product: bounded functionals may have no representing vector.
- Writing $\ker T^* = \operatorname{ran}T^\perp$ without closures: $(\ker T^*)^\perp$ is the closure of $\operatorname{ran}T$, which can be strictly larger than $\operatorname{ran}T$.
- Confusing $T^*T$ with $TT^*$: both have norm $\|T\|^2$ but they are different operators (for the shift, $S^*S = I$ and $SS^* \ne I$).

@practice self
prompt: Let $H$ be a real Hilbert space and $a\colon H \times H \to \mathbb{R}$ bilinear with $|a(u,v)| \le M\|u\|\|v\|$ and $a(u,u) \ge c\|u\|^2$ for some $M, c > 0$ and all $u, v$. Prove the Lax–Milgram theorem: for every $\varphi \in H^*$ there is a unique $u \in H$ with $a(u,v) = \varphi(v)$ for all $v \in H$, and $\|u\| \le \|\varphi\|/c$.
hint: Use Theorem 1 twice: once to define a bounded operator $A$ with $a(u,v) = \langle Au, v\rangle$, once to write $\varphi = \langle\cdot, f\rangle$.
hint: Show $\|Au\| \ge c\|u\|$, then prove $\operatorname{ran}A$ is closed and has trivial orthogonal complement.
solution: For fixed $u$, $v \mapsto a(u,v)$ is bounded with norm at most $M\|u\|$, so Theorem 1 gives $Au \in H$ with $a(u,v) = \langle Au, v\rangle$ and $\|Au\| \le M\|u\|$; $A$ is linear by uniqueness. Coercivity gives $c\|u\|^2 \le a(u,u) = \langle Au, u\rangle \le \|Au\|\|u\|$, hence $\|Au\| \ge c\|u\|$. Thus $A$ is injective. The range is closed: if $Au_n \to w$ then $\|u_n - u_m\| \le \|Au_n - Au_m\|/c$, so $(u_n)$ is Cauchy, converges to some $u$, and $Au = w$ by continuity. The range is dense: if $w \perp \operatorname{ran}A$ then $0 = \langle Aw, w\rangle = a(w,w) \ge c\|w\|^2$, so $w = 0$. A closed dense subspace is all of $H$, so $A$ is a bijection. Given $\varphi$, write $\varphi = \langle\cdot, f\rangle$ with $\|f\| = \|\varphi\|$ (Theorem 1) and let $u = A^{-1}f$: then $a(u,v) = \langle Au, v\rangle = \langle f, v\rangle = \varphi(v)$. Uniqueness follows from injectivity of $A$, and $c\|u\| \le \|Au\| = \|f\| = \|\varphi\|$.
rubric: Defines $A$ via the Riesz representation theorem and obtains $\|Au\| \ge c\|u\|$ from coercivity.
rubric: Proves $\operatorname{ran}A$ is closed (Cauchy argument) and dense (test with $w \perp \operatorname{ran}A$ and coercivity), concluding surjectivity.
rubric: Represents $\varphi$ by $f$ and solves $Au = f$, with uniqueness and the bound $\|u\| \le \|\varphi\|/c$.
