@article
Suppose each of infinitely many operators $T_i$ is bounded, and for each fixed $x$ the numbers $\|T_ix\|$ stay bounded as $i$ varies. Must the norms $\|T_i\|$ be bounded? In general there is no reason: a supremum over uncountably many $x$ could escape to infinity. The surprising answer is yes, provided the domain is complete, and the reason is a purely topological fact about complete metric spaces.

### The Baire category theorem

A subset $A$ of a metric space is **nowhere dense** if its closure has empty interior. A set is **meager** (first category) if it is a countable union of nowhere dense sets.

**Theorem 1 (Baire category theorem).** Let $(X,d)$ be a nonempty complete metric space and $U_1, U_2, \dots$ dense open subsets. Then $\bigcap_nU_n$ is dense in $X$. Equivalently, $X$ is not a countable union of closed sets with empty interior, and in particular $X$ is not meager in itself.

*Proof.* Let $W \subseteq X$ be nonempty and open; we find a point of $W \cap \bigcap_nU_n$. Since $U_1$ is dense, $W \cap U_1$ is nonempty open, so contains a closed ball $\overline B(x_1, r_1)$ with $r_1 < 1$. Inductively, $B(x_n, r_n) \cap U_{n+1}$ is nonempty and open, so contains a closed ball $\overline B(x_{n+1}, r_{n+1})$ with $r_{n+1} < 2^{-(n+1)}$. For $m \ge n$ all $x_m$ lie in $\overline B(x_n, r_n)$, so $d(x_m, x_k) \le 2r_n < 2^{1-n}$ for $m, k \ge n$: $(x_n)$ is Cauchy and converges to some $x$. Then $x \in \overline B(x_n, r_n)$ for every $n$, so $x \in W \cap U_n$ for all $n$. For the equivalent form, apply this to the complements $U_n = X \setminus A_n$ of closed sets $A_n$ with empty interior. $\blacksquare$

Completeness is needed: $\mathbb{Q}$ is the countable union of its points, each nowhere dense, and the dense open sets $\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{q\}$ have empty intersection.

### The uniform boundedness principle

**Theorem 2 (Banach–Steinhaus).** Let $X$ be a Banach space, $Y$ a normed space and $\mathcal{F} \subseteq B(X,Y)$ any family of bounded operators. If $\sup_{T \in \mathcal{F}}\|Tx\| < \infty$ for every $x \in X$, then $\sup_{T\in\mathcal{F}}\|T\| < \infty$.

*Proof.* For $n \in \mathbb{N}$ let $A_n = \{x \in X : \|Tx\| \le n \text{ for all } T \in \mathcal{F}\} = \bigcap_T\{x : \|Tx\| \le n\}$, a closed set. By hypothesis $X = \bigcup_nA_n$, so by Theorem 1 some $A_n$ has nonempty interior: there are $x_0$ and $r > 0$ with $\overline B(x_0, r) \subseteq A_n$. For $\|z\| \le 1$ and $T \in \mathcal{F}$, both $x_0$ and $x_0 + rz$ lie in $A_n$, so $\|rTz\| = \|T(x_0 + rz) - Tx_0\| \le 2n$. Hence $\|T\| \le 2n/r$ for all $T \in \mathcal{F}$. $\blacksquare$

The hypotheses are sharp. *Completeness of $X$:* on $c_{00}$ with the sup norm let $T_nx = nx_n$. For each $x$ the sequence $(T_nx)$ is eventually $0$, hence bounded, but $\|T_n\| = n$. *Linearity and boundedness of each $T$:* the principle is about families of bounded linear maps. Completeness of $Y$ is not needed.

### Convergent sums and duality

A typical use: if $\sum a_nx_n$ converges for all $x$ in a Banach space of sequences, then $a$ lies in the dual space. The partial sums $\varphi_N(x) = \sum_{n\le N}a_nx_n$ are bounded functionals, pointwise bounded because convergent sequences are bounded, so $\sup_N\|\varphi_N\| < \infty$. Computing $\|\varphi_N\|$ by Lessons 2 and 5 then bounds $a$.

### Fourier series of continuous functions can diverge

Let $C(\mathbb{T})$ be the continuous $2\pi$-periodic functions with the sup norm (a Banach space, as in Lesson 1). The $N$th partial sum at $0$ is $S_Nf(0) = \sum_{|n|\le N}\hat f(n) = \frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(t)D_N(t)\,dt$, with the Dirichlet kernel $D_N(t) = \sum_{|n|\le N}e^{int} = \dfrac{\sin((N + \tfrac12)t)}{\sin(t/2)}$. The functional $\varphi_N(f) = S_Nf(0)$ has norm equal to the **Lebesgue constant** $L_N = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|D_N|$ (the proof of Lesson 2, Theorem 3, with $f_\varepsilon = D_N/(|D_N| + \varepsilon)$, adapts verbatim).

**Lemma.** $L_N \ge \dfrac{4}{\pi^2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\dfrac1k$, so $L_N \to \infty$.

*Proof.* Since $\sin(t/2) \le t/2$ on $[0,\pi]$ and $D_N$ is even, $L_N = \frac1\pi\int_0^\pi|D_N| \ge \frac1\pi\int_0^\pi\frac{2|\sin((N+\frac12)t)|}{t}\,dt = \frac2\pi\int_0^{(N+\frac12)\pi}\frac{|\sin u|}{u}\,du$. On $[(k-1)\pi, k\pi]$ we have $1/u \ge 1/(k\pi)$ and $\int|\sin u|\,du = 2$, so the last integral is at least $\sum_{k=1}^N\frac{2}{k\pi}$. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary.** There is $f \in C(\mathbb{T})$ whose Fourier series diverges at $t = 0$ (the partial sums $S_Nf(0)$ are unbounded).

*Proof.* If $\sup_N|\varphi_N(f)| < \infty$ for every $f \in C(\mathbb{T})$, Theorem 2 would give $\sup_NL_N = \sup_N\|\varphi_N\| < \infty$, contradicting the Lemma. $\blacksquare$

A refinement (quoted): the set of such $f$ is a dense $G_\delta$, so divergence is the typical behavior in $C(\mathbb{T})$. Similarly, quoted without proof, the continuous functions that are differentiable at even one point form a meager subset of $C[0,1]$. Note the contrast with Lesson 4: for $f \in L^2$ the partial sums always converge in $L^2$.

```python
import math


def lebesgue_constant(n, steps=20000):
    """Midpoint-rule value of (1/pi) * integral_0^pi |D_n(t)| dt."""
    total = 0.0
    for i in range(steps):
        t = (i + 0.5) * math.pi / steps
        total += abs(math.sin((n + 0.5) * t) / math.sin(t / 2))
    return total / steps


print([round(lebesgue_constant(n), 3) for n in (1, 5, 20)])
```

The numbers grow like $\frac{4}{\pi^2}\log n$, slowly; numerics suggest the growth but only the argument above proves it.

@example
title: A series that converges against all of $\ell^2$
problem: Let $a = (a_n)$ be a real sequence such that $\sum_na_nx_n$ converges for every $x \in \ell^2$. Prove that $a \in \ell^2$.
step: Define $\varphi_N(x) = \sum_{n\le N}a_nx_n$ on $\ell^2$. Each $\varphi_N$ is a bounded linear functional, represented (Lesson 5) by $(a_1, \dots, a_N, 0, \dots)$, so $\|\varphi_N\| = (\sum_{n\le N}a_n^2)^{1/2}$.
step: For fixed $x$ the sequence $(\varphi_N(x))_N$ converges, hence is bounded. So the family $\{\varphi_N\}$ is pointwise bounded on the Banach space $\ell^2$.
step: By Theorem 2 there is $C$ with $\|\varphi_N\| \le C$ for all $N$, i.e. $\sum_{n\le N}a_n^2 \le C^2$ for all $N$.
step: Letting $N \to \infty$, $\sum a_n^2 \le C^2 < \infty$, so $a \in \ell^2$. $\blacksquare$
answer: $a \in \ell^2$, by the uniform boundedness principle.

@example
title: Why completeness of the domain cannot be dropped
problem: Show that the family $T_n\colon c_{00} \to \mathbb{R}$, $T_nx = nx_n$ (sup norm on $c_{00}$) is pointwise bounded but not uniformly bounded, and locate the failure in the proof of Theorem 2.
step: For $x \in c_{00}$ there is $K$ with $x_n = 0$ for $n > K$, so $\sup_n|T_nx| = \max_{n\le K}n|x_n| < \infty$. The family is pointwise bounded.
step: $\|T_n\| = \sup_{\|x\|_\infty \le 1}n|x_n| = n$, attained at $x = e_n \in c_{00}$. So $\sup_n\|T_n\| = \infty$.
step: In the proof, $A_k = \{x : n|x_n| \le k \text{ for all } n\} = \{x : |x_n| \le k/n\}$ is closed in $c_{00}$ and $c_{00} = \bigcup_kA_k$, but each $A_k$ has empty interior in $c_{00}$: a ball around $x \in A_k$ contains $x + \delta e_n$ with $n\delta > k$ for large $n$. Baire's theorem is unavailable because $c_{00}$ is meager in itself, being the union of the finite-dimensional (nowhere dense) subspaces $\operatorname{span}\{e_1, \dots, e_m\}$.
answer: The family is pointwise bounded with $\|T_n\| = n$; $c_{00}$ is not complete, so Baire's theorem does not apply.

@mistakes
- Applying the uniform boundedness principle to an incomplete domain, such as $c_{00}$ or the polynomials: the conclusion can fail.
- Proving only that each $\|T\|$ is finite. The theorem is about the supremum over the family, and it needs the *pointwise* bound for *every* $x$ in $X$ (or at least on a non-meager set).
- Concluding that a Fourier series diverges "everywhere" or for "most points" from $L_N \to \infty$: the argument gives divergence at one point for some continuous function.
- Thinking nowhere dense means "small in measure" or "countable": the Cantor set is uncountable and nowhere dense, and $\mathbb{Q}$ is countable but dense.

prompt: Prove that an infinite-dimensional Banach space $X$ has no countable Hamel basis (a Hamel basis is a basis in the algebraic sense: every vector is a unique finite linear combination). Give an infinite-dimensional normed space that does have a countable Hamel basis.
hint: Write $X$ as the union of the increasing finite-dimensional subspaces spanned by the first $n$ basis vectors.
hint: A proper subspace of a normed space has empty interior.
solution: Suppose $\{b_1, b_2, \dots\}$ is a Hamel basis and let $X_n = \operatorname{span}\{b_1, \dots, b_n\}$, so $X = \bigcup_nX_n$. Each $X_n$ is finite-dimensional, hence closed (Lesson 1, Theorem 3). It is a proper subspace because $X$ is infinite-dimensional. A proper subspace $Y$ has empty interior: if $B(y, r) \subseteq Y$ then for any $z$ with $\|z\| < r$ we get $z = (y + z) - y \in Y$, so $Y$ contains a ball around $0$, and then by scaling $Y = X$. Thus $X$ is a countable union of closed sets with empty interior, contradicting Baire's theorem for the complete space $X$. Example: $c_{00}$ with the sup norm has the countable Hamel basis $\{e_k\}$; by the result just proved it is not complete (consistent with Lesson 1).
rubric: Writes $X$ as a countable union of finite-dimensional subspaces $X_n$ and shows each is closed and proper.
rubric: Proves that a proper subspace has empty interior (scaling argument).
rubric: Applies Baire's theorem for the contradiction and gives $c_{00}$ as the example.

@practice self
prompt: Let $f_n\colon [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}$ be continuous functions converging pointwise to a function $f\colon [0,1]\to\mathbb{R}$ at every point. Prove that the set of points at which $f$ is continuous is dense in $[0,1]$ (it is in fact a dense $G_\delta$).
hint: For $\varepsilon > 0$ let $G_\varepsilon$ be the set of points having a neighborhood on which $f$ varies by at most $\varepsilon$. It is open; prove it is dense.
hint: Given an interval $J$, apply Baire's theorem to the closed sets $F_N = \{x \in J : |f_n(x) - f_m(x)| \le \varepsilon/4 \text{ for all } n, m \ge N\}$.
solution: Fix $\varepsilon > 0$. Let $G_\varepsilon$ be the set of $x \in [0,1]$ having an open (relative) neighborhood $I$ with $|f(y) - f(z)| \le \varepsilon$ for all $y, z \in I$; $G_\varepsilon$ is open by definition. Density: let $J$ be a nondegenerate closed interval in $[0,1]$. For $N \in \mathbb{N}$ put $F_N = \{x \in J : |f_n(x) - f_m(x)| \le \varepsilon/4 \ \forall n, m \ge N\}$, an intersection of closed sets because each $f_n$ is continuous. Since $(f_n(x))$ converges, it is Cauchy, so $J = \bigcup_NF_N$. $J$ is a complete metric space, so by Baire's theorem some $F_N$ has nonempty interior relative to $J$; since the interior of $J$ is dense in $J$, there is an open interval $I \subseteq F_N$. For $x \in I$, letting $m \to \infty$ in $|f_N(x) - f_m(x)| \le \varepsilon/4$ gives $|f_N(x) - f(x)| \le \varepsilon/4$. Choose $x_0 \in I$; by continuity of $f_N$ at $x_0$ there is an open interval $I' \ni x_0$, $I' \subseteq I$, with $|f_N(y) - f_N(z)| \le \varepsilon/4$ for $y, z \in I'$. For $y, z \in I'$: $|f(y) - f(z)| \le |f(y) - f_N(y)| + |f_N(y) - f_N(z)| + |f_N(z) - f(z)| \le 3\varepsilon/4 < \varepsilon$. So $I' \subseteq G_\varepsilon \cap J$, and $G_\varepsilon$ is dense. By Baire's theorem in $[0,1]$, $\bigcap_kG_{1/k}$ is dense, and $f$ is continuous at each of its points (for every $\varepsilon = 1/k$ the oscillation near $x$ is at most $\varepsilon$).
rubric: Defines the open sets $G_\varepsilon$ and reduces the claim to their density via the intersection $\bigcap_k G_{1/k}$.
rubric: Applies Baire's theorem to the closed sets $F_N$ in an interval to find a subinterval where all $f_n$ ($n \ge N$) are uniformly $\varepsilon/4$-close, and passes to the limit to compare $f$ with $f_N$.
rubric: Uses continuity of the single function $f_N$ and a three-term estimate to bound the variation of $f$ on a subinterval.
