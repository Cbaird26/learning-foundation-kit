@article
A bounded linear bijection between Banach spaces has a bounded inverse. This is not obvious: for a bijection between general normed spaces it is false, and the inverse of a continuous bijection of infinite-dimensional topological spaces is rarely continuous. In finite dimensions every linear map is bounded and the question never arises. Baire's theorem makes it true here, and the consequences (the closed graph theorem especially) are among the most efficient tools for proving that an operator is bounded without estimating anything.

### The open mapping theorem

**Theorem 1 (open mapping theorem).** Let $X, Y$ be Banach spaces and $T \in B(X,Y)$ surjective. Then $T$ is an open map: $T(U)$ is open in $Y$ for every open $U \subseteq X$.

*Proof.* Write $B_r$ for the open ball of radius $r$ about $0$ in $X$ and $V_r$ for that in $Y$.

*Step 1: $\overline{T(B_1)}$ contains a ball $V_\delta$.* Since $T$ is onto, $Y = \bigcup_nT(B_n) = \bigcup_n n\,T(B_1)$, so $Y = \bigcup_n\overline{n\,T(B_1)}$. By Baire's theorem some closed set $n\overline{T(B_1)}$ has nonempty interior, hence so does $\overline{T(B_1)}$: it contains a ball $B(y_0, \varepsilon)$. The set $K = \overline{T(B_1)}$ is symmetric and convex, so for $\|y\| < \varepsilon$ both $y_0 + y$ and $y_0$ lie in $K$, and $y = (y_0 + y) - y_0 \in K - K \subseteq 2K$ (for a convex symmetric $K$, $K - K \subseteq 2K$). Therefore $V_{\varepsilon/2} \subseteq K$; put $\delta = \varepsilon/2$.

*Step 2: $T(B_1) \supseteq V_{\delta/2}$.* Scaling Step 1: $V_{\delta/2^k} \subseteq \overline{T(B_{2^{-k}})}$ for each $k \ge 0$. Let $\|y\| < \delta/2$. Since $y \in \overline{T(B_{1/2})}$, choose $x_1 \in B_{1/2}$ with $\|y - Tx_1\| < \delta/4$. Then $y - Tx_1 \in V_{\delta/4} \subseteq \overline{T(B_{1/4})}$, so choose $x_2 \in B_{1/4}$ with $\|y - Tx_1 - Tx_2\| < \delta/8$, and so on: $x_k \in B_{2^{-k}}$ and $\|y - T(x_1 + \dots + x_k)\| < \delta 2^{-k-1}$. Since $\sum\|x_k\| < 1$ and $X$ is complete, $x = \sum x_k$ converges with $\|x\| < 1$, and by continuity $Tx = y$. This is where completeness of $X$ is used (Step 1 used completeness of $Y$).

*Step 3.* If $U$ is open and $y = Tx$ with $x \in U$, choose $r$ with $x + B_r \subseteq U$; then $T(U) \supseteq y + rT(B_1) \supseteq y + V_{r\delta/2}$. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary 2 (bounded inverse theorem).** If $X, Y$ are Banach and $T \in B(X,Y)$ is a bijection, then $T^{-1}$ is bounded.

*Proof.* $T^{-1}$ is continuous because $(T^{-1})^{-1}(U) = T(U)$ is open for open $U$ by Theorem 1. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary 3 (comparison of norms).** If a vector space $X$ is complete for two norms $\|\cdot\|_1$ and $\|\cdot\|_2$ and $\|x\|_1 \le C\|x\|_2$ for all $x$, then the norms are equivalent.

*Proof.* The identity map $(X, \|\cdot\|_2) \to (X, \|\cdot\|_1)$ is a bounded bijection between Banach spaces; its inverse is bounded by Corollary 2. $\blacksquare$

### The closed graph theorem

The **graph** of a linear map $T\colon X \to Y$ is $\Gamma(T) = \{(x, Tx)\} \subseteq X \times Y$. It is closed exactly when: $x_n \to x$ and $Tx_n \to y$ imply $y = Tx$.

**Theorem 4 (closed graph theorem).** Let $X, Y$ be Banach spaces and $T\colon X \to Y$ linear, defined on all of $X$, with closed graph. Then $T$ is bounded.

*Proof.* The space $X \times Y$ with $\|(x,y)\| = \|x\| + \|y\|$ is a Banach space, and $\Gamma(T)$ is a closed subspace, hence a Banach space. The projection $\pi\colon \Gamma(T) \to X$, $(x, Tx) \mapsto x$, is linear, bounded (norm $\le1$) and bijective. By Corollary 2 its inverse is bounded: $\|x\| + \|Tx\| \le C\|x\|$ for all $x$. In particular $\|Tx\| \le C\|x\|$. $\blacksquare$

To prove that $T$ is bounded it suffices to check the closed graph condition, where one may *assume* that $x_n \to x$ and that $Tx_n$ converges. That extra convergence is the gain over a direct estimate.

### Hypotheses and counterexamples

- *Completeness of the codomain:* the identity $(C[0,1], \|\cdot\|_\infty) \to (C[0,1], \|\cdot\|_1)$ is a bounded bijection, but its inverse is unbounded (Lesson 1), so Corollary 2 fails when $Y$ is not complete.
- *Completeness of the domain in the closed graph theorem:* $D\colon (C^1[0,1], \|\cdot\|_\infty) \to (C[0,1], \|\cdot\|_\infty)$, $Df = f'$, has closed graph (if $f_n \to f$ and $f_n' \to g$ uniformly then $f \in C^1$ and $f' = g$ by Lesson 1) but is unbounded; the domain is not complete.
- *Everywhere defined:* a closed operator with a proper dense domain, like this $D$, may be unbounded. The closed graph theorem is about operators defined on all of $X$.

### Finite-dimensional preview

For a matrix $A$, the largest $r$ with $B(0,r) \subseteq A(B(0,1))$ is the smallest singular value $\sigma_{\min}(A) = 1/\|A^{-1}\|$ when $A$ is invertible: the quantitative content of the open mapping theorem. In infinite dimensions the best constant is $1/\|T^{-1}\|$ whenever $T$ is a bijection of Banach spaces.

@example
title: Hellinger–Toeplitz via the closed graph theorem
problem: Let $H$ be a Hilbert space and $T\colon H \to H$ a linear map defined on all of $H$ with $\langle Tx, y\rangle = \langle x, Ty\rangle$ for all $x, y$. Prove that $T$ is bounded.
step: $H$ is a Banach space and $T$ is everywhere defined, so by Theorem 4 it suffices to show that $\Gamma(T)$ is closed.
step: Let $x_n \to x$ and $Tx_n \to y$. For any $z \in H$, $\langle y, z\rangle = \lim\langle Tx_n, z\rangle$ by continuity of the inner product.
step: By symmetry, $\langle Tx_n, z\rangle = \langle x_n, Tz\rangle \to \langle x, Tz\rangle = \langle Tx, z\rangle$.
step: Hence $\langle y - Tx, z\rangle = 0$ for all $z$; take $z = y - Tx$ to get $y = Tx$. The graph is closed, so $T$ is bounded. $\blacksquare$
answer: A symmetric everywhere-defined operator on a Hilbert space is automatically bounded.

@example
title: Algebraic complements that are closed have bounded projections
problem: Let $X$ be a Banach space with closed subspaces $M, N$ such that every $x$ is uniquely $x = m + n$ with $m \in M$, $n \in N$. Show that the projection $Px = m$ is bounded.
step: $P$ is linear by uniqueness of the decomposition, defined on all of $X$. Check the closed graph condition.
step: Let $x_k \to x$ and $Px_k \to y$. Since $Px_k \in M$ and $M$ is closed, $y \in M$.
step: Also $x_k - Px_k \in N$ and $x_k - Px_k \to x - y$; since $N$ is closed, $x - y \in N$.
step: So $x = y + (x - y)$ with $y \in M$ and $x - y \in N$; by uniqueness $Px = y$. The graph is closed and $P$ is bounded. $\blacksquare$
answer: $P$ is bounded; so there is $C$ with $\|m\| \le C\|m + n\|$ for $m \in M$, $n \in N$.

@mistakes
- Quoting the bounded inverse theorem for normed spaces that are not both complete: it fails for $(C[0,1],\|\cdot\|_\infty) \to (C[0,1],\|\cdot\|_1)$.
- Claiming a closed operator is bounded without checking that it is defined on all of the Banach space $X$.
- In the closed graph check, forgetting to assume both $x_n \to x$ and $Tx_n \to y$ (assuming only $x_n \to x$ is circular, since it asks for $Tx_n$ to converge).
- Confusing "open map" with "continuous": the open mapping theorem says surjective bounded operators between Banach spaces are open.

@practice num
prompt: Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$ act on $\mathbb{R}^2$ with the Euclidean norm. Find the largest $r$ such that the open ball $\{y : \|y\| < r\}$ is contained in $A(\{x : \|x\| < 1\})$. Give $r$ to four decimal places.
answer: 0.4142
tol: 0.0001
check: 1 / np.linalg.norm(np.linalg.inv(np.array([[1.0, 2.0], [0.0, 1.0]])), 2)
hint: $y \in A(B_1)$ exactly when $\|A^{-1}y\| < 1$. How small can $\|y\|$ be if $\|A^{-1}y\| = 1$?
hint: The answer is $1/\|A^{-1}\|$, the smallest singular value of $A$.
solution: $y \in A(B_1)$ iff $\|A^{-1}y\| < 1$. The ball $\{\|y\| < r\}$ lies in $A(B_1)$ iff $\|A^{-1}y\| < 1$ whenever $\|y\| < r$, iff $r\|A^{-1}\| \le 1$. So $r = 1/\|A^{-1}\|$. Here $A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & -2\\0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$, and $\|A^{-1}\|$ is its largest singular value, the square root of the largest eigenvalue $3 + 2\sqrt2$ of $(A^{-1})^\top A^{-1}$. So $r = 1/\sqrt{3 + 2\sqrt2} = 1/(1 + \sqrt2) = \sqrt2 - 1 \approx 0.4142$.

@practice self
prompt: Let $X, Y$ be Banach spaces and $T \in B(X,Y)$. Prove that the following are equivalent: (i) $T$ is injective and $\operatorname{ran}T$ is closed; (ii) there is $c > 0$ with $\|Tx\| \ge c\|x\|$ for all $x \in X$.
hint: For (i)$\Rightarrow$(ii) apply the bounded inverse theorem to $T\colon X \to \operatorname{ran}T$.
hint: For (ii)$\Rightarrow$(i) take $Tx_n \to y$ and show $(x_n)$ is Cauchy.
solution: (i)$\Rightarrow$(ii): $\operatorname{ran}T$ is a closed subspace of the Banach space $Y$, hence a Banach space, and $T\colon X \to \operatorname{ran}T$ is a bounded bijection. By Corollary 2 its inverse $S\colon \operatorname{ran}T \to X$ is bounded. For $x \in X$, $\|x\| = \|S(Tx)\| \le \|S\|\|Tx\|$, so (ii) holds with $c = 1/\|S\|$ (if $X = \{0\}$ there is nothing to prove). (ii)$\Rightarrow$(i): if $Tx = 0$ then $c\|x\| \le 0$, so $x = 0$ and $T$ is injective. Let $Tx_n \to y$ in $Y$. Then $c\|x_n - x_m\| \le \|Tx_n - Tx_m\|$, so $(x_n)$ is Cauchy and converges to some $x \in X$ because $X$ is complete; by continuity $Tx = \lim Tx_n = y$, so $y \in \operatorname{ran}T$ and the range is closed.
rubric: Identifies $\operatorname{ran}T$ as a Banach space and applies the bounded inverse theorem to the bijection onto it.
rubric: Converts the bound on $T^{-1}$ into the lower bound $\|Tx\| \ge c\|x\|$ with an explicit constant.
rubric: Proves the converse with the Cauchy argument, using completeness of $X$, and notes injectivity.

@practice self
prompt: Let $X, Y$ be Banach spaces and $T\colon X \to Y$ a linear map (not assumed bounded) such that $\psi \circ T \in X^*$ for every $\psi \in Y^*$. Prove that $T$ is bounded.
hint: Prove that the graph is closed: if $x_n \to x$ and $Tx_n \to y$, compare $\psi(y)$ and $\psi(Tx)$ for every $\psi \in Y^*$.
hint: Use the Hahn–Banach theorem to conclude that two vectors with equal values under all functionals are equal.
solution: Apply the closed graph theorem (Theorem 4); $T$ is defined on all of the Banach space $X$. Let $x_n \to x$ in $X$ and $Tx_n \to y$ in $Y$. Fix $\psi \in Y^*$. By continuity of $\psi$, $\psi(Tx_n) \to \psi(y)$. By hypothesis $\psi \circ T$ is continuous on $X$, so $\psi(Tx_n) = (\psi\circ T)(x_n) \to (\psi \circ T)(x) = \psi(Tx)$. Hence $\psi(y) = \psi(Tx)$ for all $\psi \in Y^*$, i.e. $\psi(y - Tx) = 0$ for every $\psi$. By Corollary 3(c) of Lesson 6 (the dual separates points), $y - Tx = 0$. So the graph is closed and $T$ is bounded.
rubric: Reduces boundedness to closedness of the graph and sets up the sequence correctly with both $x_n \to x$ and $Tx_n \to y$.
rubric: Uses the hypothesis to pass to the limit in $\psi(Tx_n)$ along $x_n \to x$, comparing with the limit along $Tx_n \to y$.
rubric: Invokes the Hahn–Banach separation of points to conclude $y = Tx$.
