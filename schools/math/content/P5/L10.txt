@article
Finite matrices are compact in the sense that matters: they map bounded sets to bounded sets in a finite-dimensional space, which are relatively compact. Compact operators are the infinite-dimensional operators that still behave like matrices. Most integral operators, the inverses of differential operators, and the operators in spectral problems are compact, and for them the eigenvalue theory (next lesson) is nearly as clean as for matrices.

### Definition and basic properties

Let $X, Y$ be normed spaces. A linear map $T\colon X \to Y$ is **compact** if $T(B_X)$ is relatively compact (has compact closure) in $Y$, where $B_X$ is the closed unit ball. Equivalently, for every bounded sequence $(x_n)$ the sequence $(Tx_n)$ has a convergent subsequence. A compact operator is bounded. Write $K(X,Y)$ for the set of compact operators.

**Theorem 1.** (a) A bounded operator of finite rank is compact. (b) If $Y$ is a Banach space, $K(X,Y)$ is a closed subspace of $B(X,Y)$: a norm limit of compact operators is compact. (c) If $T$ is compact, $S \in B(W,X)$ and $R \in B(Y,Z)$ then $RTS$ is compact.

*Proof.* (a) The image of the ball is a bounded subset of a finite-dimensional space, hence relatively compact. (b) Let $T_n \to T$ in norm with $T_n$ compact. Given $\varepsilon > 0$ choose $n$ with $\|T - T_n\| < \varepsilon/3$ and a finite $\varepsilon/3$-net $y_1, \dots, y_k$ for $T_n(B_X)$ (total boundedness). For $x \in B_X$ pick $y_i$ with $\|T_nx - y_i\| < \varepsilon/3$; then $\|Tx - y_i\| < 2\varepsilon/3 < \varepsilon$. So $T(B_X)$ is totally bounded, and since $Y$ is complete its closure is compact. (c) A bounded sequence $(x_n)$ in $W$ gives a bounded sequence $(Sx_n)$; a subsequence of $(TSx_n)$ converges and $R$ is continuous. $\blacksquare$

**Lemma 2 (Riesz's lemma).** Let $M$ be a proper closed subspace of a normed space $X$ and $0 < \theta < 1$. There is $u \in X$ with $\|u\| = 1$ and $\operatorname{dist}(u, M) > \theta$.

*Proof.* Pick $x \notin M$; then $d = \operatorname{dist}(x, M) > 0$. Choose $m \in M$ with $\|x - m\| < d/\theta$ and put $u = (x - m)/\|x - m\|$. For $m' \in M$, $\|u - m'\| = \|x - m - \|x-m\|m'\|/\|x - m\| \ge d/\|x - m\| > \theta$. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary 3.** The identity on a normed space $X$ is compact if and only if $\dim X < \infty$. (If $\dim X = \infty$ use Lemma 2 repeatedly: given $x_1, \dots, x_n$ of norm $1$, the span is a proper closed subspace, so there is a unit vector $x_{n+1}$ at distance $> \tfrac12$ from it. The sequence has $\|x_n - x_m\| > \tfrac12$, so no subsequence converges.)

### Compact operators on Hilbert space

**Theorem 4.** Let $H$ be a Hilbert space and $T \in B(H)$. (a) $T$ is compact if and only if $T$ is a norm limit of finite-rank operators. (b) If $T$ is compact then $T^*$ is compact. (c) $T$ is compact if and only if $x_n \rightharpoonup x$ implies $Tx_n \to Tx$ in norm.

*Proof.* (a) ($\Leftarrow$) Theorem 1(a),(b). ($\Rightarrow$) Given $\varepsilon > 0$, the set $T(B_H)$ has a finite $\varepsilon$-net $y_1, \dots, y_k$. Let $P$ be the orthogonal projection onto $\operatorname{span}\{y_i\}$. For $x \in B_H$ pick $y_i$ with $\|Tx - y_i\| < \varepsilon$; since $PTx$ is the nearest point of the span to $Tx$, $\|Tx - PTx\| \le \|Tx - y_i\| < \varepsilon$. So $\|T - PT\| \le \varepsilon$ with $PT$ of finite rank. (b) If $\|T - F_n\| \to 0$ with $F_n$ of finite rank then $F_n^*$ has finite rank (its range lies in $(\ker F_n)^\perp$, which has dimension $\operatorname{rank}F_n$) and $\|T^* - F_n^*\| = \|T - F_n\| \to 0$ (Lesson 5). (c) ($\Rightarrow$) Let $x_n \rightharpoonup x$; then $(x_n)$ is bounded and $Tx_n \rightharpoonup Tx$ because $\langle Tx_n, z\rangle = \langle x_n, T^*z\rangle \to \langle x, T^*z\rangle$. If $Tx_n \not\to Tx$, some subsequence has $\|Tx_{n_k} - Tx\| \ge \varepsilon$; by compactness a further subsequence converges in norm, necessarily to its weak limit $Tx$, a contradiction. ($\Leftarrow$) A bounded sequence has a weakly convergent subsequence (Lesson 9, Theorem 2), whose image converges in norm. $\blacksquare$

### Integral operators

**Theorem 5 (Hilbert–Schmidt kernels).** Let $K \in L^2([0,1]^2)$ and $(T_Kf)(x) = \int_0^1K(x,y)f(y)\,dy$. Then $T_K$ is a bounded operator on $L^2[0,1]$ with $\|T_K\| \le \|K\|_{L^2([0,1]^2)}$, and $T_K$ is compact.

*Proof.* By Fubini, for almost every $x$ the function $K(x,\cdot)$ is in $L^2$, and by Cauchy–Schwarz $|T_Kf(x)|^2 \le \int|K(x,y)|^2dy\,\|f\|_2^2$; integrating over $x$ gives $\|T_Kf\|_2 \le \|K\|_2\|f\|_2$. For compactness: finite sums $K_n(x,y) = \sum_ia_i(x)b_i(y)$ with $a_i, b_i \in L^2[0,1]$ are dense in $L^2([0,1]^2)$ (quoted from P1: the indicator functions of measurable rectangles span a dense subspace of $L^2$ of the product measure). Choose $K_n \to K$ in $L^2$; each $T_{K_n}$ has range in $\operatorname{span}\{a_i\}$, so finite rank, and $\|T_K - T_{K_n}\| = \|T_{K - K_n}\| \le \|K - K_n\|_2 \to 0$. By Theorem 1(b), $T_K$ is compact. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 6 (continuous kernels).** If $K$ is continuous on $[0,1]^2$, the operator $T_K$ is compact on $(C[0,1], \|\cdot\|_\infty)$ (practice problem).

### Diagonal operators and shifts

The diagonal operator $D = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_n)$ on $\ell^2$ with $\lambda_n \to 0$ is compact (norm limit of the truncations); if $\lambda_n \not\to 0$ it is not compact (Example 1). The shifts are not compact: $\|Se_n - Se_m\| = \sqrt2$.

```python
def kernel_matrix(K, N):
    """Midpoint discretization of the integral operator with kernel K.

    A[i][j] = K(x_i, x_j) / N, x_i = (i + 1/2) / N.
    """
    xs = [(i + 0.5) / N for i in range(N)]
    return [[K(x, y) / N for y in xs] for x in xs]
```

Applying the matrix to the sampled values of $f$ approximates $T_Kf$ at the nodes. This discretization (the Nyström method) is the basis of the numerical project in Lesson 12; it is a computation, not a proof of compactness.

@example
title: A diagonal operator is compact if and only if $\lambda_n \to 0$
problem: Let $D e_n = \lambda_ne_n$ on $\ell^2$ with bounded $(\lambda_n)$. Prove that $D$ is compact if and only if $\lambda_n \to 0$.
step: ($\Leftarrow$) Let $D_N$ be the diagonal operator with entries $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_N, 0, 0, \dots$, which has finite rank.
step: For $x \in \ell^2$, $\|(D - D_N)x\|^2 = \sum_{n>N}|\lambda_nx_n|^2 \le \sup_{n>N}|\lambda_n|^2\|x\|^2$, so $\|D - D_N\| \le \sup_{n>N}|\lambda_n| \to 0$.
step: By Theorem 1(a),(b), $D$ is compact.
step: ($\Rightarrow$) Suppose $\lambda_n \not\to 0$: there are $\varepsilon > 0$ and indices $n_1 < n_2 < \cdots$ with $|\lambda_{n_k}| \ge \varepsilon$.
step: The bounded sequence $(e_{n_k})$ has $\|De_{n_k} - De_{n_j}\|^2 = |\lambda_{n_k}|^2 + |\lambda_{n_j}|^2 \ge 2\varepsilon^2$ for $j \ne k$. So $(De_{n_k})$ has no Cauchy subsequence, hence no convergent subsequence, and $D$ is not compact. $\blacksquare$
answer: $D$ is compact if and only if $\lambda_n \to 0$.

@example
title: The kernel $\min(x,y)$ is Hilbert–Schmidt
problem: Show that $(Tf)(x) = \int_0^1\min(x,y)f(y)\,dy$ is compact on $L^2[0,1]$ and bound its norm.
step: The kernel $K(x,y) = \min(x,y)$ is continuous and bounded by $1$ on $[0,1]^2$, hence in $L^2([0,1]^2)$, so Theorem 5 applies and $T$ is compact.
step: Compute $\|K\|_2^2 = \int_0^1\int_0^1\min(x,y)^2\,dy\,dx = 2\int_0^1\int_0^xy^2\,dy\,dx = 2\int_0^1\frac{x^3}{3}\,dx = \frac16$ (by symmetry the region $y < x$ is half of the square).
step: Hence $\|T\| \le 1/\sqrt6 \approx 0.408$.
step: The true norm is $4/\pi^2 \approx 0.405$ (Lesson 11): the Hilbert–Schmidt bound is close but not equal.
answer: $T$ is compact with $\|T\| \le 1/\sqrt6$.

@mistakes
- Thinking "bounded on bounded sets implies compact": the identity is bounded on bounded sets and is not compact when $\dim X = \infty$.
- Using strong (pointwise) limits: $P_N \to I$ strongly on $\ell^2$ with each $P_N$ of finite rank, yet $I$ is not compact. The limit must be in operator norm.
- Believing the Hilbert–Schmidt condition $\sum\|Te_n\|^2 < \infty$ is necessary for compactness: $D = \operatorname{diag}(1/\sqrt n)$ is compact (its entries tend to $0$) but $\sum\|De_n\|^2 = \sum 1/n = \infty$.
- Forgetting that Theorem 1(b) needs a Banach codomain $Y$ so that totally bounded sets have compact closure.

@practice self
prompt: Let $H$ be a separable Hilbert space with orthonormal basis $(e_n)$, and let $T \in B(H)$ satisfy $\sum_n\|Te_n\|^2 < \infty$ (a Hilbert–Schmidt operator). Prove that $T$ is compact.
hint: Let $P_N$ be the projection onto $\operatorname{span}\{e_1, \dots, e_N\}$. Estimate $\|T - TP_N\|$ using Cauchy–Schwarz.
hint: Write $(I - P_N)x = \sum_{n>N}\langle x, e_n\rangle e_n$ and apply $T$ termwise, which is allowed by continuity.
solution: $TP_N$ has finite rank. For $x \in H$, $(I - P_N)x = \sum_{n>N}x_ne_n$ with $x_n = \langle x, e_n\rangle$, converging in $H$; by continuity of $T$, $T(I - P_N)x = \sum_{n>N}x_nTe_n$. Then $\|T(I - P_N)x\| \le \sum_{n>N}|x_n|\|Te_n\| \le \Big(\sum_{n>N}|x_n|^2\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\sum_{n>N}\|Te_n\|^2\Big)^{1/2} \le \|x\|\Big(\sum_{n>N}\|Te_n\|^2\Big)^{1/2}$. Hence $\|T - TP_N\| \le (\sum_{n>N}\|Te_n\|^2)^{1/2}$, the tail of a convergent series, which tends to $0$. So $T$ is a norm limit of finite-rank operators and is compact by Theorem 4(a) (or Theorem 1(a),(b)).
rubric: Approximates $T$ by the finite-rank operators $TP_N$.
rubric: Bounds $\|T(I-P_N)x\|$ via the triangle inequality (justified by continuity of $T$) and Cauchy–Schwarz, obtaining the tail of the series.
rubric: Concludes compactness from norm convergence of finite-rank operators.

@practice self
prompt: Let $K\colon [0,1]^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ be continuous and $(Tf)(x) = \int_0^1K(x,y)f(y)\,dy$ for $f \in C[0,1]$. Prove that $T$ is a compact operator on $(C[0,1], \|\cdot\|_\infty)$ using the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem.
hint: Show that $T(B)$ is uniformly bounded and equicontinuous when $B$ is the unit ball of $C[0,1]$.
hint: Use uniform continuity of $K$ on the compact square to get one $\delta$ that works for all $f \in B$.
solution: Let $B = \{f : \|f\|_\infty \le 1\}$ and $M = \max|K|$. For $f \in B$, $Tf$ is continuous (by uniform continuity of $K$, as below) and $|Tf(x)| \le \int|K(x,y)||f(y)|\,dy \le M$, so $T(B)$ is uniformly bounded. Given $\varepsilon > 0$, uniform continuity of $K$ on $[0,1]^2$ gives $\delta > 0$ with $|K(x,y) - K(x',y)| < \varepsilon$ whenever $|x - x'| < \delta$ (for all $y$). Then for $f \in B$ and $|x - x'| < \delta$: $|Tf(x) - Tf(x')| \le \int|K(x,y) - K(x',y)||f(y)|\,dy \le \varepsilon$. So $T(B)$ is equicontinuous. By the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem, a uniformly bounded equicontinuous subset of $C[0,1]$ is relatively compact. Hence $T(B)$ is relatively compact and $T$ is compact.
rubric: Proves uniform boundedness of $T(B)$ by $\max|K|$.
rubric: Proves equicontinuity with a single $\delta$ independent of $f \in B$, using uniform continuity of $K$.
rubric: Cites the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem with its hypotheses (compact domain, uniformly bounded and equicontinuous family) and concludes.

model:
```python
def hs_estimate(K, N):
    """Midpoint-rule estimate of the squared L2([0,1]^2) norm of K."""
    nodes = [(i + 0.5) / N for i in range(N)]
    total = 0.0
    for x in nodes:
        for y in nodes:
            total += K(x, y) ** 2
    return total / (N * N)
```
hint: Two nested loops over the nodes; square the kernel value, then divide by $N^2$ (each cell has area $1/N^2$).
solution: Each grid cell has area $1/N^2$, so the double integral is approximated by the average of $K(x_i, x_j)^2$ over all $N^2$ nodes. Forgetting to square $K$ returns $0.25$ for `x * y` with $N=1$; dividing by $N$ instead of $N^2$ is the other typical slip. As a check on a computer, the estimate converges to $\|K\|_2^2$, an upper bound for $\|T_K\|^2$; it proves nothing about compactness, which is a theorem about the kernel.
