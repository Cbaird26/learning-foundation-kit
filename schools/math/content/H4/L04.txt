@article
A **rational function** is a quotient of polynomials, $r(x) = \dfrac{p(x)}{q(x)}$ with $q$ not identically zero. Everything interesting happens in two places: where $q(x) = 0$, and at the far ends of the graph.

### Step 1: factor completely

Factor numerator and denominator. Cancel **common factors**, not common terms. The factored form tells you almost everything.

- **Domain:** all real numbers except the zeros of the *original* denominator.
- **Hole:** if a factor $(x - c)$ cancels, the graph has a hole at $x = c$. Its height is the value of the *simplified* formula at $c$.
- **Vertical asymptote:** a zero of the denominator that survives cancellation. Near it, $|r(x)|$ grows without bound.
- **$x$-intercepts:** zeros of the numerator that survive cancellation.
- **$y$-intercept:** $r(0)$, if $0$ is in the domain.

### Behavior near a vertical asymptote

Look at the sign of $r$ on each side of $x = c$. If the surviving factor $(x - c)$ has **odd** power, the sign flips across the asymptote (one side goes to $+\infty$, the other to $-\infty$). If the power is **even**, both sides go the same way.

### Step 2: the ends

Let $m$ be the degree of the numerator and $n$ the degree of the denominator.

| Comparison | End behavior |
|---|---|
| $m < n$ | horizontal asymptote $y = 0$ |
| $m = n$ | horizontal asymptote $y = \dfrac{\text{leading coefficient of } p}{\text{leading coefficient of } q}$ |
| $m = n + 1$ | slant asymptote: the quotient (a line) from polynomial division |
| $m > n + 1$ | no horizontal or slant asymptote |

For example, $\dfrac{x^2 + 1}{x - 1} = x + 1 + \dfrac{2}{x - 1}$. The remainder term $\frac{2}{x-1}$ shrinks to $0$ at the ends, so the graph hugs the line $y = x + 1$.

### A horizontal asymptote is about the ends only

A graph may cross its horizontal asymptote in the middle. To test, solve $r(x) = y_0$ where $y_0$ is the asymptote's height. A solution means the graph meets the asymptote there; it usually crosses, though it can also just touch it.

### Analyze by hand, then check with a tool

This block's deliverable is the habit of working **without a graphing tool first**: domain, intercepts, holes, asymptotes and a sign chart, then a sketch. Only then draw it with technology and compare. Disagreements teach you the most.

@example
title: Hole, asymptotes and intercepts
problem: Analyze $r(x) = \dfrac{2(x-1)(x+2)}{(x+2)(x-3)}$.
step: The common factor $(x+2)$ cancels. There is a hole at $x = -2$. The simplified formula is $\dfrac{2(x-1)}{x-3}$, so the hole sits at height $\dfrac{2(-3)}{-5} = \dfrac{6}{5}$.
step: The factor $(x - 3)$ survives in the denominator, so $x = 3$ is a vertical asymptote. The domain excludes $-2$ and $3$.
step: The surviving numerator factor $(x-1)$ gives the only $x$-intercept, $x = 1$. The $y$-intercept is $r(0) = \dfrac{2(-1)}{-3} = \dfrac{2}{3}$.
step: Degrees are equal ($1$ and $1$ after canceling), and the leading coefficients give $2/1$, so $y = 2$ is the horizontal asymptote.
step: Near $x = 3$: just right of $3$, the simplified value is $\frac{2(2)}{\text{small positive}} \to +\infty$; just left of $3$, it goes to $-\infty$. The sign flips because the power of $(x-3)$ is odd.

@example
title: A slant asymptote
problem: Find the asymptotes of $r(x) = \dfrac{x^2 + 1}{x - 1}$.
step: The denominator is zero at $x = 1$ and the numerator is $2$ there, so there is no cancellation. The line $x = 1$ is a vertical asymptote.
step: The numerator degree $2$ is one more than the denominator degree $1$, so there is a slant asymptote.
step: Divide: $x^2 + 1 = (x - 1)(x + 1) + 2$, so $r(x) = x + 1 + \dfrac{2}{x-1}$.
step: The slant asymptote is $y = x + 1$.

@mistakes
- Canceling terms instead of factors. For example $\dfrac{x^2 + 3}{x}$ is not $x + 3$.
- Calling every zero of the denominator a vertical asymptote. If the same factor cancels, it is a hole.
- Believing a graph can never cross its horizontal asymptote. The asymptote describes only the far ends.
- Using the ratio of leading coefficients when the degrees differ.

vars: x

answer: 2x + 1
check: lambda x: np.polyval(np.polydiv([2, 3, -1], [1, 1])[0], x)

@quiz num
prompt: The graph of $r(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - 4x + 3}{x^2 - 1}$ has a hole. Find the $y$-coordinate of the hole.
answer: -1
check: lim(lambda x: (x**2 - 4*x + 3)/(x**2 - 1), 1)
hint: Factor numerator and denominator and find the factor they share.
hint: Cancel the common factor, then evaluate what is left at the $x$-value of the hole.
solution: $r(x) = \dfrac{(x-1)(x-3)}{(x-1)(x+1)}$. The factor $x - 1$ cancels, so the hole is at $x = 1$. The simplified formula $\dfrac{x-3}{x+1}$ gives $\dfrac{-2}{2} = -1$.

@quiz ms
prompt: Let $r(x) = \dfrac{3x^2 + 1}{x^2 - 4x + 4}$. Select every true statement.
choice*: The line $x = 2$ is a vertical asymptote, and $r(x) \to +\infty$ on both sides of it.
choice*: The line $y = 3$ is a horizontal asymptote.
choice*: The graph has no $x$-intercepts.
choice: The $y$-intercept is $1$.
choice: There is a hole at $x = 2$.
hint: Factor the denominator: $x^2 - 4x + 4 = (x - 2)^2$.
hint: Check whether the numerator is ever zero, and compare the signs of numerator and denominator near $x = 2$.
solution: The denominator is $(x-2)^2$ and the numerator is $13$ at $x = 2$, so nothing cancels: $x = 2$ is a vertical asymptote, and since the power is even and the numerator is positive, $r \to +\infty$ on both sides. The degrees are equal, so the horizontal asymptote is $y = 3/1 = 3$. The numerator $3x^2 + 1 > 0$ never vanishes, so there are no $x$-intercepts. Finally $r(0) = \tfrac14$, not $1$.
