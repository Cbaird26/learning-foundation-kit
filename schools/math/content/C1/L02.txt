@lesson
title: Trig integrals
summary: Integrate products of powers of sine, cosine, tangent and secant by choosing the right identity, and compare two methods that give the same antiderivative up to a constant.
objective: Integrate powers of sine and cosine by peeling off one factor when a power is odd and by using half-angle identities when both powers are even.
objective: Integrate products of tangent and secant powers with a substitution that uses the derivative of tangent or secant.
objective: Explain why two correct antiderivatives found by different methods differ by a constant, and verify this by differentiating.
reading: MIT 18.01SC Single Variable Calculus: look up the sessions on trigonometric integrals in the integration techniques material.

@article
Try to integrate $\sin^3 x$. There is no power rule for sine, but one identity changes everything: $\sin^3 x=\sin^2 x\cdot\sin x=(1-\cos^2 x)\sin x$. Now $u=\cos x$ works, because $-\sin x\,dx$ is exactly $du$, and the integral turns into a polynomial in $u$. That is the whole idea of this lesson: **use an identity so that a substitution becomes possible.**

### Powers of sine and cosine

For $\int\sin^m x\cos^n x\,dx$, look at the exponents.

- **At least one odd power.** Say $m$ is odd. Peel off one $\sin x$, rewrite the remaining even power of sine with $\sin^2 x=1-\cos^2 x$, and substitute $u=\cos x$, $du=-\sin x\,dx$. If $n$ is odd instead, peel off one $\cos x$ and use $u=\sin x$.
- **Both powers even.** Peeling fails, because the leftover factor is not a function of the other trig function. Lower the powers with the half-angle identities shown below, and use $\sin x\cos x=\frac12\sin 2x$ when it helps.

$$\sin^2 x=\frac{1-\cos 2x}{2},\qquad \cos^2 x=\frac{1+\cos 2x}{2}.$$

For example, $\int\cos^2 x\,dx=\frac{x}{2}+\frac{\sin 2x}{4}+C$. The $\frac14$ appears because integrating $\cos 2x$ gives $\frac12\sin 2x$: running the chain rule backwards divides by the inner derivative $2$.

### Powers of tangent and secant

Recall that $\frac{d}{dx}\tan x=\sec^2 x$, $\frac{d}{dx}\sec x=\sec x\tan x$ and $\sec^2 x=1+\tan^2 x$. For $\int\tan^m x\sec^n x\,dx$:

- **$n$ even, $n\ge2$:** peel off $\sec^2 x$, convert everything else to $\tan x$, and let $u=\tan x$.
- **$m$ odd, $n\ge1$:** peel off $\sec x\tan x$, convert the rest to $\sec x$ with $\tan^2 x=\sec^2 x-1$, and let $u=\sec x$.
- **No secant at all:** write $\tan^2 x=\sec^2 x-1$ to lower the power. For example, $\tan^3 x=\tan x\,(\sec^2 x-1)$.

Two results to remember: $\int\tan x\,dx=\ln|\sec x|+C$ and $\int\sec x\,dx=\ln|\sec x+\tan x|+C$.

### Different frequencies

An integral such as $\int\sin 3x\cos 2x\,dx$ has no powers to peel. Use a product-to-sum identity,

$$\sin A\cos B=\tfrac12\big[\sin(A+B)+\sin(A-B)\big],$$

to turn the product into a sum that you integrate term by term.

### When two methods disagree

Often more than one method works, and the answers look different. That is not an error: **two antiderivatives of the same function differ by a constant.** To compare methods, differentiate each result (both must return the original integrand) or convert one answer into the other with an identity. Then judge which method took fewer steps and fewer chances to slip.

@example
title: An odd power of sine
problem: Find $\int\sin^3 x\cos^2 x\,dx$.
step: The power of sine is odd, so peel off one $\sin x$ and rewrite the other two with $\sin^2 x=1-\cos^2 x$: $\sin^3 x\cos^2 x\,dx=(1-\cos^2 x)\cos^2 x\,\sin x\,dx$.
step: Let $u=\cos x$, so $du=-\sin x\,dx$. The integral becomes $-\int(1-u^2)u^2\,du=-\int(u^2-u^4)\,du$.
step: Integrate in $u$: $-\left(\frac{u^3}{3}-\frac{u^5}{5}\right)=-\frac{u^3}{3}+\frac{u^5}{5}$.
step: Substitute back: $\int\sin^3 x\cos^2 x\,dx=-\frac{\cos^3 x}{3}+\frac{\cos^5 x}{5}+C$.
step: Check by differentiating: $\cos^2 x\sin x-\cos^4 x\sin x=\cos^2 x\sin x\,(1-\cos^2 x)=\sin^3 x\cos^2 x$. It matches the integrand.

@example
title: Two methods, one integrand
problem: Evaluate $\int\sin x\cos x\,dx$ by two methods and explain why the answers look different.
step: Method A: let $u=\sin x$, so $du=\cos x\,dx$. Then $\int u\,du=\frac{u^2}{2}$, which gives $\frac{\sin^2 x}{2}+C$.
step: Method B: use $\sin 2x=2\sin x\cos x$, so $\sin x\cos x=\frac12\sin 2x$ and $\int\frac12\sin 2x\,dx=-\frac{\cos 2x}{4}+C$.
step: The two answers do not look alike. Rewrite B with $\cos 2x=1-2\sin^2 x$: $-\frac{\cos 2x}{4}=-\frac14+\frac{\sin^2 x}{2}$.
step: So A and B differ by the constant $\frac14$, which the arbitrary $+C$ absorbs. Differentiating either one returns $\sin x\cos x$, so both are correct.
step: Comparison: Method A is shorter here. Method B is the one that still works for $\sin 3x\cos 2x$, where no substitution is available.

@mistakes
- Integrating $\cos 2x$ as $\sin 2x$ and forgetting the factor $\frac12$ that comes from the inner function $2x$.
- Using the peel-off trick when both powers are even, or dropping the minus sign in $du=-\sin x\,dx$ when $u=\cos x$.
- Deciding that two answers cannot both be right because they do not look identical, instead of checking whether they differ by a constant.

@practice num
prompt: Evaluate $\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^2 x\,dx$. Give an exact answer.
answer: pi/4
check: quad(lambda x: sin(x)**2, 0, pi/2)
hint: The power of sine is even, so peeling does not work. Use the half-angle identity $\sin^2 x=\frac{1-\cos 2x}{2}$.
hint: The integral becomes $\frac12\int_0^{\pi/2}(1-\cos 2x)\,dx$. An antiderivative of $\cos 2x$ is $\frac12\sin 2x$.
solution: $\frac12\left[x-\frac{\sin 2x}{2}\right]_0^{\pi/2}=\frac12\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-0\right)=\frac{\pi}{4}$.

@practice expr
prompt: Find an antiderivative of $\cos^3 x$. Omit the $+C$.
vars: x
lead: $\int\cos^3 x\,dx =$
answer: sin(x) - sin(x)^3/3
check: lambda x: quad(lambda t: cos(t)**3, 0, x)
const: yes
hint: The power of cosine is odd: write $\cos^3 x=\cos^2 x\cdot\cos x$.
hint: Replace $\cos^2 x$ by $1-\sin^2 x$ and let $u=\sin x$, so $du=\cos x\,dx$.
solution: $\int(1-u^2)\,du=u-\frac{u^3}{3}$, so $\int\cos^3 x\,dx=\sin x-\frac{\sin^3 x}{3}+C$. Check: $\cos x-\sin^2 x\cos x=\cos^3 x$.

@practice mc
prompt: Which first step is best for $\int\sin^4 x\,dx$?
choice*: Rewrite $\sin^4 x=\left(\frac{1-\cos 2x}{2}\right)^2$ with the half-angle identity.
choice: Peel off one $\sin x$ and substitute $u=\cos x$.
choice: Substitute $u=\sin x$, since $\sin x$ appears in the integrand.
choice: Use the power rule to get $\frac{\sin^5 x}{5}$.
hint: Peeling and substituting work only when what is left over is $du$ times a function of $u$.
hint: Is the power of sine odd or even?
solution: The power is even, so no single factor can be peeled off to leave a usable $du$; reducing the power with $\sin^2 x=\frac{1-\cos 2x}{2}$ is the standard move. Substituting $u=\sin x$ needs a $\cos x\,dx$ that is not there, and the power rule fails because $\frac{d}{dx}\sin^5 x=5\sin^4 x\cos x$, not $5\sin^4 x$.

@practice num
prompt: Evaluate $\int_0^{\pi/4}\tan^3 x\,dx$. Give an exact answer.
answer: (1 - ln(2))/2
check: quad(lambda x: tan(x)**3, 0, pi/4)
hint: There is no secant, so lower the power with $\tan^2 x=\sec^2 x-1$.
hint: $\int\tan^3 x\,dx=\int\tan x\sec^2 x\,dx-\int\tan x\,dx$. Use $u=\tan x$ for the first piece and $\int\tan x\,dx=\ln|\sec x|$ for the second.
solution: $\int_0^{\pi/4}\tan x\sec^2 x\,dx=\left[\frac{\tan^2 x}{2}\right]_0^{\pi/4}=\frac12$ and $\int_0^{\pi/4}\tan x\,dx=\ln\sec\frac{\pi}{4}=\ln\sqrt2=\frac12\ln 2$. The value is $\frac12-\frac12\ln 2=\frac{1-\ln 2}{2}$.

@practice self
prompt: Evaluate $\int\sin x\cos^3 x\,dx$ by two different methods: first with $u=\cos x$, then by peeling off one $\cos x$ and using $u=\sin x$. Show that your two answers differ by a constant, and say which method you prefer and why.
hint: With $u=\cos x$ the factor $\sin x\,dx$ becomes $-du$, and no identity is needed.
hint: For the second method write $\cos^3 x=\cos^2 x\cdot\cos x=(1-\sin^2 x)\cos x$.
hint: To compare the answers, use $\cos^4 x-\sin^4 x=(\cos^2 x-\sin^2 x)(\cos^2 x+\sin^2 x)=1-2\sin^2 x$.
solution: Method A: $u=\cos x$, $du=-\sin x\,dx$, so $\int\sin x\cos^3 x\,dx=-\int u^3\,du=-\frac{\cos^4 x}{4}+C$. Method B: $u=\sin x$, $du=\cos x\,dx$, so $\int\sin x\,(1-\sin^2 x)\cos x\,dx=\int(u-u^3)\,du=\frac{\sin^2 x}{2}-\frac{\sin^4 x}{4}+C$. Difference, B minus A: $\frac{\sin^2 x}{2}-\frac{\sin^4 x}{4}+\frac{\cos^4 x}{4}=\frac{\sin^2 x}{2}+\frac{1-2\sin^2 x}{4}=\frac14$, a constant. Both differentiate to $\sin x\cos^3 x$. Method A is shorter: one substitution and a single power, with no identity to apply.
rubric: Method A gives $-\frac{\cos^4 x}{4}+C$, with the sign from $du=-\sin x\,dx$ handled correctly.
rubric: Method B gives $\frac{\sin^2 x}{2}-\frac{\sin^4 x}{4}+C$ after rewriting $\cos^2 x=1-\sin^2 x$.
rubric: The answers are shown to differ by the constant $\frac14$ (using an identity), or both are differentiated to recover the integrand.
rubric: A reason for the preferred method is given in terms of the number of steps or the chance of error.

@quiz num
prompt: Evaluate $\int_0^{\pi}\sin^2 x\cos^2 x\,dx$. Give an exact answer.
answer: pi/8
check: quad(lambda x: sin(x)**2*cos(x)**2, 0, pi)
hint: Both powers are even. Start with $\sin x\cos x=\frac12\sin 2x$.
hint: Then $\sin^2 x\cos^2 x=\frac14\sin^2 2x$. Apply the half-angle identity to $\sin^2 2x$, which gives $\frac{1-\cos 4x}{2}$.
solution: $\sin^2 x\cos^2 x=\frac14\sin^2 2x=\frac{1-\cos 4x}{8}$. Then $\int_0^{\pi}\frac{1-\cos 4x}{8}\,dx=\frac18\left[x-\frac{\sin 4x}{4}\right]_0^{\pi}=\frac{\pi}{8}$.

answer: tan(x) + tan(x)^3/3
check: lambda x: quad(lambda t: 1/cos(t)**4, 0, x)

const: yes
hint: The power of secant is even. Peel off $\sec^2 x$.
