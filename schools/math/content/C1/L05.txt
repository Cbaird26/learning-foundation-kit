@article
Can an infinitely long region have a finite area? Compute the area under $y=\frac{1}{x^2}$ from $x=1$ out to $x=b$: $\int_1^b x^{-2}\,dx=1-\frac1b$. As $b$ grows this approaches $1$, so the area all the way to infinity is **finite**. Do the same for $y=\frac1x$ and you get $\ln b$, which grows without bound, so that area is **infinite**. Both curves flatten to zero, yet the verdicts differ: the rate of decay matters.

### Two kinds of improper integral

An integral is **improper** if an endpoint is infinite or the integrand blows up somewhere on the interval. Define it as a limit.

- **Infinite interval:** $\displaystyle\int_a^\infty f(x)\,dx=\lim_{b\to\infty}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$, and similarly for $(-\infty,b]$.
- **Unbounded integrand** at an endpoint, say $f\to\pm\infty$ as $x\to b^-$: $\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=\lim_{t\to b^-}\int_a^t f(x)\,dx$.

If the limit exists and is finite, the integral **converges** to that value; otherwise it **diverges**. For $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}$ or a blow-up in the interior, split at a convenient point. The whole integral converges only if **every** piece converges.

### The p-integrals

Two families serve as yardsticks for everything else:

$$\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^p}=\frac{1}{p-1}\ \text{ if } p>1,\ \text{ and diverges if } p\le 1,$$

$$\int_0^1\frac{dx}{x^p}=\frac{1}{1-p}\ \text{ if } p<1,\ \text{ and diverges if } p\ge 1.$$

The first says a tail must decay faster than $\frac1x$. The second says a spike at $0$ must be weaker than $\frac1x$.

### Deciding without evaluating: comparison

Suppose $0\le f(x)\le g(x)$ for all $x$ in the range of integration. Then:

- if $\int g$ converges, so does $\int f$ (a smaller area under a finite one is finite);
- if $\int f$ diverges, so does $\int g$.

For example, $\frac{1}{x^2+1}\le\frac{1}{x^2}$, so $\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^2+1}$ converges, and you know it before computing anything. The other two directions tell you nothing.

### Beware the interior singularity

The integral $\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dx}{x^2}$ is **not** $\left[-\frac1x\right]_{-1}^{1}=-2$. The integrand is positive, so the answer cannot be negative. It blows up at $0$, so split there: $\int_0^1\frac{dx}{x^2}$ has $p=2\ge1$ and diverges, hence the whole integral diverges.

The habit to build: **decide convergence first**, then evaluate only if it converges.

@example
title: An integral that needs parts and a limit
problem: Evaluate $\int_0^\infty xe^{-x}\,dx$.
step: Replace the infinite limit by a variable bound: $\int_0^\infty xe^{-x}\,dx=\lim_{b\to\infty}\int_0^b xe^{-x}\,dx$.
step: Integrate by parts with $u=x$ and $dv=e^{-x}\,dx$, so $du=dx$ and $v=-e^{-x}$: $\int xe^{-x}\,dx=-xe^{-x}+\int e^{-x}\,dx=-xe^{-x}-e^{-x}$.
step: Evaluate from $0$ to $b$: $\left(-be^{-b}-e^{-b}\right)-\left(0-1\right)=1-be^{-b}-e^{-b}$.
step: Take the limit. Both $e^{-b}\to0$ and $be^{-b}=\frac{b}{e^{b}}\to0$, because an exponential beats any power. The integral converges to $1$.

@example
title: Convergence by comparison
problem: Decide whether $\int_1^\infty\frac{2+\sin x}{x^{3/2}}\,dx$ converges.
step: This integrand has no elementary antiderivative, so compare it with something simpler instead of evaluating it.
step: Since $-1\le\sin x\le1$, we have $1\le2+\sin x\le3$. Hence $0<\frac{2+\sin x}{x^{3/2}}\le\frac{3}{x^{3/2}}$ for $x\ge1$.
step: The larger integral $\int_1^\infty\frac{3}{x^{3/2}}\,dx$ converges, because $p=\frac32>1$. Its value is $3\cdot\frac{1}{\frac32-1}=6$.
step: By comparison the original integral converges, and its value lies between $\int_1^\infty x^{-3/2}\,dx=2$ and $6$.

@mistakes
- Substituting $\infty$ into an antiderivative as though it were a number, instead of taking a limit.
- Ignoring a blow-up inside the interval, as in "evaluating" $\int_{-1}^1\frac{dx}{x^2}$ as $-2$.
- Believing that an integrand tending to $0$ guarantees convergence. The integrand $\frac1x$ is the standard counterexample.
- Using comparison backwards: concluding that $\int f$ diverges because the larger $\int g$ diverges, or that $\int g$ converges because the smaller $\int f$ converges. Neither follows.

@practice num
prompt: Evaluate $\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^3}$. Give an exact answer.
answer: 1/2
check: quad(lambda x: 1/x**3, 1, inf)
hint: Replace the infinite limit: $\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^3}=\lim_{b\to\infty}\int_1^b x^{-3}\,dx$.
hint: An antiderivative of $x^{-3}$ is $-\frac{1}{2x^2}$. What happens to its value at $b$ as $b\to\infty$?
solution: $\int_1^b x^{-3}\,dx=\left[-\frac{1}{2x^2}\right]_1^b=\frac12-\frac{1}{2b^2}\to\frac12$ as $b\to\infty$.

@practice num
prompt: Evaluate $\int_0^8\frac{dx}{\sqrt[3]{x}}$. Give an exact answer.
answer: 6
check: quad(lambda x: x**(-1/3), 0, 8)
hint: The integrand blows up at $x=0$, so write the integral as $\lim_{t\to0^+}\int_t^8 x^{-1/3}\,dx$.
hint: An antiderivative of $x^{-1/3}$ is $\frac32x^{2/3}$. Evaluate it at $8$ and at $t$, then let $t\to0^+$.
solution: $\int_t^8 x^{-1/3}\,dx=\frac32\left(8^{2/3}-t^{2/3}\right)=\frac32\left(4-t^{2/3}\right)\to6$ as $t\to0^+$.

choice*: $\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^{3/2}}$
choice: $\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x}}$
choice: $\int_0^1\frac{dx}{x^{3/2}}$

solution: $\int_1^\infty x^{-p}\,dx$ converges for $p>1$, so $p=\frac32$ converges and $p=\frac12$ diverges. $\int_0^1 x^{-p}\,dx$ converges for $p<1$, so $p=\frac12$ converges and $p=\frac32$ diverges.

@practice num
prompt: Evaluate $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{x^2+4}$. Give an exact answer.
answer: pi/2
check: quad(lambda x: 1/(x**2+4), -inf, inf)
hint: Both ends are improper, so each half-line integral must converge. Split at $0$ or use the symmetry of the integrand.
hint: An antiderivative is $\frac12\arctan\frac{x}{2}$. Recall that $\arctan u\to\pm\frac{\pi}{2}$ as $u\to\pm\infty$.
solution: $\left[\frac12\arctan\frac x2\right]_{-\infty}^{\infty}=\frac12\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\right)=\frac{\pi}{2}$. Each half-line integral converges (to $\frac{\pi}{4}$), so splitting is legitimate.

@practice self
prompt: Use the comparison test to decide whether each integral converges. Do not evaluate them; name the comparison function and state which inequality you use. (a) $\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^2+\sqrt{x}}$. (b) $\int_2^\infty\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}$.
hint: For (a), is the denominator larger or smaller than $x^2$? That tells you the direction of the inequality.
hint: For (b), compare $\sqrt{x^2-1}$ with $x$ for $x\ge2$, and recall the p-integral with $p=1$.
solution: (a) For $x\ge1$, $x^2+\sqrt x\ge x^2$, so $0<\frac{1}{x^2+\sqrt x}\le\frac{1}{x^2}$. Since $\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^2}$ converges ($p=2>1$), the given integral converges. (b) For $x\ge2$, $\sqrt{x^2-1}<x$, so $\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}>\frac1x>0$. Since $\int_2^\infty\frac{dx}{x}$ diverges ($p=1$), the given integral diverges.
rubric: In (a) the comparison function $\frac{1}{x^2}$ is named and the inequality $\frac{1}{x^2+\sqrt x}\le\frac{1}{x^2}$ is stated in the correct direction.
rubric: In (a) the conclusion (converges) is justified by the convergence of the larger integral with $p=2$.
rubric: In (b) the comparison function $\frac1x$ is named and the inequality $\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}>\frac1x$ is stated in the correct direction.
rubric: In (b) the conclusion (diverges) is justified by the divergence of the smaller integral with $p=1$.

@quiz num
prompt: Evaluate $\int_0^1\ln x\,dx$. Give an exact answer.
answer: -1
check: quad(lambda x: log(x), 0, 1)
hint: The integrand is unbounded at $0$, so write $\lim_{t\to0^+}\int_t^1\ln x\,dx$ and integrate by parts.
hint: An antiderivative of $\ln x$ is $x\ln x-x$. You will need the fact that $t\ln t\to0$ as $t\to0^+$.
solution: $\int_t^1\ln x\,dx=\left[x\ln x-x\right]_t^1=(0-1)-(t\ln t-t)=-1-t\ln t+t\to-1$, since $t\ln t\to0$ as $t\to0^+$.

@quiz num
prompt: Evaluate $\int_0^{\infty}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x}\,(1+x)}$. Give an exact answer.
answer: pi
check: quad(lambda x: 1/(sqrt(x)*(1+x)), 0, 1) + quad(lambda x: 1/(sqrt(x)*(1+x)), 1, inf)
hint: Both ends are improper, and each piece must converge. Near $0$ the integrand behaves like $x^{-1/2}$.
hint: Try $u=\sqrt{x}$, so $x=u^2$ and $dx=2u\,du$. The new integrand is a standard arctangent form.
solution: With $u=\sqrt x$ the integral becomes $\int_0^\infty\frac{2u\,du}{u(1+u^2)}=2\int_0^\infty\frac{du}{1+u^2}=2\left[\arctan u\right]_0^\infty=2\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}=\pi$. Near $0$ the integrand is like $x^{-1/2}$ ($p<1$) and near $\infty$ like $x^{-3/2}$ ($p>1$), so both pieces converge.
