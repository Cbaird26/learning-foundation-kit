@article
Write down $a_n=\frac{n}{n+1}$: $\frac12,\frac23,\frac34,\frac45,\dots$ The terms creep up toward $1$ and never leave. A **sequence** is a list $a_1,a_2,a_3,\dots$, that is, a function whose input is a positive integer $n$, and the central question is what happens to $a_n$ as $n$ grows.

### Limits of sequences

We write $\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=L$ when $a_n$ can be made as close to $L$ as we please by taking $n$ large enough. Precisely: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is an $N$ such that $|a_n-L|<\varepsilon$ whenever $n>N$. A sequence with a finite limit **converges**. Otherwise it **diverges**, either to $\pm\infty$ (like $a_n=n$) or by oscillating (like $a_n=(-1)^n$).

The usual limit laws hold for sums, products and quotients (with a nonzero limit in the denominator). Three more tools:

- **Function trick.** If $a_n=f(n)$ and $\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=L$, then $a_n\to L$. This is how you use L'Hopital's rule: apply it to $f$, never to the sequence itself.
- **Continuity.** If $a_n\to L$ and $g$ is continuous at $L$, then $g(a_n)\to g(L)$.
- **Squeeze theorem.** If $b_n\le a_n\le c_n$ and $b_n\to L$, $c_n\to L$, then $a_n\to L$. For example $\left|\frac{\sin n}{n}\right|\le\frac1n$, so $\frac{\sin n}{n}\to0$.

### Standard limits

$$\lim_{n\to\infty}r^n=0\ \ (|r|<1),\qquad \lim_{n\to\infty}\Big(1+\frac{x}{n}\Big)^n=e^{x},\qquad \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x^n}{n!}=0.$$

For large $n$ the growth order is $\ln n\ll n^p\ll a^n\ll n!\ll n^n$ (with $p>0$ and $a>1$). A ratio of an earlier item over a later one tends to $0$.

### Monotone sequences

A sequence is **increasing** if $a_{n+1}\ge a_n$ for all $n$, and **bounded above** if some number $M$ satisfies $a_n\le M$ for all $n$. The **Monotone Convergence Theorem** says that every increasing sequence that is bounded above converges, and so does every decreasing sequence that is bounded below. It proves convergence without knowing the limit in advance.

That matters for a recursive sequence $a_{n+1}=g(a_n)$. First prove that it is monotone and bounded. Only then pass to the limit in the recursion to get $L=g(L)$ and solve for $L$.

@example
title: A limit with an exponent
problem: Find $\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{1/n}$.
step: Rewrite with the exponential function: $n^{1/n}=e^{(\ln n)/n}$. It is enough to find the limit of the exponent.
step: Let $f(x)=\frac{\ln x}{x}$. As $x\to\infty$ this has the form $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$, so L'Hopital's rule applies to $f$: $\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1/x}{1}=0$. Hence $\frac{\ln n}{n}\to0$.
step: The exponential function is continuous at $0$, so $e^{(\ln n)/n}\to e^0=1$.
step: Sanity check: $10^{1/10}\approx1.259$, $100^{1/100}\approx1.047$, $1000^{1/1000}\approx1.007$. The terms are indeed approaching $1$.

@example
title: A recursive sequence
problem: Let $a_1=\sqrt2$ and $a_{n+1}=\sqrt{2+a_n}$. Show that the sequence converges and find its limit.
step: Compute a few terms: $a_1\approx1.414$, $a_2\approx1.848$, $a_3\approx1.962$. They appear to increase toward $2$.
step: Bounded above by $2$: we have $a_1<2$, and if $a_n<2$ then $a_{n+1}=\sqrt{2+a_n}<\sqrt4=2$. By induction $a_n<2$ for all $n$.
step: Increasing: $a_{n+1}^2-a_n^2=(2+a_n)-a_n^2=(2-a_n)(1+a_n)>0$ because $0<a_n<2$. Hence $a_{n+1}>a_n$.
step: By the Monotone Convergence Theorem the sequence converges to some $L$. Only now take limits in the recursion: $L=\sqrt{2+L}$, so $L^2-L-2=(L-2)(L+1)=0$.
step: Since $L\ge a_1>0$, the root $L=-1$ is impossible, so $L=2$.

@mistakes
- Applying L'Hopital's rule to the sequence $a_n$ itself. Differentiate the function $f(x)$ with $f(n)=a_n$ instead.
- Taking the limit of a recursion before proving convergence. For $a_1=1$, $a_{n+1}=2a_n$ the equation $L=2L$ gives $L=0$, yet $a_n=2^{n-1}\to\infty$.
- Believing that a bounded sequence must converge (think of $(-1)^n$), or that a monotone sequence must converge (think of $a_n=n$). The theorem needs both properties.
- Treating $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$ or $\infty-\infty$ as a number instead of rewriting the expression, for example by dividing by the highest power or multiplying by a conjugate.

@practice num
prompt: Find $\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{3n^2+n}{2n^2-5}$.
answer: 3/2
check: liminf(lambda n: (3*n**2 + n)/(2*n**2 - 5))
hint: Divide the numerator and the denominator by the highest power of $n$, which is $n^2$.
hint: You get $\frac{3+1/n}{2-5/n^2}$. What do $\frac1n$ and $\frac{1}{n^2}$ do as $n\to\infty$?
solution: $\frac{3n^2+n}{2n^2-5}=\frac{3+1/n}{2-5/n^2}\to\frac{3}{2}$.

@practice num
prompt: Find $\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{4^{n+1}-3^n}{2\cdot4^n+5}$.
answer: 2
check: (4**101 - 3**100)/(2*4**100 + 5)
hint: The dominant term in both numerator and denominator is a multiple of $4^n$. Divide everything by $4^n$.
hint: $\frac{3^n}{4^n}=\left(\frac34\right)^n$ and $\frac{5}{4^n}$ both tend to $0$ by the standard limit for $r^n$ with $|r|<1$.
solution: $\frac{4\cdot4^n-3^n}{2\cdot4^n+5}=\frac{4-(3/4)^n}{2+5/4^n}\to\frac42=2$.

@practice num
prompt: Find $\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\sqrt{n^2+n}-n\right)$.
answer: 1/2
check: liminf(lambda n: sqrt(n**2 + n) - n)
hint: This has the form $\infty-\infty$. Multiply and divide by the conjugate $\sqrt{n^2+n}+n$.
hint: The numerator becomes $(n^2+n)-n^2=n$. Divide the top and bottom of the resulting fraction by $n$.
solution: $\sqrt{n^2+n}-n=\frac{n}{\sqrt{n^2+n}+n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+1/n}+1}\to\frac{1}{1+1}=\frac12$.

@practice self
prompt: Let $a_1=1$ and $a_{n+1}=\frac12\left(a_n+\frac{2}{a_n}\right)$. Prove that the sequence converges and find its limit.
hint: Show by induction that every term is positive. Then use $a_{n+1}\ge\sqrt{a_n\cdot\frac{2}{a_n}}$ (the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality) to get a lower bound.
hint: Compute $a_{n+1}-a_n$ and use the lower bound to decide its sign.
solution: All terms are positive by induction. For positive $a_n$, the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality gives $a_{n+1}=\frac12\left(a_n+\frac{2}{a_n}\right)\ge\sqrt{a_n\cdot\frac{2}{a_n}}=\sqrt2$, so $a_n\ge\sqrt2$ for all $n\ge2$. For $n\ge2$, $a_{n+1}-a_n=\frac{1}{a_n}-\frac{a_n}{2}=\frac{2-a_n^2}{2a_n}\le0$, so the sequence is decreasing from $n=2$ on and bounded below by $\sqrt2$. By the Monotone Convergence Theorem it converges to some $L\ge\sqrt2$. Now pass to the limit in the recursion: $L=\frac12\left(L+\frac2L\right)$, so $L=\frac2L$, $L^2=2$ and $L=\sqrt2$.
rubric: Shows that the terms are positive and bounded below by $\sqrt2$ (from $n=2$ on).
rubric: Shows the sequence is decreasing from $n=2$ on, using the sign of $2-a_n^2$.
rubric: States the Monotone Convergence Theorem and applies it before taking limits in the recursion.
rubric: Solves $L=\frac12\left(L+\frac2L\right)$ to get $L=\sqrt2$, rejecting the negative root.

@quiz num
prompt: Find $\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{n-1}{n+1}\right)^n$. Give an exact answer.
answer: e^(-2)
check: liminf(lambda n: ((n - 1)/(n + 1))**n)
hint: Write $\frac{n-1}{n+1}=1-\frac{2}{n+1}$ to match the standard limit $\left(1+\frac{x}{m}\right)^m\to e^x$.
hint: Set $m=n+1$. Then $\left(1-\frac{2}{m}\right)^{n}=\left(1-\frac{2}{m}\right)^{m}\left(1-\frac{2}{m}\right)^{-1}$, and the last factor tends to $1$.
solution: $\left(1-\frac{2}{n+1}\right)^{n}=\left(1-\frac{2}{n+1}\right)^{n+1}\Big/\left(1-\frac{2}{n+1}\right)\to\frac{e^{-2}}{1}=e^{-2}$.

@quiz mc
prompt: A student argues: "Let $a_1=1$ and $a_{n+1}=2a_n$. If the limit $L$ exists, then $L=2L$, so $L=0$." What is wrong with the conclusion?
choice*: The equation $L=2L$ is only valid if the sequence converges, and here $a_n=2^{n-1}\to\infty$.
choice: The limit of $2a_n$ is not $2L$.
choice: The terms $a_{n+1}$ and $a_n$ have different limits.
choice: The equation $L=2L$ has the solution $L=2$, not $L=0$.
hint: Compute the first few terms $a_1,a_2,a_3,\dots$ and look at what they do.
solution: The sequence is $1,2,4,8,\dots$ and diverges to infinity. Passing to the limit in the recursion is legitimate only after convergence has been proved, for example with the Monotone Convergence Theorem. The other statements are false: if a limit existed, the limit laws would give $\lim 2a_n=2L$, the terms $a_{n+1}$ and $a_n$ would share the limit $L$, and $L=2L$ has the single solution $L=0$.
