@article
The heat equation $u_t=k\,u_{xx}$ models diffusion: heat in a rod, dye in still water, a pollutant in a thin pipe. The constant $k>0$ has units of length$^2$/time. Last week's method gives, for ends held at $0$ on $[0,L]$,
$$u(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_n\,e^{-k(n\pi/L)^2t}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L},\qquad b_n=\frac2L\int_0^Lf(x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx.$$

### Three qualitative facts

- **High modes die first.** Mode $n$ decays at rate $k(n\pi/L)^2$, which grows like $n^2$. After a while only the slowest mode survives: $u\approx b_1e^{-k\pi^2t/L^2}\sin\frac{\pi x}{L}$. The natural time scale is $\frac{L^2}{k\pi^2}$.
- **Smoothing.** For any $t>0$ the factors $e^{-kn^2\pi^2t/L^2}$ crush the coefficients faster than any power of $n$, so $u$ is infinitely differentiable in $x$ even if $f$ has jumps.
- **Maximum principle.** On $[0,L]\times[0,T]$ the maximum and minimum of $u$ occur at $t=0$ or at the ends $x=0,L$. Diffusion never creates a new hot spot in the interior. (Week 8 uses energy to prove uniqueness.)

### Nonzero end temperatures

Suppose $u(0,t)=A$ and $u(L,t)=B$ are constants. The **steady state** $u_s(x)$ solves $u_s''=0$ with those ends: $u_s(x)=A+(B-A)\frac xL$. The difference $v=u-u_s$ satisfies the heat equation with zero ends and initial data $f-u_s$. Hence
$$u(x,t)=u_s(x)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_n\,e^{-k(n\pi/L)^2t}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L},$$
where $b_n$ are the sine coefficients of $f-u_s$. As $t\to\infty$, $u\to u_s$.

### The whole line and the heat kernel

On $\mathbb R$ the Fourier transform turns $u_t=ku_{xx}$ into $\hat u_t=-k\xi^2\hat u$, so $\hat u(\xi,t)=\hat f(\xi)e^{-k\xi^2t}$. The factor $e^{-k\xi^2t}$ is the transform of a Gaussian, and by the convolution theorem
$$u(x,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}G(x-y,t)\,f(y)\,dy,\qquad G(x,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi kt}}\,e^{-x^2/(4kt)}.$$
The **heat kernel** $G$ always has total area $1$, has standard deviation $\sqrt{2kt}$, and becomes a tall narrow spike as $t\to0^+$ (an approximate identity). So each point of the answer is a Gaussian-weighted average of the initial data, with a window that widens like $\sqrt t$.

### Computing a partial sum

```python
import numpy as np

L, k, t = np.pi, 1.0, 0.1
n = np.arange(1, 200, 2)            # odd modes only
b = 4 / (n * np.pi)                 # sine coefficients of f = 1
decay = np.exp(-k * (n * np.pi / L) ** 2 * t)
u = lambda x: np.sum(b * decay * np.sin(n * np.pi * x / L))
print(round(u(L / 2), 3))           # 0.999: the center has barely cooled
```

@example
title: A uniformly warm rod with cold ends
problem: Solve $u_t=u_{xx}$ on $0<x<\pi$ with $u(0,t)=u(\pi,t)=0$ and $u(x,0)=1$. Estimate the midpoint temperature at $t=1$.
step: $b_n=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi\sin nx\,dx=\frac{2(1-(-1)^n)}{n\pi}$, which is $\frac{4}{n\pi}$ for odd $n$ and $0$ for even $n$.
step: Therefore $u(x,t)=\frac4\pi\sum_{n\ \mathrm{odd}}\frac{e^{-n^2t}}{n}\sin nx$.
step: At $x=\frac\pi2$ and $t=1$: the first term is $\frac4\pi e^{-1}\approx0.4684$, and the next is $-\frac{4}{3\pi}e^{-9}\approx-5\times10^{-5}$. So $u(\frac\pi2,1)\approx0.4683$.
step: Interpretation: after one time unit the profile is essentially $\frac4\pi e^{-1}\sin x$, a single slowly decaying arch. For very small $t$ the temperature stays near $1$ in the middle and drops to $0$ in thin layers of width about $\sqrt t$ at the ends.

@example
title: Fixed end temperatures
problem: Solve $u_t=u_{xx}$ on $0<x<\pi$ with $u(0,t)=0$, $u(\pi,t)=\pi$ and $u(x,0)=0$.
step: The steady state solves $u_s''=0$ with $u_s(0)=0$ and $u_s(\pi)=\pi$, so $u_s(x)=x$.
step: Let $v=u-x$. Then $v_t=v_{xx}$, $v(0,t)=v(\pi,t)=0$ and $v(x,0)=-x$.
step: $b_n=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi(-x)\sin nx\,dx=-\frac2\pi\cdot\frac{\pi(-1)^{n+1}}{n}=\frac{2(-1)^n}{n}$.
step: $u(x,t)=x+\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{2(-1)^n}{n}e^{-n^2t}\sin nx$.
step: Check: at $t=0$ the sum is $x-x=0$, because $x=\sum\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\sin nx$ on $(0,\pi)$ (week 1). As $t\to\infty$, $u\to x$.

@mistakes
- Using the zero-ends series when the end temperatures are not zero; subtract the steady state first.
- Subtracting the steady state from $u$ but forgetting to subtract it from the initial condition as well.
- Expecting a jump in the initial data to travel or persist for $t>0$; the heat equation smooths it immediately.
- Reading $e^{-x^2/(4kt)}$ as a Gaussian of variance $kt$; its variance is $2kt$.
