@article
Separation of variables turns one PDE in two variables into two ODEs. It works when the PDE and the boundary conditions are **linear and homogeneous**, because then sums of solutions are again solutions.

### The model problem

$$u_t=k\,u_{xx}\quad(0<x<L,\ t>0),\qquad u(0,t)=u(L,t)=0,\qquad u(x,0)=f(x).$$
Think of a rod of diffusivity $k>0$ whose ends are held at temperature $0$.

### Step 1: separate

Look for **product solutions** $u=X(x)T(t)$. Substituting and dividing by $kXT$ gives
$$\frac{T'(t)}{k\,T(t)}=\frac{X''(x)}{X(x)}.$$
The left side depends only on $t$, the right side only on $x$, so both equal one constant, which we call $-\lambda$:
$$X''+\lambda X=0,\qquad T'+k\lambda T=0.$$

### Step 2: the eigenvalue problem

The boundary conditions require $X(0)=X(L)=0$ (otherwise $T\equiv0$). If $\lambda\le0$, the only solution is $X\equiv0$. If $\lambda=\mu^2>0$ then $X=A\cos\mu x+B\sin\mu x$; $X(0)=0$ forces $A=0$, and $X(L)=0$ needs $\sin\mu L=0$, that is $\mu=\frac{n\pi}{L}$. So
$$\lambda_n=\left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^2,\qquad X_n(x)=\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L},\qquad T_n(t)=e^{-k\lambda_nt},\qquad n=1,2,3,\dots$$
These **eigenvalues** and **eigenfunctions** are selected by the boundary conditions; nothing in the ODE itself restricts $\lambda$.

### Step 3: superpose and match

Every $u_n=e^{-k\lambda_nt}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}$ satisfies the PDE and the ends, and so does any sum. At $t=0$ we need a sine series for $f$, and week 2's orthogonality gives
$$u(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_n\,e^{-k(n\pi/L)^2t}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L},\qquad b_n=\frac2L\int_0^Lf(x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx.$$

### Other ends, other eigenfunctions

| conditions on $X$ | eigenfunctions | eigenvalues |
|---|---|---|
| $X(0)=X(L)=0$ | $\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}$ | $\left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^2$, $n\ge1$ |
| $X'(0)=X'(L)=0$ | $\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L}$ | $\left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^2$, $n\ge0$ (includes the constant) |
| $X(0)=0,\ X'(L)=0$ | $\sin\frac{(n-\frac12)\pi x}{L}$ | $\left(\frac{(n-\frac12)\pi}{L}\right)^2$, $n\ge1$ |

The same idea works for other equations. For the wave equation $u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx}$ the time ODE becomes $T''+c^2\lambda T=0$, and for Laplace's equation $u_{xx}+u_{yy}=0$ it becomes $Y''-\lambda Y=0$. The spatial eigenvalue problem is the same in all three cases, which is why one Fourier series toolkit serves them all.

@example
title: A two-mode initial temperature
problem: Solve $u_t=u_{xx}$ for $0<x<\pi$, $t>0$, with $u(0,t)=u(\pi,t)=0$ and $u(x,0)=\sin x+3\sin 2x$.
step: With $k=1$ and $L=\pi$ the eigenvalues are $\lambda_n=n^2$, the eigenfunctions are $\sin nx$, and the time factors are $e^{-n^2t}$.
step: The initial condition is already a sine series with $b_1=1$, $b_2=3$ and every other $b_n=0$ (coefficients are unique, by orthogonality).
step: Therefore $u(x,t)=e^{-t}\sin x+3e^{-4t}\sin 2x$.
step: Check: $u_t=-e^{-t}\sin x-12e^{-4t}\sin2x=u_{xx}$, $u(0,t)=u(\pi,t)=0$, and $u(x,0)=\sin x+3\sin2x$. The second mode decays four times faster than the first.

@example
title: Eigenvalues for mixed ends
problem: Find all eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of $X''+\lambda X=0$ with $X(0)=0$ and $X'(L)=0$.
step: For $\lambda=0$, $X=Ax+B$; $X(0)=0$ gives $B=0$ and $X'(L)=A=0$. For $\lambda=-\mu^2<0$, $X=A\cosh\mu x+B\sinh\mu x$; $X(0)=0$ gives $A=0$ and $X'(L)=B\mu\cosh\mu L=0$ gives $B=0$. So only $\lambda>0$ is possible.
step: Let $\lambda=\mu^2>0$. Then $X=A\cos\mu x+B\sin\mu x$, and $X(0)=0$ gives $A=0$, so $X=B\sin\mu x$.
step: Then $X'(L)=B\mu\cos\mu L=0$, so $\cos\mu L=0$ and $\mu L=\left(n-\tfrac12\right)\pi$ for $n=1,2,\dots$
step: Hence $\lambda_n=\left(\frac{(n-\frac12)\pi}{L}\right)^2$ and $X_n=\sin\frac{(n-\frac12)\pi x}{L}$. The first one is a quarter wave: it vanishes at the fixed end and is flat at the free end.

@mistakes
- Writing the separation constant with the wrong sign, so that the $x$ equation is $X''-\lambda X=0$ and the $t$ equation has the opposite sign; keep $X''+\lambda X=0$ together with $T'+k\lambda T=0$.
- Applying the initial condition to a single product solution instead of to the superposition.
- Forgetting to rule out $\lambda\le0$ (and, for Neumann ends, forgetting that $\lambda=0$ gives the constant eigenfunction).
- Using the sine coefficient formula $\frac2L\int_0^L$ for a problem whose eigenfunctions are cosines or the mixed family.

@practice self
prompt: Use separation of variables for $u_t=ku_{xx}$ on $0<x<L$ with insulated ends $u_x(0,t)=u_x(L,t)=0$ and $u(x,0)=f(x)$. (a) Find the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions. (b) Write the series solution with its coefficient formulas. (c) What does $u(x,t)$ approach as $t\to\infty$, and why is that physically sensible?
hint: The boundary conditions become $X'(0)=X'(L)=0$. Treat $\lambda=0$ separately from $\lambda>0$.
hint: For $\lambda=\mu^2>0$, $X=A\cos\mu x+B\sin\mu x$; apply $X'(0)=0$ first.
solution: (a) $X''+\lambda X=0$ with $X'(0)=X'(L)=0$. For $\lambda=0$, $X=$ constant works. For $\lambda=\mu^2>0$, $X'(0)=0$ gives $B=0$, and $X'(L)=-A\mu\sin\mu L=0$ gives $\mu=\frac{n\pi}{L}$. So $\lambda_n=\left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^2$, $X_n=\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L}$, $n=0,1,2,\dots$ (b) $u(x,t)=\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_ne^{-k(n\pi/L)^2t}\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L}$ with $a_n=\frac2L\int_0^Lf(x)\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx$. (c) Every term with $n\ge1$ decays, so $u\to\frac{a_0}{2}=\frac1L\int_0^Lf\,dx$, the average of the initial temperature. This is sensible because insulated ends let no heat escape, so the total heat is conserved and ends up spread evenly.
rubric: Treats $\lambda=0$ separately and obtains the constant eigenfunction, with $n$ starting at $0$ for cosines.
rubric: Obtains $\lambda_n=(n\pi/L)^2$ and $X_n=\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L}$ by applying $X'(0)=0$ then $X'(L)=0$.
rubric: Writes the cosine series with $a_n=\frac2L\int_0^Lf\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L}dx$ and the constant term $\frac{a_0}{2}$.
rubric: States that $u\to$ the average of $f$ and links it to conservation of heat with insulated ends.

@quiz num
prompt: The heat problem on $(0,\pi)$ with ends at $0$ and $k=1$ has initial temperature $f(x)=x(\pi-x)$. Compute the first sine coefficient $b_1=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi f(x)\sin x\,dx$.
answer: 8/pi
check: 2/pi*quad(lambda x: x*(pi-x)*sin(x), 0, pi)
hint: Expand $x(\pi-x)\sin x=\pi x\sin x-x^2\sin x$ and integrate each term by parts.
hint: $\int_0^\pi x\sin x\,dx=\pi$ and $\int_0^\pi x^2\sin x\,dx=\pi^2-4$.
solution: $\int_0^\pi x(\pi-x)\sin x\,dx=\pi\cdot\pi-(\pi^2-4)=4$, so $b_1=\frac2\pi\cdot4=\frac8\pi\approx2.55$.
