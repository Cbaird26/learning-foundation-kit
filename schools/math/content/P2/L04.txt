@article
In $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ the number $6$ factors as $2\cdot 3$ and as $(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$. Unique factorization fails, yet ideals still factor well there. Rings, ideals and localization are the language in which such failures, and their repairs, are stated. In this lesson every ring is commutative with $1$, and $1 \ne 0$ unless said otherwise.

### Prime and maximal ideals

An ideal $P \subsetneq R$ is **prime** if $ab \in P$ implies $a \in P$ or $b \in P$ (equivalently $R/P$ is a domain), and **maximal** if no ideal lies strictly between $P$ and $R$ (equivalently $R/P$ is a field). Maximal ideals are prime; the converse fails: $(0)$ is prime in $\mathbb{Z}$ and $(x)$ is prime in $\mathbb{Q}[x,y]$, but neither is maximal.

**Theorem (existence of maximal ideals).** Every proper ideal $I$ of a ring $R$ lies in a maximal ideal.

*Proof.* Let $\mathcal{S}$ be the set of proper ideals containing $I$, ordered by inclusion; it is nonempty. The union of a chain in $\mathcal{S}$ is an ideal that does not contain $1$, so is in $\mathcal{S}$. By Zorn's lemma $\mathcal{S}$ has a maximal element, which is a maximal ideal. $\blacksquare$

**Lemma.** Let $S \subseteq R$ be a multiplicative set ($1 \in S$, closed under products) and $I$ an ideal with $I \cap S = \emptyset$. An ideal $P$ maximal among ideals containing $I$ and disjoint from $S$ is prime.

*Proof.* Such a $P$ exists by Zorn as above. Suppose $a, b \notin P$ but $ab \in P$. Then $P + (a)$ and $P + (b)$ strictly contain $P$, so meet $S$: $s = p + ra$ and $t = p' + r'b$ with $s,t\in S$, $p,p'\in P$. Then $st \in P + (ab) = P$, but $st \in S$, contradicting $P \cap S = \emptyset$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem.** The **nilradical** (the set of nilpotent elements) equals the intersection of all prime ideals of $R$.

*Proof.* A nilpotent $x$ with $x^n = 0 \in P$ lies in every prime $P$. Conversely let $x$ be not nilpotent. Then $S = \{1, x, x^2, \dots\}$ misses $0$, so the Lemma with $I = 0$ gives a prime $P$ disjoint from $S$, so $x \notin P$. $\blacksquare$

### The Chinese remainder theorem

Ideals $I, J$ are **comaximal** if $I + J = R$.

**Theorem (CRT).** If $I_1, \dots, I_n$ are pairwise comaximal, then $R \to \prod R/I_k$ is surjective with kernel $\bigcap I_k = I_1\cdots I_n$, so $R/\bigcap I_k \cong \prod R/I_k$.

*Proof for $n = 2$.* Write $1 = a + b$ with $a \in I$, $b \in J$. For $(x,y)$ put $r = xb + ya$. Modulo $I$, $a \equiv 0$ and $b \equiv 1$, so $r \equiv x$; modulo $J$, $r \equiv y$. The kernel is $I \cap J$, and $I\cap J = (I\cap J)(a+b) \subseteq IJ \subseteq I \cap J$. For general $n$ induct, using that $I_1\cdots I_{n-1}$ is comaximal with $I_n$ (multiply $1 = a_k + b_k$ over $k < n$). $\blacksquare$

### Factorization in domains

In a domain, $p$ is **irreducible** if it is a nonunit that is not a product of two nonunits, and **prime** if $(p)$ is a prime ideal. Prime elements are irreducible; in a PID irreducibles are prime. The hierarchy is

$$\text{field} \subset \text{Euclidean domain} \subset \text{PID} \subset \text{UFD} \subset \text{domain},$$

and every inclusion is strict: $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is a UFD (by Gauss's lemma, stated here without proof) but not a PID, since $(2,x)$ is not principal; $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is a domain that is not a UFD (Example 1).

### Localization

For a multiplicative set $S$ define $S^{-1}R$ as the set of fractions $r/s$ with $r/s = r'/s'$ when $t(rs' - r's) = 0$ for some $t \in S$. It is a ring with $R \to S^{-1}R$, $r \mapsto r/1$. **Universal property:** if $f\colon R\to T$ is a ring homomorphism with $f(S) \subseteq T^\times$, there is a unique homomorphism $S^{-1}R \to T$ with $r/s \mapsto f(r)f(s)^{-1}$. Also $S^{-1}R = 0$ if and only if $0 \in S$.

**Prime ideals of a localization (outline).** The maps $P \mapsto S^{-1}P$ and $Q \mapsto Q \cap R$ (contraction) are inverse bijections between prime ideals of $R$ disjoint from $S$ and prime ideals of $S^{-1}R$; one checks that $Q\cap R$ is prime and that $S^{-1}P\cap R = P$ for $P$ disjoint from $S$. For a prime $P$, take $S = R\setminus P$: the **local ring** $R_P$ has the unique maximal ideal $PR_P$, and its primes correspond to the primes of $R$ contained in $P$.

The code applies the CRT to two congruences with coprime moduli.

```python
def egcd(a, b):
    """Return (g, x, y) with a*x + b*y == g == gcd(a, b)."""
    if b == 0:
        return a, 1, 0
    g, x, y = egcd(b, a % b)
    return g, y, x - (a // b) * y


def crt_pair(r1, m1, r2, m2):
    """Solve x = r1 (mod m1), x = r2 (mod m2) when gcd(m1, m2) = 1."""
    _, u, v = egcd(m1, m2)                    # m1*u + m2*v = 1
    return (r1 * m2 * v + r2 * m1 * u) % (m1 * m2)


print(crt_pair(2, 9, 5, 11))                  # 38
```

Here $38 \equiv 2 \pmod 9$ and $38 \equiv 5 \pmod{11}$; the function is the CRT proof ($a = m_1u$, $b = m_2v$). It fails silently when the moduli share a factor.
@example
title: Z[√-5] is not a UFD
problem: Show that $2$ is irreducible but not prime in $R = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, and that $6$ has two different factorizations into irreducibles.
step: Use the norm $N(a + b\sqrt{-5}) = a^2 + 5b^2$, which is multiplicative. A unit has norm $1$, so the units are $\pm 1$, and no element has norm $2$ or $3$ (the equation $a^2 + 5b^2 = 2$ or $3$ has no integer solution).
step: Irreducible: if $2 = \alpha\beta$ with nonunits, then $N(\alpha)N(\beta) = 4$ with both factors greater than $1$, so $N(\alpha) = 2$, impossible. The same argument shows $3$ is irreducible. For $1\pm\sqrt{-5}$, of norm $6$, a splitting would need norms $2$ and $3$, impossible. So all four are irreducible.
step: Not prime: $2 \mid 6 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$, but $2 \nmid 1\pm\sqrt{-5}$, because $(1\pm\sqrt{-5})/2$ is not in $R$.
step: Two factorizations: $6 = 2\cdot 3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$. The irreducibles $2$ and $1 \pm\sqrt{-5}$ are not associates (the only associates of $2$ are $\pm2$). So factorization into irreducibles is not unique.
answer: $2$ is irreducible, not prime, and $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is not a UFD.
@example
title: The local ring Z_(2)
problem: Describe the ring $\mathbb{Z}_{(2)}$ of fractions $a/b$ with $b$ odd, its units, and its prime ideals.
step: It is the localization $S^{-1}\mathbb{Z}$ with $S = \mathbb{Z}\setminus(2)$, the set of odd integers, which is multiplicative.
step: Units: $a/b$ is a unit exactly when $a$ is odd, because then $b/a$ is also in the ring; if $a$ is even then $a/b \in 2\mathbb{Z}_{(2)}$, which does not contain $1$.
step: Hence the nonunits form the ideal $2\mathbb{Z}_{(2)}$, which is therefore the unique maximal ideal, and $\mathbb{Z}_{(2)}$ is local.
step: Primes of $\mathbb{Z}$ disjoint from $S$ are $(0)$ and $(2)$ (every other nonzero prime $(q)$ contains the odd number $q$). So the primes of $\mathbb{Z}_{(2)}$ are $(0)$ and $2\mathbb{Z}_{(2)}$.
answer: $\mathbb{Z}_{(2)}$ is local with exactly two primes, $(0)$ and $2\mathbb{Z}_{(2)}$.
@mistakes
- Treating "prime" and "irreducible" as the same in every domain. In $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ the element $2$ is irreducible but not prime; they agree in a PID.
- Asserting a prime ideal is maximal. This holds in $\mathbb{Z}/n$ and in other finite rings but fails for $(x)$ in $\mathbb{Q}[x,y]$ and for $(0)$ in $\mathbb{Z}$.
- Applying the CRT to ideals that are not comaximal. The map $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/4\times\mathbb{Z}/6$ is not onto, because $(4)+(6) = (2) \ne \mathbb{Z}$.
- Forgetting that $S^{-1}R$ can collapse: if $S$ contains a zero divisor, $R\to S^{-1}R$ is not injective, and if $0 \in S$ the localization is the zero ring.

```python
def crt(r1, m1, r2, m2):
    """Solve x = r1 (mod m1), x = r2 (mod m2); return (x, lcm) or None."""
    def egcd(a, b):
        if b == 0:
            return a, 1, 0
        g, x, y = egcd(b, a % b)
        return g, y, x - (a // b) * y

    g, u, _ = egcd(m1, m2)                    # m1*u + m2*v = g
    if (r2 - r1) % g != 0:
        return None
    lcm = m1 // g * m2
    t = ((r2 - r1) // g * u) % (m2 // g)
    return (r1 + m1 * t) % lcm, lcm
```
hint: A solution exists exactly when $r_1 \equiv r_2 \pmod{\gcd(m_1,m_2)}$.
hint: Write $x = r_1 + m_1 t$ and solve $m_1 t \equiv r_2 - r_1 \pmod{m_2}$ after dividing by the gcd.
solution: Let $g = \gcd(m_1, m_2)$ and $m_1u + m_2v = g$. The congruence $m_1 t \equiv r_2 - r_1 \pmod{m_2}$ is solvable if and only if $g \mid r_2 - r_1$; then dividing by $g$ gives $(m_1/g)t \equiv (r_2-r_1)/g \pmod{m_2/g}$, solved by $t = u\,(r_2 - r_1)/g$. The solution is unique modulo $\mathrm{lcm}(m_1,m_2)$. The coprime formula of the lesson is the case $g = 1$; it would return a wrong residue when $g > 1$.

@quiz self
prompt: Let $P$ be a prime ideal of a commutative ring $R$ and $S = R\setminus P$. Prove that the localization $R_P = S^{-1}R$ is a local ring: its nonunits form an ideal.
hint: Decide when $a/s$ is a unit, and show that $a/s$ with $a \in P$ is a nonunit.
hint: You need to show $a/s = p/t$ with $p \in P$ forces $a \in P$.
solution: Let $a/s \in R_P$ with $a\in R$, $s\in S$. If $a \notin P$ then $a \in S$, so $s/a \in R_P$ and $(a/s)(s/a) = 1$: $a/s$ is a unit. Now let $I = PR_P = \{p/s : p \in P,\ s \in S\}$, an ideal (it is the image of $P$ extended). If $a/s \in I$, say $a/s = p/t$ with $p\in P$, $t \in S$, then $u(at - ps) = 0$ for some $u \in S$, so $uta = ups \in P$. Since $u, t \notin P$ and $P$ is prime, $a \in P$. Thus $a/s \in I$ if and only if $a \in P$. If $a \in P$, then $a/s$ is not a unit: a unit $a/s$ has $(a/s)(b/t) = 1$ for some $b/t$, so $v(ab - st) = 0$ with $v\in S$, then $vst = vab \in P$, contradicting $v,s,t\notin P$. Hence the nonunits are exactly $I$, an ideal, and $I$ is the unique maximal ideal: any proper ideal consists of nonunits, so lies in $I$. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Shows $a/s$ is a unit when $a \notin P$ by exhibiting the inverse $s/a$.
rubric: Shows that $a/s \in PR_P$ if and only if $a \in P$, using the definition of equality of fractions and primality of $P$.
rubric: Shows that elements with $a \in P$ are not units, concludes the nonunits form the ideal $PR_P$, and that it is the unique maximal ideal.
