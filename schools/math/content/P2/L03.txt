@article
Every group generated by two elements $a, b$ is a quotient of a "universal" such group with no relations at all. The **free group** is that group, and a **presentation** says which relations to impose. Qualifying exams ask you to show that a given set of generators and relators defines a specific group, so you need a repeatable recipe.

### The free group

Let $X$ be a set. A **word** is a finite sequence of letters $x$ and $x^{-1}$ ($x \in X$); it is **reduced** if no letter is next to its inverse. The **free group** $F(X)$ is the set of words modulo the equivalence generated by deleting a pair $xx^{-1}$ or $x^{-1}x$, with multiplication by concatenation.

**Theorem (normal form).** Every element of $F(X)$ is represented by exactly one reduced word.

*Proof.* Every word reduces to a reduced word by deleting cancelling pairs, so existence holds. For uniqueness (the van der Waerden argument), let $W$ be the set of reduced words. Each letter $y \in X \cup X^{-1}$ gives a map $W \to W$: put $y$ in front of $w$ and cancel if $w$ begins with $y^{-1}$. This map is a bijection with inverse given by $y^{-1}$. So we get a homomorphism from the free monoid on $X\cup X^{-1}$ to $\mathrm{Sym}(W)$ in which $xx^{-1}$ and $x^{-1}x$ act as the identity; it factors through $F(X)$. A reduced word $w = y_1\cdots y_k$ sends the empty word to $w$. Hence two different reduced words act differently, so they are different elements of $F(X)$. $\blacksquare$

**Universal property.** For every group $G$ and map $f\colon X \to G$ there is exactly one homomorphism $F(X) \to G$ extending $f$. (Define it on words by $x^{-1} \mapsto f(x)^{-1}$; it respects cancellation, and uniqueness holds because $X$ generates.)

### Presentations and von Dyck's theorem

Given $R \subseteq F(X)$, let $N$ be the normal closure of $R$ (the smallest normal subgroup containing it). The group $\langle X \mid R\rangle = F(X)/N$ is the group with **generators** $X$ and **relators** $R$.

**Theorem (von Dyck).** Let $G$ be a group and $g_x \in G$ for $x \in X$ such that each relator $r \in R$ evaluates to $1$ when each $x$ is replaced by $g_x$. Then $x \mapsto g_x$ extends to a homomorphism $\langle X \mid R\rangle \to G$, and it is onto if the $g_x$ generate $G$.

*Proof.* The universal property gives $\varphi\colon F(X) \to G$ with $\varphi(r) = 1$ for $r \in R$. Its kernel is normal and contains $R$, hence contains $N$. So $\varphi$ factors through $F(X)/N$. $\blacksquare$

**Recipe to prove $\langle X \mid R\rangle \cong G$ for a finite group $G$ generated by elements $g_x$.** (1) Check the relators hold in $G$: von Dyck gives a surjection $\langle X\mid R\rangle \to G$, so $|\langle X\mid R\rangle| \ge |G|$. (2) Use the relators to put every word in a normal form with at most $|G|$ possibilities. Then $|\langle X\mid R\rangle| \le |G|$, and the surjection between finite sets of equal size is an isomorphism. Skipping step (2) proves nothing: $\mathbb{Z}/2 \times \mathbb{Z}/2$ satisfies the relators $a^2, b^2$, yet $\langle a,b \mid a^2, b^2\rangle$ is the infinite dihedral group.

### Subgroups and abelianization

**Theorem (Nielsen–Schreier), stated without proof.** Every subgroup of a free group is free. If $F$ is free of finite rank $r$ and $H \le F$ has finite index $n$, then $H$ is free of rank $n(r-1) + 1$. So $F_2$ contains free groups of every finite rank, and rank does not decrease on passing to a subgroup of finite index.

**Abelianization.** By the universal property of the quotient, $\langle X\mid R\rangle^{\mathrm{ab}} = G/[G,G]$ is $\mathbb{Z}^X$ modulo the subgroup spanned by the exponent-sum vectors of the relators (the vector whose $x$-entry is the total exponent of $x$ in $r$). This is a quotient of $\mathbb{Z}^n$ by a lattice, computed by integer row operations (Week 6). Abelianization gives cheap invariants: if two presentations have different abelianizations, the groups differ.

**The word problem.** In general no algorithm decides whether a word is trivial in $\langle X\mid R\rangle$ (Novikov and Boone). For small groups, one uses coset enumeration (Todd–Coxeter) or normal forms.

Words are lists of nonzero integers: $k$ is the generator $x_k$ and $-k$ its inverse. Free reduction with a stack:

```python
def reduce_word(w):
    """Freely reduce a word given as a list of nonzero integers."""
    out = []
    for letter in w:
        if out and out[-1] == -letter:
            out.pop()
        else:
            out.append(letter)
    return out


print(reduce_word([1, 2, -2, -1, 3, 3, -3]))   # [3]
```

Because the stack always holds a reduced word, one left-to-right pass suffices; the normal-form theorem says the answer does not depend on the order of cancellations.
@example
title: The dihedral group has the presentation with r^n, s^2 and (sr)^2
problem: Prove that $\langle r, s \mid r^n,\ s^2,\ (sr)^2\rangle$ ($n \ge 3$) is isomorphic to the group $D_n$ of symmetries of a regular $n$-gon, which has order $2n$.
step: Lower bound. In $D_n$ let $r$ be the rotation by $2\pi/n$ and $s$ a reflection. Then $r^n = 1$, $s^2 = 1$ and $sr$ is a reflection, so $(sr)^2 = 1$. The elements $r, s$ generate $D_n$, so by von Dyck there is a surjection $G \to D_n$ and $|G| \ge 2n$.
step: Upper bound. In $G$, $(sr)^2 = 1$ gives $srs = r^{-1}$, and since $s = s^{-1}$ we get $s r s^{-1} = r^{-1}$. So conjugation by $s$ maps $\langle r\rangle$ to itself, as does conjugation by $r$, so $\langle r \rangle$ is normal in $G$.
step: Since $G = \langle r, s\rangle$, $\langle r\rangle$ is normal and $s^2 = 1$, every element is $r^i s^j$ with $0 \le i < n$ and $0 \le j < 2$. So $|G| \le 2n$.
step: The surjection $G \to D_n$ is a surjection between finite sets of the same size $2n$, so it is an isomorphism.
answer: The group is $D_n$, of order $2n$.
@example
title: Abelianization from exponent sums
problem: Find the abelianization of $G = \langle a, b \mid a^2,\ b^3,\ (ab)^3\rangle$ and of $H = \langle r, s \mid r^4,\ s^2,\ (sr)^2\rangle$.
step: For $G$, the exponent-sum vectors in $\mathbb{Z}^2$ of $a^2$, $b^3$, $(ab)^3$ are $(2,0)$, $(0,3)$ and $(3,3)$. We compute $\mathbb{Z}^2$ modulo the span $L$ of these vectors.
step: Subtract $(0,3)$ from $(3,3)$ to get $(3,0)$. Then $(3,0)$ and $(2,0)$ give $(3,0) - (2,0) = (1,0)$, so $L$ contains $(1,0)$ and $(0,3)$. It equals the span of those two, since $(2,0) = 2(1,0)$ and $(3,3) = 3(1,0) + (0,3)$.
step: So $G^{\mathrm{ab}} \cong \mathbb{Z}^2/\langle (1,0),(0,3)\rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}/3$. (This is consistent with $G \cong A_4$, whose abelianization is $A_4/V_4 \cong \mathbb{Z}/3$.)
step: For $H$, the vectors are $(4,0)$, $(0,2)$ and $(2,2)$. Then $(2,2) - (0,2) = (2,0)$ and $(4,0) = 2(2,0)$, so $L = \langle (2,0),(0,2)\rangle$ and $H^{\mathrm{ab}} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\times\mathbb{Z}/2$, of order $4$, as expected for the dihedral group of order $8$ (its commutator subgroup has order $2$).
answer: $G^{\mathrm{ab}} \cong \mathbb{Z}/3$ and $H^{\mathrm{ab}} \cong (\mathbb{Z}/2)^2$.
@mistakes
- Verifying only that the relators hold in $G$ and concluding $\langle X \mid R\rangle \cong G$. That proves only a surjection onto $G$; you also need an upper bound on the order of the presented group.
- Forgetting that the normal closure of $R$ is divided out, not merely the subgroup generated by $R$. For example the relator $srs^{-1}r$ does not make $r$ trivial; it forces conjugates of itself to be trivial too.
- Reading a presentation with no more relators than generators as always finite. The free abelian group $\mathbb{Z}^2 = \langle a,b \mid [a,b]\rangle$ and the group $\langle a,b \mid a^2, b^2\rangle$ have infinite order.
- Applying the abelianization rule to relators by their length instead of their exponent sums: only the total exponent of each generator matters, so $aba^{-1}b$ contributes $(0,2)$.

model:
```python
def satisfies(images, relators):
    """True if every relator word is the identity under the given images."""
    n = len(images[0])
    inverses = []
    for p in images:
        q = [0] * n
        for i, x in enumerate(p):
            q[x] = i
        inverses.append(q)
    for word in relators:
        for start in range(n):
            point = start
            for letter in word:
                if letter > 0:
                    point = images[letter - 1][point]
                else:
                    point = inverses[-letter - 1][point]
            if point != start:
                return False
    return True
```
hint: Follow each starting point through the letters of the word, applying the permutation for a positive letter and its inverse for a negative letter.
hint: A relator is the identity only if every starting point returns to itself.
solution: Evaluate each relator on every point $0,\dots,n-1$: apply the permutation of each letter in order, using the inverse permutation for a negative letter, and require the point to return to its start. The order of application matters; applying letters from right to left evaluates a different word, and the last test, with two generators of $S_5$, is a relator that is the identity in one order only.

solution: Upper bound. In $G$, $jij^{-1} = i^{-1}$, so $\langle i\rangle$ is normalized by $j$ (and by $i$), hence is normal in $G = \langle i, j\rangle$; its order is at most $4$ since $i^4 = 1$. The quotient $G/\langle i\rangle$ is generated by the image of $j$, and $j^2 = i^2 \in \langle i\rangle$, so the quotient has order at most $2$. Hence $|G| \le 4\cdot 2 = 8$. Lower bound. In $Q_8$ put $i \mapsto \mathbf{i}$, $j \mapsto \mathbf{j}$: $\mathbf{i}^4 = 1$, $\mathbf{j}^2 = -1 = \mathbf{i}^2$, and $\mathbf{j}\mathbf{i}\mathbf{j}^{-1} = \mathbf{j}\mathbf{i}(-\mathbf{j}) = -\mathbf{j}\mathbf{i}\mathbf{j} = -(-\mathbf{k})\mathbf{j} = \mathbf{k}\mathbf{j} = -\mathbf{i} = \mathbf{i}^{-1}$. Since $\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}$ generate $Q_8$, von Dyck gives a surjection $G \to Q_8$, so $|G| \ge 8$. Thus $|G| = 8$ and the surjection $G \to Q_8$ is a bijection, an isomorphism. $\blacksquare$

@quiz num
prompt: Let $G = \langle a, b \mid a^4,\ b^6,\ a^2b^3\rangle$. The abelianization $G^{\mathrm{ab}}$ is finite. What is its order?
answer: 12
check: math.gcd(math.gcd(4 * 6 - 0 * 0, 4 * 3 - 0 * 2), 0 * 3 - 6 * 2)
hint: The relator exponent-sum vectors are $(4,0)$, $(0,6)$ and $(2,3)$ in $\mathbb{Z}^2$.
hint: Use integer row operations to reduce the lattice, or note that the order of $\mathbb{Z}^2/L$ equals the gcd of the $2\times 2$ minors of the spanning vectors.
solution: Rows $(4,0)$, $(0,6)$, $(2,3)$. Subtract $(2,3)$ twice from $(4,0)$: $(0,-6)$, equivalent to $(0,6)$. So the lattice is spanned by $(2,3)$ and $(0,6)$, and $\mathbb{Z}^2/L$ has order $|\det| = |2\cdot 6 - 3\cdot 0| = 12$. In fact it is cyclic, generated by the class of $(1,1)$ (the map $(x,y)\mapsto 3x+2y \bmod 12$ is onto and kills $L$).

```python
def cyclic_reduce(w):
    """Freely reduce w, then strip matching inverse ends."""
    out = []
    for letter in w:
        if out and out[-1] == -letter:
            out.pop()
        else:
            out.append(letter)
    start, end = 0, len(out)
    while end - start >= 2 and out[start] == -out[end - 1]:
        start += 1
        end -= 1
    return out[start:end]
```
hint: Reduce first with a stack. Only then compare the first and last letters.
solution: Stripping the ends of an unreduced word is wrong: in `[1, 2, -2, 3, -1]` the ends `1` and `-1` cancel and leave `[2, -2, 3]`, which still needs reduction. Reducing first gives `[1, 3, -1]`, and then stripping the matching ends leaves `[3]`. A reduced word of length $1$ is already cyclically reduced because a single letter is not the inverse of itself.
