@article
Why can the general quintic not be solved by radicals while the quartic can? The group-theoretic half of the answer is a dichotomy between groups built from abelian pieces (solvable) and groups, like $A_5$, that contain a nonabelian simple piece. This week gives the tools; Week 11 applies them.

### Composition series and Jordan–Hölder

A **subnormal series** of $G$ is $G = G_0 \trianglerighteq G_1 \trianglerighteq \dots \trianglerighteq G_r = 1$, each $G_{i+1}$ normal in $G_i$. It is a **composition series** if every factor $G_i/G_{i+1}$ is simple. Every finite group has one: choose a maximal proper normal subgroup $G_1$ of $G$ (so $G/G_1$ is simple) and repeat in $G_1$.

**Theorem (Jordan–Hölder).** Let $G$ be a finite group. Any two composition series of $G$ have the same length, and their lists of factors agree up to reordering and isomorphism.

*Proof.* Induct on $|G|$; the case $G = 1$ is trivial. Let $G \trianglerighteq G_1 \trianglerighteq \cdots$ and $G \trianglerighteq H_1 \trianglerighteq \cdots$ be composition series. If $G_1 = H_1$, apply induction to $G_1$. Otherwise, $G_1H_1$ is a normal subgroup containing $G_1$; since $G_1 \ne H_1$ and $H_1$ is maximal normal, $H_1 \not\subseteq G_1$ (if $H_1 \subseteq G_1 \subsetneq G$ then maximality forces $H_1 = G_1$), so $G_1H_1 \supsetneq G_1$, and maximality of $G_1$ gives $G_1H_1 = G$. Put $K = G_1 \cap H_1$. By the second isomorphism theorem, $G_1/K \cong G_1H_1/H_1 = G/H_1$ and $H_1/K \cong G/G_1$, both simple. Choose a composition series of $K$. Then $G_1 \trianglerighteq K \trianglerighteq \cdots$ and $G_1 \trianglerighteq G_2 \trianglerighteq \cdots$ are composition series of $G_1$, so by induction they have the same factors; the first has factors $G/H_1$ and those of $K$. So the factors of the $G_i$-series are $G/G_1$, $G/H_1$ and those of $K$. Symmetrically the $H_i$-series has factors $G/H_1$, $G/G_1$ and those of $K$. $\blacksquare$

The factors do not determine the group: $\mathbb{Z}/4$ and $(\mathbb{Z}/2)^2$ both have two factors $\mathbb{Z}/2$.

### Solvable groups

$G$ is **solvable** if it has a subnormal series with abelian factors. The **derived series** is $G^{(0)} = G$, $G^{(k+1)} = [G^{(k)}, G^{(k)}]$. A group is solvable if and only if $G^{(k)} = 1$ for some $k$ (an abelian-factor series forces each term to contain the next derived subgroup; conversely the derived series itself has abelian factors). A finite group is solvable if and only if its composition factors are cyclic of prime order.

**Theorem.** (a) Subgroups and quotients of solvable groups are solvable. (b) If $N \trianglelefteq G$ and both $N$ and $G/N$ are solvable, then $G$ is solvable.

*Proof.* (a) $H^{(k)} \subseteq G^{(k)}$ for $H \le G$, and $(G/N)^{(k)} = G^{(k)}N/N$. (b) If $(G/N)^{(k)} = 1$ then $G^{(k)} \subseteq N$, and if $N^{(l)} = 1$ then $G^{(k+l)} = (G^{(k)})^{(l)} \subseteq N^{(l)} = 1$. $\blacksquare$

$S_n$ is solvable if and only if $n \le 4$, since $A_n$ is simple and nonabelian for $n \ge 5$ (recalled from C11). Two deep criteria, **stated here without proof**: Burnside's $p^aq^b$ theorem (1904) says a group of order $p^aq^b$ ($p, q$ primes) is solvable; the Feit–Thompson theorem (1963) says every group of odd order is solvable.

### Nilpotent groups

The **upper central series** is $Z_0 = 1$ and $Z_{i+1}/Z_i = Z(G/Z_i)$. $G$ is **nilpotent** of **class** $c$ if $Z_c = G$ with $c$ least. Nilpotent implies solvable.

**Theorem.** A finite $p$-group $G$ is nilpotent. *Proof.* The class equation gives $|G| = |Z(G)| + \sum [G:C_G(x_i)]$ with every index a positive power of $p$, so $p \mid |Z(G)|$ and $Z(G) \ne 1$. If $Z_i \ne G$, then $G/Z_i$ is a nontrivial $p$-group with nontrivial center, so $Z_{i+1} \supsetneq Z_i$. The series strictly increases until it reaches $G$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem.** For a finite group $G$ the following are equivalent: (i) $G$ is nilpotent; (ii) every proper subgroup $H$ satisfies $H \subsetneq N_G(H)$; (iii) every Sylow subgroup is normal; (iv) $G$ is the direct product of its Sylow subgroups.

*Proof of (i)$\Rightarrow$(ii).* Let $H \subsetneq G$ and let $i$ be largest with $Z_i \subseteq H$ (so $i < c$). Take $x \in Z_{i+1}\setminus H$. For $h \in H$, $xZ_i$ is central in $G/Z_i$, so $[x,h] \in Z_i \subseteq H$ and $xhx^{-1} = [x,h]h \in H$. Thus $x \in N_G(H)\setminus H$. *Proof of (ii)$\Rightarrow$(iii).* Let $P$ be Sylow and $N = N_G(P)$. If $g \in N_G(N)$ then $gPg^{-1} \subseteq N$ is a Sylow subgroup of $N$; $P$ is normal in $N$, hence the unique Sylow $p$-subgroup of $N$ by Sylow 2, so $gPg^{-1} = P$ and $g \in N$. So $N_G(N) = N$, and (ii) forces $N = G$. $\blacksquare$ The step (iii)$\Rightarrow$(iv) is Practice 5 below, and (iv)$\Rightarrow$(i) holds because $Z_i(A\times B) = Z_i(A)\times Z_i(B)$ and $p$-groups are nilpotent.

Nilpotent is strictly stronger than solvable: $S_3$ is solvable but $Z(S_3) = 1$.

The code lists the orders $|Z_0|, |Z_1|, \dots$ for the symmetries of a regular octagon, as permutations of $\{0,\dots,7\}$ (a list `p` with `p[i]` the image of `i`; here tuples).

```python
def mul(g, h):
    return tuple(g[h[i]] for i in range(len(g)))     # apply h first


def inverse(g):
    r = [0] * len(g)
    for i, x in enumerate(g):
        r[x] = i
    return tuple(r)


def upper_central_orders(G):
    """Orders of Z_0, Z_1, ... for a group G given as a list of tuples."""
    Z = {tuple(range(len(G[0])))}
    orders = [1]
    while len(Z) < len(G):
        Z = {x for x in G
             if all(mul(mul(x, g), mul(inverse(x), inverse(g))) in Z
                    for g in G)}
        orders.append(len(Z))
    return orders


n = 8
G = [tuple((i + k) % n for i in range(n)) for k in range(n)]
G += [tuple((k - i) % n for i in range(n)) for k in range(n)]
print(upper_central_orders(G))
```

It prints `[1, 2, 4, 16]`, so the dihedral group of order $16$ has class $3$. (The loop would run forever on a group with $Z_{i+1} = Z_i \ne G$, such as $S_3$; a robust version stops when the order stops growing.)

title: Series of S_4
problem: Find a composition series of $S_4$ and its derived series, and decide whether $S_4$ is solvable and nilpotent.
step: Take $V = \{e, (1\,2)(3\,4), (1\,3)(2\,4), (1\,4)(2\,3)\}$. Then $S_4 \trianglerighteq A_4 \trianglerighteq V \trianglerighteq \langle (1\,2)(3\,4)\rangle \trianglerighteq 1$ is a subnormal series: $V$ is normal in $S_4$ (a union of conjugacy classes) and the last two terms are normal because $V$ is abelian.
step: The factors have orders $2$, $3$, $2$, $2$ (indices $24/12$, $12/4$, $4/2$, $2/1$), all prime, hence simple. So this is a composition series, with factors $\mathbb{Z}/2, \mathbb{Z}/3, \mathbb{Z}/2, \mathbb{Z}/2$, all cyclic of prime order.
step: The derived series is $S_4' = A_4$: $S_4/A_4$ is abelian, so $S_4' \subseteq A_4$, and $S_4'$ is a normal subgroup containing the commutator $(1\,2)(1\,3)(1\,2)(1\,3)$ of two transpositions, which is a $3$-cycle, hence all $3$-cycles, which generate $A_4$. Next $A_4' = V$: $A_4/V$ has order $3$, so $A_4' \subseteq V$; $A_4$ is nonabelian so $A_4' \ne 1$, and a $3$-cycle permutes the three involutions of $V$ transitively, so no proper nontrivial subgroup of $V$ is normal in $A_4$. Finally $V' = 1$. So $S_4^{(3)} = 1$ and $S_4$ is solvable.
step: It is not nilpotent: a nilpotent group has all Sylow subgroups normal, but $S_4$ has $n_3 = 4$ Sylow $3$-subgroups.
answer: Composition factors $\mathbb{Z}/2, \mathbb{Z}/3, \mathbb{Z}/2, \mathbb{Z}/2$; derived length $3$; solvable, not nilpotent.
@example
title: A_4 is solvable but not nilpotent
problem: Show that $A_4$ is solvable, and that its upper central series never reaches $A_4$.
step: $V = \{e, (1\,2)(3\,4), (1\,3)(2\,4), (1\,4)(2\,3)\}$ is a normal abelian subgroup of $A_4$ with $A_4/V$ of order $3$, hence cyclic. By part (b) of the extension theorem, $A_4$ is solvable.
step: Compute $Z(A_4)$. An element of $A_4$ commuting with all of $A_4$ lies in the centralizer of $(1\,2\,3)$, which is $\langle (1\,2\,3)\rangle$ of order $3$, and in the centralizer of $(1\,2\,4)$, which is $\langle (1\,2\,4)\rangle$; these meet only in $e$. So $Z(A_4) = 1$.
step: Then $Z_1 = 1$ and $Z_2/Z_1 = Z(A_4/1) = 1$, and so on: $Z_i = 1$ for all $i$, never $A_4$. So $A_4$ is not nilpotent.
step: This agrees with criterion (iii): $A_4$ has four Sylow $3$-subgroups, so they are not normal.
answer: $A_4$ is solvable with $Z(A_4) = 1$, hence not nilpotent.
@mistakes
- Confusing solvable with nilpotent. $S_3$ has composition factors of prime order and is solvable, but $Z(S_3) = 1$, so it is not nilpotent.
- Reading Jordan–Hölder as saying the composition series themselves coincide, or that the factors determine the group. Only the multiset of factors, up to isomorphism, is an invariant; $\mathbb{Z}/4$ and $(\mathbb{Z}/2)^2$ share theirs.
- Computing the derived subgroup as the subgroup generated by commutators of the generators. It is the normal subgroup generated by all commutators, usually larger.
- Applying "normal subgroups with solvable quotient" in the wrong direction: a group with a solvable normal subgroup need not be solvable ($A_5$ has the solvable normal subgroup $1$).

model:
```python
def derived_length(gens, n):
    """Length of the derived series of <gens>, or None if not solvable."""
    def mul(g, h):
        return tuple(g[h[i]] for i in range(n))

    def inverse(g):
        r = [0] * n
        for i, x in enumerate(g):
            r[x] = i
        return tuple(r)

    def generate(gs):
        identity = tuple(range(n))
        group = {identity}
        frontier = [identity]
        while frontier:
            new = []
            for x in frontier:
                for g in gs:
                    y = mul(g, x)
                    if y not in group:
                        group.add(y)
                        new.append(y)
            frontier = new
        return group

    group = generate([tuple(g) for g in gens])
    k = 0
    while len(group) > 1:
        comms = {mul(mul(a, b), mul(inverse(a), inverse(b)))
                 for a in group for b in group}
        nxt = generate(list(comms))
        if len(nxt) == len(group):
            return None
        group = nxt
        k += 1
    return k
```
hint: Generate the whole group, then form the commutators of all pairs of its elements, not only of the generators.
hint: The derived subgroup is generated by those commutators. Stop when the group becomes trivial, or when a step does not shrink it.
solution: Repeat the step $G \mapsto G'$, where $G'$ is generated by the commutators of all pairs of elements, until the group is trivial (return the number of steps) or does not shrink (the series is stuck at a perfect nontrivial group, so return `None`). @practice self
prompt: Let $G$ be a finite group in which every Sylow subgroup is normal. Prove that $G$ is the internal direct product of its Sylow subgroups, and deduce that $G$ is nilpotent.
hint: Let $P_1, \dots, P_k$ be the Sylow subgroups for the distinct primes dividing $|G|$. Show two normal subgroups with trivial intersection commute elementwise.
hint: Compare $|P_1\cdots P_k|$ with $|G|$, and recall that finite $p$-groups are nilpotent.
solution: Let $p_1,\dots,p_k$ be the primes dividing $|G|$ and $P_i$ the (unique, normal) Sylow $p_i$-subgroup. For $i \ne j$, $P_i \cap P_j = 1$, since the order of the intersection divides both $|P_i|$ and $|P_j|$, which are powers of distinct primes. For $x \in P_i$, $y \in P_j$, the commutator $xyx^{-1}y^{-1}$ lies in $P_j$ (as $xyx^{-1} \in P_j$) and in $P_i$ (as $x (yx^{-1}y^{-1}) \in P_i$), hence equals $1$, so $P_i$ and $P_j$ commute elementwise. By induction on $m$, $P_1\cdots P_m$ is a subgroup (a product of normal subgroups) of order $|P_1|\cdots|P_m|$, because $P_{m+1} \cap P_1\cdots P_m = 1$ (orders are coprime). For $m = k$ the product has order $|G|$, so $G = P_1\cdots P_k$, and the map $P_1\times\cdots\times P_k \to G$, $(x_1,\dots,x_k)\mapsto x_1\cdots x_k$ is a homomorphism (the factors commute) that is surjective, hence bijective by order. Each $P_i$ is nilpotent, and $Z_c(A\times B) = Z_c(A)\times Z_c(B)$, so a finite direct product of nilpotent groups is nilpotent. $\blacksquare$
