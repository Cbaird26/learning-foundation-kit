@article
The extension $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ has degree $3$ but only the identity automorphism, while $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2},\omega)/\mathbb{Q}$, with $\omega$ a primitive cube root of unity, has degree $6$ and six automorphisms. Galois theory explains the difference and turns questions about fields into questions about finite groups. C11 introduced the main objects; this lesson gives the proofs a qualifying exam asks for.

### Separable and normal extensions

An algebraic element is **separable** over $F$ if its minimal polynomial has distinct roots in an algebraic closure; an algebraic extension is separable if all its elements are. For an irreducible $f\in F[x]$: $f$ has a repeated root if and only if $\gcd(f,f')\ne1$ if and only if $f\mid f'$ (the gcd divides the irreducible $f$) if and only if $f'=0$, since $\deg f'<\deg f$. So irreducible polynomials are separable in characteristic $0$ and, in characteristic $p$, exactly when $f\notin F[x^p]$. Finite fields are **perfect**: every irreducible polynomial is separable. The standard inseparable example is $x^p-t\in\mathbb{F}_p(t)[x]$: it is irreducible, and equals $(x-t^{1/p})^p$ in an extension.

A finite extension $K/F$ is **normal** if every irreducible polynomial of $F[x]$ with a root in $K$ splits in $K$; equivalently $K$ is the splitting field of some polynomial. It is **Galois** if it is normal and separable, equivalently the splitting field of a separable polynomial, equivalently $|\mathrm{Aut}(K/F)|=[K:F]$; then $\mathrm{Gal}(K/F)=\mathrm{Aut}(K/F)$. In general $|\mathrm{Aut}(K/F)|\le[K:F]$ (recalled from C11).

### Artin's theorem and the fundamental theorem

**Theorem (Artin).** Let $K$ be a field, $G$ a finite group of automorphisms of $K$, and $F=K^G$ the fixed field. Then $[K:F]=|G|$, and $\mathrm{Gal}(K/F)=G$.

*Proof.* Let $G=\{\sigma_1=\mathrm{id},\dots,\sigma_n\}$. Since $G\subseteq\mathrm{Aut}(K/F)$, $n\le|\mathrm{Aut}(K/F)|\le[K:F]$. Suppose $[K:F]>n$, and take $F$-linearly independent $a_1,\dots,a_{n+1}\in K$. The $n$ equations $\sum_j\sigma_i(a_j)x_j=0$ ($1\le i\le n$) in $n+1$ unknowns have a nonzero solution in $K$. Choose one with the fewest nonzero entries and scale so that some $x_r=1$. For $\tau\in G$, applying $\tau$ to the equations (and permuting them, since $\sigma\mapsto\tau\sigma$ permutes $G$) shows $\tau(x)$ is also a solution, so $x-\tau(x)$ is a solution with $r$-th entry $0$ and fewer nonzero entries; by minimality it is $0$. Thus every $x_j$ is fixed by $G$, i.e. lies in $F$. The equation for $\sigma_1=\mathrm{id}$ is $\sum a_jx_j=0$, an $F$-linear dependence, a contradiction. So $[K:F]=n$, and then $|\mathrm{Aut}(K/F)|=n$ gives $\mathrm{Gal}(K/F)=G$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem (fundamental theorem of Galois theory).** Let $K/F$ be a finite Galois extension with group $G$. The maps $H\mapsto K^H$ and $E\mapsto\mathrm{Gal}(K/E)$ are mutually inverse, inclusion-reversing bijections between subgroups $H$ of $G$ and intermediate fields $E$. Moreover $[K:K^H]=|H|$ and $[K^H:F]=[G:H]$, and $E/F$ is normal (hence Galois) if and only if $\mathrm{Gal}(K/E)$ is normal in $G$, in which case $\mathrm{Gal}(E/F)\cong G/\mathrm{Gal}(K/E)$.

*Proof of the bijection.* If $H\le G$, Artin gives $[K:K^H]=|H|$ and $\mathrm{Gal}(K/K^H)=H$. If $E$ is intermediate, $K$ is the splitting field of the same separable polynomial over $E$, so $K/E$ is Galois and $|\mathrm{Gal}(K/E)|=[K:E]$; with $H=\mathrm{Gal}(K/E)$ we have $E\subseteq K^H$ and $[K:K^H]=|H|=[K:E]$, so $E=K^H$. *Normality (outline).* For $\sigma\in G$, $\mathrm{Gal}(K/\sigma E)=\sigma H\sigma^{-1}$. Since every embedding of $E$ over $F$ extends to an element of $G$, $E/F$ is normal if and only if $\sigma E=E$ for all $\sigma$, if and only if $H$ is normal; restriction $G\to\mathrm{Gal}(E/F)$ is then onto with kernel $H$. $\blacksquare$

### Computing Galois groups

If $f\in F[x]$ is separable of degree $n$ with splitting field $K$, then $\mathrm{Gal}(K/F)$ acts faithfully on the $n$ roots, so is a subgroup of $S_n$. It is transitive if and only if $f$ is irreducible, so $[F(\alpha):F]=n$ divides $|G|$. If $\mathrm{char}\,F\ne2$, $G\subseteq A_n$ if and only if the discriminant of $f$ is a square in $F$ (stated here without proof). For a cubic with distinct roots over $\mathbb{Q}$: $G$ is $S_3$ if irreducible with non-square discriminant, $A_3$ if irreducible with square discriminant. Typical exam strategy: find $[K:F]$ by a tower of degrees, then identify the group inside $S_n$ by its order, or exhibit explicit automorphisms.

The code finds the discriminant of a monic cubic $x^3+ax^2+bx+c$:

```python
def cubic_discriminant(a, b, c):
    """Discriminant of x^3 + a x^2 + b x + c."""
    return a * a * b * b - 4 * b ** 3 - 4 * a ** 3 * c - 27 * c * c + 18 * a * b * c


print(cubic_discriminant(0, -3, 1), cubic_discriminant(0, 0, -2))
```

It prints `81 -108`: $x^3-3x+1$ has square discriminant, so its Galois group over $\mathbb{Q}$ is $A_3$ (it is irreducible, having no rational root), while $x^3-2$ has non-square discriminant and group $S_3$.
@example
title: The Galois group of x^4 - 2
problem: Find the Galois group of $x^4-2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ and the number of intermediate fields of its splitting field.
step: The roots are $\pm\alpha,\pm i\alpha$ with $\alpha=\sqrt[4]{2}$, so the splitting field is $K=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha,i)$. By Eisenstein at $2$, $x^4-2$ is irreducible, so $[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}]=4$.
step: $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)\subset\mathbb{R}$ does not contain $i$, and $i$ satisfies $x^2+1$, so $[K:\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)]=2$ and $[K:\mathbb{Q}]=8=|G|$.
step: $G$ acts faithfully on the $4$ roots, so $G\subseteq S_4$ has order $8$, a Sylow $2$-subgroup of $S_4$, which is the dihedral group $D_4$. Concretely $\sigma\colon\alpha\mapsto i\alpha,\ i\mapsto i$ has order $4$ and complex conjugation $\tau$ satisfies $\tau\sigma\tau=\sigma^{-1}$.
step: $D_4$ has $10$ subgroups ($1$, five of order $2$, three of order $4$, and $D_4$). By the fundamental theorem there are $10$ intermediate fields including $\mathbb{Q}$ and $K$.
answer: $\mathrm{Gal}(x^4-2/\mathbb{Q})\cong D_4$ of order $8$, with $10$ intermediate fields.
@example
title: A non-normal extension and its normal closure
problem: Show that $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ is not normal, and describe the Galois correspondence for the splitting field of $x^3-2$.
step: $x^3-2$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ (Eisenstein at $2$) and has the real root $\beta=\sqrt[3]{2}$ in $\mathbb{Q}(\beta)\subset\mathbb{R}$, but its other roots $\omega\beta,\omega^2\beta$ are not real. So the polynomial does not split in $\mathbb{Q}(\beta)$: the extension is not normal, and $\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{Q}(\beta)/\mathbb{Q})$ is trivial (an automorphism sends $\beta$ to a root in $\mathbb{Q}(\beta)$).
step: The splitting field is $K=\mathbb{Q}(\beta,\omega)$ with $[K:\mathbb{Q}]=6$, since $3\mid[K:\mathbb{Q}]$ and $2\mid[K:\mathbb{Q}]$ ($\omega\notin\mathbb{R}$). The group $G\subseteq S_3$ has order $6$, so $G\cong S_3$.
step: The subgroups of $S_3$ are $1$, three of order $2$, $A_3$ and $S_3$. The fixed field of $A_3$ is $\mathbb{Q}(\omega)$ (degree $2$). The fixed fields of the three subgroups of order $2$ are $\mathbb{Q}(\beta)$, $\mathbb{Q}(\omega\beta)$ and $\mathbb{Q}(\omega^2\beta)$, each of degree $3$.
step: Only $A_3$ is normal in $S_3$, so $\mathbb{Q}(\omega)/\mathbb{Q}$ is the only proper nontrivial normal intermediate extension. The three cubic fields are conjugate and not normal.
answer: $K=\mathbb{Q}(\beta,\omega)$ has Galois group $S_3$; the intermediate fields are $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{Q}(\omega)$, three conjugate cubic fields, and $K$.
@mistakes
- Treating $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ as Galois because $x^3-2$ is irreducible and separable. Normality requires all roots to lie in the field.
- Believing every inseparable polynomial comes from a bad field. Over a perfect field, such as a finite field or a field of characteristic $0$, irreducible polynomials are separable; the standard counterexample is $x^p-t$ over $\mathbb{F}_p(t)$.
- Reading $[K:F]=|G|$ as valid for every extension. It holds for Galois extensions; for $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ the degree is $3$ and the group has order $1$.
- Assuming the Galois group of a polynomial is $S_n$ because it is irreducible of degree $n$. Irreducibility gives only transitivity; $x^4-2$ has group $D_4$ of order $8<24$.

model:
```python
from math import isqrt


def cubic_galois_order(a, b, c):
    """Order of the Galois group of x^3 + a x^2 + b x + c over Q."""
    def is_square(n):
        return n >= 0 and isqrt(n) ** 2 == n

    bound = abs(c)
    roots = [r for r in range(-bound, bound + 1)
             if r ** 3 + a * r * r + b * r + c == 0]
    if roots:
        r = roots[0]
        p = a + r                       # f = (x - r)(x^2 + p x + q)
        q = b + r * p
        return 1 if is_square(p * p - 4 * q) else 2
    disc = (a * a * b * b - 4 * b ** 3 - 4 * a ** 3 * c - 27 * c * c
            + 18 * a * b * c)
    return 3 if is_square(disc) else 6
```
hint: Look for an integer root dividing $c$. If there is one, divide it out and test whether the remaining quadratic splits over $\mathbb{Q}$.
hint: If there is no rational root the cubic is irreducible; then use the discriminant $a^2b^2-4b^3-4a^3c-27c^2+18abc$.
solution: A cubic with a rational root $r$ factors as $(x-r)(x^2+px+q)$; the splitting field is $\mathbb{Q}$ if the quadratic's discriminant $p^2-4q$ is a rational square and a quadratic extension otherwise. Without a rational root the cubic is irreducible, and its Galois group is $A_3$ of order $3$ or $S_3$ of order $6$ according to whether the discriminant is a square. Using only the discriminant test fails on `x^3 - x` (discriminant $4$, a square, but the group is trivial).

prompt: Compute the Galois group of $f(x)=x^4-10x^2+1$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, with proof.
hint: The roots are $\pm\sqrt2\pm\sqrt3$. Show $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2+\sqrt3)=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)$ and find its degree.

solution: Put $\gamma=\sqrt2+\sqrt3$. Then $\gamma^2=5+2\sqrt6$ and $(\gamma^2-5)^2=24$, so $\gamma^4-10\gamma^2+1=0$ and the roots of $f$ are $\pm\sqrt2\pm\sqrt3$ (each satisfies the same equation). Let $K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)$, which contains all roots, and is generated by them, so it is the splitting field. We have $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2):\mathbb{Q}]=2$ and $\sqrt3\notin\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$ (if $\sqrt3=a+b\sqrt2$ then squaring gives $3=a^2+2b^2+2ab\sqrt2$, forcing $ab=0$, and then $3=a^2$ or $3=2b^2$, both impossible for rational $a,b$), so $[K:\mathbb{Q}]=4$. Hence $|G|=4$. An automorphism is determined by the images of $\sqrt2\in\{\pm\sqrt2\}$ and $\sqrt3\in\{\pm\sqrt3\}$; since $|G|=4$ all four sign choices occur. Each such automorphism squares to the identity, so $G\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\times\mathbb{Z}/2$. The four sign changes send $\gamma$ to all four roots of $f$, so $G$ acts transitively on the roots and $f$ is irreducible; as a subgroup of $S_4$, $G$ is the Klein four-group acting regularly on the roots. $\blacksquare$
