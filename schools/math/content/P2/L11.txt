@article
A qualifying exam asks: "Show that the equation $x^5-6x+3=0$ cannot be solved by radicals." The proof computes a Galois group, identifies it as $S_5$, and uses that $S_5$ is not solvable. Finite fields and cyclotomic fields are the other two places where the Galois group can be written down completely.

### Finite fields

**Theorem.** Let $p$ be prime and $q=p^n$. (a) There is a field with $q$ elements, unique up to isomorphism: the splitting field of $x^q-x$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$. (b) Its multiplicative group is cyclic. (c) In an algebraic closure, $\mathbb{F}_{p^m}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ if and only if $m\mid n$. (d) $\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{F}_{p^n}/\mathbb{F}_p)$ is cyclic of order $n$, generated by the Frobenius map $\varphi(x)=x^p$.

*Proof.* (a) Let $g=x^q-x$. Since $g'=-1$, $g$ is separable and has $q$ roots in its splitting field $K$. In characteristic $p$, $(a+b)^q=a^q+b^q$, so the roots form a subfield, which contains $\mathbb{F}_p$ and so equals $K$; hence $|K|=q$. Conversely, in a field of order $q$ every element satisfies $x^q=x$ (Lagrange in the group of order $q-1$, and $0$), so the field is the splitting field of $g$, unique up to isomorphism. (b) A finite subgroup of the multiplicative group of a field is cyclic (Week 6). (c) If $\mathbb{F}_{p^m}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$, then $p^n=(p^m)^{[\,\mathbb{F}_{p^n}:\mathbb{F}_{p^m}]}$, so $m\mid n$. If $m\mid n$ then $p^m-1\mid p^n-1$, so $x^{p^m}-x$ divides $x^{p^n}-x$ and the elements of $\mathbb{F}_{p^m}$ lie in $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$. (d) $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ is the splitting field of a separable polynomial, so the extension is Galois of degree $n$. The map $\varphi$ is an injective homomorphism fixing $\mathbb{F}_p$, so is an automorphism. If $\varphi^k=\mathrm{id}$ for $0<k<n$, all $q$ elements would be roots of $x^{p^k}-x$, which has at most $p^k<q$ roots. So $\varphi$ has order $n$. $\blacksquare$

**Counting irreducibles.** Over $\mathbb{F}_q$, a monic irreducible $f$ of degree $d$ has its roots in $\mathbb{F}_{q^d}$, so $f\mid x^{q^n}-x$ if and only if $d\mid n$ (by (c)); and $x^{q^n}-x$ is squarefree. So $x^{q^n}-x$ is the product of all monic irreducibles of degree dividing $n$, giving $q^n=\sum_{d\mid n}d\,N_q(d)$ and by Möbius inversion
$$N_q(n)=\frac1n\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(n/d)\,q^d.$$

### Cyclotomic extensions

Let $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/n}$ and $\Phi_n(x)=\prod_{\gcd(k,n)=1}(x-\zeta^k)$, a monic polynomial of degree $\varphi(n)$ with integer coefficients (from $x^n-1=\prod_{d\mid n}\Phi_d$ by division).

**Theorem.** $\Phi_n$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$, and $\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)/\mathbb{Q})\cong(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$.

*Proof.* Let $f\in\mathbb{Z}[x]$ be the monic minimal polynomial of $\zeta$ (it has integer coefficients by Gauss's lemma, because it divides $x^n-1$), and $x^n-1=fg$ with $g\in\mathbb{Z}[x]$ monic. Let $p$ be a prime with $p\nmid n$; we show $f(\zeta^p)=0$. If not, then $g(\zeta^p)=0$, so $\zeta$ is a root of $g(x^p)$ and $f\mid g(x^p)$ in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$. Modulo $p$, $g(x^p)\equiv g(x)^p$, so every irreducible factor of $\bar f$ divides $\bar g$. Then $x^n-1=\bar f\bar g$ has a repeated factor in $\mathbb{F}_p[x]$; but its derivative $nx^{n-1}$ is coprime to it since $p\nmid n$, so $x^n-1$ is separable, a contradiction. So $f(\zeta^p)=0$; applying this repeatedly to the primes of $k$ shows $f(\zeta^k)=0$ whenever $\gcd(k,n)=1$. So $\deg f=\varphi(n)$ and $f=\Phi_n$. Each $\sigma\in\mathrm{Gal}$ sends $\zeta\mapsto\zeta^{a}$ with $a\in(\mathbb{Z}/n)^\times$, and $\sigma\mapsto a$ is an injective homomorphism into a group of order $\varphi(n)=|\mathrm{Gal}|$, hence an isomorphism. $\blacksquare$

### Solvability by radicals

**Theorem (Galois).** Let $F$ have characteristic $0$ and $f\in F[x]$. The roots of $f$ lie in an extension obtained by successively adjoining radicals if and only if $\mathrm{Gal}(f)$ is solvable. This is stated here without proof; in outline, adjoining $n$-th roots after adjoining roots of unity gives a cyclic extension (Kummer theory), so a radical tower gives a series with cyclic quotients, and conversely a solvable group gives such a tower.

**Corollary (Abel–Ruffini).** Since $S_n$ is not solvable for $n\ge5$, the general polynomial of degree $n\ge5$ is not solvable by radicals; specific quintics with Galois group $S_5$ exist (Example 2).

Elements of $\mathbb{F}_{2^n}$ are polynomials over $\mathbb{F}_2$ modulo an irreducible polynomial; encode a polynomial as an integer whose bit $i$ is the coefficient of $x^i$. The code lists the generators of the cyclic group $\mathbb{F}_{13}^\times$ (its primitive roots).

```python
def order(g, p):
    """Multiplicative order of g modulo the prime p."""
    k, x = 1, g % p
    while x != 1:
        x = x * g % p
        k += 1
    return k


print([g for g in range(1, 13) if order(g, 13) == 12])
```

It prints `[2, 6, 7, 11]`: there are $\varphi(12)=4$ generators, as there must be in a cyclic group of order $12$ (this illustrates part (b) for $p=13$ and is not a proof).
@example
title: Counting irreducible polynomials over F_3
problem: How many monic irreducible polynomials of degree $4$ are there over $\mathbb{F}_3$?
step: Apply $N_q(n)=\frac1n\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(n/d)q^d$ with $q=3$, $n=4$. The divisors of $4$ are $1,2,4$.
step: The Möbius values are $\mu(4/1)=\mu(4)=0$, $\mu(4/2)=\mu(2)=-1$, $\mu(4/4)=\mu(1)=1$.
step: So $N_3(4)=\frac14\bigl(0\cdot3-1\cdot3^2+1\cdot3^4\bigr)=\frac14(81-9)=18$.
step: Check by the degree identity: $3^4=81=1\cdot N_3(1)+2\cdot N_3(2)+4\cdot N_3(4)=3+2\cdot3+4\cdot18=81$, using $N_3(1)=3$ and $N_3(2)=(9-3)/2=3$.
answer: There are $18$ monic irreducible quartics over $\mathbb{F}_3$.
@example
title: A quintic that cannot be solved by radicals
problem: Show that $f(x)=x^5-6x+3$ has Galois group $S_5$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, so that its roots are not expressible by radicals.
step: Irreducible: $f$ is Eisenstein at $3$. So $G=\mathrm{Gal}(f)\subseteq S_5$ is transitive, and $5\mid|G|$.
step: Real roots: $f(-2)=-17<0$, $f(-1)=8>0$, $f(1)=-2<0$, $f(2)=23>0$, so there are roots in $(-2,-1)$, $(-1,1)$ and $(1,2)$. Since $f'=5x^4-6$ has exactly two real zeros, Rolle's theorem allows at most three real roots. So $f$ has exactly $3$ real roots and $2$ non-real roots, which are complex conjugates.
step: A $5$-cycle: since $5\mid|G|$, Cauchy's theorem gives an element of order $5$, which in $S_5$ is a $5$-cycle $\sigma$.
step: A transposition: complex conjugation restricts to an automorphism of the splitting field that fixes the three real roots and swaps the two non-real ones. It is a transposition $\tau$.
step: Generation: let $\tau=(i\,j)$. Since $5$ is prime, some power $\sigma^k$ maps $i$ to $j$, and it is again a $5$-cycle. Relabel the roots along the cycle of $\sigma^k$ so that $\sigma^k=(1\,2\,3\,4\,5)$ and $\tau=(1\,2)$. Conjugating $\tau$ by powers of $\sigma^k$ gives $(2\,3)$, $(3\,4)$, $(4\,5)$, so $G$ contains all adjacent transpositions, which generate $S_5$. So $G=S_5$.
step: $S_5$ is not solvable (it has the nonabelian simple composition factor $A_5$), so by Galois's criterion the roots cannot be expressed by radicals.
answer: $\mathrm{Gal}(x^5-6x+3/\mathbb{Q})\cong S_5$, which is not solvable.
@mistakes
- Concluding that $\mathrm{Gal}(f)=S_p$ for an irreducible $f$ of prime degree $p$ without a transposition. For $x^5-2$ the group is the affine group of order $20$, which is solvable.
- Counting irreducible polynomials by $q^n/n$. The correct count is $\frac1n\sum\mu(n/d)q^d$; for $q=3$, $n=4$ it is $18$, not $81/4$.

```python
def gf2n_mul(a, b, m):
    """Product in GF(2)[x]/(m), elements as integers (bit i = coeff of x^i)."""
    n = m.bit_length() - 1
    result = 0
    while b:
        if b & 1:
            result ^= a
        b >>= 1
        a <<= 1
        if (a >> n) & 1:
            a ^= m
    return result
```
hint: Multiplying by $x$ shifts the bits left by one. If the degree reaches $n$, subtract (xor) the modulus.
hint: Add the shifted copies of $a$ for each set bit of $b$ (addition is xor), reducing after each shift.
solution: This is shift-and-add multiplication with addition replaced by xor. Process the bits of `b` from the lowest: add the current multiple of `a` when the bit is set, then multiply `a` by $x$ with a left shift, and when that creates an $x^n$ term replace it by the modulus's lower terms by xoring with `m`. Reducing after each shift keeps `a` below $2^n$; forgetting this overflows past bit $n$ and gives wrong products (the tests in $\mathbb{F}_{16}$ and $\mathbb{F}_{32}$ catch it).

```python
def count_irreducible(q, n):
    """Number of monic irreducible polynomials of degree n over F_q."""
    def mobius(m):
        result = 1
        p = 2
        while m > 1:
            if m % p == 0:
                m //= p
                if m % p == 0:
                    return 0
                result = -result
            p += 1
        return result

    total = sum(mobius(n // d) * q ** d for d in range(1, n + 1) if n % d == 0)
    return total // n
```
hint: Loop over the divisors $d$ of $n$ and weight $q^d$ by $\mu(n/d)$.
hint: The sum is always divisible by $n$, so integer division is exact.
solution: Compute the Möbius function by trial division, sum $\mu(n/d)q^d$ over the divisors $d$ of $n$, and divide by $n$. For $q=5$, $n=3$: $\frac13(5^3-5)=40$. For $q=4$, $n=2$ (a prime power that is not prime): $\frac12(16-4)=6$. The formula holds for every prime power $q$, not only for primes.
