@article
Articles I, II and III of the Constitution create three branches. Each has its own powers and is limited by the others.

### Congress

Congress has two chambers. The **House of Representatives** has 435 voting members, apportioned among the states by population, who serve two-year terms. The **Senate** has 100 members, two per state, who serve six-year terms. Congress has the power to tax, borrow, regulate interstate and foreign commerce, declare war, raise armies and make laws "necessary and proper" for carrying out its powers. The House has the sole power to begin impeachment by majority vote; the Senate holds the trial and needs a two-thirds vote to convict. The Senate also confirms appointments and ratifies treaties by a two-thirds vote.

### How a bill becomes a law

1. A member introduces a **bill**. Bills about raising revenue must begin in the House.
2. A **committee** studies it, may hold hearings and revises ("marks up") it, and can send it to the full chamber or let it die.
3. The full chamber debates and votes. In the House a simple majority passes a bill (218 if all 435 members vote). In the Senate, debate can be ended by **cloture**, which requires 60 votes under current Senate rules, though many votes are decided by a simple majority.
4. The other chamber must pass the same text. If the versions differ, a conference committee or further votes resolve differences.
5. The bill goes to the president, who may **sign** it, **veto** it, or take no action. If Congress is in session and the president takes no action for ten days (Sundays excepted), the bill becomes law without a signature. If Congress adjourns and the president does not sign, a **pocket veto** occurs.
6. Congress can **override** a veto by a two-thirds vote in each chamber.

### The presidency

The president is head of state and head of the executive branch, serves four-year terms, and is limited to two elected terms by the Twenty-second Amendment (1951). The president enforces laws, commands the armed forces, makes treaties with Senate approval, appoints judges and officials with Senate confirmation, and may grant pardons. Cabinet departments and independent agencies carry out laws and write regulations that have the force of law within the authority Congress gives them. Presidents also issue executive orders to direct the executive branch; courts and Congress can limit them.

### The courts and judicial review

Article III creates one Supreme Court and lets Congress create lower courts. The federal system has 94 district courts, 13 courts of appeals (12 regional circuits plus the Federal Circuit) and the Supreme Court, which currently has nine justices (a number set by statute). Federal judges are nominated by the president, confirmed by the Senate and serve "during good Behavior," which usually means for life. State courts handle most cases; federal courts hear cases involving the Constitution, federal law, disputes between states and some other matters.

**Judicial review** is the power of courts to decide that a law or action conflicts with the Constitution and is therefore invalid. The Constitution does not state it in so many words. In *Marbury v. Madison* (1803), the Court held that a provision of a federal law conflicted with the Constitution, and wrote: "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is." Americans continue to debate how judges should interpret the Constitution: some stress the original meaning of its text, while others stress how its principles apply to changing circumstances. The Court usually accepts a case if at least four justices vote to hear it, and it decides by majority vote.

### Why it matters

Each branch can check the others. Congress can pass laws, the president can veto, and courts can strike down laws, while the Senate confirms judges and Congress and the states can amend the Constitution. Together, these checks slow decisions and limit any one branch.

@example
title: Following a bill with a veto
problem: A bill passes both chambers, the president vetoes it, and the House has 435 members voting and the Senate 100. How many votes does each chamber need to override?
step: The Constitution requires two-thirds of each chamber to override a veto.
step: House: $\frac{2}{3} \times 435 = 290$ votes.
step: Senate: $\frac{2}{3} \times 100 = 66.7$, so 67 votes (rounding up to a whole vote).
step: If either chamber falls short, the veto stands and the bill does not become law.

@mistakes
- Believing the president can make laws; the president signs or vetoes laws passed by Congress and can issue orders only within delegated authority.
- Confusing the number of votes to pass a bill (majority) with the number to override a veto (two-thirds) or to end debate in the Senate (60).
- Saying judicial review is written in the Constitution; it was established through the decision in Marbury v. Madison and later practice.
