@article
Cells divide to grow, to replace damaged cells and (in single-celled organisms) to reproduce. Before a eukaryotic cell divides it must copy its DNA and then distribute identical sets of chromosomes to two daughter cells.

### The cell cycle

The cycle has two main parts:
- **Interphase**, when the cell grows and prepares: **G1** (growth, normal function), **S** (DNA synthesis: each chromosome is replicated), **G2** (more growth and preparation for division). Cells that stop dividing are said to be in **G0**.
- **M phase**, consisting of **mitosis** (nuclear division) and **cytokinesis** (division of the cytoplasm).

Most of the cycle is interphase; mitosis is usually only a small fraction.

### Chromosomes and chromatids

After S phase each chromosome consists of two identical **sister chromatids** joined at the **centromere**. A human body cell has 46 chromosomes ($2n = 46$): 23 from each parent. In G1 it has 46 chromosomes (46 DNA molecules); after S phase it still has 46 chromosomes, but each has two chromatids, so 92 chromatids and 92 DNA molecules. When sister chromatids separate in anaphase, each is counted as an independent chromosome.

### Stages of mitosis (PMAT)

1. **Prophase:** chromosomes condense; the mitotic spindle forms from microtubules and centrosomes; the nuclear envelope breaks down.
2. **Metaphase:** chromosomes line up at the **metaphase plate**, attached to spindle microtubules at their kinetochores.
3. **Anaphase:** sister chromatids separate and are pulled toward opposite poles; the cell elongates.
4. **Telophase:** nuclear envelopes re-form, chromosomes decondense, spindle disappears.

**Cytokinesis** follows: animal cells pinch in by a **cleavage furrow** (a contractile ring of actin); plant cells build a **cell plate** that becomes a new wall. The result is two genetically identical diploid cells.

### Control of the cycle

Checkpoints stop the cycle until conditions are right: the **G1 checkpoint** (is the cell large enough, nutrients present, DNA undamaged?), the **G2 checkpoint** (is DNA fully and correctly replicated?) and the **spindle (M) checkpoint** (are all chromosomes attached to the spindle?). Progress is driven by **cyclins**, proteins whose levels rise and fall, which activate **cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs)**.

**Cancer** is uncontrolled cell division caused by mutations in genes that control the cycle. **Proto-oncogenes** (which stimulate division) can mutate into **oncogenes** that are stuck "on". **Tumor suppressor genes** (such as $p53$ and $Rb$) normally halt the cycle or trigger **apoptosis** (programmed cell death) when DNA is damaged; losing them removes the brakes. Cancer usually needs several mutations.

### Estimating how long a phase lasts

If you count cells in a sample, the fraction in each phase estimates the fraction of time spent in that phase:
$$\text{time in phase} = \frac{\text{cells in phase}}{\text{total cells counted}} \times \text{length of cycle}.$$

@example
title: Estimating the duration of mitosis
problem: In a sample of 500 onion root tip cells, 40 are in mitosis. The cell cycle takes 20 hours. Estimate how long mitosis lasts.
step: The fraction of cells in mitosis is $40/500 = 0.08$.
step: Time in mitosis is this fraction of the cycle: $0.08 \times 20$ hours.
step: Mitosis lasts about $1.6$ hours.

@mistakes
- Saying the chromosome number doubles in S phase; the DNA doubles but each chromosome now has two chromatids, so the chromosome count stays the same.
- Mixing up metaphase (chromosomes lined up) and anaphase (chromatids pulled apart).
- Thinking mitosis produces variation; it produces genetically identical cells unless a mutation occurs.
- Calling cytokinesis a stage of mitosis; it is a separate process that usually overlaps with late mitosis.

@quiz num
