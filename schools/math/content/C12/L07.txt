@article
The partial sums of $\sum x^n/n!$ are polynomials, and they converge to $e^x$ uniformly on every bounded interval. Can any continuous function be approximated this way, by polynomials, uniformly? We first understand which functions are limits of polynomials in the most natural way, power series, and then prove that, perhaps surprisingly, *every* continuous function on a closed interval is one in the uniform sense.

### Power series

Consider $\sum_{n \ge 0} a_n x^n$. Let $S = \{|y| : \sum a_n y^n \text{ converges}\}$ and $R = \sup S \in [0,\infty]$, the *radius of convergence*. (By $0 \in S$ the set is nonempty.)

**Theorem 1.** Let $0 \le r < R$. Then $\sum a_n x^n$ converges absolutely and uniformly on $[-r,r]$. In particular it converges at every $x$ with $|x| < R$, and for $|x| > R$ it diverges.

*Proof.* Since $r < R = \sup S$ there is $y$ with $|y| > r$ and $\sum a_n y^n$ convergent. Its terms tend to $0$, so $|a_n y^n| \le M$ for all $n$. For $|x| \le r$,
$$|a_n x^n| = |a_n y^n|\left|\frac{x}{y}\right|^n \le M q^n, \qquad q = \frac{r}{|y|} < 1 .$$
Since $\sum M q^n < \infty$, the Weierstrass M-test gives uniform and absolute convergence on $[-r,r]$. Divergence for $|x| > R$ is the definition of $R$. $\blacksquare$

Every compact interval $[a,b] \subseteq (-R,R)$ lies in $[-r,r]$ with $r = \max(|a|,|b|) < R$, so **the series converges uniformly on compact subintervals of its interval of convergence**. Uniform convergence on the whole open interval can fail: $\sum x^n$ has $R = 1$, but the tail $x^N/(1-x)$ is unbounded on $(-1,1)$.

**Theorem 2.** On $(-R,R)$ the sum $f(x) = \sum a_n x^n$ is continuous and differentiable, and $f'(x) = \sum_{n \ge 1} n a_n x^{n-1}$; the derived series has the same radius $R$.

*Proof.* Continuity: uniform limit of polynomials on each $[-r,r]$ (L05, Theorem 1). With $y, M, q$ as above and $|x| \le r$: $|n a_n x^{n-1}| \le \frac{M}{|y|}\, n\, q^{n-1}$, and $\sum n q^{n-1} < \infty$ by the ratio test, so the derived series converges uniformly on $[-r,r]$ and, converging at $0$, satisfies the hypotheses of Corollary 3 of the last lesson. Hence $f' = \sum n a_n x^{n-1}$ on $[-r,r]$, for every $r<R$. The derived series cannot converge at any $y$ with $|y| > R$: the argument of Theorem 1 applied to it would give absolute convergence at some $y'$ with $R < |y'| < |y|$, and then $|a_n y'^n| \le |y'|\,|n a_n y'^{n-1}|$ would make $\sum a_n y'^n$ converge, contradicting the definition of $R$. $\blacksquare$

For example, differentiating $\sum_{n \ge 0} x^n = 1/(1-x)$ gives $\sum_{n \ge 1} n x^{n-1} = 1/(1-x)^2$ on $(-1,1)$.

### The Weierstrass approximation theorem

**Theorem 3 (Weierstrass, 1885).** If $f\colon [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous and $\varepsilon > 0$, there is a polynomial $p$ with $|f(x) - p(x)| < \varepsilon$ for all $x \in [0,1]$.

The proof uses the **Bernstein polynomial** $B_n f(x) = \sum_{k=0}^n f(k/n)\, p_{n,k}(x)$, where $p_{n,k}(x) = \binom nk x^k (1-x)^{n-k}$.

*Identities.* By the binomial theorem $\sum_k p_{n,k}(x) = (x + 1 - x)^n = 1$. Since $k\binom nk = n\binom{n-1}{k-1}$, $\sum_k k\,p_{n,k}(x) = nx\sum_{k \ge 1}\binom{n-1}{k-1}x^{k-1}(1-x)^{n-k} = nx$. Similarly $k(k-1)\binom nk = n(n-1)\binom{n-2}{k-2}$ gives $\sum_k k(k-1)p_{n,k}(x) = n(n-1)x^2$. Expanding $(k - nx)^2 = k(k-1) + k - 2nxk + n^2x^2$:
$$\sum_{k=0}^n (k - nx)^2 p_{n,k}(x) = n(n-1)x^2 + nx - 2n^2x^2 + n^2x^2 = nx(1-x) \le \frac n4 .$$

*Proof of the theorem.* $f$ is bounded, $|f| \le M$, and uniformly continuous on $[0,1]$ (Heine–Cantor). Choose $\delta > 0$ with $|f(s) - f(t)| < \varepsilon/2$ whenever $|s - t| < \delta$. Since $\sum p_{n,k} = 1$ and $p_{n,k} \ge 0$ on $[0,1]$,
$$|B_nf(x) - f(x)| \le \sum_k |f(k/n) - f(x)|\,p_{n,k}(x) .$$
Split the indices. If $|k/n - x| < \delta$ the term is at most $\frac\varepsilon2 p_{n,k}(x)$; together these contribute at most $\varepsilon/2$. If $|k/n - x| \ge \delta$ then $1 \le (k-nx)^2/(n\delta)^2$, and $|f(k/n) - f(x)| \le 2M$, so these terms contribute at most
$$2M\sum_k \frac{(k-nx)^2}{n^2\delta^2}p_{n,k}(x) \le \frac{2M}{n^2\delta^2}\cdot\frac n4 = \frac{M}{2n\delta^2} .$$
This is less than $\varepsilon/2$ once $n > M/(\varepsilon\delta^2)$, uniformly in $x$. Hence $\sup_{[0,1]} |B_nf - f| < \varepsilon$. $\blacksquare$

For a continuous function on $[a,b]$ substitute $x = a + (b-a)t$. The theorem says polynomials are dense in $C[0,1]$ with the sup metric.

```python
from math import comb


def bernstein(f, n, x):
    """Value at x of the nth Bernstein polynomial of f on [0, 1]."""
    return sum(f(k / n) * comb(n, k) * x ** k * (1 - x) ** (n - k)
               for k in range(n + 1))


print(bernstein(lambda t: t * t, 4, 0.5))
for n in (10, 100, 1000):
    print(n, round(bernstein(lambda t: abs(t - 0.5), n, 0.5), 6))
```

The first line prints $0.3125 = x^2 + x(1-x)/n$ at $x = 1/2$, $n = 4$. For $f(t) = |t - 1/2|$, whose value at $1/2$ is $0$, the Bernstein values at $1/2$ are $0.123047$, $0.039795$, $0.012613$: convergence is slow, about $1/\sqrt n$, at the corner of $f$. This is evidence about the rate at one point, not a proof.

@example
title: Radius of convergence and uniform convergence
problem: Find the radius of convergence of $\sum_{n \ge 1} \dfrac{n x^n}{3^n}$ and decide whether the series converges uniformly on $(-3,3)$ and on $[-2.5, 2.5]$.
step: Ratio test on the coefficients: $\left|\dfrac{(n+1)x^{n+1}/3^{n+1}}{n x^n/3^n}\right| = \dfrac{n+1}{n}\cdot\dfrac{|x|}{3} \to \dfrac{|x|}{3}$, which is $<1$ iff $|x| < 3$. So $R = 3$.
step: On $[-2.5, 2.5]$ we have $|n x^n/3^n| \le n(5/6)^n =: M_n$, and $\sum n(5/6)^n < \infty$ by the ratio test, so the M-test gives uniform convergence (this is Theorem 1 with $r = 2.5 < 3$).
step: On $(-3,3)$ the series does not converge uniformly: the single term $n x^n/3^n$ at $x \to 3$ approaches $n$, so $\sup_{(-3,3)}|n x^n/3^n| = n \not\to 0$, which violates the necessary condition that uniformly convergent series have terms tending to $0$ uniformly.
answer: $R = 3$; uniform on $[-2.5,2.5]$ but not on $(-3,3)$.

@example
title: Summing a series by differentiation
problem: Find a closed form for $\sum_{n \ge 1} n x^n$ on $(-1,1)$.
step: Start from the geometric series $\sum_{n \ge 0} x^n = 1/(1-x)$, with $R = 1$.
step: By Theorem 2, differentiate term by term on $(-1,1)$: $\sum_{n \ge 1} n x^{n-1} = \dfrac{1}{(1-x)^2}$.
step: Multiply by $x$ (multiplication by a constant, for fixed $x$, commutes with the sum): $\sum_{n \ge 1} n x^n = \dfrac{x}{(1-x)^2}$.
step: Check: at $x = 1/2$ the series is $\sum n/2^n = 2$ and the formula gives $\frac{1/2}{1/4} = 2$.
answer: $\sum_{n \ge 1} n x^n = \dfrac{x}{(1-x)^2}$ for $|x| < 1$.

@mistakes
- Believing that uniform convergence holds on the whole open interval of convergence: it holds on each $[-r,r]$ with $r<R$, not necessarily on $(-R,R)$.
- Assuming a power series behaves the same at the endpoints: $\sum x^n$ diverges at both, $\sum x^n/n$ converges at $-1$ but not at $1$, and $\sum x^n/n^2$ converges at both.
- Thinking the Weierstrass theorem says every continuous function has a power series: it says it is a uniform limit of *some* sequence of polynomials, whose coefficients typically change completely with $n$ (e.g. $|x - 1/2|$ has no Taylor series at $1/2$).
- Using the theorem on a non-compact domain: $1/x$ on $(0,1]$ is continuous but is not a uniform limit of polynomials, because polynomials are bounded on $[0,1]$ and a uniform limit of bounded functions is bounded.

model:
```python
from math import comb


def bernstein_degree(f, eps, grid, nmax):
    """Least n <= nmax with grid error of B_n f below eps, else None."""
    for n in range(1, nmax + 1):
        worst = 0.0
        for x in grid:
            b = sum(f(k / n) * comb(n, k) * x ** k * (1 - x) ** (n - k)
                    for k in range(n + 1))
            worst = max(worst, abs(b - f(x)))
        if worst < eps:
            return n
    return None
```
hint: For each $n$ evaluate the Bernstein sum at every grid point and track the largest error.
hint: Stop at the first $n$ whose largest error is below eps; return `None` after the loop ends.
solution: Loop over $n$, evaluate $B_nf$ at the grid points with the binomial-weighted sum, and compare with $f$. For $f(t) = t^2$ the error is exactly $x(1-x)/n$, with maximum $1/(4n)$ at $x = 1/2$, so the first $n$ with $1/(4n) < 0.012$ is $21$. For the corner function $|t - 1/2|$ the error at $1/2$ is about $0.4/\sqrt n$, too slow for $n \le 40$ and eps $0.05$. Endpoints are always reproduced exactly, since $B_nf(0) = f(0)$ and $B_nf(1) = f(1)$.

@practice self
prompt: Let $f\colon [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}$ be continuous with $\int_0^1 f(x)\,x^n\,dx = 0$ for every integer $n \ge 0$. Prove that $f = 0$.
hint: By linearity $\int f p = 0$ for every polynomial $p$. Approximate $f$ itself by polynomials and look at $\int f^2$.
solution: By linearity $\int_0^1 f p\,dx = 0$ for every polynomial $p$. By the Weierstrass theorem choose polynomials $p_k$ with $\sup|f - p_k| \to 0$. Then $\left|\int_0^1 f^2 - \int_0^1 f p_k\right| \le \int_0^1 |f|\,|f - p_k| \le \sup|f|\cdot\sup|f-p_k| \to 0$, so $\int_0^1 f^2 = \lim \int_0^1 f p_k = 0$. Since $f^2$ is continuous and nonnegative with integral $0$, $f^2 = 0$ (if $f(x_0)^2 > 0$ then $f^2 > f(x_0)^2/2$ on an interval around $x_0$, giving a positive integral). So $f = 0$. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Extends the hypothesis from monomials to all polynomials by linearity.
rubric: Uses the Weierstrass theorem to take $p_k \to f$ uniformly and passes to the limit in $\int f p_k$.
rubric: Concludes $\int f^2 = 0$ implies $f = 0$ by continuity.
