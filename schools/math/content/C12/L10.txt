@article
Let $f(x,y) = \dfrac{x^2y}{x^2+y^2}$ for $(x,y) \ne (0,0)$ and $f(0,0) = 0$. Along the axes $f = 0$, so both partial derivatives at the origin are $0$. If a "derivative" were just the list of partials, the best linear approximation to $f$ near $0$ would be the zero function. But along the line $y = x$, $f(t,t) = t/2$, which is not close to $0$ relative to $t$. The right definition must see all directions at once.

### The derivative as a linear map

We use the Euclidean norm $\|\cdot\|$ on every $\mathbb{R}^k$, and for a matrix $A$ the operator norm $\|A\| = \sup_{\|v\|=1}\|Av\|$, so $\|Av\| \le \|A\|\,\|v\|$. Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be open. A map $f\colon U \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is **differentiable at $a \in U$** if there is a linear map $A\colon \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ with
$$\lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\|f(a+h) - f(a) - Ah\|}{\|h\|} = 0 .$$
Then $A$ is unique, written $Df(a)$. Taking $h = t e_j$ shows that the $j$th column of $A$ is $\partial f/\partial x_j(a)$: so $Df(a)$ is the **Jacobian matrix** $\left(\partial f_i/\partial x_j\right)_{i,j}$ of size $m \times n$. A differentiable map is continuous at $a$, since $\|f(a+h) - f(a)\| \le \|Ah\| + o(\|h\|)$. For the opening example the partials are $0$ but $f(t,t) = t/2$ is not $o(t)$, so $f$ is *not* differentiable at $0$: existence of partials is weaker than differentiability.

**Theorem 1 (chain rule).** If $f$ is differentiable at $a$ and $g$ is differentiable at $b = f(a)$, then $g\circ f$ is differentiable at $a$ and $D(g\circ f)(a) = Dg(b)\,Df(a)$.

*Proof.* Let $A = Df(a)$, $B = Dg(b)$. Write $f(a+h) = b + Ah + r(h)$ with $\|r(h)\|/\|h\| \to 0$ and $g(b+k) = g(b) + Bk + \|k\|\sigma(k)$ with $\sigma(k) \to 0$ as $k \to 0$ ($\sigma(0)=0$). Put $k = k(h) = Ah + r(h)$, so $k \to 0$ as $h \to 0$ and $\|k\| \le (\|A\| + 1)\|h\|$ for small $h$. Then
$$g(f(a+h)) - g(b) - BAh = Br(h) + \|k\|\sigma(k).$$
The first term has norm at most $\|B\|\,\|r(h)\| = o(\|h\|)$; the second is at most $(\|A\|+1)\|h\|\,\|\sigma(k)\| = o(\|h\|)$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 2.** If all partial derivatives of $f\colon U \to \mathbb{R}^m$ exist and are continuous on $U$ (we say $f$ is $C^1$), then $f$ is differentiable at every point of $U$ and $Df$ is the Jacobian matrix.

*Proof for $n = 2$, $m = 1$* (general $n$ adds one step per variable; for $m>1$ apply it to each component). Let $a = (a_1,a_2)$, $h = (h_1,h_2)$ small. Write
$$f(a+h) - f(a) = [f(a_1 + h_1, a_2 + h_2) - f(a_1, a_2 + h_2)] + [f(a_1, a_2 + h_2) - f(a_1,a_2)] .$$
By the one-variable mean value theorem there are $\theta_1, \theta_2 \in (0,1)$ with the right side equal to $\partial_1 f(a_1 + \theta_1h_1, a_2 + h_2)\,h_1 + \partial_2 f(a_1, a_2 + \theta_2 h_2)\,h_2$. Subtract $\partial_1 f(a)h_1 + \partial_2 f(a)h_2$: by continuity of the partials at $a$ the differences of partials tend to $0$ as $h \to 0$, and $|h_i| \le \|h\|$, so the error is $o(\|h\|)$. $\blacksquare$

### The mean value inequality

**Theorem 3.** Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be open and convex, $f\colon U \to \mathbb{R}^m$ differentiable with $\|Df(x)\| \le M$ for all $x \in U$. Then $\|f(x) - f(y)\| \le M\|x - y\|$ for all $x, y \in U$.

*Proof.* Let $v = f(x) - f(y)$; assume $v \neq 0$. Define $\varphi(t) = \langle v, f(y + t(x-y))\rangle$ for $t \in [0,1]$. By the chain rule $\varphi'(t) = \langle v, Df(y + t(x-y))(x-y)\rangle$. The mean value theorem gives $\tau \in (0,1)$ with $\|v\|^2 = \varphi(1) - \varphi(0) = \varphi'(\tau) \le \|v\|\,M\,\|x-y\|$ (Cauchy–Schwarz). Divide by $\|v\|$. $\blacksquare$

So a bound on the derivative is a Lipschitz bound, and if it is below $1$ and $f$ maps a closed convex set into itself, $f$ is a contraction (previous lesson).

### Checking a derivative numerically

```python
from math import sin, hypot


def f(x, y):
    return (x * x * y, x + sin(y))


def remainder_ratio(a, h, jac):
    """||f(a+h) - f(a) - J h|| / ||h|| for the 2x2 matrix jac."""
    fa = f(*a)
    fb = f(a[0] + h[0], a[1] + h[1])
    r = [fb[i] - fa[i] - jac[i][0] * h[0] - jac[i][1] * h[1]
         for i in range(2)]
    return hypot(*r) / hypot(*h)
```

At $a = (1,2)$ the Jacobian of this $f$ is $\begin{pmatrix}4 & 1\\ 1 & \cos 2\end{pmatrix}$. For $h = (t,-t)$ with $t = 0.1, 0.01, 0.001$ the ratio is about $0.0334$, $0.00322$, $0.000322$, shrinking in proportion to $t$, as the definition requires. Three values of $t$ in one direction support the claim but do not prove it: the definition requires all directions $h \to 0$, and the proof is Theorem 2.

@example
title: Chain rule with Jacobians
problem: Let $f(x,y) = (x + y,\ xy)$ and $g(u,v) = (uv,\ u^2 - v)$. Compute $D(g\circ f)(1,2)$.
step: $f(1,2) = (3,2)$ and $Df(x,y) = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 1\\ y & x\end{pmatrix}$, so $Df(1,2) = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 1\\ 2 & 1\end{pmatrix}$.
step: $Dg(u,v) = \begin{pmatrix}v & u\\ 2u & -1\end{pmatrix}$, so $Dg(3,2) = \begin{pmatrix}2 & 3\\ 6 & -1\end{pmatrix}$.
step: Multiply in the order $Dg(f(a))\,Df(a)$: $\begin{pmatrix}2 & 3\\ 6 & -1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1\\ 2 & 1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}8 & 5\\ 4 & 5\end{pmatrix}$.
step: Check directly: $g\circ f = (x^2y + xy^2,\ (x+y)^2 - xy)$, and at $(1,2)$ the partial derivatives are $2xy + y^2 = 8$, $x^2 + 2xy = 5$, $2(x+y) - y = 4$, $2(x+y) - x = 5$.
answer: $D(g\circ f)(1,2) = \begin{pmatrix}8 & 5\\ 4 & 5\end{pmatrix}$.

@example
title: Directional derivatives that are not linear
problem: Show that $f(x,y) = \dfrac{x^2y}{x^2+y^2}$ with $f(0,0) = 0$ is continuous at the origin and has all directional derivatives there, but is not differentiable at $(0,0)$.
step: Continuity: $|f(x,y)| \le |y|\,\dfrac{x^2}{x^2+y^2} \le |y| \to 0$.
step: For a unit vector $u = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$: $f(tu)/t = \cos^2\theta\sin\theta$ for $t \ne 0$, so the directional derivative $D_uf(0) = \cos^2\theta\sin\theta$ exists for every $\theta$.
step: If $f$ were differentiable at $0$ with derivative $A$, then $D_uf(0) = Au$ would be linear in $u$. The partials are $D_{(1,0)}f = 0$ and $D_{(0,1)}f = 0$, so $A = 0$ and $Au = 0$. But $D_uf(0) \ne 0$ at $\theta = \pi/4$ (it equals $\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt2}$).
answer: $f$ is continuous and has all directional derivatives at $0$, but is not differentiable there.

@mistakes
- Equating "all partial derivatives exist" with differentiability: you need continuity of the partials near the point (a sufficient condition), or the limit definition.
- Multiplying Jacobians in the wrong order: the chain rule is $Dg(f(a))\,Df(a)$, and the sizes must match ($(p\times m)(m\times n)$).
- Evaluating $Dg$ at $a$ instead of at $f(a)$.
- Thinking a finite-difference test proves differentiability: ratios that shrink along one line or a few directions are evidence only.

model:
```python
def jacobian(f, x, h=1e-5):
    """Central-difference Jacobian of f at x, as a list of rows."""
    n = len(x)
    columns = []
    for j in range(n):
        xp = list(x)
        xm = list(x)
        xp[j] += h
        xm[j] -= h
        fp = f(xp)
        fm = f(xm)
        columns.append([(a - b) / (2 * h) for a, b in zip(fp, fm)])
    m = len(columns[0])
    return [[columns[j][i] for j in range(n)] for i in range(m)]
```
hint: Compute column $j$ by perturbing only the $j$th coordinate up and down by $h$.
hint: Collect the columns first, then transpose them into rows.
solution: Each column of the Jacobian is the central difference of the whole vector $f$ in one coordinate direction. Copy the input list before perturbing it, so `x` is not changed. Central differences have error of order $h^2$ for smooth $f$ (exact for polynomials of degree at most $2$ in each variable), while one-sided differences have error of order $h$, which the tolerance $10^{-6}$ with $h = 10^{-5}$ rejects.
