@article
The Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem says a bounded sequence of real numbers has a convergent subsequence. Is there a version for sequences of functions? Boundedness alone is not enough: $f_n(x) = \sin(nx)$ on $[0,\pi]$ is bounded by $1$, but oscillates faster and faster, and no subsequence converges uniformly. What extra condition tames the oscillation?

### Equicontinuity

Let $K$ be a compact metric space and $\mathcal{F}$ a family of functions $K \to \mathbb{R}$. We say $\mathcal{F}$ is **uniformly bounded** if there is $M$ with $|f(x)| \le M$ for all $f \in \mathcal{F}$ and $x \in K$. It is **equicontinuous** if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $\delta > 0$ such that $|f(x) - f(y)| < \varepsilon$ for *all* $f \in \mathcal{F}$ and all $x,y \in K$ with $d(x,y) < \delta$. So one $\delta$ serves every function in the family and every point. (For a compact $K$ it is enough to ask this at each point separately, by the same compactness argument as the Heine–Cantor theorem.)

Examples: if every $f \in \mathcal{F}$ is Lipschitz with a common constant $L$, then $\mathcal{F}$ is equicontinuous with $\delta = \varepsilon/L$. A finite family of continuous functions on a compact space is equicontinuous (take the smallest of finitely many $\delta$'s). The family $\{\sin(nx)\}$ on $[0,\pi]$ is not: the points $0$ and $\pi/(2n)$ are within $\pi/(2n)$ of each other but $|\sin(n\cdot\pi/(2n)) - \sin 0| = 1$.

### The Arzelà–Ascoli theorem

**Theorem 1 (Arzelà–Ascoli).** Let $K$ be a compact metric space and $(f_n)$ a uniformly bounded, equicontinuous sequence of functions $K \to \mathbb{R}$. Then $(f_n)$ has a subsequence that converges uniformly on $K$ (to a continuous function).

*Proof.* *Step 1: a countable dense set.* By Step 1 in the proof of Theorem 4 of Lesson 3, for each $k$ finitely many balls of radius $1/k$ with centers in $K$ cover $K$. Let $D = \{q_1, q_2, \dots\}$ be the countable set of all these centers (for all $k$). Every $x \in K$ is within $1/k$ of a point of $D$ for each $k$, so $D$ is dense.

*Step 2: diagonal subsequence.* The real sequence $(f_n(q_1))$ is bounded, so by the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem there is a subsequence $(f_{1,n})$ of $(f_n)$ along which it converges. Inductively, from $(f_{j-1,n})$ extract a subsequence $(f_{j,n})$ along which the values at $q_j$ converge. Let $g_j = f_{j,j}$, the diagonal sequence. For each $i$, $(g_j)_{j \ge i}$ is a subsequence of $(f_{i,n})$, so $(g_j(q_i))$ converges for every $i$.

*Step 3: uniformly Cauchy.* Let $\varepsilon > 0$. Take $\delta$ from equicontinuity. By compactness $K$ is covered by finitely many balls $B(y_1,\delta/2), \dots, B(y_m,\delta/2)$. Each ball is a nonempty open set, so it contains a point $d_i \in D$. For $x \in K$ pick $i$ with $x \in B(y_i,\delta/2)$; then $d(x,d_i) \le d(x,y_i) + d(y_i,d_i) < \delta$. The $m$ real sequences $(g_j(d_i))_j$ are Cauchy, so there is $J$ with $|g_j(d_i) - g_l(d_i)| < \varepsilon$ for all $i \le m$ and $j,l \ge J$. For $x$ and $i$ as above and $j,l \ge J$,
$$|g_j(x) - g_l(x)| \le |g_j(x) - g_j(d_i)| + |g_j(d_i) - g_l(d_i)| + |g_l(d_i) - g_l(x)| < 3\varepsilon .$$
So $(g_j)$ is uniformly Cauchy, hence converges uniformly (L05, Theorem 2). If the $f_n$ are continuous the limit is continuous by L05, Theorem 1. $\blacksquare$

The proof also gives a **corollary**: if $(f_n)$ is equicontinuous on a compact $K$ and converges pointwise, then it converges uniformly. Indeed, Step 3 only used convergence at the finitely many points $d_i$. Conversely, a uniformly convergent sequence of continuous functions on a compact $K$ is uniformly bounded and equicontinuous. (The full Arzelà–Ascoli theorem also states that a subset of $C(K)$ with the sup metric is compact if and only if it is closed, bounded and equicontinuous; we proved the half that produces subsequences.)

### What the theorem is for

It is a compactness theorem for spaces of functions: closed bounded subsets of $C(K)$ are not compact in general, but those that are also equicontinuous are. It is the standard tool for producing a limit function (a solution of a differential equation, a minimizer) when no formula is available. It also yields **negative information** by contrapositive: if a uniformly bounded sequence has no uniformly convergent subsequence, it cannot be equicontinuous.

@example
title: A family that fails equicontinuity
problem: Show that $f_n(x) = x^n$ on $[0,1]$ is uniformly bounded but not equicontinuous.
step: Uniformly bounded: $0 \le x^n \le 1$ on $[0,1]$.
step: Suppose it were equicontinuous. By Arzelà–Ascoli a subsequence $f_{n_k}$ would converge uniformly to a continuous function $g$.
step: Uniform convergence implies pointwise convergence, so $g(x) = \lim x^{n_k}$, which is $0$ for $x<1$ and $1$ at $x = 1$. This function is not continuous: contradiction.
step: A direct witness: with $x_n = 1 - 1/n$ and $y_n = 1$, $|x_n - y_n| = 1/n \to 0$ but $|f_n(x_n) - f_n(y_n)| = 1 - (1 - 1/n)^n \to 1 - 1/e$, so no single $\delta$ works for $\varepsilon = 1/2$.
answer: $\{x^n\}$ is uniformly bounded but not equicontinuous on $[0,1]$.

@example
title: A Lipschitz family has a uniformly convergent subsequence
problem: Let $f_n\colon [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}$ satisfy $f_n(0) = 0$ and $|f_n(x) - f_n(y)| \le 3|x-y|$. Show that some subsequence converges uniformly.
step: Uniform bound: $|f_n(x)| = |f_n(x) - f_n(0)| \le 3x \le 3$.
step: Equicontinuity: given $\varepsilon$ take $\delta = \varepsilon/3$; then $|x - y| < \delta$ implies $|f_n(x) - f_n(y)| < \varepsilon$ for every $n$.
step: $[0,1]$ is compact, so Arzelà–Ascoli gives a uniformly convergent subsequence, and its limit $f$ satisfies $f(0) = 0$ and $|f(x) - f(y)| \le 3|x-y|$ (pass to the limit in the inequalities).
answer: A subsequence converges uniformly, to a $3$-Lipschitz function vanishing at $0$.

@mistakes
- Dropping uniform boundedness: constant functions $f_n = n$ are equicontinuous but no subsequence converges.
- Dropping equicontinuity: $\sin(nx)$ is bounded but has no uniformly convergent subsequence.
- Thinking "each $f_n$ is continuous" is equicontinuity: it needs one $\delta$ for all $n$ at once, as $x^n$ shows.
- Applying the theorem on a non-compact domain: let $f_n(x) = \max(0, 1 - |x - n|)$ on $\mathbb{R}$. These tent functions are bounded by $1$ and $1$-Lipschitz, yet $f_n \to 0$ pointwise while $\sup f_n = 1$, so no subsequence converges uniformly. Compactness of $K$ is used in the covering step.

answer: 12
check: max(2 * c * x for c in (0, 3) for x in (0, 2))
hint: $f_c'(x) = 2cx$; maximize over both $c$ and $x$.
solution: $|f_c'(x)| = 2cx$ is largest at $c = 3$, $x = 2$, giving $12$. So the family is $12$-Lipschitz and hence equicontinuous with $\delta = \varepsilon/12$; it is also uniformly bounded by $3 \cdot 4 = 12$.

@practice self
prompt: Let $\sum a_n x^n$ converge at a point $y_0 \ne 0$. Prove that for every $r$ with $0 \le r < |y_0|$ the series converges uniformly on $[-r, r]$. Deduce that if $R$ is the radius of convergence, the series converges uniformly on every compact interval $[a,b] \subset (-R, R)$.
hint: A convergent series has terms tending to $0$, hence bounded terms. Compare with a geometric series and apply the M-test.
solution: Since $\sum a_n y_0^n$ converges, $a_n y_0^n \to 0$, so $|a_n y_0^n| \le M$ for all $n$. For $|x| \le r$ put $q = r/|y_0| < 1$; then $|a_n x^n| = |a_n y_0^n|\,|x/y_0|^n \le M q^n$. Since $\sum M q^n$ converges, the Weierstrass M-test gives uniform convergence on $[-r,r]$. For the deduction, let $[a,b] \subset (-R,R)$ and $r_0 = \max(|a|,|b|) < R$. By definition of $R$ as a supremum there is a point $y_0$ with $|y_0| > r_0$ at which the series converges, so by the first part the series converges uniformly on $[-r_0, r_0] \supseteq [a,b]$. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Obtains a bound $|a_n y_0^n| \le M$ from convergence at $y_0$.
rubric: Bounds $|a_n x^n|$ by $M q^n$ with $q = r/|y_0| < 1$ and applies the M-test.
rubric: Passes to a compact $[a,b]$ by choosing $y_0$ beyond $\max(|a|,|b|)$ using the definition of the radius.
