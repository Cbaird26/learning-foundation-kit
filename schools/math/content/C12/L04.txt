@article
The function $f(x) = 1/x$ is continuous on $(0,1)$, yet near $0$ a tiny change in $x$ makes a huge change in $f(x)$: no single step size $\delta$ works for the whole interval. On $[1/2, 1]$ one step size does work. The difference between these two behaviors is the topic of this lesson.

### Continuity

Let $f\colon X \to Y$ be a map of metric spaces $(X, d_X)$, $(Y, d_Y)$. It is *continuous at $a$* if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $\delta > 0$ with $d_Y(f(x), f(a)) < \varepsilon$ whenever $d_X(x,a) < \delta$; it is *continuous* if it is continuous at every point.

**Theorem 1.** For $f\colon X \to Y$ the following are equivalent: (a) $f$ is continuous; (b) $x_n \to a$ implies $f(x_n) \to f(a)$; (c) $f^{-1}(U)$ is open in $X$ for every open $U \subseteq Y$.

*Proof.* (a)$\Rightarrow$(b): given $\varepsilon$, take $\delta$ from (a) and $N$ with $d(x_n,a) < \delta$ for $n \ge N$. (b)$\Rightarrow$(c): suppose $U$ is open and $f^{-1}(U)$ is not. Then some $a \in f^{-1}(U)$ has no ball $B(a,1/n) \subseteq f^{-1}(U)$; pick $x_n \in B(a,1/n)$ with $f(x_n) \notin U$. Then $x_n \to a$, so $f(x_n) \to f(a) \in U$; but $U$ is open, so $f(x_n) \in U$ for large $n$, a contradiction. (c)$\Rightarrow$(a): given $a$ and $\varepsilon$, the set $f^{-1}(B(f(a),\varepsilon))$ is open and contains $a$, so it contains some $B(a,\delta)$. $\blacksquare$

### Compactness and continuity

**Theorem 2.** If $K$ is compact and $f\colon K \to Y$ is continuous, then $f(K)$ is compact.

*Proof.* Let $y_n = f(x_n)$ be a sequence in $f(K)$. A subsequence $x_{n_k}$ converges to some $x \in K$, and $f(x_{n_k}) \to f(x)$ by Theorem 1(b); so $(y_n)$ has a subsequence converging to $f(x) \in f(K)$. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary 3 (extreme value theorem).** If $K \ne \emptyset$ is compact and $f\colon K \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous, then $f$ is bounded and attains a maximum and a minimum.

*Proof.* $f(K)$ is compact in $\mathbb{R}$, hence closed and bounded (Heine–Borel). A nonempty bounded set has a supremum $M$; since $f(K)$ is closed, $M \in f(K)$, say $M = f(p)$, which is the maximum. The minimum is the same. $\blacksquare$

### Uniform continuity

$f$ is *uniformly continuous* if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $\delta > 0$ such that $d_X(x,y) < \delta$ implies $d_Y(f(x),f(y)) < \varepsilon$ for *all* $x,y \in X$: $\delta$ does not depend on the point. Lipschitz maps ($d(f(x),f(y)) \le L\, d(x,y)$) are uniformly continuous with $\delta = \varepsilon/L$.

**Theorem 4 (Heine–Cantor).** A continuous function $f\colon K \to Y$ on a compact metric space is uniformly continuous.

*Proof.* Suppose not. Then for some $\varepsilon > 0$ and every $n$ there are $x_n, y_n \in K$ with $d(x_n,y_n) < 1/n$ and $d(f(x_n), f(y_n)) \ge \varepsilon$. By compactness a subsequence $x_{n_k} \to p \in K$; then $d(y_{n_k}, p) \le d(y_{n_k},x_{n_k}) + d(x_{n_k},p) \to 0$, so $y_{n_k} \to p$ as well. By continuity $f(x_{n_k}) \to f(p)$ and $f(y_{n_k}) \to f(p)$, so $d(f(x_{n_k}), f(y_{n_k})) \to 0$, contradicting $\ge \varepsilon$. $\blacksquare$

Compactness cannot be dropped: $1/x$ on $(0,1)$ fails ($x_n = 1/n$, $y_n = 1/(2n)$ get closer while $|f(x_n) - f(y_n)| = n \to \infty$), and so does $x^2$ on $\mathbb{R}$, as the first worked example shows.

### Connectedness

A metric space $X$ is *disconnected* if $X = U \cup V$ with $U, V$ nonempty, disjoint and open in $X$; otherwise it is *connected*. A subset is connected if it is connected in its own metric. For example, $\mathbb{Q}$ is disconnected: $U = \{q < \sqrt 2\}$, $V = \{q > \sqrt 2\}$ are open in $\mathbb{Q}$.

**Theorem 5.** Every interval $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ is connected.

*Proof.* Suppose $I = A \cup B$ with $A, B$ disjoint, nonempty and open in $I$. Pick $a \in A$, $b \in B$; by renaming assume $a < b$, so $[a,b] \subseteq I$. Let $s = \sup(A \cap [a,b])$, so $s \in [a,b] \subseteq I$. If $s \in A$, then $s < b$ (as $b \in B$) and there is $\varepsilon > 0$ with $(s - \varepsilon, s + \varepsilon) \cap I \subseteq A$; points $t$ with $s < t < \min(s+\varepsilon, b)$ lie in $A \cap [a,b]$ and exceed $s$, a contradiction. If $s \in B$, then $s > a$ and there is $\varepsilon \in (0, s-a]$ with $(s-\varepsilon, s+\varepsilon) \cap I \subseteq B$, so $A \cap [a,b] \subseteq [a, s-\varepsilon]$ and $\sup(A \cap [a,b]) \le s - \varepsilon < s$, a contradiction. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 6.** If $X$ is connected and $f\colon X \to Y$ is continuous, then $f(X)$ is connected.

*Proof.* Suppose $f(X) = U \cup V$ with $U,V$ disjoint, nonempty, open in $f(X)$. By Theorem 1(c) applied to $f\colon X \to f(X)$, the sets $f^{-1}(U)$ and $f^{-1}(V)$ are open, disjoint, nonempty and cover $X$, contradicting connectedness. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary 7 (intermediate value theorem).** If $f\colon X \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous, $X$ is connected, and $f(p) < c < f(q)$, then $f(x) = c$ for some $x$. Indeed $f(X)$ is a connected subset of $\mathbb{R}$; if it missed $c$ then $f(X) = (f(X) \cap (-\infty,c)) \cup (f(X) \cap (c,\infty))$ would be a separation. So connected subsets of $\mathbb{R}$ contain the whole interval between any two of their points.

@example
title: A function that is not uniformly continuous on all of $\mathbb{R}$
problem: Show that $f(x) = x^2$ is uniformly continuous on $[0,3]$ but not on $\mathbb{R}$.
step: On $[0,3]$ we have $|x^2 - y^2| = |x+y|\,|x-y| \le 6|x-y|$, so $f$ is Lipschitz with $L = 6$, and $\delta = \varepsilon/6$ works for all $x,y$. (Alternatively $[0,3]$ is compact, and Heine–Cantor applies.)
step: On $\mathbb{R}$ take $x_n = n$ and $y_n = n + 1/n$. Then $|x_n - y_n| = 1/n \to 0$.
step: But $|f(y_n) - f(x_n)| = |2 + 1/n^2| \ge 2$ for every $n$. Hence for $\varepsilon = 2$ no $\delta$ works: choose $n > 1/\delta$.
answer: $x^2$ is uniformly continuous on $[0,3]$, but not on $\mathbb{R}$.

@example
title: A fixed point from the intermediate value theorem
problem: Show that every continuous $f\colon [0,1] \to [0,1]$ has a fixed point, that is, a point $x$ with $f(x) = x$.
step: Define $g(x) = f(x) - x$ on $[0,1]$; it is continuous, and $[0,1]$ is connected (Theorem 5).
step: $g(0) = f(0) \ge 0$ and $g(1) = f(1) - 1 \le 0$, because $f$ takes values in $[0,1]$.
step: If $g(0) = 0$ or $g(1) = 0$ we are done. Otherwise $g(0) > 0 > g(1)$, and the intermediate value theorem (Corollary 7) gives $x \in (0,1)$ with $g(x) = 0$.
answer: There is $x \in [0,1]$ with $f(x) = x$.

@mistakes
- Confusing continuity with uniform continuity: $1/x$ on $(0,1)$ and $x^2$ on $\mathbb{R}$ are continuous but not uniformly continuous.
- Claiming continuous maps preserve closed or bounded sets: $f(x) = 1/(1+x^2)$ maps the closed set $\mathbb{R}$ onto the non-closed $(0,1]$, and $1/x$ maps the bounded set $(0,1)$ onto the unbounded $(1,\infty)$. Only *compactness* and *connectedness* are preserved.
- Applying the intermediate value theorem to a domain that is not connected: $f = -1$ on $[-2,-1]$ and $f = 1$ on $[1,2]$ is continuous on $[-2,-1] \cup [1,2]$ but never takes the value $0$.
- Believing a continuous function on a non-compact set cannot attain a maximum: compactness is sufficient, not necessary ($\sin x$ attains its maximum on $\mathbb{R}$), but without it the maximum may fail to exist ($x$ on $(0,1)$).

@practice num
prompt: Let $f(x) = x^3$ on $[-2,2]$. Find the smallest Lipschitz constant $L$ with $|f(x) - f(y)| \le L|x-y|$ for all $x, y \in [-2,2]$.
answer: 12
check: max(3 * x * x for x in [i / 1000 for i in range(-2000, 2001)])
hint: By the mean value theorem the best constant is $\sup |f'|$ on the interval.
solution: $f'(x) = 3x^2$ has supremum $12$ on $[-2,2]$, attained at $x = \pm 2$. The mean value theorem gives $|f(x) - f(y)| \le 12|x-y|$, and for $y = 2 - h$, $x = 2$ the ratio tends to $12$, so $12$ is the smallest constant.

@practice self
prompt: Let $K$ be a nonempty compact metric space and $f\colon K \to \mathbb{R}$ continuous. Prove that $f$ attains its maximum, and that $f$ is uniformly continuous. Do not quote the extreme value theorem or Heine–Cantor.
hint: For the maximum take a sequence $x_n$ with $f(x_n) \to \sup f$ and use a convergent subsequence. For uniform continuity argue by contradiction with sequences $x_n, y_n$ with $d(x_n,y_n) \to 0$.
solution: Maximum: first, $f$ is bounded above; if not pick $x_n$ with $f(x_n) > n$, take a subsequence $x_{n_k} \to p \in K$, and continuity gives $f(x_{n_k}) \to f(p)$, which is finite, contradicting $f(x_{n_k}) > n_k$. Let $M = \sup f(K)$ and choose $x_n$ with $f(x_n) \to M$. A subsequence $x_{n_k} \to p \in K$, and continuity gives $f(p) = \lim f(x_{n_k}) = M$. So $f(p) = M$ is the maximum. Uniform continuity: suppose not; then for some $\varepsilon > 0$ there are $x_n, y_n$ with $d(x_n, y_n) < 1/n$ and $|f(x_n) - f(y_n)| \ge \varepsilon$. A subsequence $x_{n_k} \to p$, and then $y_{n_k} \to p$ too, since $d(y_{n_k},p) \le d(y_{n_k},x_{n_k}) + d(x_{n_k},p)$. By continuity $f(x_{n_k}) - f(y_{n_k}) \to f(p) - f(p) = 0$, contradicting $|f(x_{n_k}) - f(y_{n_k})| \ge \varepsilon$. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Shows $f$ is bounded above (or directly takes a maximizing sequence) and extracts a convergent subsequence by compactness.
rubric: Uses sequential continuity to identify the limit value as the supremum.
rubric: Sets up the negation of uniform continuity with sequences $x_n, y_n$ and shows both subsequences converge to the same point.
rubric: Derives the contradiction from continuity at that point.

@quiz mc
prompt: Let $f\colon [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}$ be continuous with $f(0) = -1$ and $f(1) = 2$. Which statement is guaranteed?
choice*: $f(x) = 0$ for at least one $x \in [0,1]$.
choice: $f(x) = 0$ for exactly one $x \in [0,1]$.
choice: $f$ is increasing on $[0,1]$.
choice: $f(1/2) = 1/2$.
hint: Which property of $[0,1]$ does the intermediate value theorem use?
solution: $[0,1]$ is connected and $f$ is continuous, so $f([0,1])$ is an interval containing $-1$ and $2$, hence $0$. The function $f$ may cross zero several times or oscillate, so uniqueness, monotonicity and a specific value at $1/2$ are not guaranteed.

@quiz num
prompt: Let $f(x) = 1/x$ on $[1/4, \infty)$. Find the smallest Lipschitz constant $L$ with $|f(x) - f(y)| \le L|x - y|$ for all $x, y \ge 1/4$.
answer: 16
check: max(1 / x ** 2 for x in [0.25 + i / 100 for i in range(1000)])
hint: The best constant is $\sup |f'|$ on the domain; where is $|f'|$ largest?
solution: $|f'(x)| = 1/x^2$ is decreasing, so its supremum on $[1/4,\infty)$ is attained at $x = 1/4$ and equals $16$. So $f$ is Lipschitz with $L = 16$, hence uniformly continuous, even though the domain is not compact.
