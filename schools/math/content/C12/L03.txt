@article
The function $1/x$ is continuous on $(0,1]$ but unbounded; on $[0,1]$ every continuous function is bounded (C8). Something about $[0,1]$ prevents a sequence from escaping: any sequence has a subsequence that settles down, and the limit stays inside. That property, which $(0,1]$ and $\mathbb{R}$ lack, is *compactness*.

### Definitions

**Definition.** A metric space $K$ is *compact* if every sequence in $K$ has a subsequence that converges to a point of $K$. A subset $A \subseteq X$ is compact if it is compact in the restricted metric. (This is often called *sequential compactness*; Theorem 4 shows it equals the open-cover definition.) A set $A$ is *bounded* if $A \subseteq B(x_0, R)$ for some $x_0$ and $R$.

**Theorem 1.** A compact set $K$ in a metric space $X$ is closed and bounded.

*Proof.* Closed: if $x_n \in K$ and $x_n \to x \in X$, some subsequence converges to a point of $K$; subsequences of a convergent sequence have the same limit, so $x \in K$, and $K$ is closed by Theorem 2 of the last lesson. Bounded: if not, fix $x_0 \in K$ and pick $x_n \in K$ with $d(x_n,x_0) > n$. A subsequence converges to some $x \in K$, so $d(x_{n_k},x_0) \le d(x_{n_k},x) + d(x,x_0)$ stays bounded, contradicting $d(x_{n_k},x_0) > n_k \to \infty$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 2.** A closed subset $F$ of a compact set $K$ is compact.

*Proof.* A sequence in $F$ lies in $K$, so a subsequence converges to some $x \in K$; since $F$ is closed, $x \in F$. $\blacksquare$

### The Heine–Borel theorem

**Theorem 3 (Heine–Borel).** A subset $K \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ (Euclidean metric) is compact if and only if it is closed and bounded.

*Proof.* ($\Rightarrow$) is Theorem 1. ($\Leftarrow$) Let $x^{(p)} \in K$. Bounded means each coordinate sequence is bounded in $\mathbb{R}$. By the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem of C8 choose a subsequence along which the first coordinates converge, from it a further subsequence along which the second coordinates converge, and so on for $n$ coordinates. The final subsequence converges coordinatewise, so it converges in $\mathbb{R}^n$ (as in Theorem 4 of the last lesson: $\|x - y\| \le \sqrt{n} \max_i |x_i - y_i|$). Its limit lies in $K$ because $K$ is closed. $\blacksquare$

### Open covers

An *open cover* of $K$ is a family of open sets $(U_\alpha)$ with $K \subseteq \bigcup_\alpha U_\alpha$.

**Theorem 4.** For a metric space $K$ the following are equivalent: (a) $K$ is compact (sequentially); (b) every open cover of $K$ has a finite subcover.

*Proof.* (a)$\Rightarrow$(b). *Step 1.* For each $\delta > 0$, $K$ is covered by finitely many balls $B(x_i, \delta)$ with $x_i \in K$. If not, choose $x_1 \in K$, then $x_2 \notin B(x_1,\delta)$, and inductively $x_{k+1} \notin B(x_1,\delta) \cup \dots \cup B(x_k,\delta)$. Then $d(x_i,x_j) \ge \delta$ for $i \ne j$, so no subsequence is Cauchy, hence none converges, contradicting (a).
*Step 2 (Lebesgue number).* Given an open cover $(U_\alpha)$ there is $\delta > 0$ such that every ball $B(x,\delta)$, $x \in K$, lies in a single $U_\alpha$. If not, for each $n$ pick $x_n$ with $B(x_n, 1/n)$ inside no $U_\alpha$. A subsequence $x_{n_k} \to x \in K$; $x \in U_\beta$ for some $\beta$, and $B(x,\varepsilon) \subseteq U_\beta$ for some $\varepsilon$. For large $k$, $d(x_{n_k},x) < \varepsilon/2$ and $1/n_k < \varepsilon/2$, so $B(x_{n_k}, 1/n_k) \subseteq B(x,\varepsilon) \subseteq U_\beta$, a contradiction.
*Step 3.* Take $\delta$ from Step 2 and finitely many balls $B(x_i,\delta)$ covering $K$ from Step 1; each lies in some $U_{\alpha_i}$, so $U_{\alpha_1}, \dots, U_{\alpha_m}$ cover $K$.

(b)$\Rightarrow$(a). Let $(x_n)$ be a sequence with no subsequence converging in $K$. For each $y \in K$ there is $r_y > 0$ such that $B(y,r_y)$ contains $x_n$ for only finitely many $n$: otherwise for each $j$ there are infinitely many $n$ with $d(x_n,y) < 1/j$, and choosing $n_1 < n_2 < \dots$ with $d(x_{n_j},y) < 1/j$ gives a subsequence converging to $y$. The balls $B(y,r_y)$ cover $K$; finitely many cover it, so each $x_n$, $n \in \mathbb{N}$, lies in one of finitely many balls, each containing only finitely many terms. This is impossible for infinitely many indices. So every sequence has a convergent subsequence. $\blacksquare$

### Closed and bounded is not enough

In the discrete metric on $\mathbb{N}$ the whole space is closed and bounded (every distance is $\le 1$), but the sequence $1,2,3,\dots$ has no convergent subsequence, since distinct terms are at distance $1$. In $C[0,1]$ with the sup metric the closed unit ball $\{f : \sup|f| \le 1\}$ is closed and bounded but not compact: a sup-convergent subsequence of $f_n(t) = t^n$ would converge pointwise to the same limit, which is continuous as a uniform limit (Theorem 5 of the last lesson), yet the pointwise limit of $t^n$ is $0$ on $[0,1)$ and $1$ at $t = 1$. Thus Heine–Borel is a special fact about $\mathbb{R}^n$, and the equivalence in Theorem 4 is the general truth. Finally $(0,1]$ is not compact: the open sets $U_n = (1/n, 2)$ cover it, but any finite subfamily has union $(1/N, 2)$ for the largest index $N$, missing the point $1/(N+1)$.

@example
title: Using Heine–Borel
problem: Is $K = \{(x,y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : x^2 + y^2 \le 4,\ y \ge 1\}$ compact?
step: $K$ is bounded: $x^2 + y^2 \le 4$ gives $\|(x,y)\| \le 2$, so $K \subseteq B(0,3)$.
step: $K$ is closed: if $(x_n,y_n) \in K$ converge to $(x,y)$, the inequalities $x_n^2 + y_n^2 \le 4$ and $y_n \ge 1$ survive in the limit, since the limit of a sequence of numbers $\le 4$ is $\le 4$ and similarly for $\ge 1$. So $(x,y) \in K$, and $K$ is closed by the sequence criterion.
step: By the Heine–Borel theorem, a closed bounded subset of $\mathbb{R}^2$ is compact.
answer: Yes, $K$ is compact.

@example
title: An open cover with no finite subcover
problem: Show that $A = \{1/k : k \in \mathbb{N}\}$ is not compact in $\mathbb{R}$ by giving an open cover with no finite subcover, and say which Heine–Borel hypothesis fails.
step: Let $U_k = (1/k - \tfrac{1}{4k^2},\ 1/k + \tfrac{1}{4k^2})$. Each $U_k$ is open, and $1/k \in U_k$, so the $U_k$ cover $A$.
step: The gap between neighbors satisfies $\tfrac1k - \tfrac1{k+1} = \tfrac{1}{k(k+1)} > \tfrac{1}{4k^2} + \tfrac{1}{4(k+1)^2}$, so $U_k$ contains no other point $1/j$ with $j \ne k$. Hence removing any $U_k$ leaves $1/k$ uncovered, and no finite subfamily covers $A$.
step: $A$ is bounded, so $A$ must fail to be closed: its limit point $0$ is not in $A$.
answer: $(U_k)$ has no finite subcover, and $A$ is not closed because $1/k \to 0 \notin A$.

@mistakes
- Equating "closed and bounded" with "compact" in every metric space: the discrete metric on an infinite set and the unit ball of $C[0,1]$ are counterexamples.
- Taking a subsequence limit without checking it lies in the set: $x_n = 1/n$ in $(0,1]$ has no subsequence converging in $(0,1]$.
- Using a cover to prove compactness: one open cover with a finite subcover proves nothing; compactness needs a finite subcover of every open cover.
- Forgetting that compactness is intrinsic: $[0,1]$ is compact whether viewed in $\mathbb{R}$ or on its own, while "closed" depends on the ambient space (every space is closed in itself).

@practice self
prompt: Prove that the union of two compact subsets $A, B$ of a metric space $X$ is compact.
hint: Take a sequence in $A \cup B$; infinitely many of its terms lie in $A$ or infinitely many lie in $B$.
solution: Let $(x_n)$ be a sequence in $A \cup B$. For infinitely many indices $n$, $x_n \in A$, or for infinitely many, $x_n \in B$; say $A$. The terms with those indices form a subsequence in $A$, which has a further subsequence converging to a point of $A \subseteq A \cup B$. This is a subsequence of $(x_n)$ converging in $A \cup B$. The case of $B$ is identical. $\blacksquare$

@quiz mc

choice: In every metric space, closed and bounded subsets are compact.
