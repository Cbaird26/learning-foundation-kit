@article
Does $\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^1 f_n = \int_0^1 \lim_{n\to\infty} f_n$? Take $f_n(x) = n x (1 - x^2)^n$ on $[0,1]$. For $x \in (0,1)$ we have $0 < 1 - x^2 < 1$, so $n x (1-x^2)^n \to 0$, and $f_n(0) = f_n(1) = 0$; the pointwise limit is $0$, with integral $0$. But substituting $u = 1 - x^2$,
$$\int_0^1 n x (1-x^2)^n\, dx = \frac{n}{2}\int_0^1 u^n\, du = \frac{n}{2(n+1)} \longrightarrow \frac12 .$$
So the limit of the integrals is $\tfrac12 \ne 0$. Interchanging a limit with an integral needs a hypothesis.

### Integrals

**Theorem 1.** Let $f_n\colon [a,b] \to \mathbb{R}$ be continuous and $f_n \to f$ uniformly. Then $f$ is continuous and $\int_a^b f_n \to \int_a^b f$.

*Proof.* $f$ is continuous by Theorem 1 of the last lesson, so it is Riemann integrable (C8). For every $n$,
$$\left|\int_a^b f_n - \int_a^b f\right| \le \int_a^b |f_n - f| \le (b-a)\sup_{[a,b]}|f_n - f| \to 0 . \qquad \blacksquare$$

(The same proof works when each $f_n$ is only Riemann integrable, once one knows the uniform limit is integrable.) Applied to partial sums: if $\sum g_k$ converges uniformly on $[a,b]$ with each $g_k$ continuous, then $\int_a^b \sum_k g_k = \sum_k \int_a^b g_k$. The hypothesis is sufficient but not necessary; the second worked example shows convergence of integrals without uniform convergence.

### Derivatives

Uniform convergence of $f_n$ alone says nothing about derivatives. Let $f_n(x) = \sin(nx)/\sqrt n$. Then $\sup |f_n| \le 1/\sqrt n \to 0$, so $f_n \to 0$ uniformly, but $f_n'(x) = \sqrt n \cos(nx)$, and $f_n'(0) = \sqrt n \to \infty$. The correct hypothesis concerns the derivatives.

**Theorem 2.** Let $f_n\colon [a,b] \to \mathbb{R}$ be differentiable (one-sided at the endpoints), suppose $f_n' \to g$ uniformly on $[a,b]$, and suppose $(f_n(x_0))$ converges for some $x_0 \in [a,b]$. Then $f_n$ converges uniformly on $[a,b]$ to a differentiable function $f$, and $f' = g$.

*Proof.* *Step 1: uniform convergence.* By the mean value theorem applied to $f_n - f_m$, for all $x,t$ there is $\xi$ between them with
$$|(f_n - f_m)(x) - (f_n - f_m)(t)| = |(f_n' - f_m')(\xi)|\,|x-t| \le |x - t|\sup|f_n' - f_m'| .$$
Taking $t = x_0$: $|f_n(x) - f_m(x)| \le |f_n(x_0) - f_m(x_0)| + (b-a)\sup|f_n' - f_m'|$. Both terms are small for large $m,n$ (the second because $(f_n')$ is uniformly Cauchy), so $(f_n)$ is uniformly Cauchy and converges uniformly to some $f$.
*Step 2: derivative.* Fix $c \in [a,b]$ and for $t \ne c$ put $\varphi_n(t) = \dfrac{f_n(t) - f_n(c)}{t - c}$ and $\varphi(t) = \dfrac{f(t) - f(c)}{t-c}$. Given $\varepsilon > 0$ choose $N$ with $\sup|f_n' - f_m'| < \varepsilon$ for $m,n \ge N$. The mean value inequality above gives $|\varphi_n(t) - \varphi_m(t)| \le \varepsilon$; letting $m \to \infty$ yields $|\varphi_n(t) - \varphi(t)| \le \varepsilon$ for all $t \ne c$ and $n \ge N$. Likewise $|f_N'(c) - g(c)| \le \varepsilon$. Since $f_N$ is differentiable at $c$ there is $\delta > 0$ with $|\varphi_N(t) - f_N'(c)| < \varepsilon$ for $0 < |t - c| < \delta$. For those $t$,
$$|\varphi(t) - g(c)| \le |\varphi(t) - \varphi_N(t)| + |\varphi_N(t) - f_N'(c)| + |f_N'(c) - g(c)| < 3\varepsilon .$$
So $f'(c) = \lim_{t \to c} \varphi(t) = g(c)$. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary 3 (termwise differentiation).** If each $g_k$ is differentiable on $[a,b]$, $\sum g_k'$ converges uniformly on $[a,b]$, and $\sum g_k(x_0)$ converges for some $x_0$, then $\sum g_k$ converges uniformly to a differentiable function with derivative $\sum g_k'$.

### The pattern

Each interchange is an exchange of two limits, and it is never free. For integrals, uniform convergence of $f_n$ suffices. For derivatives, one needs uniform convergence of the derivatives plus convergence at one point. For power series (next lesson) the hypotheses hold automatically on compact subintervals of the interval of convergence. When testing a claimed interchange, the checklist is: which functions converge, in which sense, on which set?

@example
title: Termwise differentiation of a power series
problem: Show that $f(x) = \sum_{n \ge 1} x^n/n$ satisfies $f'(x) = 1/(1-x)$ on $(-1,1)$, and use this to evaluate $\sum_{n \ge 1} \dfrac{1}{n\,2^n}$.
step: Fix $r \in (0,1)$ and work on $[-r,r]$. The differentiated series is $\sum x^{n-1}$ with $|x^{n-1}| \le r^{n-1}$, and $\sum r^{n-1} < \infty$, so it converges uniformly on $[-r,r]$ by the M-test.
step: At $x_0 = 0$ the series for $f$ converges (every term is $0$). By Corollary 3, $f$ is differentiable on $[-r,r]$ and $f'(x) = \sum_{n \ge 1} x^{n-1} = 1/(1-x)$.
step: Since $r<1$ was arbitrary, $f' = 1/(1-x)$ on $(-1,1)$, and $f(0) = 0$. The function $-\log(1-x)$ has the same derivative and value at $0$, so $f(x) = -\log(1-x)$ on $(-1,1)$ (two functions with equal derivatives on an interval differ by a constant).
step: At $x = 1/2$: $\sum_{n \ge 1} \dfrac{1}{n 2^n} = f(1/2) = -\log(1/2) = \log 2$.
answer: $\sum_{n\ge 1} \dfrac{1}{n 2^n} = \log 2$.

@example
title: Integrals converge without uniform convergence
problem: Let $f_n(x) = n x^n (1 - x)$ on $[0,1]$. Decide whether $\lim \int_0^1 f_n = \int_0^1 \lim f_n$, and whether $f_n$ converges uniformly.
step: Pointwise: for $x \in [0,1)$, $n x^n \to 0$, and $f_n(1) = 0$. So $f_n \to 0$ pointwise on $[0,1]$.
step: Integrals: $\int_0^1 n x^n(1-x)\,dx = n\left(\tfrac{1}{n+1} - \tfrac{1}{n+2}\right) = \dfrac{n}{(n+1)(n+2)} \to 0 = \int_0^1 0$. So the interchange holds here.
step: Not uniform: at $x_n = n/(n+1)$, $f_n(x_n) = \left(\tfrac{n}{n+1}\right)^{n+1} \to e^{-1}$, so $\sup |f_n| \not\to 0$.
step: So Theorem 1 gives a sufficient condition only; the interchange can hold without uniform convergence, and the verdict must be checked case by case.
answer: The integrals converge to $0 = \int \lim f_n$, although $f_n \not\to 0$ uniformly.

@mistakes
- Assuming $\lim \int f_n = \int \lim f_n$ whenever $f_n \to f$ pointwise: $f_n = n x (1-x^2)^n$ converges pointwise to $0$ but its integrals tend to $1/2$.
- Requiring uniform convergence of the functions for the derivative theorem: it is the derivatives $f_n'$ that must converge uniformly ($\sin(nx)/\sqrt n \to 0$ uniformly, derivatives diverge).
- Forgetting the base point: $f_n(x) = n$ (constants) has $f_n' = 0 \to 0$ uniformly, yet $f_n$ does not converge, so condition on $f_n(x_0)$ is needed.
- Claiming that failure of the hypothesis proves the interchange false: the theorems are sufficient, as the example $n x^n(1-x)$ shows.

answer: 2.5
tol: 0.001
check: sum(abs(-1 + (i + 0.5) * 3 / 6000) for i in range(6000)) * 3 / 6000
hint: Interchange the limit and the integral, then integrate $|x|$ over $[-1,0]$ and $[0,2]$ separately.
solution: The functions are continuous and converge uniformly: $0 \le \sqrt{x^2 + 1/n} - |x| = \frac{1/n}{\sqrt{x^2+1/n} + |x|} \le \frac{1}{\sqrt n}$. By Theorem 1 the limit equals $\int_{-1}^2 |x|\,dx = \tfrac12 + 2 = 2.5$.

@practice self
prompt: Let $f_n(x) = x^n/n$ on $[0,1]$. Show that $f_n \to 0$ uniformly on $[0,1]$, that $f_n'$ converges pointwise but not uniformly, and that $\lim f_n'(1) \ne (\lim f_n)'(1)$ (derivatives at the endpoint are one-sided). Say which hypothesis of Theorem 2 fails.
hint: $f_n'(x) = x^{n-1}$. Find its pointwise limit and compare it with the limit function of $f_n$.
solution: $0 \le f_n(x) \le 1/n$ on $[0,1]$, so $f_n \to 0$ uniformly and $f = 0$, $f' = 0$. The derivatives are $f_n'(x) = x^{n-1}$, which converge pointwise to $g(x) = 0$ for $x < 1$ and $g(1) = 1$. The convergence is not uniform, since $g$ is discontinuous while each $f_n'$ is continuous (Theorem 1 of the last lesson). At $x = 1$: $\lim f_n'(1) = 1$ but $f'(1) = 0$. Theorem 2 fails because $f_n'$ does not converge uniformly on $[0,1]$; the other hypotheses (differentiability, convergence at a point) hold. On $[0, r]$ with $r < 1$, $\sup|x^{n-1}| = r^{n-1} \to 0$, so there the theorem applies and gives $f'=0=\lim f_n'$.
