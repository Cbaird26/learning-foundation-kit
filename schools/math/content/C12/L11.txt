@article
In one variable, if $f'(a) \ne 0$ then $f$ is strictly monotone near $a$ and has a differentiable inverse with derivative $1/f'(f^{-1}(y))$. The plane map $f(x,y) = (x^2 - y^2,\ 2xy)$ (squaring a complex number) is not injective: $f(-x,-y) = f(x,y)$. But near a point where the derivative is invertible it should be locally invertible, just as $x \mapsto x^2$ is near $x = 1$. This is the inverse function theorem.

### Statement

**Lemma 0.** If $A$ is an invertible $n\times n$ matrix and $\|B - A\| < 1/\|A^{-1}\|$, then $B$ is invertible.

*Proof.* For $v \ne 0$, $\|Bv\| \ge \|Av\| - \|(A-B)v\| \ge \left(\frac{1}{\|A^{-1}\|} - \|A - B\|\right)\|v\| > 0$, using $\|v\| = \|A^{-1}Av\| \le \|A^{-1}\|\|Av\|$. So $B$ is injective, hence invertible. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 1 (inverse function theorem).** Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be open, $f\colon U \to \mathbb{R}^n$ continuously differentiable ($C^1$), $a \in U$, and $Df(a)$ invertible. Then there are open sets $V \ni a$ and $W \ni f(a)$ such that $f\colon V \to W$ is a bijection, its inverse $g\colon W \to V$ is $C^1$, and $Dg(f(x)) = Df(x)^{-1}$ for $x \in V$.

*Proof.* Put $A = Df(a)$, $c = \|A^{-1}\|$. By continuity of $Df$ choose $r > 0$ such that the closed ball $\overline{B}(a,r) \subseteq U$ and $\|Df(x) - A\| \le \frac{1}{2c}$ on it; by Lemma 0, $Df(x)$ is invertible there.

*Step 1: a contraction.* For $y \in \mathbb{R}^n$ let $\varphi_y(x) = x - A^{-1}(f(x) - y)$. Then $\varphi_y(x) = x$ if and only if $f(x) = y$. We have $D\varphi_y(x) = A^{-1}(A - Df(x))$, of norm at most $c\cdot\frac{1}{2c} = \frac12$. By the mean value inequality (Lesson 10, Theorem 3, on the open ball, extended to the closed ball by continuity), $\|\varphi_y(x) - \varphi_y(x')\| \le \frac12\|x - x'\|$ for $x,x' \in \overline{B}(a,r)$.

*Step 2: a self-map.* Let $W = B(f(a), \frac{r}{2c})$ and $y \in W$. For $x \in \overline{B}(a,r)$,
$$\|\varphi_y(x) - a\| \le \|\varphi_y(x) - \varphi_y(a)\| + \|\varphi_y(a) - a\| \le \tfrac r2 + \|A^{-1}(y - f(a))\| < \tfrac r2 + c\cdot\tfrac{r}{2c} = r .$$
So $\varphi_y$ maps the complete space $\overline{B}(a,r)$ into $B(a,r)$ and is a contraction. By the contraction mapping principle, for each $y \in W$ there is exactly one $x = g(y) \in \overline{B}(a,r)$ with $f(x) = y$, and it lies in the open ball. Let $V = B(a,r) \cap f^{-1}(W)$, which is open since $f$ is continuous. Then $f\colon V \to W$ is a bijection with inverse $g$.

*Step 3: $g$ is Lipschitz.* From $\varphi_y(x) - \varphi_y(x') = (x - x') - A^{-1}(f(x) - f(x'))$ we get $\|x - x'\| \le \frac12\|x - x'\| + c\|f(x) - f(x')\|$, hence $\|x - x'\| \le 2c\|f(x) - f(x')\|$. Thus $\|g(y) - g(y')\| \le 2c\|y - y'\|$, and $g$ is continuous.

*Step 4: $g$ is differentiable.* Fix $y \in W$, $x = g(y)$, $B = Df(x)$ (invertible). For small $k$ put $h = g(y+k) - g(y)$; then $k = f(x+h) - f(x) = Bh + \rho(h)$ with $\|\rho(h)\|/\|h\| \to 0$ as $h \to 0$. So $h = B^{-1}k - B^{-1}\rho(h)$. By Step 3, $\|h\| \le 2c\|k\|$, so $h \to 0$ as $k \to 0$ and $\|B^{-1}\rho(h)\| = o(\|h\|) = o(\|k\|)$. Hence $Dg(y) = B^{-1} = Df(g(y))^{-1}$.

*Step 5: $Dg$ is continuous,* as the composition of the continuous maps $g$, $Df$ and matrix inversion (continuous by Cramer's rule). $\blacksquare$

The hypothesis that $Df$ is *continuous* cannot be dropped (see the practice problem on $x + 2x^2\sin(1/x)$), and the conclusion is *local*: $f(x,y) = (x^2-y^2, 2xy)$ has invertible derivative at all points except $0$ but is not injective on $\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{0\}$.

### Implicit functions

**Theorem 2 (implicit function theorem).** Let $F\colon \Omega \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be $C^1$ on an open $\Omega \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R}^m$, with $F(a,b) = 0$ and the $m\times m$ matrix $D_yF(a,b)$ invertible. Then there are an open $U \ni a$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$, an open $V \ni (a,b)$, and a $C^1$ map $g\colon U \to \mathbb{R}^m$ with $g(a) = b$ such that for $(x,y) \in V$: $F(x,y) = 0$ if and only if $x \in U$ and $y = g(x)$. Moreover $Dg(x) = -D_yF(x,g(x))^{-1}\,D_xF(x,g(x))$.

*Proof.* Define $G(x,y) = (x, F(x,y))$ on $\Omega$. Its Jacobian $\begin{pmatrix} I & 0\\ D_xF & D_yF\end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $\det D_yF(a,b) \neq 0$ at $(a,b)$, so by Theorem 1 there are open $V \ni (a,b)$ and $W' \ni (a,0)$ with $G\colon V \to W'$ a bijection with $C^1$ inverse $H$. Since $G$ preserves the first coordinate, $H(x,z) = (x, k(x,z))$ with $k$ of class $C^1$. Let $U = \{x : (x,0) \in W'\}$, open and containing $a$, and $g(x) = k(x,0)$. If $x \in U$ and $y = g(x)$ then $(x,y) = H(x,0) \in V$ and $G(x,y) = (x,0)$, i.e. $F(x,y) = 0$. Conversely if $(x,y) \in V$ and $F(x,y) = 0$ then $G(x,y) = (x,0) \in W'$, so $x \in U$ and $(x,y) = H(x,0) = (x,g(x))$. Differentiating $F(x,g(x)) = 0$ by the chain rule gives $D_xF + D_yF\,Dg = 0$. $\blacksquare$

For the circle $F(x,y) = x^2 + y^2 - 1$ at $(0,1)$ we have $F_y = 2 \ne 0$, so $y = g(x) = \sqrt{1-x^2}$ near $x = 0$, with $g'(0) = -F_x/F_y = 0$. At $(1,0)$, $F_y = 0$ and no function $y = g(x)$ exists near $x=1$, since the points of the circle near $(1,0)$ come in pairs $(x, \pm y)$; there, $F_x = 2\neq0$ lets us solve for $x$ in terms of $y$ instead.

@example
title: Local inverse of the complex squaring map
problem: For $f(x,y) = (x^2 - y^2,\ 2xy)$ show that $f$ is locally invertible near $(1,1)$ and compute $D(f^{-1})$ at $f(1,1)$.
step: $f$ is a polynomial map, hence $C^1$, with $f(1,1) = (0,2)$ and $Df(x,y) = \begin{pmatrix}2x & -2y\\ 2y & 2x\end{pmatrix}$. At $(1,1)$ this is $\begin{pmatrix}2 & -2\\ 2 & 2\end{pmatrix}$ with determinant $8 \ne 0$.
step: By the inverse function theorem there are open sets $V \ni (1,1)$ and $W \ni (0,2)$ with $f\colon V \to W$ bijective and $C^1$ inverse $g$.
step: $Dg(0,2) = Df(1,1)^{-1} = \frac18\begin{pmatrix}2 & 2\\ -2 & 2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1/4 & 1/4\\ -1/4 & 1/4\end{pmatrix}$.
step: The inverse is only local: $f(-1,-1) = (0,2)$ too, so $f$ is not injective on any neighborhood of $\{(1,1),(-1,-1)\}$ that contains both points.
answer: $D(f^{-1})(0,2) = \begin{pmatrix}1/4 & 1/4\\ -1/4 & 1/4\end{pmatrix}$.

@example
title: Derivative of an implicit function
problem: Show that $x^2y + y^3 = 2$ defines $y = g(x)$ near $(1,1)$, and find $g'(1)$.
step: Let $F(x,y) = x^2y + y^3 - 2$, a polynomial, so $C^1$. $F(1,1) = 0$.
step: $F_y = x^2 + 3y^2 = 4$ at $(1,1)$, nonzero. By the implicit function theorem there is a $C^1$ function $g$ with $g(1) = 1$ and $F(x,g(x)) = 0$ near $x = 1$.
step: $F_x = 2xy = 2$ at $(1,1)$.
step: $g'(1) = -F_x/F_y = -2/4 = -1/2$.
answer: $g'(1) = -\tfrac12$.

@mistakes
- Dropping the word "local": $Df$ invertible at every point does not make $f$ injective globally.
- Forgetting continuity of $Df$: the theorem is false for a map that is merely differentiable with $Df(a)$ invertible.
- Taking invertibility of the wrong block: to solve for $y$ you need $D_yF$ (the columns of the variables being solved for), not $D_xF$.
- Reading failure of the hypothesis as non-existence of a solution function: $F_y=0$ at a point means the theorem is silent, though a function may still exist (e.g. $y^3 - x = 0$ at $(0,0)$ gives $y = x^{1/3}$, which is not differentiable).

@practice self
prompt: Let $f(x) = x + 2x^2\sin(1/x)$ for $x \ne 0$ and $f(0) = 0$. Show that $f$ is differentiable at every point with $f'(0) = 1 \ne 0$, but that $f$ is not injective on any open interval containing $0$. Which hypothesis of the inverse function theorem fails?
hint: Compute $f'(x)$ for $x\ne0$, then evaluate it at $x = 1/(2\pi k)$. A continuous injective function on an interval is strictly monotone.
solution: For $x \neq 0$, $f'(x) = 1 + 4x\sin(1/x) - 2\cos(1/x)$, which is continuous on $x \neq 0$. At $0$: $\frac{f(h) - f(0)}{h} = 1 + 2h\sin(1/h) \to 1$, so $f'(0) = 1$ and $f$ is differentiable everywhere (hence continuous). At $x_k = 1/(2\pi k)$ we have $\sin(1/x_k) = 0$, $\cos(1/x_k) = 1$ and $f'(x_k) = -1$; at $z_k = 1/((2k+1)\pi)$ we have $\sin(1/z_k) = 0$, $\cos(1/z_k) = -1$ and $f'(z_k) = 3$. Since $f'$ is continuous near each $x_k$ and $z_k$, $f$ is strictly decreasing on a small interval around each $x_k$ and strictly increasing on a small interval around each $z_k$. Both sequences tend to $0$, so every open interval $I \ni 0$ contains both kinds of intervals. A continuous injective function on an interval is strictly monotone, which $f$ is not on $I$. So $f$ is not injective on $I$. The hypothesis that fails is continuity of $f'$ at $0$: $f'(x_k) = -1$ does not tend to $f'(0) = 1$. $\blacksquare$
