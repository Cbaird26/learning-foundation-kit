@article
A taxi in a city laid out as a grid cannot drive diagonally, so the distance it travels from $(0,0)$ to $(3,4)$ is $3 + 4 = 7$ blocks, not $5$. Yet the notions you used in C8 (limits, continuity, open intervals) only ever used one thing about the real line: a notion of distance. If we isolate exactly the properties of distance that the proofs used, we can do analysis on the plane, on spaces of functions, and on much stranger sets.

### Metric spaces

**Definition.** A *metric space* is a set $X$ with a function $d\colon X \times X \to \mathbb{R}$, the *metric*, such that for all $x, y, z \in X$:

1. $d(x,y) \ge 0$, and $d(x,y) = 0$ if and only if $x = y$;
2. $d(x,y) = d(y,x)$;
3. $d(x,z) \le d(x,y) + d(y,z)$ (the *triangle inequality*).

Examples. (a) $\mathbb{R}$ with $d(x,y) = |x-y|$. (b) On $\mathbb{R}^n$, the Euclidean metric $d_2(x,y) = \|x-y\|$, the taxicab metric $d_1(x,y) = \sum_i |x_i - y_i|$ and the max metric $d_\infty(x,y) = \max_i |x_i - y_i|$. (c) On any set, the *discrete metric*: $d(x,y) = 1$ if $x \ne y$ and $0$ if $x = y$. (d) On the set $C[0,1]$ of continuous functions $[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}$, the *sup metric* $d(f,g) = \sup_{t \in [0,1]} |f(t) - g(t)|$; the supremum is finite and attained because $|f-g|$ is continuous on a closed interval (C8). Its triangle inequality follows from $|f(t)-h(t)| \le |f(t)-g(t)| + |g(t)-h(t)| \le d(f,g) + d(g,h)$ for each $t$, then taking the sup over $t$.

For $d_1$, the triangle inequality holds because $|x_i - z_i| \le |x_i - y_i| + |y_i - z_i|$ in each coordinate, and then we sum. Not every "distance-like" formula works: $d(x,y) = |x-y|^2$ on $\mathbb{R}$ violates the triangle inequality, since $d(0,2) = 4 > 1 + 1 = d(0,1) + d(1,2)$.

### Open and closed sets

The *open ball* of radius $r > 0$ about $x$ is $B(x,r) = \{y \in X : d(x,y) < r\}$. A set $U \subseteq X$ is **open** if for every $x \in U$ there is $r > 0$ with $B(x,r) \subseteq U$. A set $F$ is **closed** if $X \setminus F$ is open.

**Theorem 1.** Every open ball $B(x,r)$ is open.

*Proof.* Let $y \in B(x,r)$ and put $s = r - d(x,y) > 0$. If $z \in B(y,s)$ then $d(x,z) \le d(x,y) + d(y,z) < d(x,y) + s = r$, so $z \in B(x,r)$. Hence $B(y,s) \subseteq B(x,r)$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 2.** In a metric space $X$: (i) $\emptyset$ and $X$ are open; (ii) any union of open sets is open; (iii) the intersection of *finitely many* open sets is open.

*Proof.* (i) is immediate. (ii) If $x \in \bigcup_\alpha U_\alpha$ then $x \in U_\beta$ for some $\beta$, there is a ball $B(x,r) \subseteq U_\beta$, and it lies in the union. (iii) Let $x \in U_1 \cap \dots \cap U_m$ and choose $r_i$ with $B(x,r_i) \subseteq U_i$. With $r = \min_i r_i > 0$ we get $B(x,r) \subseteq$ every $U_i$. $\blacksquare$

Taking complements (De Morgan), closed sets are closed under arbitrary intersections and finite unions. *Finiteness matters*: $\bigcap_{n \ge 1} (-1/n, 1/n) = \{0\}$ is an intersection of open sets in $\mathbb{R}$, but $\{0\}$ is not open.

### Closure, interior, boundary

For $A \subseteq X$: the *interior* $A^\circ$ is the union of all open sets contained in $A$; the *closure* $\overline{A}$ is the intersection of all closed sets containing $A$; the *boundary* is $\partial A = \overline{A} \setminus A^\circ$.

**Proposition.** $x \in \overline{A}$ if and only if every ball $B(x,r)$ meets $A$.

*Proof.* If some ball $B(x,r)$ misses $A$, then $X \setminus B(x,r)$ is closed (Theorem 1), contains $A$ and omits $x$, so $x \notin \overline{A}$. Conversely, if $x \notin \overline{A}$, some closed $F \supseteq A$ omits $x$; the open set $X \setminus F$ contains a ball $B(x,r)$, which misses $F \supseteq A$. $\blacksquare$

### Same set, different space

Being open depends on the ambient space: $[0,1)$ is not open in $\mathbb{R}$, but it is open in the metric space $X = [0,1]$ with the usual distance, since $B(0, 1/2) = [0,1/2)$ there. In a discrete metric space every singleton is a ball, $B(x, 1) = \{x\}$, so every subset is open and, by complement, closed.

Also, different metrics may give the same open sets. On $\mathbb{R}^n$, $d_\infty \le d_2 \le d_1 \le n\, d_\infty$, so every $d_1$-ball contains a $d_\infty$-ball of the same center and vice versa; the three metrics have exactly the same open sets, and so exactly the same limits and continuous functions (later lessons). The sup metric on $C[0,1]$ is a genuinely new geometry: the "ball" $B(f, \varepsilon)$ is the set of continuous $g$ whose graph stays in an $\varepsilon$-tube around the graph of $f$.

@example
title: Checking that a formula is a metric
problem: Show that $d(x,y) = |x^3 - y^3|$ is a metric on $\mathbb{R}$.
step: Positivity: $d(x,y) \ge 0$ as an absolute value. If $d(x,y) = 0$ then $x^3 = y^3$. The cube map is strictly increasing on $\mathbb{R}$, hence injective, so $x = y$. Conversely $d(x,x) = 0$.
step: Symmetry: $|x^3 - y^3| = |y^3 - x^3|$.
step: Triangle inequality: $d(x,z) = |x^3 - z^3| = |(x^3 - y^3) + (y^3 - z^3)| \le |x^3 - y^3| + |y^3 - z^3| = d(x,y) + d(y,z)$, by the triangle inequality for $|\cdot|$.
step: Injectivity was essential: $|x^2 - y^2|$ fails the first axiom because $d(1,-1) = 0$ with $1 \ne -1$.
answer: $d$ is a metric on $\mathbb{R}$, because $x \mapsto x^3$ is injective.

@example
title: Interior, closure and boundary in the plane
problem: In $\mathbb{R}^2$ with the Euclidean metric let $A = \{(x,y) : x^2 + y^2 < 1\} \cup \{(2,0)\}$. Find $A^\circ$, $\overline{A}$ and $\partial A$.
step: The open disc $D = \{x^2 + y^2 < 1\}$ is a ball, hence open (Theorem 1), and $D \subseteq A$, so $D \subseteq A^\circ$.
step: The point $(2,0)$ is not interior: every ball about it contains points of the form $(2+s, 0)$ with $s>0$ small, which are not in $A$. A point with $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ is not interior either, since each ball around it contains points of norm greater than $1$. Hence $A^\circ = D$.
step: By the Proposition, a point is in $\overline{A}$ if every ball meets $A$. Points of the unit circle qualify (points of $D$ approach them), and so does $(2,0) \in A$. A point with $x^2 + y^2 > 1$ other than $(2,0)$ has a small ball missing both $D$ and $(2,0)$. So $\overline{A} = \{x^2 + y^2 \le 1\} \cup \{(2,0)\}$.
step: $\partial A = \overline{A} \setminus A^\circ$ is the unit circle together with the isolated point $(2,0)$.
answer: $A^\circ$ is the open unit disc; $\overline{A}$ is the closed unit disc plus $(2,0)$; $\partial A$ is the unit circle plus $(2,0)$.

@mistakes
- Saying a set "is open" without naming the space: $[0,1)$ is open in $[0,1]$ but not in $\mathbb{R}$.
- Thinking "not open" means "closed": $[0,1)$ is neither open nor closed in $\mathbb{R}$, while $\emptyset$ and $X$ are both.
- Applying the finite-intersection rule to infinitely many open sets: $\bigcap_n (-1/n, 1/n) = \{0\}$ is not open.
- Checking only symmetry and positivity of a candidate metric and forgetting the triangle inequality: $|x-y|^2$ fails it.

@practice self
prompt: Let $(X,d)$ be a metric space, $x \in X$ and $r \ge 0$. Prove that the closed ball $\overline{B}(x,r) = \{y \in X : d(x,y) \le r\}$ is a closed set. Then give an example in which $\overline{B}(x,r)$ is not the closure of $B(x,r)$.
hint: Show the complement is open: if $d(x,y) > r$, find a ball around $y$ that stays outside $\overline{B}(x,r)$.
solution: Let $y \notin \overline{B}(x,r)$, so $s = d(x,y) - r > 0$. If $z \in B(y,s)$ then $d(x,z) \ge d(x,y) - d(y,z) > d(x,y) - s = r$ by the triangle inequality, so $z \notin \overline{B}(x,r)$. Thus $B(y,s)$ lies in the complement, the complement is open, and $\overline{B}(x,r)$ is closed. $\blacksquare$ Example: in a discrete metric space with at least two points, $B(x,1) = \{x\}$ is closed, so it equals its own closure, while $\overline{B}(x,1) = X$.
rubric: Shows the complement is open by choosing $s = d(x,y) - r > 0$.
rubric: Uses the triangle inequality in the correct direction ($d(x,z) \ge d(x,y) - d(y,z)$).
rubric: Gives a valid example, such as a discrete space, where the closed ball is strictly larger than the closure of the open ball.
