@article
Integrating $e^{-x^2}$ has no elementary antiderivative, yet $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2}\,dx = \sqrt\pi$. The classical proof squares the integral, turns the product into an integral over the plane, and switches to polar coordinates, where the integrand becomes $e^{-r^2}$ and the extra factor $r$ makes it integrable by substitution. Each step needs a theorem: that a double integral is computed by iterated integrals, and that coordinates may be changed at the cost of a Jacobian factor.

### The integral over a rectangle

Let $R = [a,b]\times[c,d]$. A partition $P \times Q$ of $R$ comes from partitions $a = x_0 < \dots < x_p = b$ and $c = y_0 < \dots < y_q = d$ and cuts $R$ into cells $R_{ij}$ of area $\Delta x_i\,\Delta y_j$. For bounded $f$ the lower and upper sums are $L(f) = \sum m_{ij}\,\Delta x_i\Delta y_j$ and $U(f) = \sum M_{ij}\,\Delta x_i\Delta y_j$, with $m_{ij}, M_{ij}$ the infimum and supremum of $f$ on $R_{ij}$. As on the line (C8), $f$ is *integrable* if $\sup L = \inf U$ over all partitions, and $\iint_R f$ is that common value. A continuous $f$ on $R$ is integrable: it is uniformly continuous on the compact $R$ (Lesson 4), so for fine partitions $M_{ij} - m_{ij} < \varepsilon$ and $U - L \le \varepsilon\cdot\text{area}(R)$.

**Theorem 1 (Fubini, continuous case).** If $f$ is continuous on $R = [a,b]\times[c,d]$, then
$$\iint_R f = \int_a^b\left(\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy\right)dx = \int_c^d\left(\int_a^b f(x,y)\,dx\right)dy .$$

*Proof.* Put $F(x) = \int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy$. It is continuous in $x$: given $\varepsilon$, uniform continuity of $f$ gives $\delta$ with $|f(x,y) - f(x',y)| < \varepsilon$ whenever $|x - x'| < \delta$, hence $|F(x) - F(x')| \le \varepsilon(d-c)$. Fix a partition $P\times Q$. For $x \in [x_{i-1}, x_i]$ and each $j$ we have $f(x,y) \ge m_{ij}$ on $[y_{j-1},y_j]$, so $F(x) \ge \sum_j m_{ij}\Delta y_j$. Integrating over $[x_{i-1},x_i]$ and summing over $i$ gives $\int_a^b F \ge L(f)$, and likewise $\int_a^b F \le U(f)$. So $\int_a^b F$ lies between every lower sum and every upper sum; the only such number is $\iint_R f$. The other order is symmetric. $\blacksquare$

For a region $D = \{(x,y) : a \le x \le b,\ \varphi(x) \le y \le \psi(x)\}$ with $\varphi \le \psi$ continuous, and $f$ continuous on $D$, the integral over $D$ is (by extending $f$ by $0$ to a rectangle, whose boundary graphs have zero area) the iterated integral $\int_a^b\int_{\varphi(x)}^{\psi(x)} f\,dy\,dx$. Often only one order is feasible, so choose the order that matches the shape of $D$.

### Change of variables

**Theorem 2 (change of variables).** Let $U, V \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ be open and $\Phi\colon U \to V$ a $C^1$ bijection with $C^1$ inverse (a diffeomorphism), and let $f$ be continuous and bounded on $V$ with $\Phi$ and $f$ extending suitably to the boundary (for instance, $U$ and $V$ bounded with boundaries of zero area). Then
$$\iint_V f(x,y)\,dx\,dy = \iint_U f(\Phi(u,v))\,\left|\det D\Phi(u,v)\right|\,du\,dv .$$

*Outline of the proof* (the full proof is long and is given in the reading). *Linear case:* for $\Phi(u) = Au$, the image of a rectangle has area $|\det A|$ times the original: this holds for the elementary matrices (scaling one coordinate by $\lambda$ multiplies areas by $|\lambda|$; a shear $(u,v) \mapsto (u + tv, v)$ preserves area by Fubini; a swap preserves area), and every invertible matrix is a product of elementary ones, with $\det$ multiplicative. *General case:* cut $U$ into tiny squares; on each, $\Phi$ is within $o(\text{size})$ of its linearization (by differentiability, uniform on compact sets by continuity of $D\Phi$ and the mean value inequality), so the image has area $|\det D\Phi|$ times the square's area up to a small error; summing the Riemann sums and refining gives the formula.

The absolute value matters: it measures *area scaling*, and area is positive even when $\Phi$ reverses orientation. **Polar coordinates** $\Phi(r,\theta) = (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$ have $D\Phi = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & -r\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$ and $\det D\Phi = r$, so $dx\,dy = r\,dr\,d\theta$.

**The Gaussian integral.** Let $I_R = \int_{-R}^{R} e^{-x^2}dx$. By Fubini, $I_R^2 = \iint_{S_R} e^{-(x^2+y^2)}$ over the square $S_R = [-R,R]^2$. With $D_\rho$ the disc of radius $\rho$ about $0$, $D_R \subseteq S_R \subseteq D_{R\sqrt2}$ and the integrand is positive, so by polar coordinates
$$\pi\left(1 - e^{-R^2}\right) = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^R e^{-r^2}r\,dr\,d\theta \le I_R^2 \le \pi\left(1 - e^{-2R^2}\right).$$
Letting $R \to \infty$ gives $I_R^2 \to \pi$, so $\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-x^2}dx = \sqrt\pi$.

### A numerical midpoint sum

```python
def midpoint2(f, a, b, c, d, n):
    """Midpoint Riemann sum of f over [a,b] x [c,d] with n x n cells."""
    hx = (b - a) / n
    hy = (d - c) / n
    total = 0.0
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(n):
            total += f(a + (i + 0.5) * hx, c + (j + 0.5) * hy)
    return total * hx * hy


f = lambda x, y: x * x + y
print(round(midpoint2(f, 0, 1, 0, 2, 10), 3), round(midpoint2(f, 0, 1, 0, 2, 20), 3))
```

It prints `2.665 2.666`, against the exact value $\int_0^1\!\int_0^2 (x^2 + y)\,dy\,dx = 2/3 + 2 = 8/3 = 2.6667$. The sums approach it, which is evidence; the proof that Riemann sums of a continuous function converge is the integrability argument above.

@example
title: Choosing the order of integration
problem: Compute $\displaystyle\iint_D (x + y)\,dA$ where $D = \{(x,y) : 0 \le x \le 1,\ x^2 \le y \le x\}$.
step: $D$ is the region between the parabola $y = x^2$ and the line $y = x$ for $0 \le x \le 1$ (they meet at $x=0$ and $x=1$), so integrate first in $y$ with $\varphi(x) = x^2$, $\psi(x) = x$.
step: Inner integral: $\int_{x^2}^{x}(x + y)\,dy = x(x - x^2) + \tfrac12(x^2 - x^4) = \tfrac32 x^2 - x^3 - \tfrac12x^4$.
step: Outer integral: $\int_0^1\left(\tfrac32x^2 - x^3 - \tfrac12x^4\right)dx = \tfrac12 - \tfrac14 - \tfrac1{10}$.
step: Simplify: $\tfrac{10 - 5 - 2}{20} = \tfrac{3}{20}$.
answer: $\iint_D (x+y)\,dA = \dfrac{3}{20}$.

@example
title: Polar coordinates
problem: Compute $\displaystyle\iint_{x^2+y^2 \le 4}(x^2 + y^2)\,dA$.
step: Use $\Phi(r,\theta) = (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$ on $U = (0,2)\times(0,2\pi)$; it maps $U$ diffeomorphically onto the disc minus a segment and the origin, a set of full area, with $|\det D\Phi| = r$.
step: The integrand is $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$, so the integral is $\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^2 r^2\cdot r\,dr\,d\theta$.
step: $\int_0^2 r^3\,dr = 4$, and $\int_0^{2\pi}d\theta = 2\pi$.
step: The product is $8\pi$.
answer: $8\pi \approx 25.13$.

@mistakes
- Omitting the Jacobian factor: in polar coordinates $dx\,dy = r\,dr\,d\theta$, not $dr\,d\theta$.
- Using $\det D\Phi$ without the absolute value, or evaluating the Jacobian of the inverse map instead of $\Phi$ (the formula uses $\Phi\colon U \to V$ with $f\circ\Phi$).
- Swapping the order of integration without changing the limits: for $D = \{0 \le y \le x \le 1\}$ the reversed iterated integral is $\int_0^1\int_y^1 \dots dx\,dy$, not $\int_0^1\int_0^1$.
- Thinking Fubini holds for every function: it needs integrability of $f$ (continuity suffices); the infinite-region version needs absolute integrability.

model:
```python
def region_sum(f, inside, a, b, c, d, n):
    """Midpoint sum of f over the cells whose midpoints satisfy inside."""
    hx = (b - a) / n
    hy = (d - c) / n
    total = 0.0
    for i in range(n):
        x = a + (i + 0.5) * hx
        for j in range(n):
            y = c + (j + 0.5) * hy
            if inside(x, y):
                total += f(x, y)
    return total * hx * hy
```
hint: Loop over the cells, compute the midpoint of cell $(i,j)$, and add `f` there only when `inside` accepts the midpoint.
solution: The midpoint of cell $(i,j)$ is $(a + (i + \tfrac12)h_x,\ c + (j + \tfrac12)h_y)$. Summing only over accepted midpoints replaces $f$ by $f\cdot 1_D$ on the rectangle, which is how an integral over a region $D$ is defined. For the triangle-like region $y < x^2$ with $n = 5$ the count of accepted midpoints is $7$, giving $7 \cdot 0.04 = 0.28$, a rough approximation of the true area $1/3$ because $n$ is small.
