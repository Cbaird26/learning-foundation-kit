@article
A friend sends you a 40-page paper with the message "you should read this." You open page 1 and begin at the first line. By page 6 you are lost in notation, and by page 12 you have forgotten why you started. Mathematicians do not read papers like novels. They read them in passes, with a question in hand, with a pencil, and they stop when they have what they need.

### The arXiv and preprints

The **arXiv** is an open online archive where authors post **preprints**: versions of papers that have not necessarily been through peer review. Authors post a preprint when they finish it, often at the same time as they submit it to a journal. Each posting has an identifier, a subject class (for example, number theory or geometric topology), an abstract page, and a record of its **versions**: authors may replace the file with a corrected or improved one, and the earlier versions remain available. The abstract page often shows a journal reference if the authors supplied one.

What a posting guarantees is limited. An archive moderator checks that a submission is appropriate for the category; no one checks that its proofs are correct. A preprint may be excellent, may contain a gap, or may be a draft. Practices differ across fields and over time, so ask your advisor how mathematicians in your area treat preprints. When you cite a result, say which version you read, and check whether a journal version exists.

### Three passes

**Pass 1: what is the paper about? (10 to 15 minutes).** Read the title, abstract, introduction and the statements of the main theorems. Skim the section headings and the list of references. Write one sentence: "The authors prove that ... under the assumption that ... using ..." If you cannot, reread the introduction; a good introduction states the result and explains the idea.

**Pass 2: how does the proof go? (1 to 2 hours).** Read the statements of all lemmas and propositions and the first few lines of each proof. Draw the dependency graph on paper: which lemma feeds which. Mark the one or two steps where the main idea lives. Identify the cited results the proof leans on and check that their hypotheses hold in the situation at hand.

**Pass 3: is it right, and can I use it? (as long as needed).** Work through the details of the parts you need, with a pencil, in your own notation. Fill in every step that the author calls "clear" or "straightforward." Try to prove each lemma before reading its proof. Stop when you understand the argument well enough to explain it aloud.

### Questions to ask on every paper

1. What problem is being solved, and why does it matter to the authors?
2. What is the exact main theorem? What are its hypotheses and conclusion?
3. What is new: the result, the method, or both?
4. What is the key idea of the proof, in two sentences?
5. Which results from earlier literature are used, and are their hypotheses satisfied?
6. What examples show the theorem is sharp or illustrate it?
7. What does the paper leave open?

### Stress-testing a statement

Treat every theorem as a claim to be attacked, kindly. Specialize it to the simplest case you can compute. Drop each hypothesis in turn and look for a counterexample; if none exists, ask why the hypothesis is there. Try small values. Computing with Python or a computer algebra system is a good way to test a conjecture, but a computation that checks cases is evidence, never a proof; and when the claim is a theorem in the literature, a failed check usually means you mis-read the hypotheses.

### Reading backward and forward

The references of a paper show where its tools come from, and a survey in the same area can supply the background that the paper assumes. To see what has happened since, look for later papers that cite it. When a paper is too hard, read an expository article or lecture notes on the same topic first, and come back.

### When you think you found an error

Check first that you have the right version and the right statement. Then write out the step in full detail. If the gap persists, talk to your advisor or a colleague before doing anything else; a polite message to the author that describes the specific step is the usual next move. Mistakes in papers happen, and many are repaired by the authors in a later version.

### Notes you can reuse

For each paper write half a page: problem, main theorem (exact), method in two sentences, hypotheses and examples, how you might use it, and open questions. Lesson 8 asks for a one-page summary of this kind.

@example
title: Stress-testing a theorem statement
problem: A paper states: "Let $p$ be an odd prime. Then $p = a^2 + b^2$ for some integers $a$ and $b$ if and only if $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$." Test the statement and explain the role of the hypothesis "odd".
step: Check primes congruent to $1$ modulo $4$: $5 = 1 + 4$, $13 = 4 + 9$, $17 = 1 + 16$, $29 = 4 + 25$. All are sums of two squares, as predicted.
step: Check primes congruent to $3$ modulo $4$: $3$, $7$, $11$. No pair of squares from $\{0, 1, 4, 9\}$ adds to $3$, $7$ or $11$. This agrees with the other direction.
step: Explain the other direction with a proof you can reconstruct: squares modulo $4$ are $0$ or $1$, so a sum of two squares is $0$, $1$ or $2$ modulo $4$, never $3$.
step: Drop the hypothesis "odd": the prime $2 = 1 + 1$ is a sum of two squares but $2 \not\equiv 1 \pmod 4$. So "if and only if $p \equiv 1$" is false for $p = 2$, and the word "odd" is needed.
answer: The statement holds for the primes tested, one direction follows from squares modulo $4$, and the hypothesis "odd" excludes the counterexample $p = 2$.

@example
title: Catching a missing hypothesis
problem: You read: "Every uniformly bounded sequence of continuous functions on $[0,1]$ has a uniformly convergent subsequence." Decide whether this is true as stated.
step: Compare with the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem, which has two hypotheses: uniform boundedness and equicontinuity. The sentence drops one of them.
step: Test with $f_n(x) = x^n$ on $[0,1]$. Then $|f_n| \le 1$, so the sequence is uniformly bounded, and each $f_n$ is continuous.
step: For each subsequence, $f_{n_k} \to f$ pointwise where $f(x) = 0$ for $x < 1$ and $f(1) = 1$. A uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous, but $f$ is not, so no subsequence converges uniformly.
step: Decision: the statement is false as written. The correct statement adds equicontinuity. When you meet this in a paper, check whether the hypothesis was dropped by accident in the introduction and appears in the theorem proper.
answer: False as stated; $f_n(x) = x^n$ is a counterexample, and equicontinuity must be added.

@mistakes
- Reading from page 1 to the end, line by line, before knowing what the paper claims.
- Using a cited theorem without checking that its hypotheses hold in your situation.
- Treating a preprint as certified: posting is not refereeing, so read critically and check for later versions.
- Believing a statement because a computer checked a few cases: it is evidence, and proofs are about all cases.

@practice num
prompt: A paper asserts that every prime $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ is a sum of two squares $p = a^2 + b^2$ with $a > b > 0$. Test it on $p = 53$ and give $a + b$.
answer: 9
check: next(a + b for a in range(1, 8) for b in range(1, a) if a * a + b * b == 53)
hint: List the squares less than $53$ and look for a pair that adds to $53$.
solution: $53 = 49 + 4 = 7^2 + 2^2$, so $a = 7$, $b = 2$ and $a + b = 9$. No other pair of positive squares sums to $53$, so the answer is unique.

@practice self
prompt: **Structured notes.** The Arzelà–Ascoli theorem states: let $K$ be a compact metric space and let $\mathcal{F}$ be a subset of $C(K)$, the continuous real-valued functions on $K$ with the supremum norm. If $\mathcal{F}$ is uniformly bounded and equicontinuous, then every sequence in $\mathcal{F}$ has a uniformly convergent subsequence. Write one page of structured notes as you would for a paper: problem, exact statement with hypotheses, the role of each hypothesis (with a counterexample for each when dropped), a one-paragraph idea of the proof, and one use.
hint: Equicontinuity means: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a $\delta > 0$ such that $d(x, y) < \delta$ implies $|f(x) - f(y)| < \varepsilon$ for every $f \in \mathcal{F}$ simultaneously.
hint: Find a family that is bounded but not equicontinuous, and one that is equicontinuous but not bounded.
solution: Problem: decide when a family of continuous functions has a uniformly convergent sequence; this is compactness in $C(K)$ (the theorem says relatively compact). Statement: $K$ compact metric; $\mathcal{F} \subseteq C(K)$ uniformly bounded (there is $M$ with $|f(x)| \le M$ for all $f\in\mathcal{F}$, $x\in K$) and equicontinuous (a single $\delta$ works for all $f$ for each $\varepsilon$); then every sequence in $\mathcal{F}$ has a uniformly convergent subsequence, whose limit is continuous. Roles of the hypotheses: without uniform boundedness, $f_n = n$ (constants) is equicontinuous but has no convergent subsequence. Without equicontinuity, $f_n(x) = x^n$ on $[0,1]$ is bounded by $1$ but no subsequence converges uniformly, since the pointwise limit is discontinuous at $1$. Compactness of $K$ is also used: on $\mathbb{R}$, the translates $f_n(x) = \varphi(x - n)$ of a nonzero continuous bump $\varphi$ supported in $(0,1)$ are bounded and equicontinuous but converge pointwise to $0$ without being uniformly Cauchy, because $\sup|f_n - f_m| = \sup \varphi$ for $n \ne m$ with disjoint supports. Idea of the proof: take a countable dense subset $D$ of $K$; use boundedness and a diagonal argument to extract a subsequence converging at every point of $D$; use equicontinuity and compactness (a finite $\delta$-net) to show the subsequence is uniformly Cauchy; completeness of $C(K)$ gives the limit. Use: to prove that a family of solutions of a differential equation with bounded and equicontinuous members has a limit, or to prove that an integral operator with continuous kernel is compact.
rubric: States the theorem exactly, including the metric on $K$ and the definition of equicontinuity.
rubric: Gives a counterexample for each dropped hypothesis, including compactness of $K$ (or says clearly which hypotheses were tested).
rubric: Describes the proof idea correctly: a countable dense set, a diagonal subsequence, and equicontinuity to upgrade to uniform convergence.

@quiz num
prompt: A paper claims: "for every integer $n \ge 1$ the number $n^2 + n + 41$ is prime." Test the claim by computer and find the smallest $n \ge 1$ for which it fails.
answer: 40
check: next(n for n in range(1, 100) if any((n * n + n + 41) % d == 0 for d in range(2, int((n * n + n + 41) ** 0.5) + 1)))
hint: For $n = 40$ factor out $41$ from $n^2 + n + 41$.
solution: For $n = 40$, $n^2 + n + 41 = 1600 + 40 + 41 = 1681 = 41^2$, which is composite. A direct computation shows that the values for $n = 1, \dots, 39$ are all prime, so the smallest failure is $n = 40$. This is evidence of why many checked cases are not a proof.

@quiz self
prompt: A paper's introduction states: "A continuous integrable function $f\colon \mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ with $\int_{\mathbb{R}} |f| < \infty$ satisfies $f(x) \to 0$ as $|x| \to \infty$." Decide whether this is true. If it is false, give a counterexample with complete verification, and state an additional hypothesis under which the claim holds.
hint: Build a function with a tall, very thin bump of height $1$ at each integer $n$.
hint: A uniformly continuous integrable function does tend to $0$; try to show why.
solution: The claim is false. For each integer $n \ge 1$ let $g_n$ be the tent function with $g_n(n) = 1$, supported on $[n - w_n, n + w_n]$ with $w_n = 1/(2n^2)$, and linear on each side. The supports are pairwise disjoint (the supports of $g_n$ and $g_{n+1}$ are separated because $w_n + w_{n+1} \le 1/2 + 1/8 < 1$). Let $f = \sum_{n \ge 1} g_n$. On each bounded interval only finitely many terms are nonzero, so $f$ is continuous. Also $f(n) = 1$ for all $n$, so $f(x) \not\to 0$. The integral of a tent of height $1$ and base $2w_n$ is $w_n = 1/(2n^2)$, so $\int |f| = \sum 1/(2n^2) = \pi^2/12 < \infty$. Repair: if in addition $f$ is uniformly continuous, then $f(x) \to 0$. Proof: suppose not; then there are $\varepsilon > 0$ and $x_k \to \infty$ in absolute value (taking a subsequence with $|x_k - x_j| > 1$) with $|f(x_k)| \ge \varepsilon$. By uniform continuity there is $\delta \in (0, 1/2)$ with $|f(y)| \ge \varepsilon/2$ for $|y - x_k| < \delta$. The intervals $(x_k - \delta, x_k + \delta)$ are disjoint, so $\int |f| \ge \sum_k 2\delta\,\varepsilon/2 = \infty$, a contradiction. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Declares the claim false and constructs a continuous integrable function that does not tend to $0$, verifying continuity, integrability and the values $f(n) = 1$.
rubric: Checks that the supports are disjoint and that the integral is a convergent series.
rubric: States and proves a correct repair, such as uniform continuity, with a valid disjoint-intervals argument.
