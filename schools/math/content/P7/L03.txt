@article
Does $\lim_{n\to\infty} \int f_n = \int \lim_{n\to\infty} f_n$? Take $f_n = n \cdot \mathbf{1}_{(0, 1/n)}$ on $[0,1]$. Each integral equals $1$, but $f_n(x) \to 0$ for every $x$, so the limit function has integral $0$. Exchanging limits and integrals is the central question of real analysis, and the answer always has the form "yes, under this hypothesis."

### Real analysis: the core results

On a measure space $(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)$:

- **Monotone convergence theorem.** If $0 \le f_1 \le f_2 \le \dots$ are measurable and $f_n \to f$ pointwise, then $\int f_n \to \int f$ (the value $+\infty$ is allowed).
- **Fatou's lemma.** If $f_n \ge 0$ are measurable, then $\int \liminf f_n \le \liminf \int f_n$.
- **Dominated convergence theorem.** If $f_n$ are measurable, $f_n \to f$ pointwise almost everywhere, and there is an integrable $g$ with $|f_n| \le g$ for all $n$, then $f$ is integrable and $\int f_n \to \int f$ (indeed $\int |f_n - f| \to 0$).

The example above violates the last hypothesis: any $g \ge \sup_n f_n$ satisfies $g(x) \ge 1/x - 1$ on $(0,1)$ and so $\int g = \infty$.

For continuous functions the corresponding facts are about **uniform convergence**: a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous, and if $f_n \to f$ uniformly on $[a,b]$ then $\int_a^b f_n \to \int_a^b f$. The **Arzelà–Ascoli theorem** says that a sequence of functions on a compact metric space $K$ that is uniformly bounded and equicontinuous has a uniformly convergent subsequence. Both hypotheses are needed: $f_n(x) = x^n$ on $[0,1]$ is uniformly bounded but not equicontinuous at $1$, and $f_n = n$ is equicontinuous but not bounded.

Other results an exam expects you to state and use: completeness of $L^p$ (the Riesz–Fischer theorem), Hölder's inequality $\int |fg| \le \|f\|_p \|g\|_q$ for $\frac1p + \frac1q = 1$, Fubini's and Tonelli's theorems (Tonelli needs nonnegativity, Fubini needs integrability), and the Baire category theorem (a nonempty complete metric space is not a countable union of nowhere dense sets).

### Complex analysis: the core results

Let $f$ be holomorphic on an open set containing the closed disc $\overline{D}(a, R)$. **Cauchy's integral formula** says that for $|z - a| < R$,
$$f(z) = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint_{|w-a|=R} \frac{f(w)}{w - z}\,dw, \qquad f^{(k)}(a) = \frac{k!}{2\pi i} \oint_{|w-a|=R} \frac{f(w)}{(w-a)^{k+1}}\,dw.$$
From the second formula, with $|f| \le M$ on the circle, come the **Cauchy estimates** $|f^{(k)}(a)| \le k!\,M / R^k$.

**Liouville's theorem.** A bounded entire function is constant.

*Proof.* Let $|f| \le M$ on $\mathbb{C}$. For any $a \in \mathbb{C}$ and any $R > 0$, the Cauchy estimate with $k = 1$ gives $|f'(a)| \le M/R$. Letting $R \to \infty$ gives $f'(a) = 0$. So $f' \equiv 0$ on the connected set $\mathbb{C}$, and $f$ is constant. $\blacksquare$

The **residue theorem**: if $f$ is holomorphic on an open set containing a simple closed positively oriented curve $\gamma$ and its interior, except for finitely many isolated singularities $a_j$ inside $\gamma$, then
$$\oint_\gamma f(z)\,dz = 2\pi i \sum_j \operatorname{Res}(f, a_j).$$
For a simple pole, $\operatorname{Res}(f, a) = \lim_{z\to a}(z-a)f(z)$. For a pole of order $m$, $\operatorname{Res}(f,a) = \frac{1}{(m-1)!}\lim_{z\to a}\frac{d^{m-1}}{dz^{m-1}}\big[(z-a)^m f(z)\big]$.

**Rouché's theorem.** If $f$ and $g$ are holomorphic on a neighborhood of a closed disc and $|g(z)| < |f(z)|$ on its boundary circle, then $f$ and $f + g$ have the same number of zeros in the open disc, counted with multiplicity.

### How exam problems are usually attacked

| If the problem says... | First try |
|---|---|
| exchange a limit and an integral | find a dominating $g$ (dominated convergence), or monotonicity |
| real integral over $\mathbb{R}$ of a rational function | close a contour in the upper half plane; check the arc estimate |
| how many zeros in a region | Rouché on circles, comparing the dominant term |
| entire and bounded, or growth bound | Cauchy estimates and Liouville |
| subsequence, compactness in $C(K)$ | Arzelà–Ascoli: bounded plus equicontinuous |

Whatever the tool, state its hypotheses and check them on the problem's data.

@example
title: A real integral by residues
problem: Evaluate $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{dx}{x^4 + 1}$.
step: Let $f(z) = 1/(z^4+1)$ and integrate over the boundary of the half disc $\{|z| < R,\ \operatorname{Im} z > 0\}$ for $R > 1$: the segment $[-R, R]$ followed by the upper semicircle $C_R$.
step: Inside the half disc, $f$ has poles where $z^4 = -1$ and $\operatorname{Im} z > 0$, namely $z_1 = e^{i\pi/4}$ and $z_2 = e^{3i\pi/4}$. Each is simple.
step: For a simple pole of $1/q(z)$, the residue is $1/q'(z_j)$, so $\operatorname{Res}(f, z_j) = 1/(4z_j^3) = z_j/(4z_j^4) = -z_j/4$.
step: The sum of residues is $-(z_1 + z_2)/4 = -(i\sqrt{2})/4$, because $z_1 + z_2 = i\sqrt2$. So the contour integral is $2\pi i \cdot (-i\sqrt2/4) = \pi\sqrt2/2$.
step: On $C_R$, $|f(z)| \le 1/(R^4 - 1)$ and the length is $\pi R$, so the integral over $C_R$ is at most $\pi R/(R^4-1) \to 0$. Letting $R \to \infty$ leaves the real integral.
answer: $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{dx}{x^4+1} = \frac{\pi}{\sqrt2}$.

@example
title: A limit under the integral sign
problem: Find $\lim_{n\to\infty} \int_0^\infty \frac{n \sin(x/n)}{x(1+x^2)}\,dx$ and justify the exchange.
step: Write $f_n(x) = \dfrac{n\sin(x/n)}{x(1+x^2)}$ for $x > 0$. Each $f_n$ is continuous, hence measurable.
step: Pointwise limit: $n \sin(x/n)/x = \sin(x/n)/(x/n) \to 1$ as $n \to \infty$, so $f_n(x) \to \dfrac{1}{1+x^2}$.
step: Domination: $|\sin t| \le |t|$ gives $|n\sin(x/n)| \le x$, so $|f_n(x)| \le \dfrac{1}{1+x^2} =: g(x)$, and $g$ is integrable on $(0, \infty)$ with integral $\pi/2$.
step: The dominated convergence theorem applies: the limit equals $\int_0^\infty \frac{dx}{1+x^2} = \arctan x\big|_0^\infty = \pi/2$.
answer: $\pi/2$.

@mistakes
- Exchanging a limit and an integral with no hypothesis: the example $n\cdot\mathbf{1}_{(0,1/n)}$ shows that the exchange can fail.
- Applying Fubini's theorem to a function whose absolute value has infinite integral; check Tonelli's theorem on $|f|$ first.
- Forgetting the arc estimate when closing a contour: the integral over the arc must be shown to tend to $0$.
- Using Rouché's theorem with the inequality in the wrong direction, or on a curve where $|g| < |f|$ is not strict.

@practice num
prompt: Compute $\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|z|=3} \dfrac{z^2+1}{z(z-1)(z-2)}\,dz$, with the circle traversed once counterclockwise. Give the value as a number.
answer: 1
check: sum([Fraction(0**2 + 1, (0 - 1) * (0 - 2)), Fraction(1**2 + 1, 1 * (1 - 2)), Fraction(2**2 + 1, 2 * (2 - 1))])
hint: All three poles lie inside the circle, and each is simple.
hint: At a simple pole $a$ the residue is $(z-a)f(z)$ evaluated at $a$.
solution: The residues are $\frac{1}{(-1)(-2)} = \frac12$ at $0$, $\frac{2}{1 \cdot (-1)} = -2$ at $1$ and $\frac{5}{2 \cdot 1} = \frac52$ at $2$. Their sum is $\frac12 - 2 + \frac52 = 1$, and dividing the integral by $2\pi i$ leaves exactly this sum.

@practice mc
prompt: Let $f_n = n\cdot \mathbf{1}_{(0,1/n)}$ on $[0,1]$ with Lebesgue measure. Then $f_n \to 0$ pointwise but $\int f_n = 1$ for every $n$. Which hypothesis of the dominated convergence theorem fails?
choice*: There is no integrable function $g$ with $f_n \le g$ for all $n$.
choice: The functions $f_n$ are not measurable.
choice: The sequence $f_n$ does not converge pointwise.
choice: The functions $f_n$ are not nonnegative.
hint: Compute $\sup_n f_n(x)$ for $x \in (0,1)$ and check whether it is integrable.
solution: Each $f_n$ is measurable, nonnegative and converges pointwise to $0$. Any dominating $g$ would satisfy $g(x) \ge 1/x - 1$ on $(0,1)$, which is not integrable, so no integrable dominating function exists.

@practice num
prompt: Evaluate $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \dfrac{dx}{(x^2+1)^2}$ to four decimal places.
answer: 1.5708
tol: 0.0001
check: quad(lambda x: 1 / (x * x + 1) ** 2, -200, 200)
hint: Close in the upper half plane; the only pole inside is $z = i$, of order $2$.
hint: For a double pole, $\operatorname{Res}(f, i) = \frac{d}{dz}\big[(z+i)^{-2}\big]$ at $z = i$.
solution: $\operatorname{Res}(f, i) = -2(z+i)^{-3}\big|_{z=i} = -2/(2i)^3 = -2/(-8i) = 1/(4i)$. So the integral is $2\pi i \cdot \frac{1}{4i} = \frac{\pi}{2} \approx 1.5708$, and the arc integral vanishes as $R\to\infty$ because $|f| \le 1/(R^2-1)^2$.

@practice ms
prompt: Which of these statements are true?
choice*: Every bounded entire function is constant.
choice*: If $f$ is entire and $|f(z)| \le |z|$ for all $z \in \mathbb{C}$, then $f(z) = cz$ for some constant $c$.
choice: An entire function that is bounded on the real axis is constant.
choice: A nonconstant entire function must have a zero.
choice: The function $\sin z$ is bounded on $\mathbb{C}$.
hint: Think of $\sin z$ and $e^z$, and apply Liouville to $f(z)/z$.
solution: The first is Liouville's theorem. For the second, $f(0) = 0$, so $f(z)/z$ extends to an entire function bounded by $1$, which is constant. The function $\sin z$ is bounded on $\mathbb{R}$ but not constant, and $|\sin(iy)| = \sinh |y| \to \infty$, so it is unbounded on $\mathbb{C}$. The function $e^z$ is nonconstant and entire with no zeros.

@practice self
prompt: Use Liouville's theorem to prove the fundamental theorem of algebra: every nonconstant polynomial $p(z) = a_n z^n + \dots + a_0$ with complex coefficients ($n \ge 1$, $a_n \ne 0$) has a root in $\mathbb{C}$.
hint: If $p$ has no root, consider $1/p$ and show it is entire and bounded.
hint: Show $|p(z)| \to \infty$ as $|z| \to \infty$ by factoring out $|z|^n$.
solution: Suppose $p$ has no root in $\mathbb{C}$. Then $h = 1/p$ is entire. We show $h$ is bounded. For $|z| \ge 1$, $|p(z)| \ge |a_n||z|^n - \sum_{k<n}|a_k||z|^k \ge |z|^n\big(|a_n| - \sum_{k<n}|a_k|/|z|^{n-k}\big) \ge |z|^n\big(|a_n| - A/|z|\big)$, where $A = \sum_{k<n}|a_k|$, using $|z|^{n-k} \ge |z|$ for $k < n$ and $|z| \ge 1$. Choose $R \ge 1$ with $R \ge 2A/|a_n|$ (and $R \ge 1$). For $|z| \ge R$ we get $|p(z)| \ge |z|^n |a_n|/2 \ge |a_n|/2$, so $|h(z)| \le 2/|a_n|$ there. On the compact disc $|z| \le R$ the continuous function $h$ is bounded. So $h$ is a bounded entire function, hence constant by Liouville's theorem, and then $p = 1/h$ is constant, contradicting $n \ge 1$. Therefore $p$ has a root. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Assumes no root and observes that $1/p$ is entire.
rubric: Proves $|p(z)| \to \infty$ (or a lower bound) with an explicit estimate on large $|z|$.
rubric: Bounds $1/p$ on the compact disc by continuity and on its complement by the estimate, then applies Liouville's theorem.
rubric: Concludes that $p$ is constant, a contradiction.

@quiz num
prompt: How many zeros, counted with multiplicity, does $z^5 + 3z + 1$ have in the annulus $1 < |z| < 2$? Use Rouché's theorem on the circles $|z|=1$ and $|z|=2$.
answer: 4
check: sum(1 < abs(r) < 2 for r in np.roots([1, 0, 0, 0, 3, 1]))
hint: On $|z| = 1$ compare $3z$ with $z^5 + 1$; on $|z| = 2$ compare $z^5$ with $3z + 1$.
solution: On $|z| = 1$, $|3z| = 3 > 2 \ge |z^5 + 1|$, so the polynomial has as many zeros in $|z| < 1$ as $3z$, which is $1$. On $|z| = 2$, $|z^5| = 32 > 7 \ge |3z+1|$, so it has $5$ zeros in $|z| < 2$. No zero lies on either circle, so the annulus contains $5 - 1 = 4$.

@quiz num
prompt: Find $\lim_{n\to\infty} \int_0^1 \left(1 + \frac{x}{n}\right)^n dx$ to three decimal places.
answer: 1.718
tol: 0.001
check: quad(lambda x: exp(x), 0, 1)
hint: For each fixed $x$, $(1 + x/n)^n \to e^x$. Find a dominating function on $[0,1]$.
solution: Since $(1 + x/n)^n \le e^x \le e$ on $[0,1]$ (because $1 + t \le e^t$), the constant $e$ is an integrable dominating function on $[0,1]$. By dominated convergence the limit is $\int_0^1 e^x dx = e - 1 \approx 1.718$.

@quiz self
prompt: Let $(X, \mu)$ be a measure space and suppose $f_n \to f$ in $L^1(\mu)$, that is, $\int |f_n - f|\,d\mu \to 0$. Prove that some subsequence $f_{n_k}$ converges to $f$ almost everywhere.
hint: Choose $n_k$ increasing with $\|f_{n_k} - f\|_1 \le 2^{-k}$ and study $\sum_k |f_{n_k} - f|$.
hint: Use the monotone convergence theorem to exchange the sum and the integral.
solution: Choose $n_1 < n_2 < \cdots$ with $\|f_{n_k} - f\|_1 \le 2^{-k}$, possible because the $L^1$ distance tends to $0$. Put $S = \sum_{k=1}^\infty |f_{n_k} - f|$, a measurable function with values in $[0, \infty]$. The partial sums are nonnegative and increase, so by the monotone convergence theorem $\int S\,d\mu = \sum_k \int |f_{n_k} - f|\,d\mu \le \sum_k 2^{-k} = 1$. Hence $S$ is integrable, so $S(x) < \infty$ for almost every $x$. At every such $x$ the series $\sum_k |f_{n_k}(x) - f(x)|$ converges, so its terms tend to $0$: $f_{n_k}(x) \to f(x)$. Thus $f_{n_k} \to f$ almost everywhere. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Chooses a subsequence with summable $L^1$ errors such as $2^{-k}$.
rubric: Applies the monotone convergence theorem to the series of nonnegative functions.
rubric: Deduces that $S < \infty$ almost everywhere and that the terms of a convergent series tend to $0$.
