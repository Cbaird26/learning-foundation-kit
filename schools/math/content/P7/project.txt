title: Expository paper and talk on a recent theorem
overview: You will choose a theorem proved in the last fifty years, read the original paper and a survey, write an eight- to twelve-page expository paper in LaTeX that a first-year graduate student could follow, and give a recorded twenty-minute talk. You will also take an unseen 180-minute mock qualifying exam across analysis, algebra and topology and grade it against a rubric.
goal: Explain a significant modern theorem accurately, with exact statements, complete proofs of the parts you present, and honest attribution.
goal: Practice the whole research-communication cycle: reading papers, planning, writing, typesetting, revising and presenting.
goal: Demonstrate qualifying-exam readiness in analysis, algebra and topology under timed conditions.
step: Week 13, day 1. Choose a theorem from the list in the material (or one your advisor approves). Check that you can state it exactly and that the proof, or a substantial part of it, is within reach. Write down the main message in one sentence.
step: Week 13, days 2 and 3. Read the original paper in three passes (Lesson 7) and one survey or set of lecture notes. Write the one-page summary from Lesson 8: problem, main result, method, why it matters, critique.
step: Week 13, days 4 and 5. Outline the paper: abstract, introduction, preliminaries, main theorem and overview, proof in steps, examples, references. Write all the statements of definitions, lemmas and the theorem exactly, and decide which proofs you will give in full and which you will cite.
step: Week 13, days 6 and 7. Write the first draft in LaTeX with theorem environments, labels, `\ref` and a bibliography. Write at least two lemma proofs in your own words, from scratch and without copying, and work at least one example by hand, without any software, before you check it with software if you wish.
step: Week 14, days 1 and 2. Revise: make a reverse outline, read the paper aloud, give it to a peer from the target audience, and fix every place where they stopped following. Check every citation and attribution against the sources. Add the sentence that the theorem and its proof are due to the original authors.
step: Week 14, days 3 and 4. Prepare and rehearse the twenty-minute talk with a time budget from Lesson 11, then record it. Keep to twenty minutes; produce captions or a transcript if possible.
step: Week 14, day 5. Take the unseen 180-minute mock qualifying exam supplied with your course materials or by a study partner (six problems chosen from eight, two in each of analysis, algebra and topology, in the style of Lessons 3 to 5). Grade it with a rubric the same day and write an error-log entry for each lost point.
step: Week 14, day 6. Write a one-page reflection: what you learned from the paper, the talk and the exam; what you would change; and your plan for the next term. Check your own program's rules for any milestone this project might count toward.

@review
## Week 13 review guide

**Week by week.** (1) Qualifying exams differ by program: read your own rules; graders reward exact statements, checked hypotheses, complete arguments and honest partial results; prepare with a cycle of learning, recall, drill, timed testing, grading and repair. (2) Build a topic inventory with importance and gap ratings, a main and a backup text per subject, a priority-weighted allocation of hours, and a twelve-week plan that ends with mocks and rest, with an error log. (3) Analysis: monotone, Fatou and dominated convergence; uniform convergence and Arzelà–Ascoli; Cauchy's integral formula, Liouville, residues and Rouché. (4) Algebra: Sylow counting, PID and UFD, Eisenstein, the tower law and Galois correspondence, the structure theorem for modules over a PID; grading with a rubric. (5) Topology and geometry: compactness and Hausdorff, $\pi_1$ of graphs, coverings, Euler characteristic, classification of surfaces, Gauss–Bonnet, the Brouwer fixed point theorem. (6) Oral exams: the layered answer (statement, example, non-example, key idea, connection), board technique, recovering from errors. (7) Reading papers in three passes and stress-testing statements. (8) Choosing an advisor and a problem; a ladder of problems; the one-page summary. (9) Writing exposition: precise statements, strategy first, credit and citation, revision. (10) LaTeX, bibliographies and preprints. (11) Talks: one message, a time budget, slides that support the speaker. (12) Career paths, refereeing and publishing; the structure of a referee report.

**Key facts to be able to state and use.**
- Dominated convergence: measurable, pointwise a.e. convergence, integrable dominating function.
- Residue theorem; Rouché: $|g| < |f|$ on the circle implies $f$ and $f+g$ have the same number of zeros inside.
- Sylow: $n_p \equiv 1 \pmod p$ and $n_p \mid m$.
- Eisenstein's criterion: $p \nmid a_n$, $p \mid a_i$ for $i<n$, $p^2 \nmid a_0$.
- A connected graph has $\pi_1$ free of rank $E - V + 1$; a closed orientable surface of genus $g$ has $\chi = 2 - 2g$; $\int K\,dA = 2\pi\chi$.
- A compact subset of a Hausdorff space is closed; a continuous bijection from a compact space onto a Hausdorff space is a homeomorphism.

**Self-check.** Can you write, from a blank page and in ten minutes each: the proof of Liouville's theorem, the proof that a group of order 15 is cyclic, the proof that a compact subset of a Hausdorff space is closed, and the proof of the Brouwer fixed point theorem in dimension two from $\pi_1(S^1) \cong \mathbb{Z}$? Can you give a two-minute spoken sketch of one proof? Can you list the hypotheses of every named theorem you have used?

**Common errors.** Exchanging limits and integrals without a hypothesis; dropping the arc estimate in a contour argument; using $n_p \mid |G|$ instead of $n_p \mid m$; confusing $[G:H]$ with $|H|$; saying "closed and bounded implies compact" outside $\mathbb{R}^n$; treating a preprint as certified; copying wording from a source; typing theorem numbers by hand; running a talk over time.

@exam self
prompt: **Mock qualifying exam answer (60 minutes for both parts).** Write complete solutions. (a) Let $f$ be an entire function with $\operatorname{Re} f(z) \ge 0$ for every $z \in \mathbb{C}$. Prove that $f$ is constant. (b) Let $G$ be a group of order $99 = 3^2 \cdot 11$. Prove that $G$ is abelian. Then state in two sentences how you would divide 60 minutes between the two parts and the final review, and which of the two you would attempt first and why.
hint: For (a), consider $e^{-f}$ and bound it. For (b), count Sylow subgroups, and recall that groups of order $p^2$ are abelian.
solution: (a) Since $\operatorname{Re} f \ge 0$, $|e^{-f(z)}| = e^{-\operatorname{Re} f(z)} \le 1$. The function $h = e^{-f}$ is entire (a composition of entire functions) and bounded, so it is constant by Liouville's theorem. Then $0 = h' = -f' e^{-f}$, and since $e^{-f}$ never vanishes, $f' \equiv 0$. As $\mathbb{C}$ is connected, $f$ is constant. $\blacksquare$ (b) Sylow: $n_{11}$ divides $9$ and $n_{11} \equiv 1 \pmod{11}$. The divisors of $9$ are $1, 3, 9$, and $3 \equiv 3$, $9 \equiv 9 \pmod{11}$, so $n_{11} = 1$. Also $n_3$ divides $11$ and $n_3 \equiv 1 \pmod 3$; the divisors are $1$ and $11$, and $11 \equiv 2 \pmod 3$, so $n_3 = 1$. Thus the Sylow $11$-subgroup $Q$ and the Sylow $3$-subgroup $P$ are normal, of orders $11$ and $9$. Their intersection is trivial since orders $11$ and $9$ are coprime. For $x \in P$ and $y \in Q$, the commutator $xyx^{-1}y^{-1}$ lies in $P \cap Q = \{e\}$ (it equals $x(yx^{-1}y^{-1})$ with $yx^{-1}y^{-1} \in P$, and equals $(xyx^{-1})y^{-1}$ with $xyx^{-1} \in Q$), so elements of $P$ and $Q$ commute. The map $P \times Q \to G$, $(x,y) \mapsto xy$, is therefore a homomorphism; it is injective because $P \cap Q$ is trivial, and the orders agree, so $G \cong P \times Q$. A group of order $9 = 3^2$ is abelian (its center is nontrivial by the class equation, and a group with $G/Z$ cyclic is abelian), and $Q$ is cyclic of order $11$. Hence $G \cong P \times Q$ is abelian. $\blacksquare$ Time plan: about 20 minutes for (a), 30 for (b), and 10 for review; start with (a), which is shorter and surest, so that credit is secured early.
rubric: Part (a): bounds $e^{-f}$ by $1$, applies Liouville's theorem, and deduces that $f$ is constant (including why $f' = 0$).
rubric: Part (b): applies Sylow (3) correctly to show both Sylow subgroups are normal, and proves $G \cong P \times Q$.
rubric: Part (b): justifies that groups of order $p^2$ are abelian and concludes.
rubric: The time plan is realistic and starts with the surest problem.

@exam num
prompt: How many zeros, counted with multiplicity, does $z^4 - 5z + 1$ have in the annulus $1 < |z| < 2$? Use Rouché's theorem on the circles $|z| = 1$ and $|z| = 2$.
answer: 3
check: sum(1 < abs(r) < 2 for r in np.roots([1, 0, 0, -5, 1]))
hint: On $|z| = 1$ compare $-5z$ with $z^4 + 1$; on $|z| = 2$ compare $z^4$ with $-5z + 1$.
solution: On $|z| = 1$, $|-5z| = 5 > 2 \ge |z^4 + 1|$, so there is $1$ zero in $|z| < 1$. On $|z| = 2$, $|z^4| = 16 > 11 \ge |-5z+1|$, so there are $4$ zeros in $|z| < 2$. No zero lies on either circle, so the annulus contains $4 - 1 = 3$.

@exam mc
prompt: While reading a preprint on the arXiv, you find a step in a proof that you believe does not follow. What is the best first course of action?
choice*: Check that you have the right version and write the step out fully; if the gap remains, discuss it with your advisor or a colleague and then contact the authors politely with the specific step.
choice: Post a public comment that the proof is wrong.
choice: Ignore it, since a posted paper has been checked.
choice: Cite the result anyway without mentioning the gap.
hint: First make sure the error is real, then use the channels that allow the authors to respond.
solution: A step that looks wrong is often a misreading or a different version, so verify first. If the gap persists, consulting a trusted colleague and writing to the authors with the specific step is the usual practice. Public accusations are premature, a preprint is not certified by posting, and citing a possibly flawed result without comment is not honest.
