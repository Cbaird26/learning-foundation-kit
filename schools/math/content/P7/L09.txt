@article
Your project is to write an eight- to twelve-page paper explaining a theorem proved in the last fifty years to a reader who has completed first-year graduate courses. Nobody expects a new result. What you offer is **exposition**: a clear route through a hard argument, with the definitions in the right place, examples that make the statement concrete, and a proof whose structure is visible. A good exposition lets a reader do what the original paper did not help them do: finish the argument and then see why it works.

### Choosing the topic and the reader

Pick a theorem whose statement you can understand and whose proof, or a central part of it, you can follow with effort. Read the original paper and at least one survey or lecture notes. Decide your reader: a first-year graduate student with the background of the qualifying exams. Everything the reader would need from beyond that background is either explained briefly or cited precisely. Say the intended audience in the introduction.

### The shape of the paper

| Part | Purpose | Typical length |
|---|---|---|
| Title and abstract | Say what theorem is explained and for whom | 100 to 150 words |
| Introduction | Statement of the result, why it matters, where it came from, roadmap | 1 to 2 pages |
| Preliminaries | Only the definitions and facts needed, with references | 1 to 2 pages |
| Main theorem and overview | The exact statement, then the idea of the proof in a page | 1 page |
| Proof, in steps | Lemmas in a logical order, each with a sentence of motivation | 3 to 5 pages |
| Examples and remarks | Special cases, sharpness, variations | 1 page |
| References | Complete, consistent entries | 1 page |

These are guides. The introduction is written last, since you cannot summarize a paper you have not yet written.

### Rules of clear mathematical writing

- **Define before use**, and use each symbol for one thing.
- **State exactly.** Hypotheses, quantifiers and conclusion appear in the statement. "The function is nice" is not a hypothesis.
- **Show the strategy first.** Before a proof of several steps, say in a sentence or two what the proof does and why it should work.
- **Give examples early.** A definition followed by an example is better than a definition followed by three lemmas.
- **Signpost.** Say what you have shown and what remains: "It remains to prove that the map is injective."
- **Do not hide steps behind "clearly".** If a step is routine, say what justifies it. If the reader should check it, say how.
- **Write sentences.** Displays are parts of sentences and end with punctuation.

### Credit, citation and originality

Every result that is not yours carries a citation, with the precise location (theorem number or section) when you can give it. If you follow an argument from a paper but reorganize it, say so: "This argument follows [4], Section 3." Paraphrase in your own words and in your own organization; never copy sentences from a paper, a book or a survey, even with changes in a few words. A short phrase in quotation marks may be quoted if the source is cited. What your exposition adds is its organization, examples, and explanations; say honestly that the theorem and its proof are due to the original authors. Rules on academic integrity are enforced by universities, so read yours.

### Drafting and revising

1. Write the skeleton: the main theorem, the lemmas, each with a one-line statement.
2. Write each proof for yourself, in full, before polishing.
3. Write the introduction and abstract last.
4. Make a **reverse outline**: one sentence per paragraph saying what it does. Paragraphs that do nothing, or two things, need revision.
5. Read the paper aloud. Awkward sentences and unclear references show up when you hear them.
6. Give it to a reader from your target audience and ask where they stopped following. Do not explain; revise.
7. Check every reference, every theorem number and every claim of the form "it is well known".

@example
title: Making a statement precise
problem: A draft says: "Zhang showed that primes cannot be too far apart infinitely often." Rewrite it as an exact statement with attribution.
step: Identify what is vague: "too far apart" and "infinitely often" have no quantifiers or quantities.
step: Write the exact quantity: let $p_n$ be the $n$th prime. The claim concerns $\liminf_{n\to\infty}(p_{n+1} - p_n)$, the least gap that occurs infinitely often.
step: Write the theorem: *there is a constant $H$ such that $p_{n+1} - p_n \le H$ for infinitely many $n$; equivalently, $\liminf (p_{n+1} - p_n) < \infty$.*
step: Add attribution with care: Zhang proved this in 2013; the value of $H$ was reduced by Maynard and the Polymath project to $246$.
answer: Theorem (Zhang, 2013; improved by Maynard and Polymath). $\liminf_{n\to\infty}(p_{n+1} - p_n) \le 246$.

@example
title: Expanding a terse step for the reader
problem: A source proof says: "Since $K$ is compact and $f$ is continuous, $f$ attains its maximum." Rewrite the step for a first-year graduate reader.
step: Name the theorem: "By the extreme value theorem for compact spaces."
step: State its hypotheses and check them: $K$ is a nonempty compact space, here a closed bounded subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ by the Heine–Borel theorem, and $f\colon K \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous.
step: State the conclusion in the form you will use it: there is $x_0 \in K$ with $f(x_0) \ge f(x)$ for every $x \in K$.
step: Check nonemptiness explicitly, because it is the hypothesis most easily forgotten: $K$ contains the point $a$ from the construction above.
answer: "$K$ is a nonempty closed bounded subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$, hence compact by Heine–Borel, and $f$ is continuous on $K$; by the extreme value theorem there is $x_0 \in K$ with $f(x_0) = \max_K f$."

@mistakes
- Writing the introduction first and then forcing the paper to match it.
- Using a notation before defining it, or using one symbol for two objects.
- Copying sentences from the source, even lightly edited; paraphrase and reorganize, and cite.
- Saying "it is well known" for a step that your reader does not know and cannot find.

@practice num
prompt: A draft claims "the function $f(x) = x^2$ is $5$-Lipschitz on $[0,3]$", meaning $|f(x) - f(y)| \le 5|x - y|$ for all $x, y \in [0, 3]$. What is the smallest constant $L$ for which $f$ is $L$-Lipschitz on $[0,3]$?
answer: 6
check: max(2 * x / 100 for x in range(301))
hint: Factor $x^2 - y^2 = (x+y)(x-y)$ and bound $x + y$ on $[0,3]$.
hint: Look at what happens as both $x$ and $y$ approach $3$.
solution: $|x^2 - y^2| = |x + y||x - y| \le 6|x - y|$ for $x, y \in [0,3]$. The constant $6$ cannot be improved: with $y = 3$ and $x = 3 - h$, the ratio $|f(x) - f(y)|/|x - y| = 6 - h \to 6$. So the draft's claim is false and the smallest constant is $6$.

@practice self
prompt: **Draft the front matter.** For an expository paper on a theorem of your choice proved in the last fifty years, write the abstract (at most 150 words) and the first paragraph of the introduction. As a model, do it for the theorem on bounded gaps between primes: the statement is that $\liminf_{n}(p_{n+1} - p_n)$ is finite, due to Zhang in 2013, with the bound improved to $246$ by Maynard and the Polymath project.
hint: The abstract states the theorem, the audience and what the paper does; the first paragraph states the result exactly and says why it matters.
hint: Avoid claims you cannot support, such as how the proof compares in difficulty with other results.
solution: Abstract. Let $p_n$ denote the $n$th prime. In 2013 Zhang proved that $p_{n+1} - p_n$ is bounded by a fixed constant for infinitely many $n$, the first unconditional result of this kind; the constant was later reduced to $246$ by Maynard and the Polymath project. This paper explains the statement and the main ideas of the sieve method behind it for a reader with first-year graduate background in analysis and number theory. We state the result, review the classical sieve ingredients, describe how an admissible tuple of linear forms leads to primes in tuples, and indicate where the argument needed new input; complete proofs of the elementary parts are given and the deep inputs are cited. First paragraph of the introduction. The primes become sparser as they grow: by the prime number theorem the average gap between consecutive primes near $x$ is about $\log x$. It is therefore natural to ask whether gaps can nevertheless stay small infinitely often. The twin prime conjecture asserts that the gap $2$ occurs infinitely often, and it remains open. In 2013, Zhang proved that the least gap that occurs infinitely often is finite: there is a constant $H$ such that $p_{n+1} - p_n \le H$ for infinitely many $n$, and the bound was improved to $H = 246$ by Maynard and the Polymath project. We explain the statement, the idea of the proof, and the techniques that made the improvement possible. (The twin prime conjecture remains open; readers should check the current status of related problems.)
rubric: The abstract states the theorem exactly, gives attribution, and says what the paper does and for whom, within the length limit.
rubric: The introduction paragraph gives context and the exact statement, and describes open problems accurately (no claim beyond what is known).
rubric: The writing is precise and free of vague phrases such as "nice" or "well known" used to hide content.

@quiz self
prompt: Rewrite this paragraph from a draft so that it is precise and readable: "Obviously the sequence converges since it is monotone and bounded so it has a limit, which is also the supremum. Then clearly the series is nice too." Assume the sequence is $a_n$ of real numbers, nondecreasing and bounded above, and the claim to be shown is that $a_n \to \sup_n a_n$.
hint: State the theorem, the hypotheses, and prove the convergence directly from the definition of the supremum.
solution: **Claim.** Let $(a_n)_{n \ge 1}$ be a nondecreasing sequence of real numbers that is bounded above, and let $s = \sup_n a_n$. Then $a_n \to s$. *Proof.* The set $\{a_n\}$ is nonempty and bounded above, so $s$ exists as a real number by the least upper bound property. Let $\varepsilon > 0$. Since $s - \varepsilon$ is not an upper bound, there is an index $N$ with $a_N > s - \varepsilon$. For every $n \ge N$ the sequence is nondecreasing, so $a_n \ge a_N > s - \varepsilon$, and $a_n \le s$ because $s$ is an upper bound. Hence $|a_n - s| < \varepsilon$ for all $n \ge N$, which is the definition of $a_n \to s$. $\blacksquare$ The second sentence of the draft ("the series is nice") makes no claim that can be checked and should be deleted or replaced by a precise statement.
rubric: States the claim with all hypotheses (nondecreasing, bounded above, real).
rubric: Proves convergence from the definition using the supremum and monotonicity, with $N$ chosen before the estimate.
rubric: Removes the vague statement rather than leaving it, and avoids "obviously" and "clearly".
