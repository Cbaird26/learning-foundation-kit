@article
You are handed a three-hour exam with eight problems and told to solve six. After forty minutes you have half a page on problem 1, a theorem you cannot quite remember for problem 2, and a growing suspicion that problem 5 is easy but you have not read it. Nothing on the exam is beyond what you have seen. The difficulty is that you have never had to produce six complete proofs, from memory, against a clock. That is a skill, and like any skill it is trained by doing it.

### What a qualifying exam is

A **qualifying exam** (often called a "qual" or a "prelim") is a checkpoint, early in a doctoral program, at which the department confirms that you command the core of the subject well enough to begin research. Practice differs by program, university and country, so treat everything below as common practice, not as a rule. Your own graduate handbook and your graduate director are the authority on your program.

Common features, with the usual variations:

| Feature | Common practice | Variations to check |
|---|---|---|
| Subjects | Real analysis, algebra, and often complex analysis, topology or geometry | Which areas, and whether you choose some |
| Written part | Timed papers of a few hours, several problems per paper, some choice | Number of papers, open or closed notes, how many problems must be solved |
| Oral part | A committee questions you at a board for about an hour | Syllabus-based, research-based, or replaced by a written proposal |
| Timing | Taken in the first or second year, often twice a year | Earliest and latest allowed date |
| Attempts | A limited number, often two | What happens after a failure |
| Passing | A threshold on a rubric, or a committee decision | Whether subjects are passed separately |

Read the rules once, carefully, and write down the dates. Many students lose months to a wrong assumption about format.

### What graders reward

Graders are mathematicians reading dozens of scripts. A good solution makes their job easy.

- **Exact statements.** When you invoke a theorem, state it with its hypotheses and say why they hold. "By dominated convergence" earns little; "the $f_n$ are measurable, converge pointwise almost everywhere, and satisfy $|f_n| \le g$ with $g$ integrable, so the dominated convergence theorem gives..." earns credit.
- **Complete arguments.** Every quantifier is explicit. Every step has a reason: a definition, a hypothesis or a named theorem.
- **Honest partial results.** If you can prove only a special case, say so and prove it. Graders give partial credit for true, clearly labeled progress and take it away for a gap disguised as a proof.
- **Organization.** Say what you will prove, prove it, and mark the end with $\blacksquare$. A reader who cannot find the main idea in ten seconds will not look for it.

### The preparation cycle

Effective preparation tends to follow one loop, repeated: **learn** a topic from a text, **recall** its statements and proofs without notes, **drill** exam-style problems, **test** under timed conditions, **grade** yourself against a rubric, and **repair** what failed. Rereading a chapter feels productive but tests nothing. Writing a proof from a blank page, then comparing it with the text, tests everything. Lesson 2 builds a reading list and a plan; Lessons 3 to 5 review the content; Lesson 6 trains the oral exam.

### Triage under the clock

Spend the first ten minutes reading every problem and marking each as *sure*, *probably*, or *no idea*. Solve the sure ones first, then the probable ones. Set a time limit per problem and move on when it expires; a second visit with a fresh mind often succeeds. Reserve the last twenty to thirty minutes for rereading, because exam errors are more often dropped hypotheses and sign slips than missing ideas.

### A first diagnostic

Before you plan anything, do the diagnostic in Practice 4 and the proof critique in Practice 5 under a timer, with no notes. Record what you could not recall. That record is the first entry of an error log you will keep for the whole course.

@example
title: Grading a plausible but false proof
problem: A student submits the following. **Claim:** if $f_n\colon [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}$ are continuous and $f_n \to f$ pointwise, then $f$ is continuous. **Proof:** fix $x \in [0,1]$ and $\varepsilon > 0$. Choose $N$ with $|f_N(y) - f(y)| < \varepsilon/3$ for all $y$. Since $f_N$ is continuous at $x$, choose $\delta > 0$ with $|f_N(y) - f_N(x)| < \varepsilon/3$ when $|y - x| < \delta$. Then $|f(y) - f(x)| \le |f(y) - f_N(y)| + |f_N(y) - f_N(x)| + |f_N(x) - f(x)| < \varepsilon$. Find the error and repair the statement.
step: Read for the sentence that does the work. The choice "$N$ with $|f_N(y) - f(y)| < \varepsilon/3$ for all $y$" is a uniform bound over $y$.
step: Pointwise convergence gives only this: for each fixed $y$ there is an $N_y$ that works. The hypothesis does not provide a single $N$ for all $y$, so the proof assumes uniform convergence without saying so.
step: The claim itself is false. Take $f_n(x) = x^n$ on $[0,1]$. Each $f_n$ is continuous, and $f_n \to f$ pointwise where $f(x) = 0$ for $x < 1$ and $f(1) = 1$. This $f$ is discontinuous at $1$.
step: Repair: assume $f_n \to f$ uniformly. Then the choice of $N$ in the proof is legitimate, and the three-term estimate shows $|f(y) - f(x)| < \varepsilon$ whenever $|y - x| < \delta$. So $f$ is continuous at every $x$.
step: Grading note: a script that writes this proof earns almost nothing; a script that finds the gap, gives $x^n$ and proves the uniform version earns full credit.
answer: The proof silently assumes uniform convergence. The statement is false for pointwise limits ($x^n$ on $[0,1]$) and true for uniform limits.

@example
title: Budgeting a three-hour paper
problem: A paper lasts $180$ minutes and has eight problems, of which you must solve six. You want $10$ minutes to read and rank the problems and $20$ minutes at the end to review. How long can you spend on each of your six problems, and how should you order them?
step: Subtract the fixed blocks: $180 - 10 - 20 = 150$ minutes remain for writing.
step: Divide by the number of problems you will submit: $150 / 6 = 25$ minutes each.
step: Treat $25$ minutes as an average, not a quota. Sure problems should take less, so schedule them first and let the slack accumulate for harder ones.
step: Choose the six after the reading pass: the six you rank *sure* or *probably*, not the six that look most impressive. Decide this before you start writing, and write the chosen numbers at the top of the paper.
answer: $25$ minutes per problem on average, with the sure problems first.

@mistakes
- Treating one program's format as universal: the number of subjects, the length of papers, the attempts allowed and the oral format all differ, so read your own handbook.
- Citing a theorem by name without checking its hypotheses on the problem at hand; graders look for exactly that check.
- Presenting a special case as a full proof: say that it is a special case and prove it, and you keep the credit.
- Studying only by rereading: recognizing a proof on the page is much easier than producing it, and the exam asks you to produce it.

@practice num
prompt: Compute the sum of the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n(n+2)}$ to three decimal places. Hint for method: partial fractions turn it into a telescoping sum.
answer: 0.75
tol: 0.001
check: sum(1 / (n * (n + 2)) for n in range(1, 100001))
hint: Write $\frac{1}{n(n+2)} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{n} - \frac{1}{n+2}\right)$.
hint: In the partial sum $S_N$ all terms cancel except $\frac12\left(1 + \frac12 - \frac{1}{N+1} - \frac{1}{N+2}\right)$.
solution: The partial sums are $S_N = \frac12\left(1 + \frac12 - \frac{1}{N+1} - \frac{1}{N+2}\right)$, which tend to $\frac12 \cdot \frac32 = \frac34$. So the sum is $0.75$.

@practice self
prompt: A student submits the following. **Claim:** if $X$ and $Y$ are metric spaces and $f\colon X \to Y$ is a continuous bijection, then $f^{-1}$ is continuous. **Proof:** let $y_n \to y$ in $Y$ and put $x_n = f^{-1}(y_n)$, $x = f^{-1}(y)$. By continuity of $f$, $f(x_n) \to f(x)$, that is, $y_n \to y$, which is what we assumed; hence $x_n \to x$. $\blacksquare$ Explain exactly what is wrong, show that the claim is false, and state and prove a corrected version in which $X$ is compact.
hint: Which sentence assumes that $x_n \to x$ without a reason?
hint: Look at the parametrization $t \mapsto (\cos t, \sin t)$ on $[0, 2\pi)$.
hint: For the repair, show that $f$ maps closed sets to closed sets, using compactness.
solution: The proof is circular. It begins with $y_n \to y$, deduces $f(x_n) \to f(x)$ from the definition of $x_n$ and $x$, and then concludes $x_n \to x$ with no argument; continuity of $f$ gives $x_n \to x$ implies $f(x_n) \to f(x)$, not the converse. The claim is false: $f\colon [0, 2\pi) \to S^1$, $f(t) = (\cos t, \sin t)$, is a continuous bijection, but with $y_n = f(2\pi - 1/n)$ we have $y_n \to (1, 0) = f(0)$ while $f^{-1}(y_n) = 2\pi - 1/n \to 2\pi \ne 0 = f^{-1}(1,0)$. Corrected statement: if $X$ is compact, $Y$ is Hausdorff (a metric space is) and $f\colon X \to Y$ is a continuous bijection, then $f^{-1}$ is continuous. Proof: let $C \subseteq X$ be closed. Since $X$ is compact, $C$ is compact; since $f$ is continuous, $f(C)$ is compact; since $Y$ is Hausdorff, $f(C)$ is closed. Now $(f^{-1})^{-1}(C) = f(C)$ is closed for every closed $C$, so $f^{-1}$ is continuous. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Identifies that the step from $f(x_n) \to f(x)$ to $x_n \to x$ is unjustified (the converse of continuity).
rubric: Gives a valid counterexample, such as the angle parametrization of the circle, and checks that $f^{-1}$ fails at the point $(1,0)$.
rubric: Proves the compact-domain version by chaining: closed implies compact, continuous image is compact, compact in a Hausdorff space is closed.

@quiz self
prompt: Prove that a continuous function $f\colon K \to \mathbb{R}$ on a compact metric space $K$ is bounded and attains its maximum. State clearly which definition of compactness you use.
hint: Use sequential compactness: every sequence in $K$ has a subsequence that converges in $K$.
hint: Choose a sequence whose values approach the supremum and extract a convergent subsequence.
solution: In a metric space, compactness is equivalent to sequential compactness; we use that every sequence in $K$ has a subsequence converging to a point of $K$. Bounded above: suppose $f$ were unbounded above. Then for each $n$ there is $x_n \in K$ with $f(x_n) > n$. Some subsequence $x_{n_k} \to x \in K$. By continuity $f(x_{n_k}) \to f(x)$, so the sequence $f(x_{n_k})$ is bounded, contradicting $f(x_{n_k}) > n_k \to \infty$. So $M = \sup_K f$ is a real number (the set $K$ is nonempty). Attained: choose $x_n \in K$ with $f(x_n) > M - 1/n$. A subsequence $x_{n_k} \to x \in K$, and $f(x) = \lim f(x_{n_k})$. Since $M - 1/n_k < f(x_{n_k}) \le M$, the limit is $M$. So $f(x) = M$. Applying the result to $-f$ shows that $f$ is bounded below and attains its minimum. $\blacksquare$
rubric: States the definition used and the nonemptiness of $K$.
rubric: Proves boundedness by contradiction with a sequence $f(x_n) > n$ and a convergent subsequence.
rubric: Chooses a maximizing sequence, passes to a convergent subsequence, and uses continuity to show the maximum is attained.
