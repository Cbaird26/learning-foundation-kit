@article
A committee member asks: "What is a compact space?" You know the definition. You say it, and then there is a pause, because the question was never really about the definition. The next question will be about an example, the next about a counterexample, and the one after that about a proof you have not rehearsed. An oral exam is a conversation designed to find the edge of your understanding. You cannot prevent the committee from finding it, but you can control how you behave when it does.

### Common formats

Oral exams differ by program. Common practice, to be checked against your own program's rules:

- a committee of two to four faculty, sometimes including your likely advisor;
- about one hour at a board, with questions drawn from a syllabus of several subjects, or from your coursework and a research proposal;
- an oral that follows a written exam, sometimes with the written results known to the committee;
- a decision at the end of the session, usually after you leave the room.

Ask your graduate director what the oral covers, how long it lasts, and whether the committee provides any guidance on the topics. Ask recent graduates, too, how questions tended to go.

### What the committee is looking for

The committee wants to know whether you understand mathematics well enough to be trusted at the next stage, so it probes depth. Typical probes:

| The committee asks | The point |
|---|---|
| "Define it." | Do you know the exact definition, with quantifiers? |
| "Give an example." | Can you produce something concrete? |
| "Give a non-example." | Do you see why each hypothesis matters? |
| "Why is this true?" | Can you give the key idea of the proof, not just the statement? |
| "What if I drop this hypothesis?" | Can you find a counterexample? |
| "Where does this appear again?" | Do you see connections between subjects? |

### The layered answer

Prepare a standard structure for any definition or theorem and use it every time.

1. **State it exactly.** Say the definition or theorem with all its hypotheses.
2. **Give an example** that is not trivial and say why it satisfies the hypotheses.
3. **Give a non-example** or a counterexample to a nearby false statement.
4. **Say the key idea of the proof** in two or three sentences, then offer to write it out at the board.
5. **Connect it** to another topic the committee might care about.

Layering gives the committee several places to ask a follow-up, and it keeps you talking about material you know.

### Board technique

Write large, left to right, top to bottom, and leave space. Write the statement before you prove it. Name your objects once and keep the names. Draw the picture (the committee can see it, even though this course cannot show it) and label it. If you need to think, say "let me think about that for a moment" and think; silence at the board is normal. Do not erase until you are finished with the idea.

### Thinking aloud and recovering from mistakes

If you do not remember a theorem, say what you do remember, and try a small case. A committee can help only with ideas it can see. If you say something wrong and notice it, say so plainly: "That is not right: if $f$ is only continuous then the limit need not be continuous. I should have said uniform convergence." A prompt correction is usually received well, and a defensive insistence is not. If a committee member says "are you sure?" take it as an invitation to recheck, not as proof that you are wrong.

If you really do not know, say so, and then say what you would try: "I do not remember the classification of groups of order $p^2 q$, but I would count Sylow subgroups, so let me try order $12$." A reasoned attempt at the edge of your knowledge shows the skill the exam is looking for.

### Practicing

Practice with a partner or a mirror, at a real board if possible, with a timer, and record yourself. The recording will show filler words, trailing sentences and a habit of facing the board; those are fixable. Ask your partner to interrupt after every answer with "why?" and "what if?". A good rule is to make three questions of increasing depth for each syllabus topic and practice answering all three.

@example
title: A layered answer on compactness
problem: The committee asks: "What is a compact space, and why is $[0,1]$ compact?" Give an answer in the five-layer format.
step: State it exactly: a topological space is compact if every cover of it by open sets has a finite subcover. In a metric space this is equivalent to every sequence having a convergent subsequence with limit in the space.
step: Example: $[0,1]$ is compact. Key idea of the proof (Heine–Borel): given an open cover, let $S$ be the set of $x \in [0,1]$ such that $[0,x]$ is covered by finitely many members; show $S$ is nonempty, take $s = \sup S$, and show $s \in S$ and $s = 1$ by using the cover member containing $s$.
step: Non-example: $(0,1)$ is not compact, since the cover by the intervals $(1/n, 1)$, $n \ge 2$, has no finite subcover; $\mathbb{R}$ is not compact either, since the cover by $(-n, n)$ fails.
step: Connection: continuous functions on compact spaces are bounded and attain their extrema; closed subsets of compact sets are compact; compact subsets of Hausdorff spaces are closed.
step: Offer: "I can write out the supremum argument at the board if you would like."
answer: A definition, an example with the idea of its proof, a non-example, a connection and an offer to write the proof.

@example
title: Handling a follow-up question
problem: After the answer above, the committee asks: "Is a closed and bounded subset of a metric space always compact?"
step: Answer directly: "No. It is true in $\mathbb{R}^n$ by the Heine–Borel theorem, but not in general."
step: Give a counterexample: the space $\ell^2$ of real sequences with $\sum x_n^2 < \infty$, with the norm $\|x\|=\sqrt{\sum x_n^2}$. The closed unit ball is closed and bounded.
step: Show it is not compact: the standard basis vectors $e_n$ lie in the ball, and $\|e_n - e_m\| = \sqrt{2}$ for $n \ne m$, so no subsequence is Cauchy, hence none converges.
step: Add the repaired statement: a subset of a complete metric space is compact if and only if it is closed and totally bounded. That is what replaces "bounded" in general.
answer: No: the closed unit ball of $\ell^2$ is closed and bounded but not compact.

@mistakes
- Stating a definition from memory without the quantifiers, which makes it wrong in a way that sounds right.
- Defending a mistake when the committee questions it; recheck out loud and correct it.
- Talking continuously and never writing: the committee needs the board to see your reasoning.
- Waiting for the committee to supply the next question; offer the example or the proof idea yourself.

@practice num
prompt: The Gaussian integers form the ring $\mathbb{Z}[i]$. How many elements does the quotient ring $\mathbb{Z}[i]/(3+i)$ have?
answer: 10
check: 3 ** 2 + 1 ** 2
hint: For $a + bi \ne 0$, the quotient $\mathbb{Z}[i]/(a+bi)$ has $a^2 + b^2$ elements.
solution: The index of the ideal generated by $\alpha = a + bi$ in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is the norm $N(\alpha) = a^2 + b^2$, the area of the fundamental parallelogram of the lattice generated by $\alpha$ and $i\alpha$. Here $N(3+i) = 9 + 1 = 10$.

@practice ms
prompt: Let $B$ be the closed unit ball $\{x : \|x\| \le 1\}$ in $\ell^2$, the space of real sequences with $\sum x_n^2 < \infty$ and norm $\|x\| = \sqrt{\sum x_n^2}$. Which statements are true?
choice*: $B$ is closed in $\ell^2$.
choice*: $B$ is bounded.
choice*: The sequence of standard basis vectors $e_n$ in $B$ has no convergent subsequence.
choice: $B$ is compact.
choice: $B$ is totally bounded.
hint: Compute the distance $\|e_n - e_m\|$ for $n \ne m$.
solution: $B$ is closed (the norm is continuous) and bounded by definition. Since $\|e_n - e_m\| = \sqrt2$ for $n \ne m$, no subsequence of $(e_n)$ is Cauchy, so none converges; hence $B$ is not compact. In a metric space, compact sets are totally bounded, so $B$ is not totally bounded either (balls of radius $1$ around finitely many points cannot cover all the $e_n$, which are pairwise at distance $\sqrt2 > 1$ and so would need distinct centers).

@practice num
prompt: How many elements does the group $GL_2(\mathbb{F}_3)$ of invertible $2\times 2$ matrices over the field with $3$ elements have?
answer: 48
check: sum(1 for a in range(3) for b in range(3) for c in range(3) for d in range(3) if (a * d - b * c) % 3 != 0)
hint: Count the possible first rows, then the second rows that are not multiples of the first.
solution: The first row can be any nonzero vector: $3^2 - 1 = 8$ choices. The second row must not lie in the span of the first, which has $3$ elements: $9 - 3 = 6$ choices. So $8 \cdot 6 = 48$.

@practice self
prompt: Run a mock oral exam on this syllabus. **Syllabus and opening questions.** (1) Analysis: state the dominated convergence theorem; state Cauchy's integral formula and deduce Liouville's theorem. (2) Algebra: state the Sylow theorems; why is $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ not a PID? (3) Topology: define the fundamental group; why is $\pi_1(S^1) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ relevant to the Brouwer fixed point theorem? **Task.** Ask a partner (or yourself, recorded) to pick three of the six questions, allow eight minutes for each, interrupt with "why?" and "what if?", and record the session. Then write a review: for each question, one thing that was strong, one gap, and the repaired answer. As a model, answer the question "Why is $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ not a PID?" in the five-layer format in a few sentences.
hint: Use the layers: exact statement, example, non-example, key idea, connection.
hint: To show an ideal of $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is not principal, suppose a generator exists and look at the constant terms of its multiples.
solution: Model answer for the Z[x] question. Statement: a PID is an integral domain in which every ideal is generated by one element. Claim: $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is an integral domain but not a PID. Example of the failure: the ideal $I = (2, x)$ of polynomials with even constant term is not principal. Proof: suppose $I = (g)$. Since $2 \in I$ we have $g \mid 2$, so $g = \pm 1$ or $\pm 2$ (the divisors of $2$ in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ have degree $0$). Since $x \in I$ we have $g \mid x$, so $g$ is a unit, because $\pm 2$ does not divide $x$. Then $I = \mathbb{Z}[x]$, but $1 \notin I$ because every element $2f + xh$ has even constant term. Contradiction. Contrast: $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ are PIDs, and $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is still a UFD (Gauss's lemma), so PID is strictly stronger than UFD; this is the standard example separating them. Connection: $(2, x)$ is a maximal ideal with quotient $\mathbb{F}_2$, so $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ has Krull dimension $2$, which a PID cannot have. Review structure: for each of the three chosen questions, write one strength, one gap and a repaired answer in the same five layers; practice the gaps again within a week.
rubric: Runs the session with the stated conditions (a timer, interruptions, a record) and reviews each question with a strength, a gap and a repair.
rubric: The model answer gives the exact definition, the counterexample ideal $(2,x)$ and a complete argument that it is not principal.
rubric: Connects the example to the distinction between PIDs and UFDs.

@quiz mc
prompt: During an oral exam you realize that something you wrote on the board is wrong. What is the best response?
choice*: Say that it is wrong, state the correct version and fix the board.
choice: Leave it and hope the committee does not notice.
choice: Defend it until the committee moves on.
choice: Erase it quietly and continue without comment.
hint: A prompt, plain correction is received better than any of the alternatives.
solution: Noticing and correcting your own error shows mathematical judgment. Leaving, defending or silently hiding it suggests that you do not distinguish a true statement from a false one.

@quiz num
prompt: How many elements does the group $SL_2(\mathbb{F}_5)$ of $2 \times 2$ matrices with determinant $1$ over the field with $5$ elements have?
answer: 120
check: sum(1 for a in range(5) for b in range(5) for c in range(5) for d in range(5) if (a * d - b * c) % 5 == 1)
hint: First count $GL_2(\mathbb{F}_5)$, then use the fact that the determinant is a surjective homomorphism onto $\mathbb{F}_5^\times$.
solution: $|GL_2(\mathbb{F}_5)| = (25 - 1)(25 - 5) = 480$. The determinant maps $GL_2(\mathbb{F}_5)$ onto $\mathbb{F}_5^\times$, which has $4$ elements, and the kernel is $SL_2(\mathbb{F}_5)$, so $|SL_2(\mathbb{F}_5)| = 480/4 = 120$.

@quiz self
prompt: A committee member asks: "Why must a group of order $p^2$, with $p$ prime, be abelian?" Give the answer you would write at the board.
hint: Use the class equation to show the center is nontrivial.
hint: Show that if $G/Z(G)$ is cyclic then $G$ is abelian.
solution: Let $|G| = p^2$ and $Z = Z(G)$. By the class equation, $|G| = |Z| + \sum [G : C_G(x_i)]$ over representatives of the conjugacy classes of size greater than $1$; each index is a divisor of $p^2$ greater than $1$, hence divisible by $p$. Since $p$ divides $|G|$, $p$ divides $|Z|$, so $|Z| \in \{p, p^2\}$. If $|Z| = p^2$, $G = Z$ is abelian. If $|Z| = p$, then $G/Z$ has order $p$, hence is cyclic, generated by $gZ$. Every element of $G$ then has the form $g^k z$ with $z \in Z$. Two such elements $g^k z$ and $g^l w$ commute, because $z, w$ are central and powers of $g$ commute with each other: $g^k z g^l w = g^{k+l} zw = g^l w g^k z$. So $G$ is abelian; but this contradicts $|Z| = p < |G|$ (the center of an abelian group is the whole group), so the case $|Z| = p$ cannot occur. Thus $|Z| = p^2$ and $G$ is abelian. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Uses the class equation to show that the center has order divisible by $p$.
rubric: Proves that $G/Z$ cyclic implies $G$ abelian by writing elements as $g^k z$.
rubric: Rules out $|Z| = p$ by contradiction and concludes that $G$ is abelian.
