@article
How many groups of order $15$ are there? A student who has memorized a table says "one". A student who has the Sylow theorems can prove it in five lines, and can prove similar facts for orders the table does not list. Algebra exams reward this second skill: using a few powerful theorems to constrain structure.

### Groups

Let $G$ be a finite group of order $p^a m$ with $p$ prime and $p \nmid m$. A **Sylow $p$-subgroup** has order $p^a$.

**The Sylow theorems.** (1) A Sylow $p$-subgroup exists. (2) Any two Sylow $p$-subgroups are conjugate. (3) The number $n_p$ of Sylow $p$-subgroups satisfies $n_p \equiv 1 \pmod p$ and $n_p \mid m$.

Consequently $n_p = 1$ if and only if the Sylow $p$-subgroup is normal. The standard exam move is to list the divisors of $m$ that are $\equiv 1 \pmod p$ and see whether only $1$ survives. Other tools: **Lagrange's theorem** (the order of a subgroup divides $|G|$), **Cauchy's theorem** (if a prime $p$ divides $|G|$ there is an element of order $p$), and the **class equation** $|G| = |Z(G)| + \sum [G : C_G(x_i)]$, the sum over representatives of the conjugacy classes of size greater than $1$. The class equation proves that a group of prime-power order has a nontrivial center.

### Rings

An integral domain is a **PID** if every ideal is principal, and a **UFD** if every nonzero nonunit factors uniquely into irreducibles. Every PID is a UFD, and the converse fails: $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is a UFD (by Gauss's lemma, $R$ a UFD implies $R[x]$ a UFD), but the ideal $(2, x)$ is not principal. If $k$ is a field then $k[x]$ is a PID (by the division algorithm).

**Eisenstein's criterion.** Let $f = a_n x^n + \dots + a_0 \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ and let $p$ be a prime with $p \nmid a_n$, $p \mid a_i$ for $i < n$, and $p^2 \nmid a_0$. Then $f$ is irreducible in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$.

*Proof.* By Gauss's lemma, a factorization of $f$ in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ into polynomials of positive degree gives one in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$: $f = gh$ with $\deg g = r \ge 1$ and $\deg h = s \ge 1$ (the leading coefficients being integers whose product is $a_n$). Reduce modulo $p$: $\bar a_n x^n = \bar g \bar h$ in $\mathbb{F}_p[x]$. By unique factorization in $\mathbb{F}_p[x]$, $\bar g = c x^r$ and $\bar h = d x^s$ for constants $c, d \ne 0$, because $\deg \bar g = r$ and $\deg \bar h = s$ (the leading coefficients are not divisible by $p$, since their product $a_n$ is not). As $r, s \ge 1$, the constant terms of $g$ and $h$ are both divisible by $p$, so $p^2$ divides $a_0$, a contradiction. $\blacksquare$

### Fields and Galois theory

The **tower law**: $[M:K] = [M:L][L:K]$ for fields $K \subseteq L \subseteq M$. A finite extension $L/K$ is **Galois** if it is the splitting field of a separable polynomial; then $|\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)| = [L:K]$. The **fundamental theorem of Galois theory**: for a finite Galois extension $L/K$ with group $G$, the maps $H \mapsto L^H$ and $E \mapsto \operatorname{Gal}(L/E)$ are inverse, inclusion-reversing bijections between subgroups of $G$ and intermediate fields, with $[L : L^H] = |H|$ and $[L^H : K] = [G : H]$; and $H$ is normal in $G$ if and only if $L^H/K$ is Galois.

### Modules and linear algebra

**Structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a PID.** Such a module is isomorphic to $R^r \oplus R/(d_1) \oplus \dots \oplus R/(d_k)$ with $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \dots \mid d_k$ nonunits. For $R = \mathbb{Z}$ this is the classification of finitely generated abelian groups; for $R = k[x]$ it gives rational canonical and Jordan forms. The order of $\mathbb{Z}^2/L$ for a sublattice $L$ spanned by the rows of an integer matrix $M$ is $|\det M|$ if $\det M \ne 0$.

### How algebra problems are usually attacked

| Problem type | Standard approach |
|---|---|
| "show a group of order $n$ is not simple / is cyclic" | Sylow counting, then normal subgroups and direct products |
| "is $f$ irreducible?" | Eisenstein, rational roots for degree at most 3, or reduce modulo $p$ |
| "degree or Galois group" | tower law, identify the splitting field, count the automorphisms |
| "classify modules or abelian groups" | structure theorem, Smith normal form |

@example
title: Every group of order 15 is cyclic
problem: Prove that a group $G$ of order $15$ is cyclic.
step: By Sylow (3), $n_3$ divides $5$ and $n_3 \equiv 1 \pmod 3$. The divisors of $5$ are $1$ and $5$, and $5 \equiv 2 \pmod 3$, so $n_3 = 1$.
step: Similarly $n_5$ divides $3$ and $n_5 \equiv 1 \pmod 5$; the divisors of $3$ are $1$ and $3$, and $3 \not\equiv 1 \pmod 5$, so $n_5 = 1$.
step: So $G$ has a unique Sylow $3$-subgroup $P$ and a unique Sylow $5$-subgroup $Q$, both normal, of orders $3$ and $5$. They are cyclic, since groups of prime order are cyclic.
step: $P \cap Q$ is a subgroup of both, so its order divides $\gcd(3,5) = 1$: $P \cap Q = \{e\}$. The map $P \times Q \to G$, $(x, y) \mapsto xy$, is a homomorphism because $xy = yx$ for $x \in P, y \in Q$ (the commutator $xyx^{-1}y^{-1}$ lies in $P \cap Q$ by normality of both).
step: The map is injective since $P \cap Q$ is trivial, and $|P \times Q| = 15 = |G|$, so it is an isomorphism: $G \cong \mathbb{Z}/3 \times \mathbb{Z}/5 \cong \mathbb{Z}/15$ by the Chinese remainder theorem. $\blacksquare$
answer: $G \cong \mathbb{Z}/15\mathbb{Z}$.

@example
title: The Galois group of x^3 - 2
problem: Find the degree of the splitting field $L$ of $x^3 - 2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ and identify $\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb{Q})$.
step: The polynomial is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ by Eisenstein's criterion with $p = 2$. Let $\alpha = 2^{1/3}$ (real). Then $[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}] = 3$.
step: The roots are $\alpha$, $\omega\alpha$, $\omega^2\alpha$ with $\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$, so $L = \mathbb{Q}(\alpha, \omega)$.
step: The number $\omega$ is a root of $x^2 + x + 1$. This polynomial has no real roots, hence none in the real field $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$, so it is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ and $[L : \mathbb{Q}(\alpha)] = 2$.
step: By the tower law, $[L:\mathbb{Q}] = 3 \cdot 2 = 6$. The extension is Galois (a splitting field in characteristic $0$), so $|\operatorname{Gal}| = 6$.
step: Each automorphism permutes the three roots and is determined by that permutation, so $\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb{Q})$ embeds in $S_3$. Both groups have order $6$, so $\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb{Q}) \cong S_3$.
answer: $[L:\mathbb{Q}] = 6$ and the Galois group is isomorphic to $S_3$.

@mistakes
- Using Sylow (3) as "$n_p$ divides $|G|$" instead of "$n_p$ divides $m$", the part of $|G|$ prime to $p$: this admits impossible values.
- Applying Eisenstein's criterion without checking $p^2 \nmid a_0$ and $p \nmid a_n$: for $x^4 + 4$ the prime $p = 2$ divides every lower coefficient, but $p^2 \mid 4$, and indeed $x^4 + 4 = (x^2 + 2x + 2)(x^2 - 2x + 2)$ is reducible.
- Saying "$x^4 + 4$ has no rational roots, so it is irreducible": for degree above $3$ a polynomial without roots can still be reducible, as the factorization above shows.
- Confusing $[L^H : K] = [G:H]$ with $|H|$; the order of $H$ equals $[L : L^H]$.

@practice num
prompt: How many abelian groups of order $72 = 2^3 \cdot 3^2$ are there, up to isomorphism?
answer: 6
check: len([(3,), (2, 1), (1, 1, 1)]) * len([(2,), (1, 1)])
hint: An abelian group of order $p^a$ corresponds to a partition of $a$.
hint: Count partitions of $3$ for the prime $2$ and of $2$ for the prime $3$, then multiply.
solution: The structure theorem splits the group into its $2$-part and $3$-part. There are $3$ partitions of $3$ ($3$, $2+1$, $1+1+1$) and $2$ partitions of $2$ ($2$, $1+1$), so $3 \cdot 2 = 6$ groups.

@practice list

answer: 1, 6

@practice mc
prompt: Which statement about the ring $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is correct?
choice*: It is a UFD but not a PID, because the ideal $(2, x)$ is not principal.
choice: It is a PID, because $\mathbb{Z}$ is a PID.
choice: It is not a UFD, because it is not a PID.
choice: It is a field, because it contains the field $\mathbb{Q}$ as a subring.
hint: Is there a polynomial in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ that divides both $2$ and $x$ and generates every element of the form $2f + xg$?
solution: $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is a UFD by Gauss's lemma. The ideal $(2, x)$ is not principal: a generator would divide $2$ and $x$, so it would be a unit, but $(2, x)$ does not contain $1$ (every element has an even constant term). Being a UFD does not require being a PID, and $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is not a field ($x$ has no inverse; $\mathbb{Q} \not\subseteq \mathbb{Z}[x]$ anyway).

@quiz mc
prompt: Let $L/K$ be a finite Galois extension with group $G$ and let $H$ be a subgroup of $G$ with fixed field $L^H$. Which statement is correct?
choice*: $L^H / K$ is Galois if and only if $H$ is a normal subgroup of $G$.
choice: $L^H/K$ is Galois for every subgroup $H$.
choice: $[L^H : K] = |H|$.
choice: Larger subgroups $H$ give larger fixed fields $L^H$.
hint: Recall the order-reversing correspondence and which index counts the degree of the fixed field over $K$.
solution: By the fundamental theorem, $L^H/K$ is Galois exactly when $H$ is normal. In general $[L^H:K] = [G:H]$, while $[L:L^H] = |H|$, and the correspondence reverses inclusion, so larger $H$ gives a smaller fixed field. For the splitting field of $x^3 - 2$ (group $S_3$), a non-normal subgroup of order $2$ gives a non-Galois intermediate field such as $\mathbb{Q}(2^{1/3})$.

@quiz num
prompt: How many elements of order exactly $4$ does the group $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$ have?
answer: 12
check: sum(1 for a in range(4) for b in range(8) if (4 * a) % 4 == 0 and (4 * b) % 8 == 0 and not ((2 * a) % 4 == 0 and (2 * b) % 8 == 0))
hint: Count the elements with $4x = 0$ and subtract those with $2x = 0$.
solution: Every element of $\mathbb{Z}/4$ satisfies $4a = 0$, and $4b = 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}/8$ for $4$ values of $b$, giving $16$ elements with $4x = 0$. Those with $2x = 0$ have $2a = 0$ ($2$ values) and $2b = 0$ ($2$ values), so there are $4$. The number of elements of order exactly $4$ is $16 - 4 = 12$.

@quiz self
prompt: Prove that every finite subgroup $G$ of the multiplicative group $F^\times$ of a field $F$ is cyclic.
hint: Compare, for each divisor $d$ of $n = |G|$, the number of elements of order $d$ with $\varphi(d)$, using that $x^d - 1$ has at most $d$ roots.
hint: Use the identity $\sum_{d \mid n} \varphi(d) = n$.
solution: Let $n = |G|$. For each $d \mid n$ let $\psi(d)$ be the number of elements of $G$ of order $d$. By Lagrange every element has order dividing $n$, so $\sum_{d\mid n}\psi(d) = n$. Claim: $\psi(d) \le \varphi(d)$ for every $d \mid n$. If $\psi(d) = 0$ this is clear. Otherwise let $a \in G$ have order $d$. The $d$ elements of $\langle a \rangle$ are roots of $x^d - 1$, a polynomial of degree $d$ over the field $F$, which has at most $d$ roots; so $\langle a\rangle$ is exactly the set of roots of $x^d - 1$ in $F$. Any element of order $d$ is a root of $x^d - 1$, hence lies in $\langle a \rangle$. A cyclic group of order $d$ has exactly $\varphi(d)$ generators, so $\psi(d) = \varphi(d)$. Now $n = \sum_{d\mid n}\psi(d) \le \sum_{d\mid n}\varphi(d) = n$, where the last equality is the standard identity (count the elements of $\mathbb{Z}/n$ by their orders). Equality holds throughout, so $\psi(d) = \varphi(d)$ for every $d \mid n$. In particular $\psi(n) = \varphi(n) \ge 1$, so $G$ has an element of order $n$ and is cyclic. $\blacksquare$

rubric: Uses the identity $\sum_{d \mid n} \varphi(d) = n$ to force equality for every $d$.
rubric: Concludes $\psi(n) = \varphi(n) \ge 1$, so there is an element of order $n$.
