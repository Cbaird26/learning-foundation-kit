@article
You have twelve pages of proof and twenty minutes. If you try to present the proof, the audience will be lost by minute five and you will be rushing by minute fifteen. A good talk does not transmit a paper; it transmits a **main message** that the audience can carry away: a statement they understand, a reason to care, and the central idea of why it is true. Everything else is cut or cited.

### Know the audience and the message

Ask who will listen. A talk to graduate students in many areas needs more motivation and fewer technicalities than a research seminar. Write the main message in one sentence: "Every continuous map of the disc has a fixed point, and the reason is that a disc cannot be pushed onto its boundary circle." If a section of the talk does not serve that sentence, it is a candidate for deletion.

### Structure and time budget

A common shape for twenty minutes:

| Segment | What it does | Minutes |
|---|---|---|
| Motivation | A question, example or puzzle that makes the audience want the result | 3 |
| Statement and example | The exact theorem and one concrete instance | 5 |
| Idea of the proof | The main steps, not the technicalities | 8 |
| Outlook | What it implies, what is open | 2 |
| Buffer | Slack for questions and slow moments | 2 |

Talks nearly always run long. Plan for less than you think fits, and decide in advance what you will drop if you fall behind.

### Slides, board or both

Practice differs: many talks use slides, some use a board, some use both, and what is expected depends on the area, the venue and the room. Slides help with pictures and long displays and keep you on schedule. A board slows the speaker to the audience's thinking speed, and is good for proofs. Ask the organizer what is available, and prepare for a projector failure.

### Designing slides

- **One idea per slide**, with a title that says it.
- **Few words.** Show what the audience needs to see while you speak; do not read the slide.
- **Large type** that can be read from the back of the room, and high contrast. Do not use color as the only way to distinguish items, since some viewers cannot tell colors apart.
- **Definitions come with an example.** Show the example first when you can.
- **Display the statement exactly** and keep it on screen while you discuss it.
- **Number the slides** so questioners can refer to them.
- **Pictures:** draw them. A diagram that you build step by step is easier to follow than a finished one.

### Compressing a proof

State the strategy, name the key lemma, and prove one step in enough detail that the audience believes the whole. Say what you are omitting: "The remaining estimates are routine but long; they are in Section 4 of the paper." Never pretend a skipped step is trivial. An honest sketch is more credible than a rushed proof.

### Rehearsing

Rehearse aloud, standing up, with a timer. Rehearse the first two minutes until you can say them without notes, since an uncertain start sets the tone. Rehearse the transitions: the sentence that connects each slide to the next. Give a practice talk to a friend who is not in your area and ask where they got lost. Most people speak at roughly 120 to 160 words per minute; time yourself and do not guess.

### Questions

Listen to the whole question. Repeat or rephrase it for the room. Answer briefly. If you do not know, say so and offer to think about it afterwards. If a question exposes a mistake, thank the questioner, correct it and move on. Nervousness is normal; preparation is the best remedy.

### Recording a talk

For a recorded talk, use a quiet room, check the audio level and the screen capture on a short test, and speak to an imagined audience. Keep to the time limit. A recording can be made in segments and edited, but avoid heavy editing; a few clean takes are better than a perfectly stitched one. Provide a transcript or captions if you can, and follow your institution's rules about recording and sharing.

@example
title: Budgeting a twenty-minute talk
problem: You will give a twenty-minute talk on the Brouwer fixed point theorem in dimension two. Allocate the time and name what you will say in each segment.
step: Motivation (3 minutes): stir a cup of coffee and ask whether some point can end where it started. State the question for the disc.
step: Statement and example (5 minutes): the exact theorem, $f\colon D^2 \to D^2$ continuous has a fixed point; one example with an explicit fixed point; one example showing the disc cannot be replaced by the open disc.
step: Idea of the proof (8 minutes): if $f$ had no fixed point, define a map $r\colon D^2 \to S^1$ fixing the boundary; show that $\pi_1(S^1) = \mathbb{Z}$ prevents such a retraction.
step: Outlook (2 minutes): higher dimensions and applications; buffer (2 minutes). Total: $3 + 5 + 8 + 2 + 2 = 20$.
answer: $3 + 5 + 8 + 2 + 2 = 20$ minutes.

@example
title: The idea of a proof in two minutes
problem: Give a two-minute spoken sketch of the proof that $[0,1]$ is compact.
step: Say what is to be shown: every cover of $[0,1]$ by open sets has a finite subcover.
step: Suppose not. Split $[0,1]$ into two halves; at least one half also has no finite subcover. Repeat to get nested closed intervals $I_1 \supseteq I_2 \supseteq \cdots$, each with no finite subcover, with lengths $2^{-n}$.
step: Nested closed intervals have a common point $x$ (completeness of $\mathbb{R}$). The point $x$ lies in some open set $U$ of the cover, and $U$ contains an interval $(x-\delta, x+\delta)$.
step: For large $n$, $I_n$ has length less than $\delta$ and contains $x$, so $I_n \subseteq U$. Then $I_n$ is covered by a single member, a contradiction.
step: Say what you omit: the existence of the common point uses the least upper bound property, which you take as known.
answer: A bisection argument: nested intervals, a common point, and one open set that covers a small enough interval.

@mistakes
- Trying to present the whole paper: choose a main message and cut the rest.
- Reading the slides aloud, or putting full proofs in small type on slides.
- Never timing a rehearsal, so that the talk runs long and the end, which holds the point, is rushed.
- Pretending a skipped step is trivial; say what you omit and where it is.

@practice self
prompt: **Open the talk.** Write a two-minute spoken opening (about 250 words) for a twenty-minute talk on a theorem of your choice. It must state a motivating question, give the exact theorem, and say what the talk will cover. As a model, use the Brouwer fixed point theorem in dimension two: every continuous map of the closed unit disc to itself has a fixed point.
hint: Open with a question the audience can answer, then give the statement, then the plan.
hint: Say the main message in one sentence.
solution: Model opening. "Stir a cup of coffee and let it settle. Is there a point of the surface that ends exactly where it started? It sounds like a question about coffee, but it is a theorem. Here is the precise statement. Let $D$ be the closed unit disc in the plane, that is, all points at distance at most $1$ from the origin. Let $f$ be any continuous function from $D$ to itself. Then there is a point $x$ in $D$ with $f(x) = x$. This is the Brouwer fixed point theorem in dimension two. One remark to fix ideas: the word closed matters. On the open interval $(0,1)$ the map $x \mapsto x/2$ is continuous and maps the interval into itself, yet it has no fixed point, because the only solution of $x/2 = x$ is $x = 0$, which is not in the interval. So the boundary cannot be thrown away. The main message of this talk is one sentence: a disc cannot be pushed onto its boundary circle without tearing, and that is why every map has a fixed point. Here is the plan. First, two examples that show what the theorem says and what it does not. Second, the proof by contradiction: if a map had no fixed point, I would build a retraction of the disc onto the circle. Third, the reason such a retraction cannot exist, using the fundamental group of the circle. I will leave out the proof that the fundamental group of the circle is the integers; I will say where it is proved. I will end with what happens in higher dimensions. By then, I hope, the cup of coffee will be a theorem."
rubric: Opens with a concrete question and moves to the exact statement with hypotheses.
rubric: States the main message in one sentence and gives a roadmap of the talk.
rubric: Says honestly what will be omitted and where it can be found, and is about the right length to speak in two minutes.

@quiz self
prompt: A draft slide shows the full statement of a theorem with six hypotheses and a ten-line proof of it in small type, with no example. Describe how you would redesign this material for a twenty-minute talk, in three or four slides.
hint: Put the example before the full statement and keep only the proof idea on screen.
solution: Slide 1 (motivation and example): show a concrete instance where the theorem's conclusion is visible, for instance a specific function or group, and state in words what is surprising. Slide 2 (the statement): display the theorem with its hypotheses, one per line, so the audience can follow them, and highlight the two hypotheses that matter most; say that the others are technical and why. Slide 3 (why the hypotheses matter): one counterexample when the main hypothesis is dropped. Slide 4 (the idea of the proof): a diagram or a three-line outline of the steps ("reduce to the compact case; prove the key estimate; conclude"), and say that the full proof is in the paper, with the section named. Transitions between slides are spoken: "So why is this true?" before the idea slide. The ten-line proof is not shown; if someone asks, the speaker has it on a backup slide after the last numbered one.
rubric: Puts an example before the abstract statement and displays the statement readably.
rubric: Replaces the long proof by the key idea and says where details can be found, keeping a backup slide.
rubric: Includes why the hypotheses matter and keeps one idea per slide.
