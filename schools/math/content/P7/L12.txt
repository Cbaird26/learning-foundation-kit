@article
"What can you do with a mathematics PhD?" is asked at every family dinner, and the honest answer is: many things, differing by country, by year and by person. This lesson describes common patterns, not guarantees. It makes no claims about salaries or about how many positions exist, because those change and vary; to learn about them, use current sources, your department's placement information, your advisor and people working in the jobs you are curious about.

### Academic paths

Research universities usually combine research, teaching and advising; teaching-focused colleges put teaching and student contact first, with research that may be smaller in scale. In many countries a recent PhD graduate holds one or more fixed-term research or teaching positions (often called **postdoctoral** positions) before a long-term appointment, but the details vary widely: some systems have long-term positions much earlier, others much later. Applications commonly include a CV, a research statement, a teaching statement, a list of publications and letters of recommendation, with deadlines posted in the advertisements. An academic career asks for a research program that grows, steady teaching, service to the department and the profession, and patience with a process that is often slow and uncertain.

### Industry, government and research laboratories

Mathematical training is used in many settings: statistics and data analysis, operations research and optimization, scientific computing, software and algorithm design, cryptography and security, finance and insurance, engineering and modeling, and research laboratories in government or industry. Employers typically value the ability to learn quickly, to define problems precisely, to reason carefully, and to program, so build programming and communication skills deliberately during your PhD. Job requirements and hiring practices differ by employer and sector; read current postings, ask people in the roles what they do, and consider internships.

### Teaching

Secondary schools, two-year and four-year colleges and universities all employ mathematics teachers, with different qualifications (a teaching credential may be required at secondary level in many places), different student populations and different rewards. Good teaching can be a fulfilling and central career, not a fallback. Teaching experience as a graduate student is useful on any path.

### Other paths

Mathematics writing and communication, education technology, policy and public service, nonprofit work and entrepreneurship also draw on mathematical training. Again, check current opportunities in your country.

### Exploring and deciding

Use a **decision matrix**. List the options and the things you care about (for example, autonomy, collaboration, location, stability, the content of the work), give each criterion a weight, rate each option, and compute weighted scores. The numbers are a way to make your reasoning visible, not an answer: if the result surprises you, ask why. Then run small experiments: attend a conference session in each area, volunteer to teach a course, do an internship, interview three people in different jobs. Decisions in careers are rarely irreversible, and keeping options open is a reasonable strategy.

### Publishing and refereeing

Common practice in mathematics is that a paper is submitted to **one journal at a time** (check each journal's policy), an editor decides whether to send it for review and chooses one or more **referees**, who read the paper and write a confidential report, and the editor replies with an acceptance, a request for revision or a rejection. Timing varies widely, and can be long. Referees are generally unpaid and volunteer their time as a service to the community.

A good referee report has a short summary of the results, an assessment of correctness and significance, a list of major concerns (a gap, an unproved claim, a missing hypothesis), a list of minor comments (typos, references), and a recommendation. Referees keep the manuscript confidential, disclose conflicts of interest, are specific and courteous, and do not use the content in their own work before publication. If you find an error as a referee, say which step fails and why. If you receive a report as an author, respond to each point, even when you disagree.

@example
title: A weighted decision matrix
problem: You are comparing two options for the year after the qualifying exams: a research assistantship with your advisor (option A) and a teaching internship plus a reading course (option B). You weight three criteria: progress on research ($3$), teaching experience ($2$), and flexibility ($1$). Ratings (1 to 5): A: research $5$, teaching $2$, flexibility $3$. B: research $3$, teaching $5$, flexibility $4$. Compute the scores.
step: Weighted score for A: $3 \cdot 5 + 2 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 3 = 15 + 4 + 3 = 22$.
step: Weighted score for B: $3 \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot 5 + 1 \cdot 4 = 9 + 10 + 4 = 23$.
step: The scores are nearly equal, so the decision depends on the weights and ratings; change the research weight from $3$ to $4$ and A scores $27$ while B scores $26$.
step: Conclusion: the matrix does not decide; it shows that the choice turns on how much weight you put on research progress, which is what to discuss with your advisor.
answer: A scores $22$ and B scores $23$; the choice is sensitive to the research weight.

@example
title: Writing the core of a referee report
problem: A submitted paper claims: "Every continuous $f\colon (0,1) \to (0,1)$ has a fixed point." The proof: "Let $g(x) = f(x) - x$. Then $g(0) = f(0) > 0$ and $g(1) = f(1) - 1 < 0$, so by the intermediate value theorem $g$ has a zero." Identify the problem as a referee.
step: Read for definitions: $f$ is defined on the open interval $(0,1)$, so $f(0)$ and $f(1)$ are not defined; the proof evaluates $f$ where it has no value.
step: Test the claim: $f(x) = x/2$ maps $(0,1)$ into itself and is continuous, but $x/2 = x$ only for $x = 0 \notin (0,1)$. So the theorem as stated is false.
step: Locate the correct statement: for a continuous $f\colon [0,1] \to [0,1]$ the same argument works, since $g(0) \ge 0$ and $g(1) \le 0$, and the intermediate value theorem gives a zero of $g$ (either an endpoint or an interior point).
step: Write the major concern concisely, with the counterexample and the repair, and recommend revision of the statement.
answer: The statement is false on the open interval ($f(x) = x/2$); the proof needs the closed interval.

@mistakes
- Treating a career path as a ranking: different paths suit different people, and availability varies by country and time.
- Relying on anecdotes or old information about hiring or salaries; use current, specific sources and ask people in the roles.
- Submitting the same paper to several journals at once, which most journals forbid; read each journal's policy.
- Writing a referee report that says only "reject" or "accept" without naming the steps in question.

@practice self
prompt: **Plan the recorded talk.** Write a plan for a recorded twenty-minute talk on your expository paper: a slide-by-slide list with the minutes for each slide and the spoken transition to the next, a rehearsal schedule, and a recording checklist. As a model, plan a talk on Huang's 2019 proof that every induced subgraph of the $n$-cube $Q_n$ on $2^{n-1}+1$ vertices has a vertex of degree at least $\sqrt{n}$, which implies that for a Boolean function $\deg(f) \le s(f)^2$ (here $s(f)$ is the sensitivity of $f$).
hint: Use the time budget from Lesson 11: motivation, statement, idea of the proof, outlook, buffer.
hint: Put the matrix $A_n$ and the interlacing step on separate slides.
solution: Model plan. Slide 1, title and question (1 minute): "A Boolean function on $n$ bits: how many input bits can a single flip affect, and how does that compare with its degree as a polynomial?" Transition: "To make this precise, two definitions." Slide 2, sensitivity and degree (3 minutes): define $s(f)$, the maximum over inputs of the number of single-bit flips that change $f$, and $\deg(f)$, the degree of the unique real multilinear polynomial for $f$; one example (the AND function on three bits has $s = 3$ at the all-ones input and degree $3$). Transition: "The question is how they are related." Slide 3, the theorem (2 minutes): $\deg(f) \le s(f)^2$, attributed to Huang (2019), answering the sensitivity conjecture; it follows from a statement about hypercubes. Slide 4, the hypercube statement (2 minutes): every induced subgraph of $Q_n$ with more than $2^{n-1}$ vertices has a vertex of degree at least $\sqrt n$; say that earlier work of Gotsman and Linial showed this implies the degree bound. Slide 5, the signed matrix (3 minutes): define $A_1$ and the recursion $A_n$; show $A_n^2 = nI$. Slide 6, eigenvalues (2 minutes): trace zero gives $\pm\sqrt n$ with equal multiplicity. Slide 7, interlacing (3 minutes): the submatrix on $2^{n-1}+1$ vertices has largest eigenvalue at least $\sqrt n$; bounded above by the maximum degree. Slide 8, outlook (2 minutes): why the proof is short, what remains. Buffer 2 minutes. Total $1+3+2+2+3+2+3+2+2=20$. Rehearsal: day 1 read the script and cut; day 2 rehearse aloud with a timer; day 3 rehearse the first two minutes five times and the transitions; day 4 give the talk to a friend outside the area; day 5 record two takes. Recording checklist: quiet room; microphone level checked on a ten-second test; screen capture and slide numbers visible; time under twenty minutes; speak to a person; save the file in a common format; add captions or a transcript if possible; check your institution's rules for sharing. Include the sentence that readers should check their own assignment's requirements.
rubric: Gives a slide-by-slide time budget totaling twenty minutes, including a buffer, with a spoken transition between slides.
rubric: Includes a realistic rehearsal schedule and a recording checklist covering audio, screen and time.
rubric: The plan communicates one main message with correct statements about the mathematics.

@quiz self
prompt: A submitted paper states: "Theorem. If $a_n \ge 0$ and $\sum a_n^2$ converges, then $\sum a_n$ converges." The proof says: "Since $a_n^2 \to 0$, we have $a_n \to 0$, so the series converges." Write a short referee report (summary, major concern with justification, recommendation).
hint: Test the claim with $a_n = 1/n$.
hint: The terms tending to $0$ is necessary, not sufficient, for convergence of a series.
solution: Summary. The paper claims that for nonnegative terms, convergence of $\sum a_n^2$ implies convergence of $\sum a_n$. Major concern. The statement is false, and the proof has a gap at its last step. The argument shows $a_n \to 0$, which is necessary for convergence of $\sum a_n$ but not sufficient. Counterexample: $a_n = 1/n$ satisfies $a_n \ge 0$ and $\sum 1/n^2 = \pi^2/6 < \infty$, but $\sum 1/n$ diverges (the partial sums over $2^k < n \le 2^{k+1}$ is at least $1/2$). Possible repair: the correct implication goes the other way for nonnegative terms, since $a_n \to 0$ eventually gives $a_n^2 \le a_n$, so convergence of $\sum a_n$ implies convergence of $\sum a_n^2$; and with an additional hypothesis such as $\sum a_n^2 < \infty$ one obtains the weaker conclusion $\sum a_n b_n < \infty$ for $\sum b_n^2 < \infty$ by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Minor comments. Please state the theorem's role in the paper, since it seems that the later results depend on it. Recommendation. Revise: the theorem must be corrected, and the authors should check which later results depend on it.
rubric: Identifies that the statement is false and gives a valid counterexample such as $a_n = 1/n$ with justification of divergence.
rubric: Locates the exact gap in the proof (necessary versus sufficient condition for convergence).
rubric: Follows a report structure with a recommendation, and suggests a correct repair or the correct converse direction.
