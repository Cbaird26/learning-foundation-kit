@article
"I like number theory" is not a research problem, and "I want to work with Professor X" is not yet a relationship. Between the interest and the thesis lie two decisions that shape the next several years: whom to work with, and on what. Both are made with incomplete information, so the goal is to gather good information cheaply and to keep options open for a while.

### How advisor relationships begin

Programs differ. In some, students are assigned a temporary advisor and choose a permanent one after the qualifying exams; in others they are admitted to a specific group. Typically a relationship grows out of a course, a reading course, a seminar talk, a summer project or a conversation after a talk. Check your program's rules for when an advisor must be chosen, how a change is made, and whether a committee is required.

### What to learn before committing

Read the faculty member's recent papers and the abstracts of their students' theses, and attend their seminar. Then ask, politely and directly:

- What does a first year of working with you look like? How often do we meet, and what do you expect me to bring?
- How do students in the group find problems: do you give them one, or do they find their own?
- How do you handle authorship and credit?
- Where have your recent students gone, and how long did their degrees take?
- Do you take students in this period, and for how many students do you have time?

Talk to current and former students without the advisor present; they usually know the day-to-day reality. Fit matters: mathematical interests, communication style, and how much independence you want. A famous advisor who is rarely available can be a worse fit than a less known one who meets weekly. No choice is guaranteed to work; changing advisors is normal and your program has a procedure for it.

### Choosing a problem

A good first problem has these properties: you find it interesting; it connects to the literature, so that there is something to read and to cite; there is a simpler version you can solve now (a warm-up), a main question (the core) and a more ambitious variant (the stretch); and partial results are publishable or at least instructive. A problem that has resisted experts for decades is a poor first project, however famous. The advisor can help judge size, so show them a ladder rather than one rung.

Sources of problems include open-problem sections of surveys, questions at the end of papers, special cases that a known theorem does not cover, generalizations of a proof technique to a new setting, and patterns found by computation. Pólya's advice in *How to Solve It* applies: if you cannot solve the problem, solve a simpler related one first, and then see what the solution teaches.

### Experiment before you commit

Computing small cases is cheap and often decides whether a problem is tractable. If a pattern appears, state it as a conjecture; if none appears, the problem may be harder or the wrong question. Remember that a pattern found in small cases is evidence, never a proof.

### Staying unstuck

Being stuck is normal. Keep a research log: date, what you tried, what failed and why, and what you will try next. Set a time box; when it ends, shrink the problem, change the tool or ask for help. Explaining the problem to a colleague, or writing it up as if for a paper, often reveals the missing idea. When you want to talk to someone, bring a specific question.

### Credit, collaboration and conflicts

Discuss authorship and credit before the work is written up. In many areas of mathematics, authors are listed alphabetically and all authors share credit, but norms vary by field, group and country, so agree openly rather than assuming. Give credit to prior work and to every idea you borrowed. If a relationship is not working, talk to your advisor first; if that fails, the graduate director, an ombudsperson or another trusted faculty member can help, and your program's rules describe what is possible.

### A one-page paper summary

Choose a paper you have read by the three passes of Lesson 7. Write at most one page with these headings: **problem**, **main result** (exact statement), **method** (two to four sentences), **why it matters**, and **your critique or questions**. A summary is useful only if a graduate student who has not read the paper could use it to decide whether to.

@example
title: Experimenting to scope a problem
problem: You wonder how many subgroups the cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ has, as a warm-up for counting subgroups of other groups. Use small cases to form a conjecture, and then prove it.
step: Count by hand: $\mathbb{Z}/1$ has $1$ subgroup; $\mathbb{Z}/2$ has $2$; $\mathbb{Z}/3$ has $2$; $\mathbb{Z}/4$ has $3$ (orders $1, 2, 4$); $\mathbb{Z}/6$ has $4$ (orders $1, 2, 3, 6$); $\mathbb{Z}/12$ has $6$.
step: Conjecture: the number of subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}/n$ is the number of positive divisors of $n$. The data $1, 2, 2, 3, 4, 6$ for $n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$ agree with this.
step: Prove: for each divisor $d$ of $n$, the subgroup generated by $n/d$ has order $d$, so there is at least one subgroup of order $d$.
step: Uniqueness: a subgroup $H$ of a cyclic group $\langle g\rangle$ of order $n$ is cyclic, generated by $g^m$ with $m$ the least positive exponent in $H$; then $m \mid n$ (otherwise a remainder would give a smaller exponent), and $H = \langle g^m \rangle$ has order $n/m$. So each subgroup corresponds to exactly one divisor, and the conjecture is a theorem.
answer: $\mathbb{Z}/n$ has exactly as many subgroups as $n$ has positive divisors.

@example
title: From a vague interest to a ladder of problems
problem: A student says: "I am interested in gaps between consecutive primes." Turn this into a warm-up, a core question and a stretch goal.
step: Warm-up with a proof: show that prime gaps can be arbitrarily large. For $m \ge 2$ the $m - 1$ consecutive integers $m! + 2, \dots, m! + m$ are all composite, since $k \mid m! + k$ for $2 \le k \le m$. So there are $m - 1$ consecutive composites for every $m$.
step: Core: read and summarize a survey on bounded gaps between primes, with its history: Zhang proved in 2013 that infinitely many pairs of primes differ by a bounded amount, and Maynard and the Polymath project improved the bound to $246$. Identify the sieve ideas used.
step: Stretch: pose a question you can explore computationally and then try to prove, such as counting how often a given gap occurs below $10^6$, comparing with a heuristic, and asking what a proof would need.
step: Check the ladder with an advisor: ask whether each rung is the right size and what reading comes first.
answer: Warm-up: arbitrarily large gaps. Core: read a survey on bounded gaps. Stretch: a computational experiment with a heuristic comparison, to discuss with an advisor.

@mistakes
- Choosing an advisor by reputation alone, without asking how the group works and how often students meet the advisor.
- Starting with a famous open problem, so that there is no way to make visible progress.
- Waiting to talk about authorship and credit until after the paper is written.
- Reporting a pattern from small cases as a theorem: state it as a conjecture and prove it.

prompt: How many subgroups does the elementary abelian group $(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^3$, of order $8$, have? Count the trivial subgroup and the whole group.
answer: 16
check: sum(1 for m in range(1, 256) if (m & 1) and all((m >> (a ^ b)) & 1 for a in range(8) for b in range(8) if (m >> a) & 1 and (m >> b) & 1))
hint: Count by order: $1$, $2$, $4$ and $8$. A subgroup of order $2$ is generated by a nonzero element.
hint: A subgroup of order $4$ is the span of two independent vectors.
solution: Order $1$: one subgroup. Order $2$: one for each of the $7$ nonzero elements. Order $4$: each corresponds to a $2$-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb{F}_2^3$, and there are $(2^3 - 1)(2^3 - 2)/((2^2-1)(2^2-2)) = 42/6 = 7$ of them. Order $8$: one. The total is $1 + 7 + 7 + 1 = 16$.

@practice self
prompt: **One-page paper summary.** Write a one-page summary of a research paper of your choice in the form: problem, main result (exact statement), method (two to four sentences), why it matters, and critique or questions. As a model, summarize the following result of Hao Huang (2019) from the information given. Result: every induced subgraph of the $n$-dimensional hypercube graph $Q_n$ on more than $2^{n-1}$ vertices has a vertex of degree at least $\sqrt{n}$. Method hints: define signed adjacency matrices by $A_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$ and $A_n = \begin{pmatrix} A_{n-1} & I \\ I & -A_{n-1}\end{pmatrix}$; use Cauchy interlacing.
hint: Show $A_n^2 = nI$ by induction, and then find the eigenvalues of $A_n$ using the trace.
hint: For a principal submatrix $B$ of size $2^{n-1} + 1$, interlacing bounds the largest eigenvalue of $B$ below by $\sqrt{n}$; bound it above by the maximum degree.
solution: Problem: how large can the maximum degree of an induced subgraph of the hypercube $Q_n$ be forced to be when the subgraph has more than half of the vertices? The question matters because earlier work of Gotsman and Linial showed it to be equivalent to the sensitivity conjecture of Nisan and Szegedy about Boolean functions. Main result: if $H$ is an induced subgraph of $Q_n$ with $2^{n-1} + 1$ vertices (or more), then $H$ has a vertex of degree at least $\sqrt{n}$. Method: $A_n$ is a symmetric $2^n \times 2^n$ matrix with entries in $\{0, \pm 1\}$ whose absolute values form the adjacency matrix of $Q_n$. By induction $A_n^2 = nI$: $A_n^2 = \begin{pmatrix} A_{n-1}^2 + I & A_{n-1} - A_{n-1} \\ A_{n-1} - A_{n-1} & I + A_{n-1}^2\end{pmatrix} = nI$ when $A_{n-1}^2 = (n-1)I$. So every eigenvalue is $\pm\sqrt{n}$; the trace of $A_n$ is $0$, so each sign has multiplicity $2^{n-1}$. Let $B$ be the principal submatrix of $A_n$ on the vertices of $H$, of size $m = 2^{n-1} + 1$. Cauchy interlacing gives $\lambda_1(B) \ge \lambda_{1 + 2^n - m}(A_n) = \lambda_{2^{n-1}}(A_n) = \sqrt{n}$. Also $\lambda_1(B)$ is at most the largest absolute row sum of $B$, which is the maximum degree of $H$. Hence $\Delta(H) \ge \sqrt{n}$. Why it matters: it settles the sensitivity conjecture through the known equivalence, with a proof of about one page, so the argument is a model of how linear algebra can solve a combinatorial problem. Critique and questions: the bound $\sqrt n$ is a stated lower bound; one can ask for which $n$ it is attained and what happens for other subgraph sizes. A reader should check the interlacing indices and the claim that the trace of $A_n$ is zero.
rubric: States the problem and the main result exactly, with the hypothesis on the number of vertices.
rubric: Describes the method correctly: signed adjacency matrix with $A_n^2 = nI$, eigenvalues $\pm\sqrt{n}$ with equal multiplicity, interlacing, and the comparison of $\lambda_1$ with the maximum degree.
rubric: Gives significance and a critique or question, and keeps to one page.

@quiz num
prompt: How many subgroups does the group $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ have? Count the trivial subgroup and the whole group.
answer: 8
check: sum(1 for m in range(1, 256) if (m & 1) and all((m >> (((a + c) % 2) * 4 + (b + d) % 4)) & 1 for a in range(2) for b in range(4) for c in range(2) for d in range(4) if (m >> (a * 4 + b)) & 1 and (m >> (c * 4 + d)) & 1))
hint: Count by order: the group has $8$ elements.
solution: Order $1$: $1$ subgroup. Order $2$: the elements of order $2$ are $(1,0)$, $(0,2)$ and $(1,2)$, giving $3$ subgroups. Order $4$: the elements of order $4$ are $(0,\pm1)$ and $(1, \pm1)$, which generate the cyclic subgroups $\langle(0,1)\rangle$ and $\langle(1,1)\rangle$ ($2$ subgroups), plus the subgroup $\{(a, b) : 2b = 0\}$, the Klein four-group, which is not cyclic ($1$ more), for $3$ in all. Order $8$: $1$. The total is $1 + 3 + 3 + 1 = 8$.
