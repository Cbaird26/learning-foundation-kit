objective: Turn the inventory into a prioritized reading list, with a main text and a backup for each subject.

@article
Suppose your department hands you a one-page syllabus: "Analysis: measure and integration, $L^p$ spaces, complex analysis. Algebra: groups, rings, fields, modules. Topology: point-set, fundamental group." You have twelve weeks. Where do you begin? Students who begin with the first chapter of the first book usually run out of time on the last subject. Students who begin with a plan make fewer, better decisions.

### Step 1: the topic inventory

Collect three sources: your program's written syllabus, the last several years of past exams if the department shares them, and the table of contents of the course sequence you took. Combine them into a list of **topics**, each small enough to study in a few sessions: "Sylow theorems", "dominated convergence theorem", "Cauchy's integral formula", "covering spaces and the lifting criterion". A subject usually breaks into twenty to forty topics.

For each topic record two numbers:

- **importance** $I$: $3$ if it appears on every exam in your sample, $2$ if on some, $1$ if rarely;
- **gap** $G$: $1$ if you can state and prove the main results unaided, up to $5$ if you cannot state the definitions.

Be honest about the gap. The way to rate it is to try: write the statement and proof from a blank page. If you cannot, the gap is at least $3$.

### Step 2: the reading list

For each subject choose a **main text** that you will work through and a **backup** whose explanation style differs from the main one, for when the main text fails you. Programs often name the texts their exams draw on, and many standard graduate texts cover the same material; for example, Rudin's *Principles of Mathematical Analysis* and Folland's *Real Analysis* for analysis, Ahlfors's *Complex Analysis* or Stein and Shakarchi for complex analysis, Dummit and Foote's *Abstract Algebra* or Lang's *Algebra* for algebra, Munkres's *Topology* and Hatcher's *Algebraic Topology* for topology. Use the ones your program and advisors recommend. Do not read cover to cover: read the sections your inventory marks, and read them with a pencil.

Add one more kind of reading: lecture notes and surveys, which are written to be read and often organize a topic better than a reference text. Many appear on the arXiv.

### Step 3: priorities and hours

The **priority** of a topic is $P = I \times G$. If you have $H$ hours to spend on a subject, give topic $t$ the share
$$H \cdot \frac{P_t}{\sum_s P_s}.$$
This is a heuristic, not a law: it sends time to the topics that are both examined and weak. Round, and keep a small reserve for surprises.

### Step 4: the twelve-week skeleton

A plan that ends with the exam works backward from it.

| Weeks | Purpose |
|---|---|
| 1 | Diagnostic, inventory, reading list |
| 2 to 8 | Learn and drill one subject cluster at a time, two or three weeks each |
| 9 to 10 | Mixed drill; first timed mocks |
| 11 | Full timed mocks, graded with a rubric |
| 12 | Repair the error log; light review; rest |

Each week repeats the cycle from Lesson 1: two sessions to learn, two to drill, one timed set of problems, and a short review of last week's error log. **Spaced review** helps: revisit a topic a day or two after first studying it, then again after a week, then after a month, always by recall and never by rereading.

### The error log

Keep one page per failure, with five fields: the problem; where you got stuck; the missing fact or idea; the repaired solution written out fully; and the date you will retry it blind. A log of thirty well-kept entries is a personal textbook of exactly the things you forget.

### Changing the plan

A plan that never changes is a plan you stopped reading. Review it at the end of each week against the log: if a topic you rated $G = 2$ keeps appearing in the log, raise its gap. Being behind schedule is information, not failure; drop the lowest-priority topic before you drop sleep or the mocks. Your program's rules may allow petitioning for a later date; ask early, not after the deadline.

@example
title: Allocating hours across topics
problem: You have $102$ hours for an analysis block. Four topics have these ratings: measure theory ($I = 3$, $G = 4$), $L^p$ spaces ($I = 3$, $G = 2$), residue calculus ($I = 2$, $G = 5$) and normal families ($I = 2$, $G = 3$). Allocate the hours by priority.
step: Compute priorities $P = I \times G$: measure theory $12$, $L^p$ spaces $6$, residue calculus $10$, normal families $6$.
step: Total priority: $12 + 6 + 10 + 6 = 34$.
step: Hours for each topic are $102 \cdot P_t / 34 = 3 P_t$: measure theory $36$, $L^p$ spaces $18$, residue calculus $30$, normal families $18$.
step: Sanity check: $36 + 18 + 30 + 18 = 102$. Then adjust by judgment, for example moving a few hours from normal families to a weekly mixed review.
answer: $36$, $18$, $30$ and $18$ hours.

@example
title: Working backward from the exam date
problem: Your written exams are at the end of week 12 and you know you recall group theory well but forget complex analysis. Sketch where in the twelve weeks each subject goes.
step: Fix the end: week 12 is for repair and rest, week 11 for full timed mocks, so learning must finish by week 10.
step: Put the weakest and most examined subject earliest, so that it gets the most review cycles: complex analysis in weeks 2 to 4.
step: Put the strong subject in the middle with fewer sessions: groups and rings in weeks 5 and 6, fields and modules in week 7.
step: Fill weeks 8 to 10 with the remaining subject and mixed sets, so that every subject is revisited at least twice before the mocks.
answer: Weak subject early (weeks 2 to 4), strong subject compressed in the middle, mixed drill in weeks 8 to 10, mocks in week 11, repair in week 12.

@mistakes
- Rating your gap by how familiar the topic feels instead of by trying to write the proof from a blank page.
- Reading a text from page 1 in order, which spends the weeks on topics that are not examined.
- Scheduling no timed mocks until the last week, so the first timed exam is the real one.
- Treating the plan as fixed: ignoring the error log means repeating the same weak spots.

@practice num
prompt: You have $120$ hours for an algebra block with three topics whose priorities $P = I \times G$ are $6$ (groups), $9$ (rings and fields) and $5$ (modules). If you allocate hours in proportion to priority, how many hours go to rings and fields?
answer: 54
check: 120 * 9 / (6 + 9 + 5)
hint: First add the three priorities.
solution: The total priority is $6 + 9 + 5 = 20$, so rings and fields receive $120 \cdot 9 / 20 = 54$ hours.

@quiz self
prompt: On a mock exam you could not prove: "if $K$ is a compact subset of a topological space $X$ and $C \subseteq K$ is closed in $X$, then $C$ is compact." Write the error-log entry (what to record in each of the five fields) and the repaired, complete proof.
hint: A set is compact if every open cover has a finite subcover. Add one more open set to a cover of $C$.
solution: Error-log entry. Problem: closed subset of a compact set is compact. Where stuck: I did not know what to do with a cover of $C$, because it does not cover $K$. Missing idea: $X \setminus C$ is open, so adding it to the cover produces a cover of $K$. Repaired solution: below. Retry: blind, in one week. Proof: let $\{U_i\}_{i \in I}$ be a collection of open subsets of $X$ whose union contains $C$. Since $C$ is closed in $X$, the set $X \setminus C$ is open. The collection $\{U_i\}_{i \in I} \cup \{X \setminus C\}$ is an open cover of $X$, hence of $K$. By compactness of $K$ it has a finite subcover $U_{i_1}, \dots, U_{i_m}$ together possibly with $X \setminus C$. Every point of $C$ lies in one of these sets and not in $X \setminus C$, so $C \subseteq U_{i_1} \cup \dots \cup U_{i_m}$. Thus every cover of $C$ by open subsets of $X$ has a finite subcover, so $C$ is compact. (Covers by sets open in $C$ reduce to this case, since each such set is $U \cap C$ with $U$ open in $X$.) $\blacksquare$
rubric: The log entry has all five fields and names the specific missing idea, not just "review compactness".
rubric: Adds the open set $X \setminus C$ to a cover of $C$ and uses closedness of $C$ to see it is open.
rubric: Extracts a finite subcover and explains why the point of $C$ must lie in a member of the original cover.
