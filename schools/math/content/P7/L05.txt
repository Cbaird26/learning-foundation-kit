@article
Draw any convex polyhedron and count vertices minus edges plus faces. A cube gives $8 - 12 + 6 = 2$, a pyramid on a square gives $5 - 8 + 5 = 2$. Now take a polyhedron shaped like a picture frame, with a hole through it, and the count is $0$. A number computed from a combinatorial structure that does not change when the shape is deformed: that is the first example of a topological invariant, and topology and geometry exams are largely about computing invariants and using them to prove that spaces differ.

### Point-set topology

A space is **compact** if every open cover has a finite subcover, **connected** if it is not a union of two disjoint nonempty open sets, and **Hausdorff** if distinct points have disjoint neighborhoods. Facts to know cold: a continuous image of a compact (connected) space is compact (connected); a compact subset of a Hausdorff space is closed; a closed subset of a compact space is compact; a continuous bijection from a compact space onto a Hausdorff space is a homeomorphism; the product of finitely many compact spaces is compact, and **Tychonoff's theorem** says the same for arbitrary products. In $\mathbb{R}^n$ (the **Heine–Borel theorem**) a set is compact if and only if it is closed and bounded; in infinite-dimensional spaces closed and bounded does not imply compact.

### Fundamental group and coverings

The **fundamental group** $\pi_1(X, x_0)$ is the group of homotopy classes of loops at $x_0$. Facts: $\pi_1(S^1) \cong \mathbb{Z}$; $\pi_1(X \times Y) \cong \pi_1(X) \times \pi_1(Y)$, so the torus has group $\mathbb{Z}^2$; a contractible space has trivial $\pi_1$. The **Seifert–van Kampen theorem** says that if $X = U \cup V$ with $U$, $V$ open, path-connected and $U \cap V$ path-connected and containing $x_0$, then $\pi_1(X)$ is the pushout of $\pi_1(U) \leftarrow \pi_1(U\cap V) \rightarrow \pi_1(V)$. For a wedge of two circles this gives the free group $F_2$.

For a connected graph with $V$ vertices and $E$ edges, $\pi_1$ is a free group of rank $E - V + 1$ (contract a spanning tree, which has $V - 1$ edges, and each remaining edge becomes a free generator).

**Covering spaces.** For a path-connected, locally path-connected, semilocally simply connected space $X$, connected covers correspond to conjugacy classes of subgroups of $\pi_1(X)$, and the number of sheets is the index of the subgroup. An $n$-sheeted cover of a finite complex multiplies the Euler characteristic by $n$.

### Surfaces and Euler characteristic

The **Euler characteristic** of a finite cell complex is $\chi = \sum_k (-1)^k c_k$, where $c_k$ is the number of $k$-cells; it equals $\sum_k (-1)^k \operatorname{rank} H_k$ and is a homotopy invariant. The **classification of closed surfaces**: every closed connected surface is a sphere, a connected sum of $g \ge 1$ tori (orientable, genus $g$, $\chi = 2 - 2g$), or a connected sum of $k \ge 1$ projective planes (nonorientable, $\chi = 2 - k$). A closed orientable surface of genus $g$ has $H_1 \cong \mathbb{Z}^{2g}$.

### Geometry

**Stokes's theorem**: for a compact oriented smooth $n$-manifold $M$ with boundary and a smooth $(n-1)$-form $\omega$, $\int_M d\omega = \int_{\partial M}\omega$. The **Gauss–Bonnet theorem**: for a compact oriented Riemannian surface $M$ without boundary, $\int_M K\,dA = 2\pi\chi(M)$, where $K$ is the Gaussian curvature. A sphere of radius $R$ has $K = 1/R^2$ and area $4\pi R^2$, so $\int K\,dA = 4\pi = 2\pi \cdot 2$. For a geodesic triangle on a sphere of radius $R$ with angles $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$, area is $R^2(\alpha+\beta+\gamma-\pi)$ (Girard's formula, a consequence of Gauss–Bonnet).

### Proof of the Brouwer fixed point theorem for the disc

**Theorem.** Every continuous $f\colon D^2 \to D^2$ of the closed unit disc has a fixed point.

*Proof.* First, there is no retraction $r\colon D^2 \to S^1$ (a continuous map with $r(x) = x$ for $x \in S^1$). Suppose $r$ existed, and let $i\colon S^1 \to D^2$ be the inclusion. Then $r\circ i = \mathrm{id}_{S^1}$, so $r_* \circ i_*$ is the identity on $\pi_1(S^1) \cong \mathbb{Z}$. But $i_*$ factors through $\pi_1(D^2) = 0$, since $D^2$ is contractible, so $r_*\circ i_*$ is the zero map, and $\mathbb{Z} \ne 0$. Contradiction.

Now suppose $f$ had no fixed point. For $x \in D^2$ the points $f(x) \ne x$ determine a ray from $f(x)$ through $x$; let $r(x)$ be the point where it meets $S^1$. The point $r(x)$ is given by an explicit formula involving a square root of a positive quantity, so $r$ is continuous, and $r(x) = x$ when $x \in S^1$. That is a retraction, which cannot exist. $\blacksquare$

@example
title: The fundamental group of K4
problem: The complete graph $K_4$ has $4$ vertices and an edge between every pair. Find $\pi_1(K_4)$.
step: Count edges: each of the $\binom{4}{2} = 6$ pairs of vertices is joined by one edge, so $E = 6$ and $V = 4$.
step: $K_4$ is connected. Choose a spanning tree, for instance the three edges from one vertex to the other three; a tree on $V$ vertices has $V - 1 = 3$ edges.
step: Contracting the tree to a point is a homotopy equivalence and leaves a wedge of circles, one for each of the $6 - 3 = 3$ edges outside the tree.
step: The fundamental group of a wedge of $3$ circles is the free group on $3$ generators (van Kampen).
answer: $\pi_1(K_4)$ is free of rank $E - V + 1 = 3$.

@example
title: Gauss–Bonnet for a surface of genus 2
problem: A closed orientable surface $M$ of genus $2$ carries a Riemannian metric. What is $\int_M K\,dA$, and what does it say about the sign of $K$?
step: The Euler characteristic of a closed orientable surface of genus $g$ is $2 - 2g$, so $\chi(M) = 2 - 4 = -2$.
step: Gauss–Bonnet gives $\int_M K\,dA = 2\pi\chi(M) = -4\pi$.
step: The total is negative, so $K$ cannot be $\ge 0$ everywhere: the surface must have regions of negative curvature, whatever the metric.
answer: $-4\pi$.

@mistakes
- Using $\chi = V - E + F$ on a cell structure that is not a cell complex, or counting an edge twice; always check on the sphere that you get $2$.
- Forgetting the hypotheses of the covering classification (locally path-connected, semilocally simply connected) and the correspondence with conjugacy classes, not subgroups.
- Claiming "closed and bounded implies compact" in a general metric space: it holds in $\mathbb{R}^n$, not in infinite-dimensional spaces.
- Applying van Kampen without checking that the intersection of the two open sets is path-connected.

@practice num
prompt: The wedge of two circles $X = S^1 \vee S^1$ has $\pi_1(X) = F_2$. A connected $5$-sheeted covering space of $X$ is a finite graph, and so has a free fundamental group. What is its rank?
answer: 6
check: 1 - 5 * (1 - 2)
hint: $\chi(X) = 1 - 2 = -1$, and an $n$-sheeted cover has Euler characteristic $n\chi(X)$.
hint: For a connected graph, rank $= 1 - \chi$.
solution: The cover is a connected graph with $\chi = 5 \cdot (-1) = -5$. For a connected graph the rank of $\pi_1$ is $1 - \chi = 6$. This agrees with the Schreier index formula: a subgroup of index $n$ in $F_2$ is free of rank $n(2-1) + 1 = 6$.

@practice self
prompt: Prove that a compact subset $K$ of a Hausdorff space $X$ is closed.
hint: Fix a point $x \notin K$ and separate $x$ from each point of $K$ by disjoint open sets.
hint: Cover $K$ by the open sets that stay away from $x$, extract a finite subcover, and intersect the corresponding neighborhoods of $x$.
solution: We show that $X \setminus K$ is open. Let $x \in X \setminus K$. For each $y \in K$, since $X$ is Hausdorff and $x \ne y$, choose disjoint open sets $U_y \ni x$ and $V_y \ni y$. The sets $\{V_y\}_{y\in K}$ form an open cover of $K$. Since $K$ is compact, finitely many cover it: $K \subseteq V_{y_1} \cup \dots \cup V_{y_n}$. (If $K$ is empty it is closed, so assume $n \ge 1$.) Put $U = U_{y_1}\cap \dots\cap U_{y_n}$, a finite intersection of open sets, so $U$ is open, and $x \in U$. For each $j$, $U \cap V_{y_j} \subseteq U_{y_j}\cap V_{y_j} = \varnothing$, so $U$ is disjoint from $V_{y_1}\cup\dots\cup V_{y_n} \supseteq K$. Hence $U \subseteq X \setminus K$ is an open neighborhood of $x$. Since $x$ was arbitrary, $X \setminus K$ is open and $K$ is closed. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Separates the fixed point $x \notin K$ from each $y \in K$ by disjoint open sets using the Hausdorff property.
rubric: Extracts a finite subcover of $K$ using compactness and intersects the finitely many neighborhoods of $x$, noting that a finite intersection is open.
rubric: Concludes that $X \setminus K$ is a union of open sets and states why the intersection avoids $K$.

prompt: Suppose a closed orientable surface has a triangulation with $10$ vertices and $24$ triangles. Every edge borders exactly two triangles. Find its genus.

@quiz self
prompt: Prove that $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{R}^2$ are not homeomorphic.
hint: Remove one point from each space and compare connectedness.
hint: To show that the plane minus a point is path-connected, join two points by a path of two straight segments through a well-chosen third point.
solution: Suppose $h\colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^2$ is a homeomorphism and let $p = h(0)$. Then $h$ restricts to a homeomorphism $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\} \to \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{p\}$. The space $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\} = (-\infty, 0) \cup (0, \infty)$ is a union of two disjoint nonempty open sets, so it is disconnected. We show that $\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{p\}$ is path-connected, hence connected. Let $x, y \in \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus\{p\}$. If the segment $[x,y]$ avoids $p$, it is a path. Otherwise choose $z$ lying on neither the line through $x$ and $p$ nor the line through $y$ and $p$ (possible, since two lines do not cover the plane). Then the segment $[x,z]$ does not contain $p$ (if it did, $z$ would lie on the line through $x$ and $p$), and likewise $[z,y]$ does not. The concatenation is a path from $x$ to $y$ in $\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{p\}$. A homeomorphism preserves connectedness, but one space is disconnected and the other is connected, a contradiction. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Restricts the homeomorphism to the complement of a point and compares connectedness, noting that connectedness is preserved by homeomorphisms.
rubric: Shows that $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$ is disconnected by exhibiting the two open sets.
rubric: Proves that $\mathbb{R}^2$ minus a point is path-connected by an explicit two-segment path, with the choice of $z$ justified.
