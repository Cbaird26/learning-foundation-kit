@article
A circle can be made by taking a segment and gluing its two ends. A cylinder is a circle times a segment, and a circle sits inside the plane. Three constructions (restrict, multiply, glue) produce most spaces in geometry, and each needs a topology with good properties.

### Subspaces

**Definition.** For $A \subseteq X$, the **subspace topology** on $A$ is $\mathcal{T}_A = \{U \cap A : U \in \mathcal{T}\}$.

It is a topology: $\emptyset = \emptyset \cap A$ and $A = X \cap A$; $\bigcup_i (U_i \cap A) = (\bigcup_i U_i) \cap A$ and $(U \cap A) \cap (V \cap A) = (U \cap V) \cap A$. For the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}$ the subspace topology on $\mathbb{Z}$ is discrete (since $\{n\} = (n - \tfrac12, n + \tfrac12) \cap \mathbb{Z}$), and on $A = [0, 1] \cup \{2\}$ the set $\{2\}$ is open while $[0, 1]$ is open too, being $A \cap (-1, \tfrac32)$.

"Open in $A$" is not "open in $X$". However, if $A$ is itself open in $X$ and $U$ is open in $A$, then $U = W \cap A$ with $W$ open in $X$, so $U$ is open in $X$. The closed sets of $A$ are the sets $C \cap A$ with $C$ closed in $X$.

**Proposition 1.** The inclusion $i\colon A \to X$ is continuous, and a map $g\colon Z \to A$ is continuous if and only if $i \circ g\colon Z \to X$ is continuous.

*Proof.* $i^{-1}(U) = U \cap A$. If $g$ is continuous so is $i \circ g$. Conversely, an open set of $A$ has the form $U \cap A$, and $g^{-1}(U \cap A) = (i \circ g)^{-1}(U)$ is open. $\blacksquare$

### Products

For spaces $X, Y$ the **product topology** on $X \times Y$ has as basis the sets $U \times V$ with $U$ open in $X$ and $V$ open in $Y$. This is a basis: the sets cover $X \times Y$, and $(U \times V) \cap (U' \times V') = (U \cap U') \times (V \cap V')$. The projections $\pi_1, \pi_2$ are continuous, since $\pi_1^{-1}(U) = U \times Y$.

**Theorem 2.** A map $f\colon Z \to X \times Y$ is continuous if and only if $\pi_1 \circ f$ and $\pi_2 \circ f$ are continuous.

*Proof.* ($\Rightarrow$) Composition of continuous maps. ($\Leftarrow$) By Theorem 1(d) of Week 3 it suffices to check basis sets: $f^{-1}(U \times V) = (\pi_1 \circ f)^{-1}(U) \cap (\pi_2 \circ f)^{-1}(V)$ is a finite intersection of open sets. $\blacksquare$

The product topology on $\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}$ (open rectangles) equals the metric topology on $\mathbb{R}^2$: a ball around a point contains a small rectangle around it, and a rectangle contains a small ball around each of its points.

### Quotients

Let $q\colon X \to Y$ be a surjection. The **quotient topology** on $Y$ declares $V \subseteq Y$ open if and only if $q^{-1}(V)$ is open in $X$. Then $q$ is called a **quotient map**. For an equivalence relation $\sim$, the set $X/{\sim}$ of classes carries the quotient topology from $x \mapsto [x]$.

**Theorem 3.** Let $q\colon X \to Y$ be a quotient map. A map $g\colon Y \to Z$ is continuous if and only if $g \circ q$ is continuous.

*Proof.* ($\Rightarrow$) is composition. ($\Leftarrow$) For $W$ open in $Z$, $q^{-1}(g^{-1}(W)) = (g \circ q)^{-1}(W)$ is open, so $g^{-1}(W)$ is open by the definition of the quotient topology. $\blacksquare$

So a continuous map $h\colon X \to Z$ that is constant on each class induces a continuous map $\bar h\colon X/{\sim} \to Z$.

**Corollary 4 (sections).** If $q\colon X \to Y$ is continuous and has a continuous right inverse $s\colon Y \to X$ with $q \circ s = \mathrm{id}_Y$, then $q$ is a quotient map. *Proof.* $q$ is surjective. If $q^{-1}(V)$ is open, then $V = s^{-1}(q^{-1}(V))$ is open. $\blacksquare$

A continuous surjection need not be a quotient map. Let $A = [0, 1) \cup [2, 3]$ and $q(x) = x$ on $[0, 1)$, $q(x) = x - 1$ on $[2, 3]$, onto $[0, 2]$. Then $q^{-1}([1, 2]) = [2, 3]$ is open in $A$, but $[1, 2]$ is not open in $[0, 2]$. Compactness will later give a useful sufficient condition (Week 7).

@example
title: A segment with its ends glued is a circle
problem: Let $\sim$ identify $0$ and $1$ in $[0, 1]$ (and nothing else). Show that $[0, 1]/{\sim}$ is homeomorphic to the circle $S^1 = \{z \in \mathbb{C} : |z| = 1\}$.
step: Let $p(t) = e^{2\pi i t}$. It is continuous and onto $S^1$, and $p(s) = p(t)$ if and only if $s = t$ or $\{s, t\} = \{0, 1\}$. So $p$ is constant on classes and induces a bijection $\bar p\colon [0, 1]/{\sim} \to S^1$, continuous by Theorem 3.
step: Key claim: $p$ is a quotient map. Let $V \subseteq S^1$ with $p^{-1}(V)$ open in $[0, 1]$; take $z \in V$. For $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$ and $0 < \varepsilon \le \tfrac12$ the arc $\{e^{2\pi i s} : |s - \theta| < \varepsilon\}$ is open in $S^1$: it equals $\{w \in S^1 : \operatorname{Re}(w e^{-2\pi i \theta}) > \cos 2\pi\varepsilon\}$.
step: If $z = p(t)$ with $0 < t < 1$, then $p^{-1}(V)$ is an open set containing $t$, so it contains $(t - \varepsilon, t + \varepsilon)$ for small $\varepsilon$, and the arc of radius $\varepsilon$ around $z$ lies in $V$. If $z = 1$, then $0, 1 \in p^{-1}(V)$, so $[0, \varepsilon) \cup (1 - \varepsilon, 1] \subseteq p^{-1}(V)$ for small $\varepsilon$, and its image is the open arc $\{e^{2\pi i s} : |s| < \varepsilon\} \subseteq V$. So $V$ is a union of open arcs, hence open.
step: Since $p$ is a quotient map, $\bar p$ is a homeomorphism: for $W$ open in $[0, 1]/{\sim}$, the preimage under $p$ of $\bar p(W)$ is the open set $q^{-1}(W)$, so $\bar p(W)$ is open in $S^1$.
answer: $[0, 1]/(0 \sim 1) \cong S^1$.

@example
title: A quotient topology on a finite set
problem: Let $X = \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ have open sets $\emptyset, \{1\}, \{2\}, \{1, 2\}, \{1, 2, 3\}, X$, and let $\sim$ have classes $\{1, 3\}$, $\{2\}$, $\{4\}$. Find the quotient topology on $X/{\sim}$.
step: Write $a = [1] = \{1, 3\}$, $b = [2] = \{2\}$, $c = [4] = \{4\}$. A subset of $\{a, b, c\}$ is open if and only if the union of its classes is open in $X$.
step: Check all eight subsets. $\emptyset$ has preimage $\emptyset$ (open). $\{a\}$ has $\{1, 3\}$ (not open). $\{b\}$ has $\{2\}$ (open). $\{c\}$ has $\{4\}$ (not open). $\{a, b\}$ has $\{1, 2, 3\}$ (open). $\{a, c\}$ has $\{1, 3, 4\}$ (not open). $\{b, c\}$ has $\{2, 4\}$ (not open). $\{a, b, c\}$ has $X$ (open).
step: So the open sets are $\emptyset, \{b\}, \{a, b\}, \{a, b, c\}$: only four, although $X$ has six.
answer: $\{\emptyset, \{b\}, \{a, b\}, \{a, b, c\}\}$.

@mistakes
- Assuming a set open in a subspace $A$ is open in $X$: $[0, 1]$ is open in $A = [0, 1] \cup \{2\}$ but not in $\mathbb{R}$.
- Believing every open set of a product is a rectangle $U \times V$: rectangles are only a basis, and open sets are unions of them (an open disk in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is not a rectangle).
- Assuming a continuous surjection is a quotient map: the map $q$ from $[0, 1) \cup [2, 3]$ onto $[0, 2]$ shows this is false.
- Checking openness of $V$ in the quotient by looking at $V$ instead of the preimage $q^{-1}(V)$, which must be a union of classes.

All eight subsets of $A$ occur ($\{2, 5\}$ is $\{2\} \cup \{5\}$), so the subspace is discrete and has $8$ open sets.

@practice self
prompt: Let $\sim$ be the relation on $X = [-2, 2]$ with $x \sim y$ if and only if $|x| = |y|$. Prove that $X/{\sim}$ is homeomorphic to $[0, 2]$ (with the standard topology).
hint: Define $\varphi(x) = |x|$ and induce a map on the quotient; build the inverse from the inclusion $[0, 2] \subseteq X$ followed by the quotient map, and use Theorem 3.
solution: Let $q\colon X \to X/{\sim}$ be the quotient map. The map $\varphi\colon X \to [0, 2]$, $\varphi(x) = |x|$, is continuous and constant on classes, so by Theorem 3 it induces a continuous $\bar\varphi\colon X/{\sim} \to [0, 2]$ with $\bar\varphi \circ q = \varphi$. Let $s\colon [0, 2] \to X/{\sim}$ be $q$ restricted to $[0, 2]$, that is, $s = q \circ i$ with $i$ the inclusion of the subspace; it is continuous as a composite of continuous maps. Then $\bar\varphi(s(t)) = |t| = t$ for $t \in [0, 2]$. And for a class $[x]$, $s(\bar\varphi([x])) = q(|x|) = [|x|] = [x]$ because $|x| \sim x$. So $s$ and $\bar\varphi$ are inverse continuous bijections, and $X/{\sim} \cong [0, 2]$. $\blacksquare$

prompt: Let $L = (0, 1) \times \{5\}$ be a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^2$ (whose topology is the product topology of two copies of the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}$). Prove that $L$ is homeomorphic to the open interval $(0, 1)$.
hint: Use Proposition 1 and Theorem 2 to show that $t \mapsto (t, 5)$ and the first projection are continuous maps.

The inverse is the restriction of the continuous projection $\pi_1$ to a subspace, which is continuous because the inclusion of the subspace followed by $\pi_1$ is a composite of continuous maps. So $h$ is a homeomorphism. $\blacksquare$
