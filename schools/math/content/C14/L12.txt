@article
Does every continuous map of a disk to itself fix some point? Stir a cup of coffee: after the motion stops, some particle is back where it started. The proof needs one computation of a fundamental group: that of the circle. This is an introduction; the full theory of covering spaces comes in the graduate course on algebraic topology.

### The covering map

Let $p\colon \mathbb{R} \to S^1$, $p(t) = e^{2\pi i t}$. Then $p(t) = p(t')$ if and only if $t - t' \in \mathbb{Z}$, and $p(a + b) = p(a) p(b)$. For $z, w \in S^1$ with $w \ne -z$ put $\theta(z, w) = \operatorname{Arg}(w/z)/2\pi \in (-\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$, with $\operatorname{Arg}$ the principal argument, continuous on $\mathbb{C} \setminus (-\infty, 0]$. Then $\theta$ is continuous, $\theta(z, z) = 0$ and $p(\theta(z, w)) = w/z$.

**Lemma 1 (uniqueness).** If $Y$ is connected and $a, b\colon Y \to \mathbb{R}$ are continuous with $p \circ a = p \circ b$, and $a(y_0) = b(y_0)$ for some $y_0$, then $a = b$. *Proof.* $a - b$ is continuous and integer valued, so constant ($\mathbb{Z}$ is discrete and $Y$ connected); it vanishes at $y_0$. $\blacksquare$

**Lemma 2 (path lifting).** For a path $\gamma\colon I \to S^1$ and $x_0$ with $p(x_0) = \gamma(0)$ there is a unique continuous $\tilde\gamma\colon I \to \mathbb{R}$ with $p \circ \tilde\gamma = \gamma$ and $\tilde\gamma(0) = x_0$.

*Proof.* $\gamma$ is uniformly continuous (Week 7 practice), so there is $N$ with $|\gamma(s) - \gamma(s')| < 2$ whenever $|s - s'| \le 1/N$; then $\gamma(s') \ne -\gamma(s)$. With $t_k = k/N$ define inductively, for $s \in [t_{k-1}, t_k]$, $\tilde\gamma(s) = \tilde\gamma(t_{k-1}) + \theta(\gamma(t_{k-1}), \gamma(s))$. This is continuous, the definitions agree at the $t_k$ (because $\theta(z, z) = 0$), and $p(\tilde\gamma(s)) = \gamma(t_{k-1}) \cdot \gamma(s)/\gamma(t_{k-1}) = \gamma(s)$ by induction. Uniqueness is Lemma 1. $\blacksquare$

**Lemma 3 (homotopy lifting).** For continuous $F\colon I \times I \to S^1$ and $y_0$ with $p(y_0) = F(0, 0)$ there is a unique continuous $\tilde F\colon I \times I \to \mathbb{R}$ with $p \circ \tilde F = F$ and $\tilde F(0, 0) = y_0$.

*Proof.* Uniqueness is Lemma 1. For existence choose $N$ with $|F(a) - F(b)| < 2$ whenever $\|a - b\|_\infty \le 1/N$, and cut $I \times I$ into $N^2$ squares, taken in the order of rows then columns. On a square $Q$ with lower left corner $c$, $\ell_Q(a) = \lambda + \theta(F(c), F(a))$, for a real $\lambda$ with $p(\lambda) = F(c)$, is continuous with $p \circ \ell_Q = F$. The part of $Q$ meeting earlier squares is empty (first square) or the union of its left and bottom edges (or one of them), which is connected and contains $c$. Choose $\lambda$ so that $\ell_Q(c)$ equals the already defined value $\tilde F(c)$ (for the first square, $\lambda = y_0$). The already defined $\tilde F$ and $\ell_Q$ are two lifts of $F$ on that connected set, agreeing at $c$, so they agree there (Lemma 1). By the pasting lemma the lifts of the squares fit together. $\blacksquare$

### The fundamental group of the circle

For a loop $\gamma$ at $1 \in S^1$ let $\deg \gamma = \tilde\gamma(1) \in \mathbb{Z}$, where $\tilde\gamma$ is the lift with $\tilde\gamma(0) = 0$ (an integer since $p(\tilde\gamma(1)) = 1$).

**Theorem 4.** $\deg$ induces an isomorphism $\pi_1(S^1, 1) \to \mathbb{Z}$.

*Proof.* *Well defined.* Let $F$ be a path homotopy from $\gamma_0$ to $\gamma_1$ (so $F(0, t) = F(1, t) = 1$) and $\tilde F$ its lift with $\tilde F(0, 0) = 0$. The maps $t \mapsto \tilde F(0, t)$ and $t \mapsto \tilde F(1, t)$ are lifts of the constant path $1$, hence integer valued and constant, so $\tilde F(1, 0) = \tilde F(1, 1)$. By uniqueness $s \mapsto \tilde F(s, 0)$ is the lift of $\gamma_0$ from $0$ and $s \mapsto \tilde F(s, 1)$ the lift of $\gamma_1$ from $\tilde F(0, 1) = 0$. So $\deg \gamma_0 = \deg \gamma_1$. *Homomorphism.* Lift $\gamma$ from $0$ to $m = \deg \gamma$, and $\delta$ from $0$ to $n$. Then following $\tilde\gamma$ by $m + \tilde\delta$ lifts $\gamma * \delta$ from $0$ and ends at $m + n$. *Surjective.* The loop $s \mapsto e^{2\pi i n s}$ lifts to $s \mapsto ns$, of degree $n$. *Injective.* If $\deg \gamma = 0$ then $\tilde\gamma$ is a loop in $\mathbb{R}$ at $0$, and $H(s, t) = (1 - t)\tilde\gamma(s)$ is a path homotopy to the constant loop; applying $p$ gives $\gamma \simeq_p e_1$. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary 5.** There is no retraction of the closed disk $D^2$ onto its boundary $S^1$. *Proof.* If $r\colon D^2 \to S^1$ were continuous with $r|_{S^1} = \mathrm{id}$, then by Week 11 (retraction example) $\pi_1(S^1, 1) \to \pi_1(D^2, 1)$ would be injective; but $\pi_1(D^2, 1)$ is trivial (convexity) and $\pi_1(S^1, 1) \cong \mathbb{Z}$. $\blacksquare$

### Winding numbers by program

For a closed polygon with vertices $z_k$ avoiding the origin whose edges miss the origin, $\operatorname{atan2}(\text{cross}, \text{dot})$ is $2\pi\theta(z_k/|z_k|, z_{k+1}/|z_{k+1}|)$, and the sum is $2\pi$ times the degree (the winding number around $0$):

```python
import math


def winding_number(points):
    """Winding number around the origin of a closed polygon (vertex list)."""
    total = 0.0
    n = len(points)
    for k in range(n):
        x1, y1 = points[k]
        x2, y2 = points[(k + 1) % n]
        cross = x1 * y2 - y1 * x2
        dot = x1 * x2 + y1 * y2
        total += math.atan2(cross, dot)
    return round(total / (2 * math.pi))


print(winding_number([(1, 1), (-1, 1), (-1, -1), (1, -1)]))
print(winding_number([(2, 1), (4, 1), (4, 3), (2, 3)]))
```

It prints `1` and `0`. A computation like this checks cases; Theorem 4 explains why the answer is an integer invariant under deformation.

@example
title: The two-dimensional Brouwer fixed point theorem
problem: Prove that every continuous $f\colon D^2 \to D^2$, with $D^2 = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : |x| \le 1\}$, has a fixed point.
step: Suppose $f(x) \ne x$ for all $x$. Put $u(x) = x - f(x) \ne 0$ and define $r(x) = x + t(x) u(x)$, the point where the ray from $f(x)$ through $x$ leaves the disk, where $t(x) \ge 0$ solves $|x + t u|^2 = 1$.
step: Expanding gives $|u|^2 t^2 + 2 \langle x, u \rangle t + |x|^2 - 1 = 0$, so $t(x) = \bigl(-\langle x, u \rangle + \sqrt{\langle x, u \rangle^2 + |u|^2 (1 - |x|^2)}\bigr)/|u|^2$. The radicand is $\ge 0$ because $|x| \le 1$, and $|u| > 0$, so $t$ and hence $r$ are continuous, with $|r(x)| = 1$.
step: $r$ fixes the boundary. If $|x| = 1$, then $\langle x, u \rangle = 1 - \langle x, f(x) \rangle \ge 1 - |f(x)| \ge 0$. If $\langle x, u \rangle = 0$, then $\langle x, f(x) \rangle = 1$ with $|x| = 1$ and $|f(x)| \le 1$, which forces $f(x) = x$ (equality in the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality), excluded by assumption; so $\langle x, u \rangle > 0$. The radicand is then $\langle x, u \rangle^2$ and $t(x) = 0$, i.e. $r(x) = x$.
step: So $r\colon D^2 \to S^1$ is a retraction, contradicting Corollary 5. Hence $f$ has a fixed point.
answer: Every continuous self-map of the closed disk has a fixed point.

@example
title: The fundamental theorem of algebra
problem: Prove that every nonconstant polynomial $p(z) = z^n + a_{n-1} z^{n-1} + \dots + a_0$ ($n \ge 1$, $a_k \in \mathbb{C}$) has a root in $\mathbb{C}$.
step: Suppose $p$ has no root. For $0 \le r \le R$ let $g_r(s) = q_r(s)/|q_r(s)|$ with $q_r(s) = p(re^{2\pi i s})/p(r)$. Each $g_r$ is a loop at $1 \in S^1$ and $(s, r) \mapsto g_r(s)$ is continuous.
step: This is a path homotopy from $g_R$ to $g_0$, and $g_0 \equiv 1$ is constant. By Theorem 4, $\deg g_R = 0$ for every $R$.
step: Take $R > \max(1, |a_0| + \dots + |a_{n-1}|)$. For $\tau \in [0, 1]$ let $p_\tau(z) = z^n + \tau(a_{n-1} z^{n-1} + \dots + a_0)$. On $|z| = R$, $|\tau(a_{n-1} z^{n-1} + \dots + a_0)| \le (\sum |a_k|) R^{n-1} < R^n = |z^n|$, so $p_\tau$ has no zero on the circle $|z| = R$.
step: The loops $h_\tau(s) = (p_\tau(Re^{2\pi i s})/p_\tau(R))/|\cdot|$ at $1$ vary continuously with $\tau$, so they are path homotopic, with $h_1 = g_R$ and $h_0(s) = e^{2\pi i n s}$, which has degree $n$. So $\deg g_R = n \ne 0$, contradicting the previous degree $0$.
answer: Every nonconstant complex polynomial has a root.

@mistakes
- Taking steps so large in the lifting construction that consecutive points can be antipodal: the principal argument $\theta(z, w)$ is not defined, or not continuous, when $w = -z$.
- Assuming Brouwer's theorem for a domain with a hole: the rotation by a quarter turn of the annulus $1 \le |z| \le 2$ has no fixed point.
- Forgetting that the fixed point theorem needs the closed disk: $f(z) = (z + 1)/2$ maps the open disk to itself with no fixed point.
- Concluding that every loop in $S^1$ is trivial because $S^1$ is a subset of the contractible plane: homotopy must take place inside $S^1$.

model:
```python
import math


def zeros_inside(coeffs, r, m=720):
    """Roots of the polynomial in |z| < r, via the winding number."""
    images = []
    for k in range(m):
        angle = 2 * math.pi * k / m
        z = complex(r * math.cos(angle), r * math.sin(angle))
        value = 0j
        for c in reversed(coeffs):
            value = value * z + c
        images.append(value)
    total = 0.0
    for k in range(m):
        a, b = images[k], images[(k + 1) % m]
        cross = a.real * b.imag - a.imag * b.real
        dot = a.real * b.real + a.imag * b.imag
        total += math.atan2(cross, dot)
    return round(total / (2 * math.pi))
```
hint: Evaluate the polynomial at each sample with Horner's rule (start from the highest coefficient), then accumulate the angle between consecutive values, wrapping the last back to the first.
solution: The image of the circle $|z| = r$ under the polynomial is a closed curve avoiding $0$, and its winding number around $0$ equals the number of roots inside the disk, counted with multiplicity (this is the argument principle of complex analysis; this item computes the winding number numerically and does not prove that statement). Summing the principal angle increments with `atan2(cross, dot)` computes it as long as consecutive samples are less than a half turn apart, which holds for `m = 720` and the degrees used here.
