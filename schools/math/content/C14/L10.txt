@article
Which topologies come from a metric? Metric spaces are Hausdorff, normal and first countable, so a space lacking one of these is not metrizable. Can we find conditions that suffice? The answer, in outline, is a theorem of Urysohn. Then we meet the most famous compact metric space that is nowhere an interval: the Cantor set.

### Urysohn's metrization theorem (outline)

**Theorem 1 (Urysohn metrization theorem).** Every second countable regular space is metrizable.

This is a theorem we outline rather than prove completely. *Outline.* (1) A second countable regular space is normal (the Lindelof property of second countable spaces is used to separate disjoint closed sets by countable families of neighborhoods). (2) Let $\{B_n\}$ be a countable basis. For each pair $(n, m)$ with $\operatorname{Cl} B_n \subseteq B_m$, Urysohn's lemma gives a continuous $f_{nm}\colon X \to [0, 1]$ equal to $1$ on $\operatorname{Cl} B_n$ and $0$ outside $B_m$. This is a countable family $\{f_k\}$. If $x \in U$ with $U$ open, regularity yields $B_n, B_m$ with $x \in B_n$, $\operatorname{Cl} B_n \subseteq B_m \subseteq U$, so some $f_k$ is $1$ at $x$ and $0$ off $U$. (3) Define $F\colon X \to [0, 1]^{\mathbb{N}}$ by $F(x) = (f_k(x))_k$ and give $[0, 1]^{\mathbb{N}}$ the metric $D(s, t) = \sum_k 2^{-k} |s_k - t_k|$. The previous step shows $F$ is injective, continuous, and maps open sets onto open subsets of $F(X)$. So $X$ is homeomorphic to a subspace of a metric space, hence metrizable.

Conversely, metrizable spaces are regular and first countable. Metrization shows a space "has distances" without exhibiting them. Consequences for counterexamples:

- A separable metrizable space is second countable (balls of rational radius around points of a countable dense set form a basis). So the **Sorgenfrey line**, which is separable but not second countable, is not metrizable even though it is regular.
- The line with two origins and an infinite cofinite space are not Hausdorff, hence not metrizable.

### The Cantor set

Let $C_0 = [0, 1]$. Form $C_{n+1}$ from $C_n$ by removing the open middle third of each of its intervals. Then $C_n$ is a union of $2^n$ disjoint closed intervals of length $3^{-n}$, and the **Cantor set** is $C = \bigcap_n C_n$. So $C_1 = [0, \tfrac13] \cup [\tfrac23, 1]$, and the total length of $C_n$ is $(2/3)^n$.

**Theorem 2 (ternary description).** $x \in C$ if and only if $x = \sum_{i \ge 1} a_i 3^{-i}$ for some $a_i \in \{0, 2\}$.

*Proof.* By induction, the intervals of $C_n$ are exactly $[s, s + 3^{-n}]$ with $s = \sum_{i \le n} a_i 3^{-i}$, $a_i \in \{0, 2\}$: such an interval has left third $[s, s + 3^{-n-1}]$ (digit $a_{n+1} = 0$) and right third $[s + 2 \cdot 3^{-n-1}, s + 3^{-n}]$ (digit $a_{n+1} = 2$), and the middle third is removed. If $x = \sum a_i 3^{-i}$ with digits in $\{0, 2\}$, then $x - s_n = \sum_{i > n} a_i 3^{-i} \in [0, 3^{-n}]$ (since $\sum_{i > n} 2 \cdot 3^{-i} = 3^{-n}$), so $x$ lies in an interval of $C_n$ for each $n$, and $x \in C$. Conversely let $x \in C$. The intervals of $C_n$ are pairwise disjoint, so $x$ lies in exactly one for each $n$, and the one at level $n + 1$ lies inside the one at level $n$; hence the digit strings are consistent and define $(a_i)$ with $x \in \bigcap_n [s_n, s_n + 3^{-n}] = \{\sum a_i 3^{-i}\}$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 3.** The Cantor set is (a) compact, (b) uncountable, (c) totally disconnected (its only connected subsets are points and $\emptyset$), (d) perfect (no isolated points), and (e) of total length zero.

*Proof.* (a) Each $C_n$ is closed, so $C$ is closed and bounded, hence compact (Heine-Borel). (b) Distinct digit sequences in $\{0, 2\}$ give distinct numbers: if the first difference is at place $k$, the numbers differ by at least $2 \cdot 3^{-k} - 3^{-k} = 3^{-k} > 0$. So $C$ is in bijection with $\{0, 2\}^{\mathbb{N}}$, which is uncountable by Cantor's diagonal argument. (c) Let $x < y$ in $C$ and choose $n$ with $3^{-n} < y - x$. They lie in different intervals of $C_n$, so a removed open interval $(g, h)$ lies between them; any $m \in (g, h)$ is not in $C$, and $C \cap (-\infty, m)$ and $C \cap (m, \infty)$ form a separation of $C$ with $x$ and $y$ in different parts. So no connected subset of $C$ contains two points (Week 5). (d) Given $x \in C$ and $n$, the endpoints of the interval of $C_n$ containing $x$ belong to $C$ (their digit strings end in all $0$s or all $2$s), and at least one differs from $x$ and is within $3^{-n}$ of it. (e) $C \subseteq C_n$ and $C_n$ has total length $(2/3)^n \to 0$. $\blacksquare$

The map $\{0, 2\}^{\mathbb{N}} \to C$, $(a_i) \mapsto \sum a_i 3^{-i}$ is a homeomorphism when $\{0, 2\}^{\mathbb{N}}$ carries the product topology of discrete factors (its basic sets fix finitely many digits and map to the sets $C \cap [s, s + 3^{-n}]$, which are open in $C$ and form a neighborhood base). We do not give all details here.

### Testing membership

Because $C$ is self-similar ($x \in C$ with $x \le \tfrac13$ iff $3x \in C$, and with $x \ge \tfrac23$ iff $3x - 2 \in C$, by shifting digits), membership of a rational number can be decided exactly by the map $x \mapsto 3x \bmod$ the outer thirds. Rationals have finitely many iterates, so the orbit repeats:

```python
from fractions import Fraction


def in_cantor(x):
    """True if the rational x in [0, 1] lies in the Cantor set."""
    seen = set()
    while x not in seen:
        seen.add(x)
        if x <= Fraction(1, 3):
            x = 3 * x
        elif x >= Fraction(2, 3):
            x = 3 * x - 2
        else:
            return False
    return True


print(in_cantor(Fraction(1, 3)), in_cantor(Fraction(3, 10)), in_cantor(Fraction(1, 2)))
```

It prints `True True False`: $\tfrac13 = 0.0222\ldots_3$ and $\tfrac{3}{10} = 0.0220022002200\ldots_3$ have only digits $0$ and $2$ in a suitable expansion, while $\tfrac12 = 0.111\ldots_3$ does not. This tests rational points; it says nothing about irrational ones.

@example
title: A function separating two parts of the Cantor set
problem: Let $A = C \cap [0, \tfrac13]$ and $B = C \cap [\tfrac23, 1]$. Show $A$ and $B$ are disjoint closed subsets of $C$, and find a continuous $f\colon C \to [0, 1]$ with $f = 0$ on $A$ and $f = 1$ on $B$ that takes only the values $0$ and $1$.
step: $A$ and $B$ are closed in $C$ (intersections of $C$ with closed intervals) and disjoint (the intervals are). Also $C \subseteq C_1 = [0, \tfrac13] \cup [\tfrac23, 1]$, so $C = A \cup B$.
step: $C$ is a metric space, so Week 9's Theorem 2 gives $f(x) = d(x, A)/(d(x, A) + d(x, B))$, continuous with $f = 0$ on $A$ and $f = 1$ on $B$.
step: Every $x \in C$ lies in $A$ or $B$, so $f(x) \in \{0, 1\}$ automatically. Hence $f$ is the indicator function of $B$ and is continuous because $A$ and $B$ are both open in $C$ (complements of each other).
step: Contrast with $[0, 1]$, where any such $f$ must take every value in $[0, 1]$ because $[0, 1]$ is connected: totally disconnected spaces admit $\{0, 1\}$-valued Urysohn functions.
answer: $f = 0$ on $C \cap [0, \tfrac13]$ and $f = 1$ on $C \cap [\tfrac23, 1]$.

@mistakes
- Thinking the Cantor set is countable because it consists of endpoints: the endpoints of removed intervals are countable, but $C$ also contains points like $\tfrac14$ and uncountably many irrationals.
- Concluding that a set with total length zero is small in every sense: $C$ is uncountable and has the cardinality of $[0, 1]$.
- Using the metrization theorem without second countability: the Sorgenfrey line is regular and separable but not metrizable.
- Forgetting "regular" includes T1: a second countable space with the indiscrete topology satisfies the separation condition vacuously but is not metrizable.

model:
```python
from fractions import Fraction


def cantor_interval(x, n):
    """Interval of the n-th Cantor stage containing x, or None."""
    left = Fraction(0)
    width = Fraction(1)
    for _ in range(n):
        width /= 3
        if x <= Fraction(1, 3):
            x = 3 * x
        elif x >= Fraction(2, 3):
            left += 2 * width
            x = 3 * x - 2
        else:
            return None
    return (left, left + width)
```
hint: Shrink the width by a factor of $3$ at every step, and add $2 \cdot$ width to the left end whenever the digit is $2$.
solution: Each step reads one ternary digit of the point and zooms in by a factor of $3$. A digit $2$ moves the left end of the current interval to the right by two thirds of the current width, and a point in the open middle third means the point was removed at that stage, so the answer is `None`. With $n = 0$ no step is made and the interval is $[0, 1]$. The point $5/27$ survives one step (its first digit is $0$) but its image $5/9$ lies in the removed middle third at the second step.

model:
```python
from fractions import Fraction


def gaps(n):
    """Open intervals removed at step n of the Cantor construction."""
    intervals = [(Fraction(0), Fraction(1))]
    for _ in range(n - 1):
        nxt = []
        for a, b in intervals:
            third = (b - a) / 3
            nxt.append((a, a + third))
            nxt.append((b - third, b))
        intervals = nxt
    return [(a + (b - a) / 3, b - (b - a) / 3) for a, b in intervals]
```
hint: Build the closed intervals of $C_{n-1}$ by splitting each into its outer thirds $n - 1$ times, then take the middle third of each.
solution: Start with $[0, 1]$ and replace each interval by its two outer thirds $n - 1$ times to get the intervals of $C_{n-1}$, in increasing order. The gaps removed at step $n$ are the middle thirds of those intervals. The $2^{n-1}$ gaps each have length $3^{-n}$, so the total removed at step $n$ is $2^{n-1}/3^n$, which is $8/81$ for $n = 4$.
