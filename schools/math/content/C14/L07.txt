@article
Last week $[0, 1]$ was compact by a supremum argument. Does the same hold for the square $[0, 1]^2$, or for a cube of any dimension? Checking covers of a square directly would be painful. The tube lemma turns it into one-dimensional arguments.

### Finite products

**Theorem 1.** If $X$ and $Y$ are compact, then $X \times Y$ is compact.

*Proof.* Let $\mathcal{A}$ be an open cover of $X \times Y$. Fix $x_0 \in X$. The slice $\{x_0\} \times Y$ is homeomorphic to $Y$ (via $y \mapsto (x_0, y)$), hence compact, so finitely many members $A_1, \dots, A_n$ of $\mathcal{A}$ cover it. Their union $N$ is open and contains $\{x_0\} \times Y$. By the tube lemma there is an open $W_{x_0} \ni x_0$ with $W_{x_0} \times Y \subseteq N$; so $W_{x_0} \times Y$ is covered by finitely many members of $\mathcal{A}$. The sets $W_x$ cover $X$, so $X = W_{x_1} \cup \dots \cup W_{x_m}$. Then $X \times Y$ is covered by the finitely many members used for each $W_{x_j} \times Y$. $\blacksquare$

By induction, any finite product of compact spaces is compact. (For infinite products the corresponding statement is Tychonoff's theorem, which we do not prove here.)

**Theorem 2 (Heine-Borel).** A subset $K \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is compact if and only if it is closed and bounded.

*Proof.* ($\Rightarrow$) $\mathbb{R}^n$ is Hausdorff, so $K$ is closed (Week 6, Theorem 2). The open balls $B(0, m)$, $m \ge 1$, cover $K$, so finitely many do, and $K$ is bounded. ($\Leftarrow$) Choose $R$ with $K \subseteq [-R, R]^n$. This cube is a product of $n$ compact intervals, so it is compact by Theorem 1 and induction (the product topology on $\mathbb{R}^n$ is the metric topology). Then $K$ is a closed subset of a compact space and so is compact. $\blacksquare$

### Compact metric spaces

A metric space $(X, d)$ is **sequentially compact** if every sequence has a convergent subsequence (with limit in $X$), **totally bounded** if for each $\varepsilon > 0$ finitely many open balls of radius $\varepsilon$ cover $X$, and **complete** if every Cauchy sequence converges.

**Theorem 3.** For a metric space $X$, compactness and sequential compactness are equivalent.

*Proof.* (compact $\Rightarrow$ sequentially compact) Let $(x_n)$ be a sequence without a convergent subsequence. Then each $x \in X$ has a radius $r_x > 0$ such that $B(x, r_x)$ contains $x_n$ for only finitely many $n$: otherwise every ball $B(x, 1/k)$ contains $x_n$ for infinitely many $n$, and we can choose indices $n_1 < n_2 < \cdots$ with $x_{n_k} \in B(x, 1/k)$, a subsequence converging to $x$. The balls $B(x, r_x)$ cover $X$; a finite subcover contains $x_n$ for only finitely many $n$ in total, which is absurd.

(sequentially compact $\Rightarrow$ compact) *Total boundedness.* If $X$ were not totally bounded, some $\varepsilon > 0$ would have no finite cover by $\varepsilon$-balls, so we could pick $x_1$, then $x_2 \notin B(x_1, \varepsilon)$, then $x_3 \notin B(x_1, \varepsilon) \cup B(x_2, \varepsilon)$, and so on. Then $d(x_i, x_j) \ge \varepsilon$ for $i \ne j$, so no subsequence is Cauchy, hence none converges. *Lebesgue number.* Let $\mathcal{U}$ be an open cover. We claim there is $\delta > 0$ such that every ball $B(x, \delta)$ lies in a member of $\mathcal{U}$. If not, for each $n$ there is a ball $B(x_n, 1/n)$ inside no member. Pass to a subsequence $x_{n_k} \to x$; pick $U \in \mathcal{U}$ and $r > 0$ with $B(x, r) \subseteq U$. For large $k$, $d(x_{n_k}, x) < r/2$ and $1/n_k < r/2$, so $B(x_{n_k}, 1/n_k) \subseteq B(x, r) \subseteq U$, a contradiction. *Finishing.* Cover $X$ by finitely many balls of radius $\delta$; each lies in a member of $\mathcal{U}$, which gives a finite subcover. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 4 (outline, not fully proved here).** A metric space is compact if and only if it is complete and totally bounded. Outline: a sequence in a totally bounded space has a Cauchy subsequence (cover by finitely many $2^{-k}$-balls, choose one containing infinitely many terms, and take a diagonal subsequence), and in a complete space a Cauchy subsequence converges; conversely a compact space is totally bounded (cover it by $\varepsilon$-balls and take a finite subcover), and a compact metric space is complete because a Cauchy sequence with a convergent subsequence converges.

Closed and bounded is not enough in a general metric space: an infinite set with the metric $d(x, y) = 1$ for $x \ne y$ is closed and bounded and has no convergent subsequence of a sequence of distinct points.

### Continuous bijections

**Theorem 5.** Let $f\colon X \to Y$ be a continuous bijection with $X$ compact and $Y$ Hausdorff. Then $f$ is a homeomorphism.

*Proof.* Let $C \subseteq X$ be closed. It is compact (Week 6, Theorem 1), so $f(C)$ is compact, hence closed in the Hausdorff space $Y$. Thus $(f^{-1})^{-1}(C) = f(C)$ is closed for every closed $C$, so $f^{-1}$ is continuous. $\blacksquare$

The same proof shows that a continuous surjection from a compact space onto a Hausdorff space is a closed map and hence a quotient map. Consequences: $[0, 1]/(0 \sim 1) \to S^1$ is a homeomorphism (a second proof of Week 4's example). On the other hand, $[0, 1)$ is not homeomorphic to $S^1$ at all: $S^1$ is compact (a continuous image of $[0, 1]$) and $[0, 1)$ is not, and compactness is a topological property. This completes the claim from Week 3 about $t \mapsto e^{2\pi i t}$ on $[0, 1)$.

@example
title: Deciding compactness in the plane
problem: Decide whether $A = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : x^2 + y^2 \le 4,\ xy \ge 1\}$ and $B = \{(x, y) : xy = 1\}$ are compact.
step: Both are subsets of $\mathbb{R}^2$, so Heine-Borel applies: compact means closed and bounded.
step: $A$ is closed: it is the intersection of the preimages of $(-\infty, 4]$ and $[1, \infty)$ under the continuous maps $x^2 + y^2$ and $xy$. It is bounded since $x^2 + y^2 \le 4$. So $A$ is compact (it is nonempty, containing $(1, 1)$).
step: $B$ is closed, as the preimage of $\{1\}$ under $xy$. It is unbounded: $(n, 1/n) \in B$ for every $n$.
step: So $B$ is not compact. Note that $B$ is closed but compactness fails for lack of boundedness.
answer: $A$ is compact; $B$ is not compact.

@example
title: Distance between disjoint closed sets in a compact space
problem: Let $(X, d)$ be a compact metric space and $A, B \subseteq X$ nonempty, closed and disjoint. Prove that $d(a, b) \ge m$ for all $a \in A$, $b \in B$, for some $m > 0$, and show this can fail without compactness.
step: $A$ and $B$ are closed subsets of a compact space, hence compact, so $A \times B$ is compact (Theorem 1).
step: The function $g(a, b) = d(a, b)$ is continuous on $A \times B$ (the metric is continuous in the product topology), so by the extreme value theorem it attains its minimum $m = d(a_0, b_0)$.
step: Since $A \cap B = \emptyset$, $a_0 \ne b_0$, so $m > 0$, and $d(a, b) \ge m$ for all $a \in A$, $b \in B$.
step: Without compactness: in $\mathbb{R}$ the sets $A = \{n : n \ge 2\}$ and $B = \{n + 1/n : n \ge 2\}$ are closed and disjoint, but $d(n, n + 1/n) = 1/n \to 0$.
answer: Disjoint closed sets in a compact metric space are at positive distance; closedness alone is not enough in $\mathbb{R}$.

@mistakes
- Believing closed and bounded implies compact in every metric space: it holds in $\mathbb{R}^n$ (Heine-Borel) and fails for infinite discrete metric spaces and for $\mathbb{Q} \cap [0, 1]$.
- Applying Theorem 5 without Hausdorff target or compact domain: the identity from the discrete to the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}$ is a continuous bijection that is not a homeomorphism.
- Proving sequential compactness by exhibiting one convergent subsequence of one sequence: it must hold for every sequence.
- Forgetting that an infinite product of compact spaces needs Tychonoff's theorem; the tube lemma argument only handles finite products.

@practice num
prompt: What is the smallest number of open intervals of length $\tfrac12$ (that is, balls of radius $\tfrac14$ in $\mathbb{R}$) needed to cover $[0, 1]$?
answer: 3
check: min(n for n in range(1, 20) if Fraction(n, 2) > 1)
hint: Open intervals do not contain their endpoints, so two intervals of length $\tfrac12$ cannot cover an interval of length $1$.
solution: One open interval of length $\tfrac12$ is too short. Two cannot work either: $[0, 1]$ is connected, so two open intervals covering it must overlap (otherwise they would separate it), and then their union is a single open interval of length less than $1$, which cannot contain $[0, 1]$. Three intervals suffice, for example $(-0.2, 0.3)$, $(0.25, 0.75)$ and $(0.7, 1.2)$, whose consecutive members overlap and whose union $(-0.2, 1.2)$ contains $[0, 1]$. So the answer is $3$.

@practice self
prompt: Let $(X, d)$ be a compact metric space and $f\colon X \to (Y, \rho)$ continuous. Prove that $f$ is uniformly continuous: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $\delta > 0$ such that $d(x, x') < \delta$ implies $\rho(f(x), f(x')) < \varepsilon$.
hint: Argue by contradiction and build two sequences $x_n, x_n'$ with $d(x_n, x_n') < 1/n$ whose images stay $\varepsilon$ apart; use sequential compactness.
solution: Suppose not. Then there is $\varepsilon > 0$ such that for every $n$ there are $x_n, x_n'$ with $d(x_n, x_n') < 1/n$ and $\rho(f(x_n), f(x_n')) \ge \varepsilon$. By Theorem 3, a subsequence $x_{n_k}$ converges to some $x \in X$. Then $d(x_{n_k}', x) \le d(x_{n_k}', x_{n_k}) + d(x_{n_k}, x) \to 0$, so $x_{n_k}' \to x$ too. By continuity of $f$ at $x$, $f(x_{n_k}) \to f(x)$ and $f(x_{n_k}') \to f(x)$, hence $\rho(f(x_{n_k}), f(x_{n_k}')) \to 0$, contradicting $\rho(f(x_{n_k}), f(x_{n_k}')) \ge \varepsilon$ for all $k$. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Negates uniform continuity correctly to get $\varepsilon$ and two sequences with $d(x_n, x_n') < 1/n$.
rubric: Extracts a convergent subsequence by sequential compactness and shows the second sequence has the same limit.
rubric: Uses continuity of $f$ at the limit to reach a contradiction.

@practice self
prompt: Let $(X, d)$ be a nonempty compact metric space and $f\colon X \to X$ a map with $d(f(x), f(y)) < d(x, y)$ for all $x \ne y$. Prove that $f$ has exactly one fixed point.
hint: Consider $g(x) = d(x, f(x))$. It is continuous, so it attains a minimum on $X$; compare $g(x_0)$ with $g(f(x_0))$.
solution: The map $f$ is continuous (it does not increase distances), so $g(x) = d(x, f(x))$ is continuous on the compact space $X$ and attains a minimum at some $x_0$. If $f(x_0) \ne x_0$, then $g(f(x_0)) = d(f(x_0), f(f(x_0))) < d(x_0, f(x_0)) = g(x_0)$ by the hypothesis applied to $x_0 \ne f(x_0)$, contradicting minimality. So $f(x_0) = x_0$. If $a \ne b$ were two fixed points, then $d(a, b) = d(f(a), f(b)) < d(a, b)$, which is absurd. $\blacksquare$ Compactness cannot be dropped: $f(x) = x + 1/x$ on $[1, \infty)$ has $d(f(x), f(y)) < d(x, y)$ for $x \ne y$ but no fixed point.
rubric: Shows $g$ is continuous and attains its minimum using compactness.
rubric: Derives the contradiction $g(f(x_0)) < g(x_0)$ if the minimum is positive.
rubric: Proves uniqueness separately.

@quiz mc
prompt: Which hypotheses make a continuous bijection $f\colon X \to Y$ automatically a homeomorphism?
