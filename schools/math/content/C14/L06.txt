@article
A continuous function on $[0, 1]$ has a largest value, but $f(x) = 1/x$ on $(0, 1]$ has none, and $f(x) = x$ on $(0, 1)$ attains neither extreme. What property of $[0, 1]$ is missing from $(0, 1)$? It is not about distances; it is about open covers.

### Definition and first examples

An **open cover** of a space $X$ is a family of open sets whose union is $X$. The space is **compact** if every open cover has a **finite subcover**. A subset $A \subseteq X$ is compact when it is compact in the subspace topology, which is equivalent to: every family of open subsets of $X$ whose union contains $A$ has a finite subfamily whose union contains $A$.

Every finite space is compact, and so is any space with finitely many open sets. The line $\mathbb{R}$ is not compact: $\{(-n, n)\}_{n \ge 1}$ has no finite subcover. The interval $(0, 1]$ is not compact: the cover $\{(1/n, 1]\}_{n \ge 1}$ has no finite subcover, because a finite subfamily is contained in its largest member $(1/N, 1]$, which misses small points. An infinite discrete space is not compact: use the singletons.

A space is **Hausdorff** if any two distinct points have disjoint open neighborhoods. Metric spaces are Hausdorff (take balls of radius half the distance).

### Basic theorems

**Theorem 1.** A closed subset $C$ of a compact space $K$ is compact.

*Proof.* Let $\{U_i\}$ be a cover of $C$ by sets open in $K$. Then $\{U_i\} \cup \{K \setminus C\}$ is an open cover of $K$, so finitely many of its members cover $K$. Dropping $K \setminus C$ (which misses $C$) leaves finitely many $U_i$ covering $C$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 2.** A compact subset $K$ of a Hausdorff space $X$ is closed.

*Proof.* Let $x \in X \setminus K$. For each $y \in K$ choose disjoint open sets $U_y \ni x$ and $V_y \ni y$. The sets $V_y$ cover $K$, so finitely many $V_{y_1}, \dots, V_{y_n}$ do. Then $U = U_{y_1} \cap \dots \cap U_{y_n}$ is an open neighborhood of $x$ disjoint from $V_{y_1} \cup \dots \cup V_{y_n} \supseteq K$. So $X \setminus K$ is open. $\blacksquare$

Hausdorff cannot be dropped: in the cofinite topology on an infinite set every subset is compact (see the first example below), including non-closed ones.

**Theorem 3.** If $f\colon X \to Y$ is continuous and $X$ is compact, then $f(X)$ is compact.

*Proof.* Given a cover of $f(X)$ by open sets $V_i$ of $Y$, the sets $f^{-1}(V_i)$ cover $X$; take a finite subcover $f^{-1}(V_{i_1}), \dots, f^{-1}(V_{i_n})$. Then $V_{i_1}, \dots, V_{i_n}$ cover $f(X)$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 4.** Every closed interval $[a, b]$ is compact.

*Proof.* Let $\mathcal{A}$ be a cover of $[a, b]$ by open subsets of $\mathbb{R}$, and let $S$ be the set of $x \in [a, b]$ such that $[a, x]$ is covered by finitely many members of $\mathcal{A}$. Then $a \in S$ and $S$ is bounded above by $b$; let $c = \sup S$. Choose $A \in \mathcal{A}$ containing $c$ and $\varepsilon > 0$ with $(c - \varepsilon, c + \varepsilon) \subseteq A$. There is $s \in S$ with $s > c - \varepsilon$. Finitely many members cover $[a, s]$; adding $A$ gives a finite family covering $[a, \min(c + \varepsilon/2, b)]$. If $c < b$ this puts a point larger than $c$ in $S$, impossible. So $c = b$ and $b \in S$. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary 5 (extreme value theorem).** If $X$ is compact and nonempty and $f\colon X \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous, then $f$ attains a maximum and a minimum.

*Proof.* $f(X)$ is compact (Theorem 3). It is closed (Theorem 2) and bounded (the cover $\{(-n, n)\}$ has a finite subcover). A nonempty closed bounded set contains its supremum and infimum. $\blacksquare$

### The tube lemma

**Tube lemma.** Let $X$ be a space, $Y$ a compact space, $x_0 \in X$, and $N$ an open subset of $X \times Y$ containing the slice $\{x_0\} \times Y$. Then there is an open $W \ni x_0$ in $X$ with $W \times Y \subseteq N$.

*Proof.* For each $y \in Y$ the point $(x_0, y) \in N$ lies in a basis set $U_y \times V_y \subseteq N$. The sets $V_y$ cover $Y$; by compactness choose $y_1, \dots, y_n$ with $Y = V_{y_1} \cup \dots \cup V_{y_n}$. Put $W = U_{y_1} \cap \dots \cap U_{y_n}$, open and containing $x_0$. For $(x, y) \in W \times Y$ pick $j$ with $y \in V_{y_j}$; since $x \in U_{y_j}$, $(x, y) \in U_{y_j} \times V_{y_j} \subseteq N$. $\blacksquare$

Compactness of $Y$ is essential: $N = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : |x| < 1/(1 + y^2)\}$ is open and contains $\{0\} \times \mathbb{R}$, but contains no strip $(-\varepsilon, \varepsilon) \times \mathbb{R}$.

**Corollary 6.** If $Y$ is compact, then $\pi_1\colon X \times Y \to X$ is a closed map. *Proof.* Let $C \subseteq X \times Y$ be closed and $x_0 \notin \pi_1(C)$. Then $N = (X \times Y) \setminus C$ is open and contains $\{x_0\} \times Y$. A tube $W \times Y \subseteq N$ gives a neighborhood $W$ of $x_0$ disjoint from $\pi_1(C)$. So $X \setminus \pi_1(C)$ is open. $\blacksquare$

@example
title: Every subset of an infinite cofinite space is compact
problem: Let $X$ be an infinite set with the cofinite topology and $A \subseteq X$ any subset. Prove that $A$ is compact. Why does this not contradict Theorem 2?
step: Let $\mathcal{U}$ be a family of open subsets of $X$ whose union contains $A$. If $A = \emptyset$, the empty subfamily works, so assume $A \ne \emptyset$.
step: Choose any $U_0 \in \mathcal{U}$ that meets $A$ (some member contains a point of $A$). Since $U_0$ is nonempty and open, $X \setminus U_0$ is finite, so $A \setminus U_0$ is finite, say $\{a_1, \dots, a_m\}$.
step: Choose $U_j \in \mathcal{U}$ containing $a_j$ for each $j$. Then $U_0, U_1, \dots, U_m$ cover $A$.
step: Take $A$ infinite with infinite complement, for instance $X = \mathbb{Z}$ and $A$ the even integers. It is compact but not closed, since closed sets are $X$ and finite sets. There is no contradiction: the cofinite topology on an infinite set is not Hausdorff, and Theorem 2 needs Hausdorff.
answer: All subsets of an infinite cofinite space are compact, and many are not closed.

@example
title: Closed and bounded is not enough outside the line
problem: Show that $A = \mathbb{Q} \cap [0, 1]$ is closed and bounded in $\mathbb{Q}$ but not compact.
step: Closed in $\mathbb{Q}$: $A = \mathbb{Q} \cap [0, 1]$ is the intersection of $\mathbb{Q}$ with a closed set of $\mathbb{R}$. Bounded: it lies in $[0, 1]$.
step: Let $c = \sqrt2/2$, irrational. For $n \ge 1$ put $U_n = \{q \in A : |q - c| > 1/n\}$, open in $A$.
step: The union of the $U_n$ is $A$: every $q \in A$ differs from $c$ by some positive amount, because $c \notin \mathbb{Q}$.
step: A finite subfamily is contained in $U_N$ for the largest index $N$. But $A$ contains a rational $q$ with $|q - c| < 1/N$ (rationals are dense), and $q \notin U_N$. So no finite subfamily covers $A$.
answer: $A$ is not compact. Heine-Borel (closed and bounded implies compact) needs the ambient space $\mathbb{R}^n$.

@mistakes
- Saying a space is compact because it is "bounded and closed" without checking the ambient space: $\mathbb{Q} \cap [0, 1]$ is not compact.
- Showing that one particular cover has a finite subcover: compactness needs a finite subcover for every open cover.
- Using Theorem 2 in a non-Hausdorff space, where compact sets need not be closed.
- Confusing the image with the preimage: the continuous image of a compact set is compact, but the preimage of a compact set need not be (a constant map from $\mathbb{R}$).

@practice num
prompt: For $n \ge 1$ let $U_n = (\tfrac{1}{n+1}, 2)$. These open sets cover $(0, 1]$. What is the smallest $m$ such that $U_1, \dots, U_m$ already cover the closed interval $[\tfrac{1}{100}, 1]$?
answer: 100
check: min(m for m in range(1, 1000) if Fraction(1, m + 1) < Fraction(1, 100))
hint: The family is increasing, so $U_1, \dots, U_m$ cover exactly the points of $U_m$; the left endpoint $\tfrac{1}{100}$ must lie in $U_m$.
solution: Since $U_1 \subseteq U_2 \subseteq \cdots$, the union of the first $m$ sets is $U_m = (\tfrac{1}{m+1}, 2)$. It contains $\tfrac{1}{100}$ exactly when $\tfrac{1}{m+1} < \tfrac{1}{100}$, i.e. $m + 1 > 100$, i.e. $m \ge 100$. So $m = 100$. For $(0, 1]$ no finite $m$ works, which is why it is not compact.

@practice self
prompt: Prove that $K = \{0\} \cup \{1/n : n \ge 1\}$ is compact as a subspace of $\mathbb{R}$, and that $L = \{1/n : n \ge 1\}$ is not.
hint: For $K$, one open set must contain $0$, and it then contains all but finitely many of the points $1/n$. For $L$, use singletons.
solution: Let $\mathcal{U}$ be a family of open subsets of $\mathbb{R}$ covering $K$. Some $U_0 \in \mathcal{U}$ contains $0$; since $U_0$ is open, it contains $(-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)$ for some $\varepsilon > 0$, hence every $1/n$ with $1/n < \varepsilon$, that is every $n > 1/\varepsilon$. Only finitely many points $1/n$ with $n \le 1/\varepsilon$ remain; choose one member of $\mathcal{U}$ containing each. These finitely many members together with $U_0$ cover $K$. For $L$: each point $1/n$ is the only point of $L$ in the open interval $(\tfrac{1}{n} - \delta_n, \tfrac{1}{n} + \delta_n)$ with $\delta_n = \tfrac{1}{2n(n+1)}$ (half the gap to the neighbors; the gap to $\tfrac{1}{n+1}$ is $\tfrac{1}{n(n+1)}$ and the gap to $\tfrac1{n-1}$ is larger). These intervals cover $L$, and none can be dropped because each contains a point of $L$ lying in no other. So no finite subfamily covers $L$. $\blacksquare$
rubric: For $K$, uses one open set around $0$ to capture all but finitely many points.
rubric: Completes the finite subcover by choosing a set for each remaining point.
rubric: For $L$, gives an explicit open cover in which each member contains exactly one point of $L$, so no proper subfamily covers.

@practice self
prompt: Let $X$ be a Hausdorff space and let $K, L \subseteq X$ be disjoint compact sets. Prove that there are disjoint open sets $U \supseteq K$ and $V \supseteq L$.
hint: First separate a single point $x \in K$ from the compact set $L$, as in the proof of Theorem 2; then use compactness of $K$.
solution: Fix $x \in K$. For each $y \in L$ choose disjoint open sets $A_y \ni x$ and $B_y \ni y$ (Hausdorff, $x \ne y$ as $K \cap L = \emptyset$). The sets $B_y$ cover $L$, so $L \subseteq B_{y_1} \cup \dots \cup B_{y_n}$ for some finite subfamily. Put $P_x = A_{y_1} \cap \dots \cap A_{y_n}$ and $Q_x = B_{y_1} \cup \dots \cup B_{y_n}$: they are open, disjoint, $x \in P_x$ and $L \subseteq Q_x$. Now the sets $P_x$ ($x \in K$) cover $K$, so $K \subseteq P_{x_1} \cup \dots \cup P_{x_m}$. Put $U = P_{x_1} \cup \dots \cup P_{x_m}$ and $V = Q_{x_1} \cap \dots \cap Q_{x_m}$. Then $U$ and $V$ are open, $K \subseteq U$ and $L \subseteq V$. Finally $U \cap V = \emptyset$: if $z \in P_{x_j} \cap V$, then $z \in P_{x_j} \cap Q_{x_j} = \emptyset$. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Separates a point from the compact set $L$ using a finite subcover and a finite intersection.
rubric: Uses compactness of $K$ to pass to finitely many points and takes the correct union and intersection.
rubric: Verifies disjointness of $U$ and $V$.
