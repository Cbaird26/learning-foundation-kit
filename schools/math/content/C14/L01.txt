@article
In calculus, a function $f$ is continuous when "nearby inputs give nearby outputs", and the $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ definition makes "nearby" precise with distances. But the proof that a composite of continuous functions is continuous never uses a number: it only uses the fact that the preimage of an open set is open. Topology keeps exactly that skeleton. What is the least structure on a set that still lets us say "open"?

### The definition

**Definition.** A **topology** on a set $X$ is a collection $\mathcal{T}$ of subsets of $X$, called the **open sets**, such that

1. $\emptyset \in \mathcal{T}$ and $X \in \mathcal{T}$;
2. the union of any family of members of $\mathcal{T}$ (finite or infinite) is in $\mathcal{T}$;
3. the intersection of any two (hence of finitely many) members of $\mathcal{T}$ is in $\mathcal{T}$.

The pair $(X, \mathcal{T})$ is a **topological space**. Standard examples:

- the **discrete** topology (every subset is open) and the **indiscrete** topology ($\mathcal{T} = \{\emptyset, X\}$);
- the **cofinite** topology on any set $X$: $U$ is open if $U = \emptyset$ or $X \setminus U$ is finite;
- the **metric topology** of a metric space $(X, d)$: $U$ is open if for every $x \in U$ there is $\varepsilon > 0$ with $B(x, \varepsilon) \subseteq U$. On $\mathbb{R}$ this is the **standard topology**.

Only *finite* intersections are required. In $\mathbb{R}$, $\bigcap_{n \ge 1} (-1/n, 1/n) = \{0\}$ is not open, although each interval is.

On $X = \{a, b, c\}$ the collection $\{\emptyset, \{a\}, \{a, b\}, X\}$ is a topology: the only unions and intersections of its members are its members again. The collection $\{\emptyset, \{a\}, \{b\}, X\}$ is not: $\{a\} \cup \{b\} = \{a, b\}$ is missing. Two topologies on one set need not combine well. On $X = \{1, 2, 3\}$ both $\{\emptyset, \{1\}, X\}$ and $\{\emptyset, \{2\}, X\}$ are topologies, but their union lacks $\{1, 2\}$. By contrast, the intersection of any family of topologies on $X$ is a topology (Practice).

### Finer and coarser

If $\mathcal{T}_1 \subseteq \mathcal{T}_2$ are topologies on $X$, then $\mathcal{T}_2$ is **finer** than $\mathcal{T}_1$ and $\mathcal{T}_1$ is **coarser**. The discrete topology is the finest and the indiscrete the coarsest. Every cofinite set of $\mathbb{R}$ is open in the standard topology, so the cofinite topology on $\mathbb{R}$ is strictly coarser than the standard one ($(0, 1)$ is standard-open but its complement is infinite).

### Bases

Listing all open sets is hopeless on $\mathbb{R}$. A **basis** is a small family from which every open set is built by unions.

**Theorem (basis criterion).** Let $\mathcal{B}$ be a collection of subsets of $X$. Then $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis for a topology on $X$ (meaning: the unions of subfamilies of $\mathcal{B}$ form a topology) if and only if

- (B1) every $x \in X$ lies in some member of $\mathcal{B}$, and
- (B2) if $B_1, B_2 \in \mathcal{B}$ and $x \in B_1 \cap B_2$, then there is $B_3 \in \mathcal{B}$ with $x \in B_3 \subseteq B_1 \cap B_2$.

*Proof.* Let $\mathcal{T}$ be the set of all unions of subfamilies of $\mathcal{B}$. Note that $U \in \mathcal{T}$ if and only if for every $x \in U$ there is $B \in \mathcal{B}$ with $x \in B \subseteq U$.

($\Leftarrow$) Assume (B1) and (B2). $\emptyset \in \mathcal{T}$ is the empty union, and $X \in \mathcal{T}$ by (B1). A union of members of $\mathcal{T}$ is a union of members of $\mathcal{B}$, so it is in $\mathcal{T}$. For $U, V \in \mathcal{T}$ and $x \in U \cap V$, choose $B_1, B_2 \in \mathcal{B}$ with $x \in B_1 \subseteq U$ and $x \in B_2 \subseteq V$; (B2) gives $B_3$ with $x \in B_3 \subseteq B_1 \cap B_2 \subseteq U \cap V$. So $U \cap V \in \mathcal{T}$.

($\Rightarrow$) If $\mathcal{T}$ is a topology, then $X \in \mathcal{T}$ is a union of members of $\mathcal{B}$, which gives (B1). For $B_1, B_2 \in \mathcal{B} \subseteq \mathcal{T}$, the set $B_1 \cap B_2$ is open, hence a union of members of $\mathcal{B}$, and one of them contains $x$. $\blacksquare$

The open intervals $(a, b)$ form a basis of the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}$, and the open balls form a basis of any metric topology. A topology can have many bases: the intervals with rational endpoints give the same topology on $\mathbb{R}$.

### Testing a finite collection

For a finite $X$, closure under pairwise unions and intersections implies closure under all unions and intersections (induction on the number of sets), so the axioms can be checked by a loop:

```python
def is_topology(X, T):
    """Check the topology axioms for a finite set X and a family T."""
    X = frozenset(X)
    T = {frozenset(U) for U in T}
    if frozenset() not in T or X not in T:
        return False
    if not all(U <= X for U in T):
        return False
    for U in T:
        for V in T:
            if U | V not in T or U & V not in T:
                return False
    return True


print(is_topology({1, 2, 3}, [set(), {1}, {2}, {1, 2}, {1, 2, 3}]))
print(is_topology({1, 2, 3}, [set(), {1}, {2}, {1, 2, 3}]))
```

This prints `True` and then `False`. A program checking small cases is evidence about those cases; the basis theorem and the proofs below are what cover infinite sets.

@example
title: The cofinite topology is a topology
problem: Let $X$ be any set and let $\mathcal{T}$ consist of $\emptyset$ and all $U \subseteq X$ with $X \setminus U$ finite. Prove that $\mathcal{T}$ is a topology on $X$.
step: Axiom 1. $\emptyset \in \mathcal{T}$ by definition. $X \setminus X = \emptyset$ is finite, so $X \in \mathcal{T}$.
step: Axiom 3 (two sets). Let $U, V \in \mathcal{T}$. If one of them is $\emptyset$, then $U \cap V = \emptyset \in \mathcal{T}$. Otherwise $X \setminus U$ and $X \setminus V$ are finite, and by De Morgan $X \setminus (U \cap V) = (X \setminus U) \cup (X \setminus V)$ is a union of two finite sets, hence finite.
step: Axiom 2. Let $\{U_i\}_{i \in I}$ be a family in $\mathcal{T}$. If every $U_i$ is empty, the union is $\emptyset$. Otherwise pick $j$ with $U_j \ne \emptyset$. Then $X \setminus \bigcup_i U_i = \bigcap_i (X \setminus U_i) \subseteq X \setminus U_j$, which is finite, so the complement of the union is finite (a subset of a finite set).
step: All three axioms hold, so $\mathcal{T}$ is a topology. The step with $U_j$ is where an infinite union is handled: one finite complement controls them all.
answer: The cofinite collection is a topology on every set $X$.

@example
title: The lower limit topology from a basis
problem: Show that $\mathcal{B} = \{[a, b) : a < b\}$ is a basis for a topology on $\mathbb{R}$, and that this topology is strictly finer than the standard topology.
step: (B1) Every $x \in \mathbb{R}$ lies in $[x, x + 1) \in \mathcal{B}$.
step: (B2) Let $x \in [a, b) \cap [c, d)$. Then $x \ge \max(a, c)$ and $x < \min(b, d)$, so $\max(a, c) < \min(b, d)$ and $[\max(a, c), \min(b, d)) \in \mathcal{B}$ contains $x$ and lies inside $[a, b) \cap [c, d)$.
step: By the basis criterion $\mathcal{B}$ generates a topology $\mathcal{T}_\ell$. It is finer than the standard topology: $(a, b) = \bigcup_{a < x < b} [x, b)$ is a union of members of $\mathcal{B}$.
step: It is strictly finer: $[0, 1)$ is open in $\mathcal{T}_\ell$ but not in the standard topology, since no interval $(-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)$ lies inside $[0, 1)$.
answer: $\mathcal{B}$ generates a topology strictly finer than the standard one (the lower limit topology).

@mistakes
- Requiring infinite intersections to be open: only finite intersections are required, and $\bigcap_n (-1/n, 1/n) = \{0\}$ shows why.
- Forgetting $\emptyset$ (or $X$) when listing a topology: a family of sets that is closed under unions and intersections but omits $\emptyset$ is not a topology.
- Demanding $B_1 \cap B_2 \in \mathcal{B}$ in the basis criterion: it is enough that each point of $B_1 \cap B_2$ lies in a smaller basis set inside $B_1 \cap B_2$.
- Thinking a union of two topologies on $X$ is a topology: the union may lack unions of its members.

```python
def is_basis(X, B):
    """Check the two basis conditions for a finite family B on X."""
    X = frozenset(X)
    B = [frozenset(b) for b in B]
    covered = frozenset().union(*B) if B else frozenset()
    if covered != X:
        return False
    for b1 in B:
        for b2 in B:
            common = b1 & b2
            for x in common:
                if not any(x in b3 and b3 <= common for b3 in B):
                    return False
    return True
```
hint: Loop over pairs of members and over the points of their intersection, and look for a member containing that point and contained in the intersection.
solution: Check (B1) by comparing the union of all members with `X`. For (B2), for every pair of members and every point of the intersection, search for a witness `B3`.
