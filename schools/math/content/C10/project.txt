material: **Starter code for the brute-force check** (fill in the group):

```python
import itertools


def canonical(coloring, group):
    """Least image of a coloring (a tuple) under the permutations in group."""
    return min(tuple(coloring[p[i]] for i in range(len(p))) for p in group)


def brute_force_count(group, k):
    n = len(group[0])
    return len({canonical(c, group)
                for c in itertools.product(range(k), repeat=n)})
```

@review
**Week 1: groups.** A group is a set with an associative operation, an identity and inverses. Identity and inverses are unique, cancellation holds, $(ab)^{-1} = b^{-1}a^{-1}$. The unit group $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$ consists of residues coprime to $n$ and has order $\varphi(n)$.

**Week 2: subgroups and cyclic groups.** One-step subgroup test ($ab^{-1} \in H$) and the finite test (closure). If $g$ has order $n$ then $g^k = e$ if and only if $n \mid k$, and $g^k$ has order $n/\gcd(n, k)$. Subgroups of cyclic groups are cyclic, one for each divisor of $n$; there are $\varphi(n)$ generators.

**Week 3: permutations.** Disjoint cycles commute; the order is the lcm of the cycle lengths; the sign is multiplicative and a $k$-cycle has sign $(-1)^{k-1}$; $|A_n| = n!/2$; $|D_n| = 2n$ with $srs = r^{-1}$.

**Week 4: cosets and Lagrange.** Left cosets partition $G$ into pieces of size $|H|$, so $|G| = [G:H]|H|$. Consequences: orders of elements divide $|G|$, groups of prime order are cyclic, Euler's theorem. The converse of Lagrange fails ($A_4$ has no subgroup of order 6).

**Week 5: normal subgroups.** $N$ is normal if $gNg^{-1} = N$ for all $g$, equivalently $gN = Ng$; coset multiplication on $G/N$ is well defined exactly then. Index-2 subgroups and centers are normal.

**Week 6: homomorphisms.** Kernels are normal, images are subgroups, injective if and only if the kernel is trivial. First isomorphism theorem: $G/\ker\varphi \cong \operatorname{im}\varphi$.

**Week 7: isomorphism theorems.** $HN/N \cong H/(H \cap N)$; $(G/N)/(K/N) \cong G/K$; the correspondence theorem; $|HN| = |H||N|/|H \cap N|$.

**Week 8: direct products and abelian groups.** The order of $(g, h)$ is the lcm; $\mathbb{Z}/m \times \mathbb{Z}/n$ is cyclic exactly when $\gcd(m, n) = 1$; every finite abelian group is a product of cyclic groups of prime-power order, and the number of abelian groups of order $\prod p_i^{e_i}$ is $\prod P(e_i)$.

**Week 9: actions.** Orbits partition $X$; stabilizers are subgroups; $|Gx| = [G : G_x]$; Cayley's theorem; the action on the cosets of $H$ has kernel $\bigcap_x xHx^{-1}$, a normal subgroup of $G$ inside $H$.

**Week 10: Burnside.** The number of orbits is the average of $|\operatorname{Fix}(g)|$; for colorings, $|\operatorname{Fix}(g)| = k^{c(g)}$.

**Week 11: conjugacy.** $|\operatorname{cl}(x)| = [G : C_G(x)]$; the class equation $|G| = |Z(G)| + \sum [G : C_G(g_i)]$; $p$-groups have nontrivial center; conjugacy classes of $S_n$ are cycle types.

**Week 12: Sylow.** Sylow $p$-subgroups exist, are conjugate, and $n_p \equiv 1 \pmod p$ with $n_p \mid m$. Use element counts or the action on Sylow subgroups to show a group is not simple.

**Self-check.** Can you prove, without notes: the subgroup test, Lagrange's theorem, that $G/N$ is a group, the first isomorphism theorem, the orbit-stabilizer theorem, Burnside's lemma, and the existence part of Sylow? Can you list the abelian groups of a given order and find $n_p$ for a given order?

**Common errors.** Reversing the order in $(ab)^{-1}$; assuming every divisor of $|G|$ is a subgroup order; calling a subgroup normal without checking conjugation; forgetting that the image of a homomorphism need not be normal; reading products of permutations left to right; counting only cycles of length at least 2 in Burnside's lemma; using $n_p \equiv 1 \pmod p$ without $n_p \mid m$.

@exam self
prompt: Let $G$ be a finite group and $p$ the smallest prime dividing $|G|$. Let $H$ be a subgroup of $G$ with $[G : H] = p$. Prove that $H$ is normal in $G$.
hint: Let $G$ act on the left cosets of $H$ by left multiplication and consider the kernel $K$ of the resulting homomorphism $G \to S_p$.
hint: Show that $[H : K]$ divides $(p-1)!$ and also divides $|G|$, and use the choice of $p$.
solution: $G$ acts on the $p$ left cosets of $H$ by left multiplication, giving a homomorphism $\varphi\colon G \to S_p$ with kernel $K = \bigcap_x xHx^{-1} \subseteq H$, which is normal in $G$. By the first isomorphism theorem $G/K$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of $S_p$, so $|G/K|$ divides $p!$ by Lagrange's theorem. Also $|G/K| = [G : H][H : K] = p\,[H : K]$. Hence $[H : K]$ divides $p!/p = (p-1)!$. On the other hand $[H : K]$ divides $|H|$, which divides $|G|$, so every prime factor of $[H : K]$ is a prime factor of $|G|$ and therefore at least $p$. But every prime factor of $(p-1)!$ is smaller than $p$. So $[H : K]$ has no prime factors, i.e. $[H : K] = 1$ and $H = K$, which is normal. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Sets up the action on cosets and identifies the kernel $K \subseteq H$ as normal in $G$.
rubric: Uses the first isomorphism theorem and Lagrange in $S_p$ to show $[H : K]$ divides $(p-1)!$.
rubric: Uses that $p$ is the smallest prime factor of $|G|$ to conclude $[H : K] = 1$, so $H = K$.

prompt: How many distinct bracelets can be made from $5$ beads of $3$ colors when two bracelets are the same if one can be obtained from the other by a rotation or a reflection (the symmetries of a regular pentagon)?
answer: 39
check: (3 ** 5 + 4 * 3 + 5 * 3 ** 3) // 10
hint: The group $D_5$ has order $10$: the identity, $4$ rotations with one $5$-cycle each, and $5$ reflections, each with two $2$-cycles and one fixed point.
hint: A reflection of a pentagon has $3$ cycles, so it fixes $3^3$ colorings.
solution: The identity fixes $3^5 = 243$ colorings, each of the $4$ nontrivial rotations fixes $3$ (one cycle), and each of the $5$ reflections fixes $3^3 = 27$ (cycles: two swapped pairs and one fixed bead). By Burnside's lemma the count is $(243 + 4 \cdot 3 + 5 \cdot 27)/10 = 390/10 = 39$.

model:
```python
def generate(gens):
    """Closure of the generators under composition, sorted."""
    n = len(gens[0])
    identity = tuple(range(n))
    group = {identity}
    frontier = [identity]
    while frontier:
        new = []
        for g in frontier:
            for h in gens:
                product = tuple(h[g[i]] for i in range(n))
                if product not in group:
                    group.add(product)
                    new.append(product)
        frontier = new
    return sorted(group)
```
hint: Start from the identity and keep multiplying by generators until no new element appears.
hint: In a finite group every inverse is a positive power, so products of the generators alone (no inverses) already give the whole group.
solution: Do a breadth-first search: keep the set of elements found so far and a frontier of new elements, multiply each frontier element by each generator, and add the products not seen before. The process stops when no new element appears. Because the group is finite, the inverse of a generator is a power of it, so the closure under products of the generators is the whole generated subgroup.
