@article
Lagrange's theorem says that a subgroup order divides $|G|$ but does not promise that a subgroup of each such order exists; $A_4$ has none of order $6$. The Sylow theorems repair this for prime powers: for the largest power of a prime dividing $|G|$ there is always a subgroup, and the number of such subgroups is tightly restricted. This is the main tool for studying groups of a given order.

### Statements

Let $G$ be a finite group of order $p^a m$ with $p$ prime, $a \ge 1$ and $p \nmid m$. A **Sylow $p$-subgroup** is a subgroup of order $p^a$. Write $n_p$ for the number of them.

**Theorem 1 (Sylow, 1872).** (1) A Sylow $p$-subgroup exists. (2) Every $p$-subgroup of $G$ is contained in a conjugate of a given Sylow $p$-subgroup $P$; in particular any two Sylow $p$-subgroups are conjugate. (3) $n_p = [G : N_G(P)]$ divides $m$, and $n_p \equiv 1 \pmod p$. Here $N_G(P) = \{g : gPg^{-1} = P\}$ is the normalizer.

**Two lemmas.** *Lemma A.* If a group $Q$ of order $p^b$ acts on a finite set $X$, then $|X| \equiv |X^Q| \pmod p$, where $X^Q$ is the set of fixed points. *Proof.* The orbits partition $X$, each orbit size divides $p^b$, and orbits of size greater than $1$ have size divisible by $p$. So $|X|$ equals the number of fixed points plus a multiple of $p$. $\blacksquare$ *Lemma B.* $\binom{p^a m}{p^a} \equiv m \pmod p$ when $p \nmid m$. *Proof.* In $\mathbb{F}_p[x]$ the identity $(1 + x)^p = 1 + x^p$ holds (the middle binomial coefficients are divisible by $p$), and repeating it gives $(1 + x)^{p^a} = 1 + x^{p^a}$. Hence $(1 + x)^{p^a m} = (1 + x^{p^a})^m$. The coefficient of $x^{p^a}$ on the left is $\binom{p^a m}{p^a}$; on the right, the only term giving $x^{p^a}$ is $m\,x^{p^a}$. $\blacksquare$

### Proofs

*Proof of (1).* Let $\Omega$ be the set of subsets of $G$ with $p^a$ elements. By Lemma B, $|\Omega| \equiv m \not\equiv 0 \pmod p$. $G$ acts on $\Omega$ by $g \cdot S = gS$. Since $p \nmid |\Omega|$, some orbit has size not divisible by $p$; pick $S$ in it and let $H = G_S$. Then $|G| = |GS|\,|H|$ and $p^a \mid |G|$ with $p \nmid |GS|$, so $p^a \mid |H|$. On the other hand, $H$ stabilizes $S$, so for a fixed $s \in S$ the map $h \mapsto hs$ is an injection $H \to S$, giving $|H| \le |S| = p^a$. So $|H| = p^a$. $\blacksquare$

*Proof of (2).* Let $Q$ be a $p$-subgroup and $P$ a Sylow $p$-subgroup. Then $Q$ acts on $G/P$ by left multiplication, and $|G/P| = m \not\equiv 0 \pmod p$. By Lemma A there is a fixed coset $gP$: $qgP = gP$ for all $q \in Q$, so $g^{-1}qg \in P$ and $g^{-1}Qg \subseteq P$, that is, $Q \subseteq gPg^{-1}$. If $Q$ is also a Sylow $p$-subgroup then $|Q| = |gPg^{-1}|$ and $Q = gPg^{-1}$. $\blacksquare$

*Proof of (3).* By (2), $G$ acts transitively by conjugation on the set of Sylow $p$-subgroups, and the stabilizer of $P$ is $N_G(P)$, so $n_p = [G : N_G(P)]$ by orbit-stabilizer. Since $P \le N_G(P)$, $[G : P] = [G : N_G(P)]\,[N_G(P) : P]$, so $n_p \mid [G:P] = m$. For the congruence, let $P$ act by conjugation on the set of Sylow $p$-subgroups. By Lemma A it suffices to show that $P$ is its only fixed point. If $Q$ is fixed, then $P \le N_G(Q)$. Now $P$ and $Q$ are both Sylow $p$-subgroups of $N_G(Q)$ (their order $p^a$ is the full $p$-part of $|N_G(Q)|$, as it divides $|G|$). By (2) applied inside $N_G(Q)$, they are conjugate there; but $Q$ is normal in $N_G(Q)$, so its only conjugate is $Q$, and $P = Q$. Hence $n_p \equiv 1 \pmod p$. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary 2 (Cauchy).** If a prime $p$ divides $|G|$, then $G$ has an element of order $p$. *Proof.* A Sylow $p$-subgroup $P$ has order $p^a > 1$. Take $x \ne e$ in $P$; its order is $p^j$ with $j \ge 1$ (Lagrange), and $x^{p^{j-1}}$ has order $p$. $\blacksquare$

### Using the theorems: is a group of order $N$ simple?

A group is **simple** if its only normal subgroups are $\{e\}$ and itself. A Sylow $p$-subgroup is normal exactly when $n_p = 1$ (since all are conjugate). The usual attacks are: (i) $n_p$ is forced to be $1$ by the congruence and divisibility; (ii) count elements: Sylow subgroups of prime order meet trivially, so $n_p$ of them have $n_p(p - 1)$ elements of order $p$; (iii) if $n_p = n > 1$, the conjugation action on the Sylow $p$-subgroups gives a homomorphism $G \to S_n$ with nontrivial image, and if $|G| > n!$ its kernel is a proper nontrivial normal subgroup. The method has limits: the alternating group $A_5$ has order $60$ and is simple, with $n_5 = 6$.

@example
title: Groups of order 15
problem: Show that every group $G$ of order $15$ is cyclic.
step: $15 = 3 \cdot 5$. For $p = 3$: $n_3 \mid 5$ and $n_3 \equiv 1 \pmod 3$; the divisors of $5$ are $1$ and $5$, and $5 \equiv 2 \pmod 3$, so $n_3 = 1$.
step: For $p = 5$: $n_5 \mid 3$ and $n_5 \equiv 1 \pmod 5$; the divisors of $3$ are $1$ and $3$, and $3 \not\equiv 1$, so $n_5 = 1$.
step: So $G$ has a unique, hence normal, subgroup $P$ of order $3$ and a unique, hence normal, subgroup $Q$ of order $5$. They intersect trivially (a common element has order dividing $\gcd(3, 5) = 1$), and $|PQ| = 15 = |G|$ by the product formula, so $PQ = G$.
step: Therefore $G \cong P \times Q \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$ (lesson 8), which is cyclic of order $15$ because $\gcd(3, 5) = 1$.
answer: The only group of order $15$ up to isomorphism is $\mathbb{Z}/15\mathbb{Z}$.

@example
title: No simple group of order 56
problem: Show that no group $G$ of order $56 = 2^3 \cdot 7$ is simple.
step: Look at $p = 7$: $n_7 \mid 8$ and $n_7 \equiv 1 \pmod 7$, so $n_7 \in \{1, 8\}$. If $n_7 = 1$, the Sylow $7$-subgroup is normal, proper and nontrivial, and we are done.
step: Suppose $n_7 = 8$. Each Sylow $7$-subgroup has prime order $7$, so two different ones intersect in $\{e\}$. The $8$ subgroups together contain $8 \cdot 6 = 48$ elements of order $7$.
step: That leaves $56 - 48 = 8$ elements that are not of order $7$. A Sylow $2$-subgroup has order $8$ and contains no element of order $7$, so it consists of exactly those $8$ elements. There is only one such set, so $n_2 = 1$ and the Sylow $2$-subgroup is normal, proper and nontrivial.
step: In both cases $G$ has a proper nontrivial normal subgroup, so $G$ is not simple.
answer: Every group of order $56$ has a normal Sylow $7$-subgroup or a normal Sylow $2$-subgroup.

@mistakes
- Reading $n_p \equiv 1 \pmod p$ without also using $n_p \mid m$. For order $90$ and $p = 3$ the number $4$ satisfies $4 \equiv 1 \pmod 3$ but does not divide $m = 10$, so $n_3 \in \{1, 10\}$.
- Using $|G|$ instead of $m$ in the divisibility: $n_p$ divides the index $m = |G|/p^a$, not $|G|$ itself (it also divides $|G|$, but this gives weaker information).
- Assuming that a Sylow subgroup is normal. It is normal exactly when $n_p = 1$; in $S_3$ there are three Sylow $2$-subgroups.
- Counting elements by multiplying $n_p$ by $p^a - 1$ when $a \ge 2$. Distinct Sylow subgroups of non-prime order can intersect nontrivially, so the element count $n_p(p - 1)$ is only valid for subgroups of prime order.

@practice num
prompt: A non-abelian group of order $21$ has $n_7 = 1$ and $n_3 = 7$. How many elements of order $3$ does it have?
answer: 14
check: 7 * (3 - 1)
hint: Each Sylow $3$-subgroup has prime order $3$, so any two intersect trivially.
solution: Each of the seven Sylow $3$-subgroups is cyclic of order $3$ and contains $2$ elements of order $3$. Distinct ones intersect trivially, so there are $7 \cdot 2 = 14$ elements of order $3$. The remaining $6$ non-identity elements have order $7$.

@practice self
prompt: Prove that no group of order $36$ is simple.
hint: Compute the possible values of $n_3$ and treat the case $n_3 \ne 1$ with the action of $G$ on its Sylow $3$-subgroups by conjugation.
hint: The action gives a homomorphism $G \to S_4$. Compare $|G| = 36$ with $|S_4| = 24$ to see that the kernel is nontrivial, and use $n_3 > 1$ to see that the kernel is not all of $G$.
solution: Here $36 = 2^2 \cdot 3^2$, and $n_3$ divides $4$ and is congruent to $1$ modulo $3$, so $n_3 \in \{1, 4\}$. If $n_3 = 1$, the Sylow $3$-subgroup is normal, proper and nontrivial. Suppose $n_3 = 4$. By Sylow's theorem (2), $G$ acts transitively on the four Sylow $3$-subgroups by conjugation, giving a homomorphism $\varphi\colon G \to S_4$. Its image is a subgroup of $S_4$, so $|\operatorname{im}\varphi| \le 24 < 36 = |G|$, and by the first isomorphism theorem $|\ker\varphi| = 36/|\operatorname{im}\varphi| \ge 36/24 > 1$, so the kernel is nontrivial. If $\ker\varphi = G$, then every element of $G$ would fix every Sylow $3$-subgroup under conjugation, so each Sylow $3$-subgroup would be normal, contradicting $n_3 = 4$. So $\ker\varphi$ is a proper nontrivial normal subgroup. Hence $G$ is not simple. $\blacksquare$

rubric: For $n_3 = 4$, defines the conjugation action on the Sylow $3$-subgroups and the homomorphism to $S_4$.
rubric: Shows the kernel is nontrivial because $36 > 24$, and proper because the action is nontrivial.
