@article
The symmetries of a square can send corner $1$ to corner $2$, $3$ or $4$, but they can never send a corner to the center. A group "acts" on the things it moves, and what it can reach from a given thing is an orbit. Counting orbits and stabilizers gives surprisingly strong information about the group itself.

### Group actions

**Definition.** An **action** of a group $G$ on a set $X$ is a rule $G \times X \to X$, $(g, x) \mapsto g \cdot x$, with $e \cdot x = x$ and $(gh) \cdot x = g \cdot (h \cdot x)$ for all $g, h \in G$, $x \in X$. For each $g$ the map $x \mapsto g \cdot x$ is a permutation of $X$ (its inverse is multiplication by $g^{-1}$), and $g \mapsto (x \mapsto g \cdot x)$ is a homomorphism $G \to \operatorname{Sym}(X)$; conversely every such homomorphism defines an action. Its kernel, $\{g : g \cdot x = x \text{ for all } x\}$, is a normal subgroup (the **kernel of the action**).

For $x \in X$, the **orbit** is $Gx = \{g \cdot x : g \in G\}$ and the **stabilizer** is $G_x = \{g \in G : g \cdot x = x\}$. For $g \in G$ the **fixed set** is $\operatorname{Fix}(g) = \{x : g \cdot x = x\}$.

**Examples.** (1) $S_n$ acts on $\{1, \dots, n\}$ by $\sigma \cdot i = \sigma(i)$. (2) $D_4$ acts on the four vertices of a square. (3) $G$ acts on itself by left multiplication, $g \cdot x = gx$. (4) $G$ acts on itself by conjugation, $g \cdot x = gxg^{-1}$ (lesson 11). (5) $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ acts on $\mathbb{Z}$ by $n \cdot x = x + 2n$, but $n \cdot x = x + n^2$ is not an action, since $(n + m)^2 \ne n^2 + m^2$.

### Orbits and stabilizers

**Theorem 1.** For an action of $G$ on $X$: (a) each stabilizer $G_x$ is a subgroup of $G$; (b) the orbits partition $X$.

*Proof.* (a) $e \in G_x$. If $g, h \in G_x$ then $(gh) \cdot x = g \cdot (h \cdot x) = g \cdot x = x$, and $g^{-1} \cdot x = g^{-1} \cdot (g \cdot x) = (g^{-1}g) \cdot x = x$. (b) Define $x \sim y$ if $y = g \cdot x$ for some $g$. It is reflexive ($g = e$), symmetric ($x = g^{-1} \cdot y$) and transitive ($z = h \cdot y = (hg) \cdot x$). The classes of this relation are the orbits. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 2 (orbit-stabilizer).** For an action of $G$ on $X$ and $x \in X$, the map $gG_x \mapsto g \cdot x$ is a well-defined bijection from the left cosets of $G_x$ onto the orbit $Gx$. Hence $|Gx| = [G : G_x]$, and if $G$ is finite, $|G| = |Gx|\,|G_x|$.

*Proof.* We have $g \cdot x = h \cdot x$ if and only if $h^{-1}g \cdot x = x$ (apply $h^{-1}$ to both sides, and conversely apply $h$), if and only if $h^{-1}g \in G_x$, if and only if $gG_x = hG_x$. Reading the chain forward shows the map is well defined and backward shows it is injective. It is onto $Gx$ by the definition of the orbit. The formula for finite $G$ is Lagrange's theorem. $\blacksquare$

For $D_4$ (order $8$) on the vertices of a square, the orbit of a vertex is all $4$ vertices, so the stabilizer has order $8/4 = 2$: the identity and the reflection through that vertex.

### Cayley's theorem and the action on cosets

**Theorem 3 (Cayley).** Every group $G$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of $\operatorname{Sym}(G)$. In particular a group of order $n$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of $S_n$.

*Proof.* Left multiplication $\lambda_g(x) = gx$ is a permutation of $G$ (inverse $\lambda_{g^{-1}}$), and $\lambda_{gh} = \lambda_g\lambda_h$ by associativity, so $g \mapsto \lambda_g$ is a homomorphism $G \to \operatorname{Sym}(G)$. If $\lambda_g = \mathrm{id}$ then $g = \lambda_g(e) = e$, so the kernel is trivial and the homomorphism is injective. Hence $G \cong \{\lambda_g\}$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 4 (action on cosets).** Let $H \le G$ have finite index $n$. Then $G$ acts on the set $G/H$ of left cosets by $g \cdot xH = gxH$, and the kernel $K = \bigcap_{x \in G} xHx^{-1}$ is a normal subgroup of $G$ contained in $H$ such that $G/K$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of $S_n$.

*Proof.* The action axioms are clear. The kernel is $\{g : gxH = xH \text{ for all } x\}$, and $gxH = xH$ if and only if $x^{-1}gx \in H$, that is, $g \in xHx^{-1}$; so the kernel is the intersection $K$. Taking $x = e$ shows $K \subseteq H$. By the first isomorphism theorem, $G/K$ is isomorphic to the image of $G \to \operatorname{Sym}(G/H) \cong S_n$. $\blacksquare$

Theorem 4 turns a subgroup of small index into a normal subgroup: if $|G| > n!$ then $K \ne \{e\}$, because $G/K$ has at most $n!$ elements. Lesson 12 uses this.

@example
title: The rotation group of the cube
problem: Let $G$ be the group of rotations of a cube (orientation-preserving symmetries). Use the orbit-stabilizer theorem to find $|G|$, and the number of rotations that fix a given vertex.
step: $G$ acts on the $6$ faces. Every face can be rotated to any other (put the face on top by a rotation), so there is one orbit, of size $6$.
step: The stabilizer of the top face consists of the rotations that keep it in place: the rotations about the vertical axis by multiples of $90^\circ$. There are $4$ of them.
step: By orbit-stabilizer, $|G| = |\text{orbit}|\cdot|\text{stabilizer}| = 6 \cdot 4 = 24$.
step: $G$ also acts on the $8$ vertices, with a single orbit (any vertex can be moved to any other by a rotation), so the stabilizer of a vertex has order $24/8 = 3$: the identity and the two rotations by $120^\circ$ about the diagonal through that vertex.
answer: $|G| = 24$, and exactly $3$ rotations fix a given vertex.

@example
title: $S_5$ acting on pairs
problem: $S_5$ acts on the set $X$ of $2$-element subsets of $\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$ by $\sigma \cdot \{a, b\} = \{\sigma(a), \sigma(b)\}$. Find the orbit of $\{1, 2\}$ and the order of its stabilizer, and describe the stabilizer.
step: Any $2$-element subset $\{a, b\}$ is the image of $\{1, 2\}$ under a permutation with $1 \mapsto a$, $2 \mapsto b$. So the orbit of $\{1, 2\}$ is all of $X$, which has $\binom{5}{2} = 10$ elements.
step: By the orbit-stabilizer theorem, the stabilizer has order $|S_5|/10 = 120/10 = 12$.
step: The stabilizer is the set of $\sigma$ that map $\{1, 2\}$ to itself, and so also map $\{3, 4, 5\}$ to itself. There are $2$ ways to permute $\{1, 2\}$ and $3! = 6$ ways to permute $\{3, 4, 5\}$, so the stabilizer has $2 \cdot 6 = 12$ elements, confirming the count.
answer: The orbit has $10$ elements, and the stabilizer has order $12$ (it is $\{\sigma : \sigma\{1, 2\} = \{1, 2\}\} \cong S_2 \times S_3$).

@mistakes
- Confusing the stabilizer with the orbit. The orbit $Gx$ is a subset of $X$; the stabilizer $G_x$ is a subgroup of $G$. Their sizes are related by $|Gx||G_x| = |G|$.
- Writing $|Gx| = |G|/|G_x|$ for an infinite group. The general statement is $|Gx| = [G : G_x]$, the number of cosets, which makes sense (and is true) even when $G$ is infinite; the division formula needs $G$ finite.
- Forgetting to check that a rule is an action: $n \cdot x = x + n^2$ for $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ satisfies $0 \cdot x = x$ but not $(n + m) \cdot x = n \cdot (m \cdot x)$.
- Saying that the stabilizer of $x$ is the same as the stabilizer of $y$ in the same orbit. They are conjugate subgroups, $G_{g \cdot x} = gG_xg^{-1}$, and generally different.

answer: 36

@practice num
prompt: Label the vertices of a regular hexagon $0, 1, \dots, 5$ in order. The dihedral group $D_6$ (order $12$) consists of the maps $i \mapsto i + k$ and $i \mapsto k - i$ modulo $6$, for $k = 0, \dots, 5$. It acts on the three diagonals $\{0, 3\}, \{1, 4\}, \{2, 5\}$ joining opposite vertices. What is the order of the stabilizer of the diagonal $\{0, 3\}$?
answer: 4
check: sum(1 for k in range(6) for f in (lambda i, k=k: (i + k) % 6, lambda i, k=k: (k - i) % 6) if {f(0), f(3)} == {0, 3})
hint: The orbit of $\{0, 3\}$ has size $3$.
solution: The action on the three diagonals is transitive (rotation by one step moves $\{0, 3\}$ to $\{1, 4\}$, and by two steps to $\{2, 5\}$), so the orbit has size $3$ and the stabilizer has order $12/3 = 4$. It consists of the rotations by $0$ and $3$ steps and the reflections $i \mapsto -i$ and $i \mapsto 3 - i$.

hint: Use the bijection from the cosets of $G_x$ to the orbit.
solution: The map $gG_x \mapsto g \cdot x$ is a bijection from the left cosets of $G_x$ onto $Gx$, so $|Gx| = [G : G_x]$. @practice self
prompt: Let a group $G$ act on a set $X$, let $x \in X$ and $y = g \cdot x$. Prove that $G_y = gG_xg^{-1}$.
hint: Show $gG_xg^{-1} \subseteq G_y$ by computing $(ghg^{-1}) \cdot y$ for $h \in G_x$.
hint: For the other inclusion, apply the first one with $g^{-1}$ in place of $g$ and the roles of $x$ and $y$ swapped.
solution: Let $h \in G_x$. Then $(ghg^{-1}) \cdot y = (ghg^{-1}) \cdot (g \cdot x) = (ghg^{-1}g) \cdot x = (gh) \cdot x = g \cdot (h \cdot x) = g \cdot x = y$, so $ghg^{-1} \in G_y$. This proves $gG_xg^{-1} \subseteq G_y$. Since $x = g^{-1} \cdot y$, the same argument with $g^{-1}$ and the pair $(y, x)$ gives $g^{-1}G_yg \subseteq G_x$, so $G_y \subseteq gG_xg^{-1}$. Hence $G_y = gG_xg^{-1}$. $\blacksquare$

@quiz mc
prompt: A group $G$ acts on a set $X$, giving a homomorphism $G \to \operatorname{Sym}(X)$. What is the kernel of this homomorphism?
choice*: The set of $g \in G$ with $g \cdot x = x$ for every $x \in X$.
choice: The stabilizer $G_x$ of a single point $x \in X$.
choice: The set of $g \in G$ with $g \cdot x = x$ for at least one $x \in X$.
choice: The orbit $Gx$ of a point $x \in X$.
hint: The kernel consists of the group elements acting as the identity permutation.
solution: A permutation is the identity exactly when it fixes every point, so the kernel is the intersection of all stabilizers. A single stabilizer $G_x$ is a subgroup that need not be normal, and the orbit is a subset of $X$, not of $G$.
