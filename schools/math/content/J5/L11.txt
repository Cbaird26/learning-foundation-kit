@article
An **ecosystem** is all the living things in an area together with the nonliving things they interact with. **Biotic** factors are living (plants, animals, fungi, bacteria). **Abiotic** factors are nonliving (sunlight, water, temperature, soil, air).

### Roles in an ecosystem

- **Producers** make their own food, usually by photosynthesis. Plants, algae and some bacteria are producers.
- **Consumers** get energy by eating other organisms: **herbivores** eat plants, **carnivores** eat animals, **omnivores** eat both. A **primary consumer** eats producers, a **secondary consumer** eats primary consumers, and so on.
- **Decomposers**, such as fungi and bacteria, break down dead organisms and wastes and return nutrients to the soil.

### Food chains and food webs

A **food chain** shows who eats whom, like grass $\to$ grasshopper $\to$ frog $\to$ snake. **Each arrow points in the direction energy flows**, from the organism that is eaten to the one that eats it. Real ecosystems have many connected chains, called a **food web**.

Each step in a chain is a **trophic level**. Producers are the first level, primary consumers the second, and secondary consumers the third.

### Energy flows, matter cycles

Energy enters most ecosystems as sunlight and flows in one direction, ending as heat. Matter, such as carbon and nitrogen, is **cycled** again and again, with decomposers helping to return it.

### The 10 percent rule

At each trophic level, organisms use most of the energy they take in for moving, growing and staying warm, and much is lost as heat. Only about **10%** of the energy at one level is stored in the bodies available to the next level. This is a rule of thumb, and the true value varies, often between 5% and 20%.

$$\text{energy at next level} \approx 0.10 \times \text{energy at this level}$$

An **energy pyramid** shows this with the producers as the wide base and each higher level narrower. This is why there are fewer top predators than herbivores, and why food chains rarely have more than four or five levels.

### Investigating at home

You can map a food web from your yard or a park by observing and listing the organisms you see, without touching animals or collecting anything. Wash your hands afterward and never taste wild plants.

@example
title: Building an energy pyramid
problem: Producers in a meadow store 10,000 kJ of energy. Using the 10 percent rule, how much energy is available at each higher trophic level?
step: Level 1 (producers): 10,000 kJ.
step: Level 2 (primary consumers): $0.10 \times 10{,}000 = 1000$ kJ.
step: Level 3 (secondary consumers): $0.10 \times 1000 = 100$ kJ.
step: Level 4 (tertiary consumers): $0.10 \times 100 = 10$ kJ. The pyramid narrows quickly, so few top predators can be supported.

@mistakes
- Pointing the arrows from predator to prey; the arrow shows the direction of energy flow, from the eaten to the eater.
- Saying energy is recycled like matter; energy flows through and is lost as heat.
- Believing 10% of the energy is "lost"; it is 90% that is lost and 10% that passes on.
