@article
Each of your traits is influenced by genes, and you have two copies of most genes: one from each parent. Different versions of a gene are called **alleles**. For example, a pea plant has a gene for flower color, with an allele for purple and an allele for white.

### Vocabulary

- **Dominant allele**: shows its trait when at least one copy is present. Written as a capital letter, like $P$.
- **Recessive allele**: shows its trait only when two copies are present. Written as a lowercase letter, like $p$.
- **Genotype**: the combination of alleles an organism has (for example $Pp$).
- **Phenotype**: the trait you can observe (for example purple flowers).
- **Homozygous**: two identical alleles ($PP$ or $pp$). **Heterozygous**: two different alleles ($Pp$).

A plant with genotype $Pp$ has purple flowers, because $P$ is dominant, but it carries the white allele.

### Punnett squares

A **Punnett square** shows all possible combinations of the alleles from the parents' gametes. Each parent gives one allele, and each is equally likely. Write one parent's two alleles across the top and the other's down the side, then fill each box with the pair of letters.

For $Bb \times Bb$ (B is dominant brown, b is recessive blue):

| | B | b |
|---|---|---|
| **B** | BB | Bb |
| **b** | Bb | bb |

The genotype ratio is $1\,BB : 2\,Bb : 1\,bb$. Three of the four boxes contain at least one $B$, so the phenotype ratio is $3$ brown $: 1$ blue.

### Probability and expected counts

Each box is $\frac{1}{4}$ of the outcomes, so the chance of a $bb$ offspring is $\frac{1}{4}$, or $0.25$. Probabilities describe chances, not guarantees. A family with four children does not always have exactly one $bb$ child. In a large group, the results come closer to the prediction:

$$\text{expected number} = \text{probability} \times \text{number of offspring}.$$

For 100 offspring of $Bb \times Bb$, you expect about $25$ with genotype $bb$.

### Beyond simple dominance

Many traits are more complicated. In **incomplete dominance**, the heterozygote is in between, like pink flowers from red and white parents. Some traits are controlled by several genes. Punnett squares work well for single genes with clear dominance.

@example
title: A cross between two heterozygous plants
problem: In pea plants, tall ($T$) is dominant over short ($t$). Two tall plants with genotype $Tt$ are crossed. What fraction of their offspring are expected to be short?
step: Each parent makes gametes $T$ or $t$ with equal chance. Set up a $2 \times 2$ square with $T$ and $t$ on each side.
step: The four boxes are $TT$, $Tt$, $Tt$ and $tt$.
step: Only $tt$ plants are short, which is one box out of four.
step: The expected fraction of short offspring is $\frac{1}{4}$, and the phenotype ratio is $3$ tall $: 1$ short.

@mistakes
- Treating a recessive trait as rare or weaker; recessive just means it needs two copies to show.
- Confusing genotype with phenotype; $Tt$ is a genotype and "tall" is a phenotype.
- Expecting results to match the ratio exactly in a small family; probabilities describe the long run.

@quiz num
prompt: Two tall plants with genotype $Tt$ are crossed, where tall ($T$) is dominant. Out of 120 offspring, how many are expected to be tall?
answer: 90
check: 120 * 3 / 4
hint: Count the boxes in the Punnett square that contain at least one $T$.
solution: Three of four boxes ($TT$, $Tt$, $Tt$) are tall, so $\frac{3}{4} \times 120 = 90$.
