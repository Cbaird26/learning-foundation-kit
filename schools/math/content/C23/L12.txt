@article
A mathematics paper is not a novel. You do not read it from the first line to the last and understand it as you go. Experienced readers use several passes, each with a different goal. Practice differs among fields and authors, so adapt this routine to what you read.

### Reading a paper in three passes

**Pass 1: what is claimed? (10 minutes)** Read the title, abstract and introduction. Find the main theorem and write it in your own words. Note which earlier results it uses (the citations) and which are new. Skim the section headings to see the shape of the argument.

**Pass 2: how does it go? (an hour or more)** Read the definitions slowly, writing an example and a non-example for each. Read the statements of the lemmas and propositions, but on a first reading skip the proofs of the technical ones. Read the proof of the main theorem and mark where each lemma is used. Keep a notebook of notation.

**Pass 3: is it right? (as long as needed)** Work through the proofs with pencil. Restate each step, check each inequality, and fill every "it is easy to see". If you cannot fill a gap after real effort, note it, since a gap may be the reader's, the author's, or a convention you have not learned yet.

### Testing a proof as you read

For each theorem you read, ask:

1. **Where is each hypothesis used?** Underline them in the statement, then find the line of the proof that needs each one. A hypothesis that is never used is suspicious, or the theorem is stronger than stated.
2. **What fails if I drop a hypothesis?** Find a counterexample. In this course: AM–GM fails for negative numbers, Fermat's little theorem fails for composite moduli, and the pigeonhole count fails if one box is added.
3. **Does it work on a small example?** Run the proof on the smallest case you can.
4. **What is the key idea, in one sentence?** If you cannot say it, you have followed the steps but not the proof.

### Preparing and giving a talk

A good talk is shorter than the proof it is about. Its job is to make the audience want the result, understand the statement, and see **one** idea. A common structure for a short talk of five to ten minutes:

| Minutes | Content |
|---|---|
| 1 | A question or puzzle that motivates the result |
| 1 | The exact statement, with all hypotheses, written on the board |
| 3 | The key idea of the proof, with one worked case |
| 1 | An example showing the result is sharp or why a hypothesis matters |
| 1 | Summary: what you proved, what to remember |

Practical points: speak to the audience and not the board; write the statement and the key formula where everyone can see them; define notation aloud when you first use it; do not read slides line by line; and rehearse out loud with a timer, since a talk that fits when you think it through usually runs over when you speak it. Plan roughly one to two minutes per slide or board panel. For questions, repeat the question, answer what was asked, and say "I do not know, let me think" when that is true.

@example
title: Where is a hypothesis used?
problem: Read the proof of the three-variable AM–GM inequality for $x, y, z \ge 0$ from the identity $a^3 + b^3 + c^3 - 3abc = \frac12 (a + b + c)\big((a-b)^2 + (b-c)^2 + (c-a)^2\big)$, with $a = x^{1/3}$, $b = y^{1/3}$, $c = z^{1/3}$. Find every place where $x, y, z \ge 0$ is used.
step: The identity holds for all real $a, b, c$ (it is a polynomial identity), so it does not use the hypothesis.
step: First use: the substitution $a = x^{1/3}$ treats $a$ as a real number with $a^3 = x$; the hypothesis $x \ge 0$ makes $a$ nonnegative, which the next step needs.
step: Second use: the right side of the identity equals $\frac12(a + b + c)$ times a sum of squares. The sum of squares is nonnegative for all reals, but $a + b + c \ge 0$ needs $a, b, c \ge 0$. Without it the inequality $a^3 + b^3 + c^3 \ge 3abc$ can fail: at $a = b = -1$, $c = 0$ it reads $-2 \ge 0$.
step: So the hypothesis is used to make $a, b, c$ nonnegative, and through that to make $a + b + c \ge 0$. Testing the proof at $a = b = -1$, $c = 0$ shows the hypothesis cannot be dropped.
answer: The hypothesis $x, y, z \ge 0$ makes $a, b, c \ge 0$, hence $a + b + c \ge 0$; the inequality fails without it.

@example
title: Budgeting a five-minute talk
problem: You must present the pigeonhole result "among any $n + 1$ integers, two have a difference divisible by $n$" in five minutes. Plan the talk.
step: Motivate in one minute: ask the audience whether, among any $13$ people, two must share a birth month, and let them answer.
step: State the result in one minute: "For every positive integer $n$ and any $n + 1$ integers, there are two whose difference is divisible by $n$." Write it on the board exactly.
step: Give the key idea in two minutes: the boxes are the $n$ remainders modulo $n$; $n + 1$ integers cannot have $n + 1$ different remainders; two share a box, so their difference is a multiple of $n$.
step: Finish in one minute with sharpness and a summary: the $n$ integers $0, 1, \dots, n - 1$ have no two with a difference divisible by $n$, so $n + 1$ is the least number that works. Recap: pigeons, boxes, conclusion.
answer: Question, statement, boxes-and-pigeons idea, sharpness example: one minute, one minute, two minutes, one minute.

@mistakes
- Reading proofs line by line from the first page, then losing the thread: read the abstract, introduction and statements first.
- Accepting a step because it is printed in a paper: check it, or mark it as unchecked.
- Putting an entire proof on slides: an audience can follow one idea, not eight lemmas.
- Never timing the talk. A talk that was never spoken aloud is not rehearsed.

@practice self
prompt: Plan a five-minute talk on the Erdős–Szekeres theorem (every sequence of $n^2 + 1$ distinct real numbers contains a monotone subsequence of length $n + 1$). Give a time budget, the exact statement you would write on the board, the key idea of the proof, and one example that shows the number $n^2 + 1$ cannot be lowered.
hint: Use the structure of a short talk: motivation, statement, key idea, sharpness, summary. Choose $n = 2$ as the example.
solution: **Time budget.** 1 minute motivation, 1 minute statement, 2 minutes key idea, 1 minute sharpness and summary. **Motivation.** Ask whether any arrangement of five numbers can avoid having three that increase or three that decrease; try to build one on the board. **Statement (board).** For every positive integer $n$, any sequence of $n^2 + 1$ distinct reals has an increasing subsequence of length $n + 1$ or a decreasing subsequence of length $n + 1$. **Key idea.** Label each term $a_k$ with the pair $(i_k, d_k)$ of lengths of the longest increasing and decreasing subsequences ending at $a_k$. If no monotone subsequence had length $n + 1$ all labels would lie in an $n \times n$ grid, which has $n^2$ cells, but there are $n^2 + 1$ terms, so two terms $j < k$ share a label. But $a_j < a_k$ would force $i_k > i_j$ and $a_j > a_k$ would force $d_k > d_j$, so labels of distinct terms differ, a contradiction. **Sharpness.** For $n = 2$ the sequence $2, 1, 4, 3$ has $4 = n^2$ terms and no monotone subsequence of length $3$, so $n^2 + 1$ cannot be lowered to $n^2$. **Summary.** Pigeons are the terms, boxes are the label pairs.
rubric: Gives a realistic time budget that adds up to five minutes and begins with a motivating question.
rubric: States the theorem exactly, with the length $n^2 + 1$, "distinct" and "monotone of length $n + 1$".
rubric: Explains the labels as pigeons and boxes and why two equal labels are impossible, and gives the $n = 2$ sharpness example.

@practice self
prompt: This is the block deliverable for weeks 11 and 12. Choose one problem from this course, for example "find the last two digits of $3^{2027}$". (a) Write the LaTeX source for a half-page solution with a problem statement, a complete argument and a final sentence on the idea. (b) Write a three-minute spoken script for the same solution and list two questions a listener might ask, with your answers.
hint: Use a displayed formula for the key computation. The spoken script should explain the idea (the powers of $3$ repeat with period $20$ modulo $100$), not read the algebra aloud.
solution: (a) The source below uses the packages `amsmath` and `amsthm` in the preamble.
```text
\section*{Problem}
Find the last two digits of $3^{2027}$.

\begin{proof}[Solution]
We work modulo $100$. Since $3^{10} = 59049 \equiv 49 \pmod{100}$,
\[
  3^{20} = (3^{10})^2 \equiv 49^2 = 2401 \equiv 1 \pmod{100},
\]
so the powers of $3$ repeat with period dividing $20$. Writing
$2027 = 20 \cdot 101 + 7$, we get
\[
  3^{2027} = (3^{20})^{101} \cdot 3^7
  \equiv 1^{101} \cdot 2187 \equiv 87 \pmod{100}.
\]
So the last two digits are $87$.
\end{proof}

\noindent\textit{Idea.} Once a power is congruent to $1$, the
powers cycle, so only the exponent modulo the period matters.
```
(b) Script (about three minutes): "Last two digits means the remainder modulo 100. Multiplying out 3 to the power 2027 is hopeless, so I look for a cycle. Three to the tenth is 59049, which leaves 49 modulo 100. Squaring 49 gives 2401, which leaves remainder 1. So three to the twentieth is 1 modulo 100, and the pattern repeats every twenty steps. I divide the exponent 2027 by 20: the quotient is 101 and the remainder is 7. So 3 to the 2027 is 3 to the twentieth, 101 times, times 3 to the seventh, and the first factor is 1. Three to the seventh is 2187, so the answer is 87." Question 1: "Why is it enough to look modulo 100?" Answer: the last two digits are the remainder on division by 100, and congruences respect multiplication. Question 2: "Does this method work for every base?" Answer: only if the base is coprime to 100; otherwise the powers never return to 1, although they can still become periodic after some point.
rubric: The LaTeX source compiles in principle: balanced environments and braces, math in `$...$` or `\[ ... \]`, and a clear problem and solution.
rubric: The mathematics is complete: the cycle $3^{20} \equiv 1 \pmod{100}$ is shown and the exponent is reduced correctly.
rubric: The script states the key idea in words and does not read formulas aloud, and gives two sensible audience questions with correct answers.
