@article
In any group of $13$ people, two were born in the same month. No one had to ask anyone anything: with $12$ months and $13$ people, some month is repeated. That is the **pigeonhole principle**, and it is the simplest tool in this course that proves something *exists* without telling you where it is.

### The principle

**Pigeonhole principle.** If more than $n$ objects are placed into $n$ boxes, some box contains at least two objects.

**Generalized form.** If $m$ objects are placed into $n$ boxes, some box contains at least $\lceil m/n \rceil$ objects.

*Proof of the generalized form.* Suppose every box contained at most $\lceil m/n \rceil - 1$ objects. Then the total number of objects would be at most $n(\lceil m/n \rceil - 1) < n \cdot \frac{m}{n} = m$, because $\lceil m/n \rceil - 1 < m/n$. This contradicts the fact that there are $m$ objects. Taking $m = n + 1$ gives the basic principle. $\blacksquare$

The principle never says *which* box. The art of using it is **designing the boxes** so that "two objects in the same box" is exactly the conclusion you want. Residues modulo $n$ are the most common boxes: two integers in the same box have a difference divisible by $n$. Other boxes: pairs $\{k, k + 8\}$ of integers, subintervals of a segment, or sub-squares of a square.

### The Erdős–Szekeres theorem

**Theorem (Erdős and Szekeres, 1935).** Every sequence of $n^2 + 1$ distinct real numbers contains an increasing subsequence of length $n + 1$ or a decreasing subsequence of length $n + 1$.

*Proof.* Let the sequence be $a_1, \dots, a_{n^2+1}$. For each position $k$ let $i_k$ be the length of the longest increasing subsequence that ends at $a_k$, and $d_k$ the length of the longest decreasing one. Suppose, for contradiction, that all $i_k, d_k \le n$. Then each pair $(i_k, d_k)$ lies in $\{1, \dots, n\} \times \{1, \dots, n\}$, which has $n^2$ elements (the boxes), while there are $n^2 + 1$ positions (the pigeons). So two positions $j < k$ have the same pair. But $a_j \ne a_k$: if $a_j < a_k$, then appending $a_k$ to a longest increasing subsequence ending at $a_j$ gives $i_k \ge i_j + 1$; if $a_j > a_k$, the same argument gives $d_k \ge d_j + 1$. Either way the pairs differ, a contradiction. $\blacksquare$

The number $n^2 + 1$ is sharp: for $n = 2$, the sequence $2, 1, 4, 3$ has $4$ terms and no monotone subsequence of length $3$. The program below checks $n = 2$ for all orderings of five numbers, and for four numbers finds the sharpness example. It is a check of finitely many cases; the proof covers every $n$.

```python
from itertools import permutations


def longest_monotone(seq):
    """Length of the longest monotone subsequence of distinct numbers."""
    inc = [1] * len(seq)
    dec = [1] * len(seq)
    for k in range(len(seq)):
        for j in range(k):
            if seq[j] < seq[k]:
                inc[k] = max(inc[k], inc[j] + 1)
            else:
                dec[k] = max(dec[k], dec[j] + 1)
    return max(inc + dec)


print(all(longest_monotone(p) >= 3 for p in permutations(range(5))))
print(min(longest_monotone(p) for p in permutations(range(4))))
```

It prints `True` and then `2`.

### Counting in two ways

To prove an identity, count one set of objects by two different methods. **Claim.** For $n \ge 1$, $\sum_{k=0}^{n} k \binom{n}{k} = n 2^{n-1}$.

*Proof.* Count the pairs (committee, chair) where the committee is a subset of $n$ people and the chair is a member of the committee. Counting by committee size $k$: there are $\binom{n}{k}$ committees of size $k$ and $k$ choices of chair, so the total is $\sum_k k\binom{n}{k}$. Counting by chair first: there are $n$ choices of chair, and the rest of the committee is an arbitrary subset of the other $n - 1$ people, $2^{n-1}$ choices, so the total is $n \cdot 2^{n-1}$. Both count the same set. $\blacksquare$

@example
title: A block of consecutive terms
problem: Prove that among any $8$ integers $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_8$ (repetitions allowed), some nonempty block of consecutive terms $a_{i+1}, \dots, a_j$ has a sum divisible by $8$.
step: Define the prefix sums $S_0 = 0$ and $S_t = a_1 + \dots + a_t$ for $t = 1, \dots, 8$. That is $9$ numbers.
step: Boxes: the remainder of $S_t$ on division by $8$. There are $8$ possible remainders, so two of the nine prefix sums have the same remainder.
step: Say $S_i \equiv S_j \pmod 8$ with $0 \le i < j \le 8$. Then $8 \mid S_j - S_i = a_{i+1} + \dots + a_j$.
step: That block is nonempty because $i < j$, so it is the block we wanted. The statement fails for fewer terms: the seven terms $1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1$ have no block with sum divisible by $8$.
answer: Some block of consecutive terms has sum divisible by $8$, and $8$ terms are needed.

@example
title: Differences of exactly 8
problem: Prove that among any $9$ distinct integers chosen from $\{1, 2, \dots, 16\}$ there are two whose difference is exactly $8$.
step: Boxes: the eight pairs $\{1, 9\}$, $\{2, 10\}$, $\dots$, $\{8, 16\}$, that is $\{k, k + 8\}$ for $k = 1, \dots, 8$. Every number from $1$ to $16$ lies in exactly one box.
step: There are $9$ chosen numbers and $8$ boxes, so two chosen numbers lie in the same box.
step: Two distinct numbers in the same box are $k$ and $k + 8$, so their difference is $8$.
step: Sharpness: the eight numbers $1, 2, \dots, 8$ have no two differing by $8$, so $9$ cannot be replaced by $8$.
answer: Any $9$ of the numbers $1, \dots, 16$ contain two that differ by $8$, and $9$ is the least number that works.

@mistakes
- Not saying what the pigeons and what the boxes are. A correct proof states both and counts them.
- Choosing boxes so that two objects in one box do not give the conclusion: boxes must be designed backward from what you want to prove.
- Forgetting the generalized form: with $m$ objects and $n$ boxes the guarantee is $\lceil m/n \rceil$, not $m/n$, and not "at least one box has exactly $m/n$".
- Omitting a sharpness check. If you claim $k$ objects suffice, find $k - 1$ objects that do not, or say you have not.

model:
```python
def shortest_block(a, m):
    """Least length of a block of consecutive terms with sum divisible by m."""
    last = {0: 0}          # remainder of prefix sum -> latest index
    total = 0
    best = None
    for j, x in enumerate(a, 1):
        total += x
        r = total % m
        if r in last:
            length = j - last[r]
            if best is None or length < best:
                best = length
        last[r] = j
    return best
```
hint: Use prefix sums modulo $m$: a block has sum divisible by $m$ exactly when two prefix sums have the same remainder.
solution: The block $a_{i+1}, \dots, a_j$ has sum $S_j - S_i$, which is divisible by $m$ exactly when $S_i \equiv S_j \pmod m$. For a fixed $j$ the shortest such block uses the latest earlier index $i$ with the same remainder, so a dictionary of the latest index of each remainder (starting with remainder $0$ at index $0$) finds the answer in one pass. Python's `%` returns a nonnegative remainder for negative sums too.

@practice self
prompt: Write a complete proof of each statement. (a) By induction, for every integer $n \ge 1$: $\sum_{k=1}^{n} k \cdot 3^k = \dfrac{(2n - 1) 3^{n+1} + 3}{4}$. (b) By the pigeonhole principle: among any $7$ integers there are two whose sum or whose difference is divisible by $10$.
hint: For (a), add the term $(k+1)3^{k+1}$ to the formula for $k$ and factor out $3^{k+1}$.
hint: For (b), put integers with remainders $r$ and $10 - r$ modulo $10$ in the same box. How many boxes do you need?
solution: (a) Base case: for $n = 1$ the sum is $3$ and the formula gives $(1 \cdot 9 + 3)/4 = 3$. Inductive step: let $k \ge 1$ and assume the formula for $k$. Then $\sum_{j=1}^{k+1} j 3^j = \frac{(2k-1)3^{k+1} + 3}{4} + (k+1)3^{k+1} = \frac{3^{k+1}(2k - 1 + 4k + 4) + 3}{4} = \frac{3^{k+1}(6k + 3) + 3}{4} = \frac{(2k + 1)3^{k+2} + 3}{4}$, because $3^{k+1}(6k+3) = 3^{k+2}(2k+1)$. This is the formula for $k + 1$, so it holds for all $n \ge 1$. (b) Boxes by remainder modulo $10$: $\{0\}$, $\{1, 9\}$, $\{2, 8\}$, $\{3, 7\}$, $\{4, 6\}$, $\{5\}$. That is $6$ boxes and every integer lies in exactly one. With $7$ integers, two lie in the same box. If they have the same remainder, their difference is divisible by $10$. Otherwise their remainders are $r$ and $10 - r$ with $1 \le r \le 4$, so their sum is $\equiv 10 \equiv 0 \pmod{10}$.

rubric: In (b), names the six boxes and the seven pigeons and justifies that two integers in one box give a sum or a difference divisible by $10$.
rubric: Both proofs are complete, with the final conclusion stated for all $n$ (a) and for any $7$ integers (b).

@quiz num
prompt: Find the least $k$ such that any $k$ distinct integers chosen from $\{1, 2, \dots, 40\}$ must include two whose difference is exactly $10$.
answer: 21
check: 1 + sum((len(range(r, 41, 10)) + 1) // 2 for r in range(1, 11))
hint: Group the numbers into boxes of the form $\{j, j + 10\}$ and ask how many numbers you can choose without taking both numbers of any box.
solution: The twenty boxes $\{j, j + 10\}$ with $j \in \{1, \dots, 10\} \cup \{21, \dots, 30\}$ partition $\{1, \dots, 40\}$. Choosing $21$ numbers puts two in one box, and they differ by $10$. The twenty numbers $1, \dots, 10$ and $21, \dots, 30$ contain no two differing by $10$ (differences within each block are less than $10$, and between the blocks at least $11$), so $20$ is not enough. The least $k$ is $21$.
