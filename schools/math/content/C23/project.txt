material: **The problem list.** Solve twelve of the following sixteen problems. All numbers are as stated; a proof is required in every case, not only an answer.

**Patterns and conjectures**

1. Let $c(n)$ be the number of subsets of $\{1, 2, \dots, n\}$, including the empty set, in which any two elements differ by at least $3$. Compute $c(n)$ for $n = 0, 1, \dots, 6$, find a recurrence, prove it, and compute $c(12)$.
2. Let $g(n)$ be the number of $3$-element subsets of $\{1, \dots, n\}$ whose sum is divisible by $3$. Compute $g(3)$, $g(6)$, $g(9)$, $g(12)$, conjecture a formula for $g(3m)$, and prove it.

**Invariants and monovariants**

3. A point starts at $(1, 0)$ in the plane and moves by the rule $(x, y) \mapsto (x + y, x - y)$. (a) Prove that the point is never at $(3, 5)$. (b) Find the point after $8$ moves and prove your answer.
4. A row of positive integers is changed by this rule: choose adjacent entries $x, y$ with $x > y$ and replace them by $y, x - y$. Prove that the process stops from every starting row, and that from the row $9, 7, 5, 3, 1$ it makes at most $20$ moves.

**The extremal principle**

5. A finite set $S$ of positive integers with at least two elements has this property: whenever $a > b$ are in $S$, the number $a - b$ is also in $S$. Prove that $S = \{m, 2m, 3m, \dots, km\}$ for some positive integers $m$ and $k$.
6. Prove that $x^2 + 5y^2 = 3z^2$ has no solution in positive integers.

**Induction**

7. Prove that $3^n \ge n^3$ for every integer $n \ge 1$. (Be careful: the natural inductive step does not work for small $n$.)
8. Let $t_1 = 1$, $t_2 = 4$ and $t_{n+2} = 4t_{n+1} - 4t_n$. Compute the first six terms, find a closed form, and prove it.

**Pigeonhole and counting**

9. Prove that every sequence of $13$ distinct real numbers contains an increasing subsequence of length $4$ or a decreasing subsequence of length $5$. Then give a sequence of $12$ distinct real numbers with no increasing subsequence of length $4$ and no decreasing subsequence of length $5$.
10. Prove by counting the same set in two ways that for integers $0 \le j \le k \le n$, $\binom{n}{k}\binom{k}{j} = \binom{n}{j}\binom{n-j}{k-j}$.

**Inequalities**

11. Prove that $(a + b + c)(a^2 + b^2 + c^2) \ge 9abc$ for all positive reals $a, b, c$, and find when equality holds.
12. Find the minimum of $\dfrac{1}{x} + \dfrac{4}{y} + \dfrac{9}{z}$ over positive reals $x, y, z$ with $x + y + z = 6$, and prove that it is the minimum.

**Number theory**

13. Find all positive integers $n$ such that $2n + 5$ divides $n^2 + 10$.
14. Prove that $n^7 - n$ is divisible by $42$ for every integer $n$.

**Functional equations and polynomials**

15. Find all functions $f$ defined on the real numbers other than $\tfrac13$ and $\tfrac23$ such that $f(x) + f\!\left(\dfrac{2x - 1}{3x - 1}\right) = x$ for every such $x$. (Check that $x \mapsto \frac{2x-1}{3x-1}$ maps this domain to itself, and that applying it three times returns $x$.)
16. A polynomial $P$ with integer coefficients satisfies $P(2) = 5$ and $P(7) = 15$. Prove that there is no integer $n$ with $P(n) = 0$.

## Week-by-week key ideas

1. **Attacking a problem.** Pólya's phases: understand, plan, carry out, look back. Keep a strategy log. Reduce a "find all" problem to a finite check and prove completeness.
2. **Small cases and conjectures.** Tabulate, take differences and ratios, state a conjecture, test it on a case you did not use, prove it. Finite agreement is never a proof.
3. **Invariants and monovariants.** An invariant (parity, residue, weighted sum) proves a target unreachable; check every move. A bounded integer-valued quantity that decreases proves termination.
4. **The extremal principle.** Choose a longest, smallest or closest object; use well-ordering for infinite descent.
5. **Induction.** Strengthen the hypothesis when the step is too weak; use as many base cases as the recurrence reaches back; locate where the step starts to work.
6. **Pigeonhole and counting.** Design boxes backward from the conclusion; generalized form $\lceil m/n \rceil$; Erdős–Szekeres; count a set in two ways.
7. **Inequalities.** AM–GM (nonnegative terms, equality iff all equal), Cauchy–Schwarz (equality iff proportional), rearrangement. Always check that equality can occur.
8. **Number theory.** Bézout's identity, Fermat's little theorem for a prime modulus, reducing modulo a good number, reducing $n + k \mid f(n)$ to $n + k \mid f(-k)$.
9. **Functional equations and polynomials.** Substitute special values and swap variables; verify the answer; a nonzero polynomial of degree $d$ has at most $d$ roots; rational root theorem; Cauchy's equation on $\mathbb{Q}$.
10. **Writing proofs.** State the claim with quantifiers, announce the method, justify every step, use sentences; contrapositive, converse, negation of quantifiers.
11. **LaTeX.** Braces group exponents and subscripts; use `\log`, `\mid`, `\le`; `align*` and theorem environments; compile often.
12. **Reading and presenting.** Three passes through a paper; ask where each hypothesis is used; plan a talk around one idea and a time budget.

## Key formulas and facts

- Pigeonhole: $m$ objects in $n$ boxes give a box with at least $\lceil m/n \rceil$.
- AM–GM: $\frac{x_1 + \dots + x_n}{n} \ge \sqrt[n]{x_1 \cdots x_n}$; Cauchy–Schwarz: $(\sum a_i b_i)^2 \le (\sum a_i^2)(\sum b_i^2)$.
- $\gcd(a, b) = ax + by$ for some integers $x, y$; $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$ when $p$ is prime and $p \nmid a$.

## Self-check

- Can you give an invariant for a process and check all its moves?
- Can you say, for a pigeonhole proof, what the pigeons and boxes are?
- Can you state where each hypothesis of AM–GM or Fermat's little theorem is used?
- Can you rewrite a messy proof in sentences with a stated method?

## Common errors

Treating computed cases as a proof; checking only some moves for an invariant; concluding reachability from equal invariants; applying AM–GM to negative numbers; one base case for a two-step recurrence; forgetting to verify the solution of a functional equation; writing "clearly" for the key step.

@exam num
prompt: A binary string of length $n$ (digits $0$ and $1$) is *good* if no three consecutive digits are equal. Let $a(n)$ be the number of good strings of length $n$. Find $a(10)$.
answer: 178
check: int(run("from itertools import product\nprint(sum(1 for s in product('01', repeat=10) if all(not (s[i] == s[i + 1] == s[i + 2]) for i in range(8))))"))
hint: Compute $a(1), a(2), a(3)$ by hand, then split good strings of length $n$ by whether the last two digits are equal.
solution: We have $a(1) = 2$ and $a(2) = 4$. For $n \ge 3$, a good string of length $n$ either has its last two digits different or equal. Those with different last digits correspond one-to-one to good strings of length $n - 1$ (remove the last digit; to go back, append the opposite of the last digit). Those with equal last digits must have a different digit before them, so removing the last two digits gives a good string of length $n - 2$, and the removed pair is determined by it (two copies of the digit opposite to its last digit). Hence $a(n) = a(n-1) + a(n-2)$. The values are $2, 4, 6, 10, 16, 26, 42, 68, 110, 178$, so $a(10) = 178$.

@exam self
prompt: Prove that $5^n + 2 \cdot 3^{n-1} + 1$ is divisible by $8$ for every integer $n \ge 1$.
hint: Let $f(n)$ be the expression. Write $f(k+1)$ as $5 f(k)$ minus something and show that something is divisible by $8$.
solution: Let $f(n) = 5^n + 2 \cdot 3^{n-1} + 1$. Base case: $f(1) = 5 + 2 + 1 = 8$, which is divisible by $8$. Inductive step: let $k \ge 1$ and assume $8 \mid f(k)$. Then $f(k+1) = 5 \cdot 5^k + 6 \cdot 3^{k-1} + 1$. Compare with $5 f(k) = 5 \cdot 5^k + 10 \cdot 3^{k-1} + 5$: the difference is $f(k+1) - 5f(k) = -4 \cdot 3^{k-1} - 4 = -4(3^{k-1} + 1)$. The number $3^{k-1}$ is odd, so $3^{k-1} + 1$ is even, and therefore $4(3^{k-1} + 1)$ is divisible by $8$. Hence $f(k+1) = 5f(k) - 4(3^{k-1} + 1)$ is a difference of two multiples of $8$ and is divisible by $8$. By induction, $8 \mid f(n)$ for all $n \ge 1$. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Checks the base case $n = 1$ and states the inductive hypothesis for an arbitrary $k \ge 1$.
rubric: Derives $f(k+1) = 5f(k) - 4(3^{k-1} + 1)$ correctly.
rubric: Justifies that $3^{k-1} + 1$ is even (because $3^{k-1}$ is odd), so the subtracted term is a multiple of $8$, and concludes by induction.

@exam ms
prompt: Which of these are valid proofs of the statement "among any $11$ integers there are two whose difference is divisible by $10$"?
choice*: Put each integer in the box given by its remainder modulo $10$. There are $11$ integers and $10$ boxes, so two integers lie in one box, and their difference is a multiple of $10$.
choice*: Suppose no two of the integers have a difference divisible by $10$. Then their $11$ remainders modulo $10$ are all different, but only $10$ remainders exist, a contradiction.
choice: Check by computer every $11$-element subset of $\{1, 2, \dots, 100\}$ and observe that each contains two such integers.
choice: Argue by induction on the number of integers: if the statement holds for $10$ integers, it holds for $11$.
hint: A valid proof must cover arbitrary integers, not just those in a range, and must have a correct inductive structure if it uses induction.
solution: The first two arguments are the pigeonhole principle, directly and by contradiction. A computer check of subsets of $\{1, \dots, 100\}$ covers only finitely many sets of small integers and does not prove the statement for all integers. The induction argument has no base case and no valid step: the statement is false for $10$ integers (take $0, 1, \dots, 9$), so it cannot be passed from $10$ to $11$.
