@article
You have found a solution. A proof is a different object: it is a text that a reader must be able to check. Here is a proof of "if $3 \mid n^2$ then $3 \mid n$" that a student handed in.

> $n^2 = 3k$, so $n = \sqrt{3k}$, which is a multiple of $3$ because $3$ is prime. Done.

Every sentence has a problem. The square root of $3k$ is not shown to be an integer; the fact that $3$ is prime is invoked but never used; and the conclusion is asserted, not derived. The student may well *know* why the claim is true. The proof does not say. This lesson is about closing that gap.

### A model rewrite

**Claim.** For every integer $n$, if $3 \mid n^2$ then $3 \mid n$.

*Proof.* We prove the contrapositive: if $3 \nmid n$, then $3 \nmid n^2$. Let $n$ be an integer not divisible by $3$. By the division algorithm, $n = 3q + 1$ or $n = 3q + 2$ for some integer $q$. In the first case $n^2 = 9q^2 + 6q + 1 = 3(3q^2 + 2q) + 1$. In the second case $n^2 = 9q^2 + 12q + 4 = 3(3q^2 + 4q + 1) + 1$. In both cases $n^2$ leaves remainder $1$ on division by $3$, so $3 \nmid n^2$. This proves the contrapositive, and therefore the claim. $\blacksquare$

Notice what the rewrite does: it announces its method, names the cases and shows exactly why they are all the cases, gives each computation, and ends by saying what has been proved.

### Habits of a readable proof

1. **State the claim completely.** Include the quantifiers and the domain: "for every integer $n$", not "if $3 \mid n^2$ then $3 \mid n$" floating without a scope.
2. **Open with the setup.** Say what you assume and what you will show: "Suppose $x$ is even; we show $x^2$ is even." Name each variable when it is first used and never reuse a letter for a second meaning.
3. **Signpost.** Announce a contradiction, a contrapositive, an induction, a case split. The reader should never wonder why you are doing a computation.
4. **Write sentences.** Use words such as *since*, *so*, *hence*, *because* and *therefore*. A chain of equals signs is fine for a computation; a chain of arrows $\Rightarrow$ with no words is not a proof.
5. **Justify each step** by a definition, a hypothesis, a computation or a named theorem whose hypotheses you checked. Replace "clearly" and "obviously" by the reason.
6. **Close.** Say what was proved and mark the end with $\blacksquare$.

### Logic that proofs depend on

For statements $P$ and $Q$, the implication $P \Rightarrow Q$ is equivalent to its **contrapositive** $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$ and to "$\neg P$ or $Q$". It is *not* equivalent to its **converse** $Q \Rightarrow P$ or its **inverse** $\neg P \Rightarrow \neg Q$. To negate a quantified statement, flip each quantifier and negate the end: the negation of "for every $x$ there is $y$ with $R(x, y)$" is "there is an $x$ such that for every $y$, not $R(x, y)$". **Order matters:** "for every integer $n$ there is an integer $m$ with $m > n$" is true, while "there is an integer $m$ such that for every integer $n$, $m > n$" is false.

### Editing a proof

| Weak phrase | Problem | Better |
|---|---|---|
| "Clearly, ..." | Hides a step | State the step and its reason |
| "It follows that ..." (nothing before) | No antecedent | "Since $x = 2k$, it follows that ..." |
| "Let $x$ be a number" | Which kind? | "Let $x$ be a positive integer" |
| "$a \Rightarrow b \Rightarrow c$" | Unclear logic | Full sentences with *so* and *since* |
| "We see that $P$" | The key step is a claim, not a proof | Prove $P$ or cite it |

Before handing a proof in, run these checks: read it aloud; ask whether a classmate who has not seen the problem could follow it; cover one line and ask if the next line still follows; check that every symbol is defined before it is used; and test the claim on one example to make sure the proof does not prove something false.

@example
title: Fixing a gap in a proof
problem: A draft says: "If $n$ is odd then $n^2 - 1$ is divisible by $8$. Proof: $n^2 - 1 = (n-1)(n+1)$. These are both even, so it is divisible by $8$." Identify the gap and write a correct proof.
step: Check the draft's logic. It is true that $n - 1$ and $n + 1$ are both even, so the product is divisible by $2 \cdot 2 = 4$. But the draft concludes divisibility by $8$ with no reason: two even numbers do not always have a product divisible by $8$ (for instance $2 \cdot 6 = 12$).
step: Find the missing fact: $n - 1$ and $n + 1$ are two consecutive even numbers, so one of them is divisible by $4$. Write $n = 2k + 1$ with $k$ an integer.
step: Compute: $n - 1 = 2k$ and $n + 1 = 2k + 2 = 2(k + 1)$, so $n^2 - 1 = 4k(k + 1)$.
step: Now $k(k+1)$ is a product of two consecutive integers, so it is even: $k(k+1) = 2j$ for an integer $j$ (one of $k$, $k+1$ is even).
step: Hence $n^2 - 1 = 4 \cdot 2j = 8j$, which is divisible by $8$.
answer: The gap was the jump from "both even" to "divisible by $8$"; the fix is $n^2 - 1 = 4k(k+1)$ with $k(k+1)$ even.

@mistakes
- Proving the converse: showing "if $3 \nmid n$ then ..." when the claim is "if $3 \mid n^2$ then ...", and not noticing which direction was proved.
- Writing a long calculation without sentences, so the reader cannot tell what is assumed, what is derived and what is the goal.
- Reusing a variable: "Let $k$ be an integer ... now let $k$ be the quotient" silently changes what $k$ means.
- Writing "clearly" for the one step that needs a reason. If you cannot explain a step in a sentence, you may not yet understand it.

choice*: Suppose $x$ is even, so $x = 2k$ for some integer $k$; we show $x^2$ is even.
choice: Suppose $x^2$ is even, so $x^2 = 2k$ for some integer $k$; we show $x$ is even.

hint: A direct proof of $P \Rightarrow Q$ assumes $P$ and derives $Q$. Which statement is $P$ here, and what must $k$ be?
solution: A direct proof assumes the hypothesis ($x$ is even) and derives the conclusion ($x^2$ is even). The choice assuming $x^2$ even starts from the conclusion, the one with a real $k$ does not say what $k$ is (it must be an integer), and the one assuming $x$ odd proves a different statement.

@practice ms
prompt: For statements $P$ and $Q$, which of these are logically equivalent to "$P \Rightarrow Q$"?
choice*: $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$ (the contrapositive).
choice*: $(\neg P) \text{ or } Q$.
choice: $Q \Rightarrow P$ (the converse).
choice: $\neg P \Rightarrow \neg Q$ (the inverse).
hint: Check the truth table when $P$ is false and $Q$ is true.
solution: $P \Rightarrow Q$ is false only when $P$ is true and $Q$ is false, which is also when $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$ is false and when "($\neg P$) or $Q$" is false. The converse and the inverse are false when $P$ is false and $Q$ is true, a case where $P \Rightarrow Q$ is true.

@practice mc
prompt: Which of these statements about integers is true?
choice*: For every integer $n$ there is an integer $m$ with $m > n$.
choice: There is an integer $m$ such that for every integer $n$, $m > n$.
choice: For every integer $n$ there is an integer $m$ with $m^2 < n$.
choice: There is a natural number $n$ such that every natural number $m$ satisfies $m \le n$.
hint: Test each statement: pick $n$ first or $m$ first, and note which order the quantifiers fix.
solution: The first is true: take $m = n + 1$. The second is false, since $n = m$ gives $m > m$. The third fails at $n = 0$ (no integer has square less than $0$). The fourth says there is a largest natural number, which is false. Swapping the order of quantifiers changes the meaning.

@practice self
prompt: Write a proof, in complete sentences, that for every integer $n$, if $n^2$ is odd then $n$ is odd. State your method at the start.
hint: Prove the contrapositive: if $n$ is even, then $n^2$ is even.
solution: We prove the contrapositive: if $n$ is even then $n^2$ is even; this is equivalent to the claim. Let $n$ be an even integer. By definition $n = 2k$ for some integer $k$. Then $n^2 = 4k^2 = 2(2k^2)$, and $2k^2$ is an integer, so $n^2$ is even. This proves the contrapositive, and therefore if $n^2$ is odd, then $n$ is odd. $\blacksquare$
rubric: Names the method (contrapositive) and states the contrapositive correctly.
rubric: Introduces $k$ as an integer from the definition of even and computes $n^2 = 2(2k^2)$.
rubric: Concludes with the original statement and not only the contrapositive.

@practice self
prompt: This is the block deliverable for weeks 9 and 10: rewrite a messy proof. Below is a draft proof of the claim "if $a$ and $b$ are integers and $3 \mid a^2 + b^2$, then $3 \mid a$ and $3 \mid b$."

Draft: "$a^2 + b^2 = 3k$. Squares mod $3$ are $0$ or $1$. If $a^2 = 1$ then $b^2 = 3k - 1 = 2$ so impossible. So $a^2 = 0$, same for $b$, done."

Rewrite it as a proof that a classmate who knows only basic remainders can follow. State the claim fully, prove the facts the draft uses without proof, justify every step, and name any case you split into.
hint: First prove the lemma: if $3 \nmid n$ then $n^2$ leaves remainder $1$ on division by $3$. Then use it for $a$ and $b$.
hint: The draft writes "=" where it means "leaves remainder"; also, from "$a^2$ leaves remainder $0$" you still have to conclude $3 \mid a$.
solution: **Claim.** For all integers $a, b$, if $3 \mid a^2 + b^2$ then $3 \mid a$ and $3 \mid b$. **Lemma.** For every integer $n$, $n^2$ leaves remainder $0$ or $1$ on division by $3$; and it leaves remainder $0$ only if $3 \mid n$. *Proof of the lemma.* Write $n = 3q + r$ with $r \in \{0, 1, 2\}$. If $r = 0$ then $n^2 = 3(3q^2)$ has remainder $0$. If $r = 1$ then $n^2 = 3(3q^2 + 2q) + 1$, and if $r = 2$ then $n^2 = 3(3q^2 + 4q + 1) + 1$; in both cases the remainder is $1$. Hence the remainder $0$ occurs only when $r = 0$, that is, only when $3 \mid n$. *Proof of the claim.* Suppose $3 \mid a^2 + b^2$. We show $3 \mid a$ by contradiction. Suppose $3 \nmid a$. By the lemma, $a^2$ leaves remainder $1$. Since $3$ divides $a^2 + b^2$, $b^2$ leaves remainder $2$ (as $1 + 2 = 3$) on division by $3$, which the lemma shows is impossible. So $3 \mid a$ and then $3 \mid a^2$, so $3 \mid b^2$, and the lemma gives $3 \mid b$. $\blacksquare$
rubric: States the claim with quantifiers and hypotheses and separates the lemma about squares modulo $3$ from the main argument.
rubric: Proves the lemma by splitting on the remainder of $n$ and computing each case.
rubric: Derives $3 \mid a$ by contradiction and then $3 \mid b$ from $3 \mid b^2$, with each step justified in a sentence and with a correct ending.

@quiz mc
prompt: Which statement is the negation of "for every integer $n > 1$ some prime divides $n$"?
choice*: There is an integer $n > 1$ that no prime divides.
choice: For every integer $n > 1$, no prime divides $n$.
choice: There is an integer $n > 1$ that some prime divides.
choice: For every integer $n > 1$, every prime divides $n$.
