@article
Place $n$ points on a circle and draw every chord between two of them, with no three chords meeting at an interior point. Into how many regions is the disk cut? Counting by hand gives $1, 2, 4, 8, 16$ for $n = 1, \dots, 5$. The pattern $2^{n-1}$ is irresistible, and it is wrong: for $n = 6$ there are $31$ regions. (The true count is $\binom{n}{4} + \binom{n}{2} + 1$, which can be proved by counting vertices, edges and faces of the resulting planar figure.) Patterns are the best source of conjectures and a poor source of certainty. This lesson is about using them well.

### The method

1. **Make a table.** Compute the quantity for $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ carefully, by hand or by a program. Organize by the natural parameters.
2. **Look for structure.** Take differences $a_{n+1} - a_n$ and ratios $a_{n+1}/a_n$. Constant first differences suggest a linear formula, constant second differences a quadratic one, and a constant ratio an exponential. Factor the values; compare with known sequences.
3. **State a conjecture precisely**, with its range of $n$.
4. **Test it on a case you did not use** to find it. Agreement on fresh data is real evidence; agreement on the data you fitted proves nothing.
5. **Prove it**, or find a counterexample. Ask *why* the pattern holds: the proof often follows the structure that made the pattern visible.

A formula fitted to $m$ values by a polynomial of degree $m - 1$ always matches those values, so a formula is only interesting if it is simple and survives fresh tests.

### A pattern in greatest common divisors

Let us compute $\gcd(2^a - 1, 2^b - 1)$ for small $a, b$.

```python
from math import gcd

for a in range(1, 7):
    print([gcd(2 ** a - 1, 2 ** b - 1) for b in range(1, 7)])
```

The output is the table

| $a \backslash b$ | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| 2 | 1 | 3 | 1 | 3 | 1 | 3 |
| 3 | 1 | 1 | 7 | 1 | 1 | 7 |
| 4 | 1 | 3 | 1 | 15 | 1 | 3 |
| 5 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 31 | 1 |
| 6 | 1 | 3 | 7 | 3 | 1 | 63 |

Every entry has the form $2^d - 1$: the diagonal entries are $2^a - 1$, and the entry for $(4, 6)$ is $3 = 2^2 - 1$ where $\gcd(4, 6) = 2$; the entry for $(6, 3)$ is $7 = 2^3 - 1$ and $\gcd(6, 3) = 3$. This suggests a conjecture.

**Theorem.** For all positive integers $a$ and $b$, $\gcd(2^a - 1, 2^b - 1) = 2^{\gcd(a, b)} - 1$.

*Proof.* Write $G(a, b) = \gcd(2^a - 1, 2^b - 1)$ and use strong induction on $a + b$. If $a = b$, then $G(a, a) = 2^a - 1 = 2^{\gcd(a,a)} - 1$. Otherwise, by symmetry, assume $a > b$. Then
$$2^a - 1 = 2^{a-b}(2^b - 1) + (2^{a-b} - 1).$$
So a common divisor of $2^a - 1$ and $2^b - 1$ divides $2^{a-b} - 1$, and a common divisor of $2^b - 1$ and $2^{a-b} - 1$ divides $2^a - 1$. The two pairs have the same common divisors, hence $G(a, b) = G(a - b, b)$. The positive integers $a - b$ and $b$ have smaller sum, so by the inductive hypothesis $G(a - b, b) = 2^{\gcd(a-b, b)} - 1$. Finally $\gcd(a - b, b) = \gcd(a, b)$, because common divisors of $a, b$ and of $a - b, b$ coincide. $\blacksquare$

The proof is the Euclidean algorithm for exponents, which is exactly what the table suggested. Notice what the table could not tell us: whether the pattern persists for large $a$ and $b$. The proof does.

### Counterexamples are data too

A single counterexample ends a conjecture. When a conjecture fails, look at *where* it fails: the failure often shows what the right statement is. In the chord problem the data $1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 31$ already says that the answer is not a power of $2$, and it points toward counting by a different structure, such as choosing four points to determine each interior crossing.

@example
title: Guess and prove a counting formula
problem: Let $E(n)$ be the number of pairs $(i, j)$ of integers with $1 \le i < j \le n$ and $i + j$ even. Find a formula for $E(n)$ and prove it.
step: Tabulate: for $n = 2, 3, \dots, 8$ the values of $E(n)$ are $0, 1, 2, 4, 6, 9, 12$. The first differences are $1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 3$, which grow by one every second step.
step: That growth pattern suggests $E(n) = \lfloor (n-1)^2 / 4 \rfloor$. It fits all seven values (for example $n = 7$: $36/4 = 9$) and we have not yet tested it on a new case, say $n = 9$: it predicts $\lfloor 64/4 \rfloor = 16$.
step: Structure: $i + j$ is even exactly when $i$ and $j$ have the same parity. Among $1, \dots, n$ there are $\lceil n/2 \rceil$ odd and $\lfloor n/2 \rfloor$ even numbers, so $E(n) = \binom{\lceil n/2 \rceil}{2} + \binom{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor}{2}$. For $n = 9$: $\binom{5}{2} + \binom{4}{2} = 10 + 6 = 16$, as predicted.
step: Prove it. If $n = 2m$: $E = 2\binom{m}{2} = m(m-1) = m^2 - m$, and $(n-1)^2/4 = (4m^2 - 4m + 1)/4 = m^2 - m + \tfrac14$, whose floor is $m^2 - m$. If $n = 2m + 1$: $E = \binom{m+1}{2} + \binom{m}{2} = \frac{(m+1)m + m(m-1)}{2} = m^2$, and $(n-1)^2/4 = m^2$.
answer: $E(n) = \lfloor (n-1)^2/4 \rfloor$.

@example
title: Lines in the plane
problem: Let $R(n)$ be the number of regions into which $n$ lines divide the plane when no two are parallel and no three pass through a common point. Find and prove a formula.
step: Draw small cases: $R(0) = 1$, $R(1) = 2$, $R(2) = 4$, $R(3) = 7$, $R(4) = 11$. The differences are $1, 2, 3, 4$.
step: Conjecture: $R(n) - R(n-1) = n$, so $R(n) = 1 + (1 + 2 + \dots + n) = 1 + \frac{n(n+1)}{2}$. Test on a case not used: this predicts $R(5) = 16$.
step: Why the difference is $n$: add the $n$-th line to $n - 1$ lines. It meets each earlier line in one point, and these $n - 1$ points are distinct (no three lines concurrent), so they cut the new line into $n$ pieces. Each piece divides one existing region into two, so exactly $n$ new regions appear.
step: Hence $R(n) = R(n-1) + n$ with $R(0) = 1$, and summing gives $R(n) = 1 + \frac{n(n+1)}{2}$. For $n = 5$ this is $16$.
answer: $R(n) = 1 + \dfrac{n(n+1)}{2}$.

@mistakes
- Fitting a formula to all the data you have and calling it confirmed: a conjecture must be tested on a case you did not use.
- Assuming a pattern continues because it held for five or six terms; the chord-region counts $1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 31$ break at the sixth.
- Computing small cases carelessly: one arithmetic slip in a table can send you to a wrong conjecture, so recompute a suspicious value in a second way.
- Stopping at the conjecture. The result of this phase is a statement to prove, not an answer.

Write `poly_degree(seq)` that takes a list of integers and returns the least $d \ge 0$ such that the $d$-th difference list has at least two entries and all its entries equal, or `None` if there is no such $d$. For example, `poly_degree([2, 7, 18, 35, 58])` returns `2`, and `poly_degree([1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32])` returns `None`. A constant list has degree $0$.
starter:
```python

```
tests:
```python
assert poly_degree([2, 7, 18, 35, 58]) == 2
assert poly_degree([5, 5, 5]) == 0
assert poly_degree([1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32]) is None
assert poly_degree([1, 8, 27, 64, 125, 216]) == 3
assert poly_degree([3, 5]) is None
assert poly_degree([0, 3, 6, 9]) == 1
assert poly_degree([1, 0, 1, 0, 1]) is None
```
model:
```python
def poly_degree(seq):
    """Least d such that the d-th differences are constant (2+ entries)."""
    current = list(seq)
    d = 0
    while len(current) >= 2:
        if all(x == current[0] for x in current):
            return d
        current = [b - a for a, b in zip(current, current[1:])]
        d += 1
    return None
```
hint: Check whether all entries are equal; if not, replace the list by its differences and add one to a counter.
solution: Repeat: if the current list has at least two entries and they are all equal, return the number of difference steps taken; otherwise take differences. When fewer than two entries remain, no degree was found. A list that merely runs out of entries (such as the powers of $2$) shows why the length condition matters: a single entry is always "constant". Constant differences of a short list are only evidence that a polynomial fits, not proof.

hint: Split $\frac{1}{k(k+2)}$ into a difference of two simple fractions so that the sum telescopes.
solution: The values are $S(1) = \frac13$, $S(2) = \frac{11}{24}$, $S(3) = \frac{21}{40}$, $S(4) = \frac{17}{30}$, $S(5) = \frac{25}{42}$, each a little below $\frac34$. Partial fractions give $\frac{1}{k(k+2)} = \frac12\left(\frac1k - \frac1{k+2}\right)$. Summing for $k = 1, \dots, n$, all terms cancel except the first two positive and the last two negative ones: $S(n) = \frac12\left(1 + \frac12 - \frac1{n+1} - \frac1{n+2}\right) = \frac34 - \frac{2n+3}{2(n+1)(n+2)}$, since $\frac{1}{n+1} + \frac{1}{n+2} = \frac{2n+3}{(n+1)(n+2)}$. Check: $n = 3$ gives $\frac34 - \frac{9}{40} = \frac{21}{40}$.
rubric: Computes the five values correctly as fractions.

@quiz mc
prompt: Six values of a sequence $a_1, \dots, a_6$ agree with a simple conjectured formula that you found by studying differences. What is the best next step?
choice*: Compute $a_7$ (and perhaps $a_8$) independently and compare with the formula, then look for a proof.
choice: Declare the formula proved, since six cases agree.
choice: Replace the formula by the degree-$5$ polynomial through all six points, which matches every value.
choice: Discard the formula unless the first differences are constant.
hint: Which step gives new information, and which only reuses the data?
solution: A prediction tested on a fresh case is real evidence, and the goal is a proof. A polynomial through the data always fits and carries no information; agreement on six values does not prove a statement for all $n$; and non-constant first differences only rule out a linear formula.
